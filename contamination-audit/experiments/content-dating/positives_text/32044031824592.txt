PREFACE TO THE FIRST EDITION 

The best knowledge of the law cannot be had from text-books alone. 

The reasons for this fact are obvious and have led to the adoption of the case system of instruction in the majority of law schools. The danger, however, of using cases alone is that at the end of a course the student finds that although he has learned many principles and their corollaries and exceptions, he lacks in a measure an orderly knowledge of the subject. An outline in the form of a brief text book, to be used in conjunction with the reading of cases, is, therefore, essential. This book is such an outline, and was designed to serve its purpose best when used with Edgar's Leading Cases on the Law of Torts. It can, however, be profitably used with any other case book, or with the reading of cases in the original reports; and even without cases, it is intended to give the student who uses it a working knowledge of the subject.

It deals with general principles of the law of torts, but since it is to be used in the State of New York, it contains New York law. Important rules of other jurisdictions, however, are set forth when they will be of value or of interest to the New York student.

OUTLINE OF THE LAW OF TORTS CHAPTER I. 

INTRODUCTION Section 1. The Seven Grand Divisions of Jurisprudence. 

Jurisprudence is generally divided into seven grand divisions, namely: 

1. Persons 2. Property 

3.

Contracts 

4.

Torts 5. Crimes 

6. 7.

Remedies Government. 

The subject matter of each of these heads is necessarily related to that of the others. This outline deals with the principle's of jurisprudence, or law, properly considered under the fourth head.

Sec. 2. History of the Subject. The law of torts as a sep arate division of jurisprudence has been dealt with by writers only in comparatively recent years. The principles governing liability in tort, being, as they are, the principles determining whether or not legal wrong to person or property has been committed, were probably the earliest known in the law. Tort liability appears to have been the first form of liability known to our Norman and Anglo-Saxon predecessors, and at least one of the first known to the Greeks, the Romans, and among the Teutonic tribes. Moses, the Hebrew law-giver, promulgated a fairly simple but well defined system of tort liability. Although as early as 1720 a treatise appeared dealing with certain particular torts, which had long been known as civil wrongs, it was not until the middle of the last century that legal scientists came to Ideal with the law of torts as a systematized branch of jurisprudence, and at about the same time the first text books on the subject appeared, namely, Hilliard, in 1859, in the United States, and Addison, in 1860, in England.

Sec. 3. A Tort Is a Breach of Legal Duty. The law of torts deals with private rights, both in property (personal and real), and in person. Thus under this head of the law are to be considered the principles determining whether private rights in property, or the private rights of personal liberty and personal security, or other private rights, have been invaded. Since the invasion of a right is a wrong, and since there never can be a right without a duty to observe it, it follows that the invasion of a private right is a breach of duty. The law of torts is, therefore, based on rights and duties arising by law. However, see 

Sections 6, 7 and 8, infra.

Sec. 4. A Purpose of the Law Is to Provide a Legal 

Remedy for Every Legal Wrong. One of the maxims of the law is "ubi jus, ibi, remedium" (where there is a right, there is a remedy). The meaning is clearer if it is said, “Where there is a wrong, there is a remedy." Although this may not be strictly true (Booth v. Rome, etc., Co., 140 N. Y. 267, Edgar's Leading 

Cases on the Law of Torts, page 41; Guest v. Reynolds, 68 Ill. 

478, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 1), for the reason that the law cannot enforce every ethical and moral right, yet one of the purposes of the law is to provide a legal remedy for every legal wrong, that is, for every breach of legal duty, which is to say, for every invasion of a private legal right (Rich v. New York 

Central and Hudson River R. R., 87 N. Y. 382, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 24; Kujek v. Goldman, 150 N. Y. 176, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 8).

Sec. 5. The Fact That An Action Is Without Precedent Is No Bar to Its Maintenance. No matter how novel the action or its form, if a breach of legal duty (injuria), coupled with legal damages (damnum), be shown by the plaintiff, a recovery will be allowed. (Kujek v. Goldman, supra.) See infra, Section 138.

Sec. 6. The Duty Breached Must Be One Imposed by the 

Law As Distinguished From Contract. It is necessary, however, that the breach of duty shown be the breach of a duty imposed not by contract or agreement between the parties, but by the law (Rich v. New York Central, etc., supra). In the Rich cases, it so happened that one of certain acts together amounting to a breach of legal duty, i. e., à tort, was a breach of contract. The New York Court of Appeals held that plaintiff might sue either for the tort or for the breach of contract.

Sec. 7. The Law and a Contract May Impose Identical 

Duties. Sometimes contracts impose upon the parties to them duties identical with those the law creates. A bailee of goods is under a legal duty to use care in his custody of them. A breach of that duty is, therefore, a tort. Since a bailment is a contract, one of the terms of which is a promise, express or implied, by the bailee, to use care, the failure to use care is not only a tort but a breach of contract. An action against a negligent bailee can, therefore, be maintained on either theory. See next section.

Sec. 8. In Order That An Action Sounding In Tort May Be Maintained, a Legal Duty Must Exist Independently of or in Ad dition to Contract. Sometimes, as in the Rich case, supra, not so clear a state of facts is presented. For, as the Court of Appeals said in that case, "Between actions plainly ex contractu and those ex delicto there exists what has been termed a border-land, where the lines of distinction are shadowy and obscure, and the tort and the contract so approach each other, and become so nearly coincident as to make their practical separation somewhat difficult." However, where both contractual and legal duties exist and have been breached, an action on either theory may be maintained. But in order to sue in tort, a plaintiff must be able to show a breach of duty imposed by law. Perhaps a contract has also imposed the duty for the breach of which the plaintiff seeks to recover. But whether a contract imposes the duty or not, the law must impose it, with or without the contract, or the plaintiff cannot sue in tort. In the case of a bailment, were it not for the contract of bailment there would not be any possession of property to create a duty of care. The failure to use care is a breach of contract. But the law steps in and says, "The failure to use care is the tort negligence. So negligence in a bailment, which is a breach of contract, may be treated as a tort, even though the contract created the duty, for when the need for care exists, whether under a contract or not, the duty to use care is a legal duty as well as a contractual one." 

Sec. 9. The Duty Need Not Be a Common Law Duty; It May Be Imposed By Statute. The greater part of English and 

American law is found in the reported opinions of courts in deciding actual cases. These precedents are followed in deciding later and similar cases. The law thus developed is called the

common law. It was under the common law that the earlier cases of civil wrongs arose, and so it was in the reported opinions of judges that the principles determining liability for torts were first laid down. Those principles are in the main still followed, the duties thus created are duties imposed by law, and the breaches of such duties torts. But we also have statutes which are the enactments of legislative bodies, and these statutes are as much a part of our law as is the common law, or case law. 

Whenever a statute imposes a civil duty, such duty is as much a duty imposed by law as if it were a common law duty, and a breach of a civil duty imposed by statute is as truly a tort as if it were a breach of a common law duty (Willy v. Mulledy, 78 

N. Y. 310; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 14).

Sec. 10. The Law of Torts Is In the Main a Common Law Subject; But Statutes May Change Common Law Duties. In the main, the law of torts rests upon common law principles. and is said to be a common law subject. It is statutory only so far as statutes have altered, added to, or abrogated common law principles. For example: At common law a landlord was under no duty to keep in repair premises demised to a tenant (Witty v. Matthews, 52 N. Y. 512), and consequently if a ceiling fell and injured a tenant, the landlord was not liable for the personal injuries. Even if the landlord had contracted with the tenant to repair the ceiling, there would have been no liability in tort in case the ceiling fell; the tenant could recover only for the breach of the contract, and the measure of damages would be the mere cost of repair (Witty v. Matthews, supra). This is because there was only a contractual (not a legal) duty to repair, and so the tenant's cause of action would be on the contract only, and not in tort. It is difficult to understand why the courts did not say in such a case what they said in making negligence in a bailment a tort, and hold that such a contract as this, to repair, creates a legal duty, the breach of which is a tort for which damages even to person, if suffered, can be recovered. Undue adherence to the old principle (itself unduly favorable to the landlord class) by which landlords were not required to repair is the reason for the decision in Witty v. Matthews. But in New York there is a statute known as the Multiple Dwellings Law, of which Section 78, formerly Tenement House Law, Section 102, places upon landlords and under certain circumstances upon ten-

ants as well a duty to keep all parts of multiple dwelling houses in repair. Consequently if a ceiling in a tenement house falls and injures a tenant he can now sue for personal injuries, if the landlord had notice of the defect (Altz v. Leiberson, 233 N. Y. 

16) and if the fault was not his own (Multiple Dwellings Law, Sec. 78). This statute applies to houses or buildings occupied or meant to be occupied by three or more families living independently of each other (Multiple Dwellings Law, Sec. 4, sub. 

9). There is no duty upon a landlord toward his tenant to repair demised premises unless those premises are within the Multiple Dwellings Law. But under the common law no such duty existed even with respect to a tenement house, so that, where multiple dwelling houses are concerned, there is now a duty imposed by statute to keep in repair. This is a "duty imposed by law"; a breach of that duty, coupled with legal damage, is a tort. 

See infra, Sec. 152.

Sec. 11. A Tort Consists of Both Injuria and Damnum; 

The Injuria May Be An Act or An Omission. Injuria (breach of legal duty) and damnum (legal damage) are elements of all torts. Both must be present, or there is no tort. The injuria may consist as well of an omission to do an act which the law imposes the duty of doing, as of the doing of an act which the law says there is a duty to refrain from doing. The basis of the duty to respect the rights of others is the maxim "So use your own as not to injure (the legal rights of) another." ("Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas.")

Sec. 12. A Tort Consists of Both Injuria and Damnum; 

The Damnum Must Proximately Follow the Injuria. The dam num (or legal damage) must be the proximate, or direct, result of the injuria. (See the discussion of "proximate cause," infra, Secs. 36-40.)

Sec. 13. The Remedy For Tort Is Generally An Action for 

Money Damages. Although we will deal with various remedies for tortious acts, such as injunction and self-help, we are primarily interested in this subject in actions for money damages to compensate injured persons for torts committed upon them. 

Sec. 14. Definition. A workable definition of a tort is, therefore, the following: A tort is a breach of duty established or imposed by law (as distinguished from duty assumed by contract, express or implied) proximately resulting in damage

(actual or inferred), for which an action for damages may be maintained.

Sec. 15. Nonfeasance, Misfeasance, and Malfeasance. The breach of duty (injuria), may, as we have seen, be an omission, or an act of commission. When it is an omission, it is frequently termed nonfeasance, which has been defined as the omission of an act which one ought to do. When an act of commission is the breach relied upon, it may be either the improper doing of what one may lawfully do (misfeasance), or the doing of that which one ought not to do at all (malfeasance).

Sec. 16. Tort Distinguished From Breach of Contract; 

Election of Remedies. We have seen that sometimes a given act or omission may amount to both a breach of contract and a tort. 

When this is true, but the two theories are inconsistent with each other, the plaintiff must elect between the two remedies. 

For example, if A, B, and C wrongfully take property belonging to D, the law says that D may treat the taking as an implied contract of sale, obligating A, B, and C to pay to D the reasonable value of the property as the purchase price of it, upon the theory that by taking it, A, B, and C impliedly promised to pay for it. Or, if D desires, he may treat the taking as a breach of the duty the law imposes upon A, B, and C to respect his (D's) rights in the property, and may sue for the tort of conversion. 

But D must choose between his remedies; he cannot avail himself of both of them, for they are inconsistent, in that, by suing in contract (for the price), D is treating ownership in the property as having passed to A, B, and C, while by suing in tort, D rests upon the theory that ownership never passed, and that the taking was a wrongful dominion over his (D's) property. The commencement of the action on the theory of contract is an irrevocable election, and plaintiff can never thereafter sue in tort on the same claim (Terry v. Munger, 121 N. Y. 161; Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 19; Zurlick v. Stankus, 125 Misc. 457).

Sec. 17. Tort Distinguished From Crime; Rules of Burden of Proof. A crime is an offense against the government; a tort is an invasion of the private rights of a person. The same act may be both a tort and a crime, as, if A assault B, A may be proceeded against in a criminal prosecution, and B may sue A for damages. In England the early view was that where the same act was both a tort and a crime, the private person lost

his rights and only the criminal prosecution could be maintained. American courts never adopted that view, which England later abandoned, and now, in both jurisdictions, the rule is that both criminal and civil actions may be prosecuted. (Civil 

Practice Act Sec. 9). Some American courts require that the civil action await the termination of the criminal proceeding, but most states, including New York, permit the two to be maintained concurrently. In criminal prosecutions proof of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt is required (Holt v. United States, 218 U. S. 245; People v. Trimarchi, 231 N. Y. 263, 267). In tort actions, as in all civil actions, proof of liability by a fair preponderance of the evidence is all that is required. (Kurz v. Doerr, 180 N. Y. 88).

CHAPTER II HARMS, OR APPARENT WRONGS, THAT ARE NOT TORTS. 

Sec. 18. Damnum Absque Injuria and Injuria Sine Damno. 

As we have seen, every tort consists of both injuria (breach of legal duty) and damnum (legal damage proximately resulting). If the one or the other is lacking, an apparent wrong or harm is not an actionable tort. Under existing theories of legal responsibility, damages alone cannot give rise liability. For example, in the case of Booth v. Rome, etc., Co., supra., Sec. 4, the plaintiff's property was damaged by the concussion of blasting done by the defendant on nearby land. There was no proof of negligence or other breach of legal duty. It was held that the plaintiff could not recover. 

The apparent harm to him was "damnum absque injuria," or damage without legal wrong. Likewise, though a legal duty has been breached, if legal damages (i. e., what the law says are proper items of damage for which the law will compensate) have not followed, there is no cause of action, and the apparent wrong is spoken of as “injuria sine damno," or legal wrong (i. e. breach of legal duty) without legal damage. An example of the latter is found wherever proof of damage is lacking in a case where the law says damage must be proved. However, it must be remembered, first, that in most cases nominal damages are allowed where there is no proof of substantial damages, and second, that in some torts, even substantial damages need not be proved to be recovered; that is because the law infers damage from the fact of the injuria in many cases, as for example in cases of criminal conversation, in which the defilement of the marriage bed is fraught with sufficient possibilities to be a damage in itself, the possibilities, when and if realized, being special damages, compensable if pleaded and proved. See infra, Sec. 47. For a discussion of the necessity of proving damages, see Chicago, etc. Ry. v. Rend, 6 Ill. App. 243, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 195.

Sec. 19. Some Harms May Therefore Be Lawful. The maxim "Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas" cannot, of course,

9. prohibit lawful acts. In reason, if the defendant had a legal right to do the thing complained of, the plaintiff cannot complain that the doing of it has damaged him. There may have been "damnum," but there was no "injuria." This is true even though the thing was done maliciously, with the very purpose of bringing about the harm the plaintiff suffered. It has, however, been frequently said in cases that a malicious abuse of a legal right is a tort.

Logically there cannot be any such thing, for the right ends where the so-called abuse begins, and so the abuse is an act of unlawfulness independently of any pre-existing right. It is more correct to say that any use of a right or license for a purpose not intended by the law is a tort in and of itself, when damage results, and renders the entire transaction void ab initio, abrogating at least temporarily the right or license and making unlawful all things done in furtherance of it, even those things which would have been lawful had there not been a departure from the lawful purpose. Examples of these so-called abuses (which are not mere abuses but actually independent wrongs) are malicious abuse of process (e. g., its use to enforce the payment of a debt) and excessive attachment (Ch. VIII, B, infra). See "misfeasance" and "malfeasance," Sec. 15, supra. 

Sec. 20. Arrest of An Innocent Person. Although the right of personal liberty is one jealously guarded by our law (as we shall see when we take up the various particular torts dealing with invasions of this right), yet the law frequently permits the deprivation of personal liberty to go unredressed. In New York, when a felony has in fact been committed, if a peace officer, even without a warrant, arrests a person whom he has reasonable and probable cause to believe to be the one who committed the felony, the peace officer is excused, although the person arrested be, in fact, innocent of crime (Code of Criminal Procedure, Sec. 177). The reason underlying this is that the public security is a matter of paramount importance, and the arrested person's right of personal liberty must sometimes be subordinated to it.

Sec. 21. The Privilege of Legislators. Another example of the sacrifice of private rights to the public good is found in that rule of Anglo-American law which excuses legislators

(members of Parliament, Congress, and state legislatures) from liability for defamatory statements made by them while acting as legislators (i.e., on the floor of the house while it is in session, or on committee work, or in communicating with the governor on legislative matters). Statements unofficially made, however, are not privileged. See Coffin v. Coffin, 4 Mass. 1;

Woods v. Winan, 122 N. Y. 445. See also U. S. Const. Art. 1, Sec. 6, par. 1; N. Y. Const. Art. III, Sec. 12. 

Sec. 22. Judicial Exemption or Privilege. For the same reasons, namely, in order to insure independent and fearless action in the administration of public affairs, a similar privilege exists in favor of judges while acting as such (Valesh v. Prince, 94 Misc. 479, affirmed 163 N. Y. Supp. 1133), and in favor of counsel in making statements material to the issue before the court (Gallagher v. Surpless, 163 N. Y. Supp. 551; Lesser v. Int. 

Trust Co., 175 A. D. 12). But the privilege does not exist unless the officer was acting as such officer, and in the case of a judge, he must have had jurisdiction of the person and the subject matter. In such a case he enjoys immunity not only from the consequences of defamation committed by him, but also from civil liability for all judicial error (Lange v. Benedict, 73 N. Y. 12).

However, a magistrate who issues a warrant without an affidavit to support it acts without jurisdiction and is liable (Blodgett v. Race, 18 Hun 132; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 225).

Sec. 23. Harms Inflicted by the Sovereignty. As we shall again see when we consider the topic "Parties to Tort Actions," the sovereignty (whether it be the Crown of England, or, in this country, the United States or one of the states) cannot be sued unless it consents. Consequently, unlawful acts (which would be torts were it not for the non-suability of the sovereign) committed by act of state are not actionable as torts; that is, without consent of the sovereignty through legislation, the government cannot be sued to redress them (United States v. Clarke, 8 

Pet. 436; Stanley v. Schwalby, 162 U. S. 225; Locke v. Stäte, 140 

N. Y. 480; see note 13 A. L. R. 1276). The rule thus limiting the liability of the sovereignty is based partly on the old idea that all acts of the sovereign are lawful because the king can do no wrong, and partly upon the reason that even if the king or the state were able to do a wrong thing, to redress it would require that the courts and the executive branch of the govern-

ment use their powers against the sovereignty which is the source of those powers. However, the officer through whom the sovereignty acts in doing an unlawful harm is liable although the sovereignty is not (United States v. Lee, 106 U. S. 196). But it must not be forgotten that this statement of liability of government officials applies only to ministerial and not judicial officers. As to the latter, the rules of privilege above discussed (Sec. 22, supra) apply.

Sec. 24. Police Power and Eminent Domain. Although the 

Constitution of the United States forbids the taking of life, liberty, or property without due process of law (See Amendments V and XIV), certain emergencies, justifying an apparent violation of this constitutional safeguard, sometimes arise. For example, the destruction of buildings in the path of a conflagration in order to check its spreading (Russell v. Mayor, 2 Denio 

461) and the isolation of those infected with contagious diseases (Crayton v. Larabee, 220 N. Y. 493) are justified under the police power, although no compensation is given to those whose liberty or property is thus affected. Legislation which has for its purpose the safeguarding of the public welfare is, by virtue of the police power, upheld as constitutional, though it may authorize the taking of liberty or property (Lawton v. Steele, 152 U. S. 133). In order to be a proper exercise of the police power, the act complained of must have for its purpose the protection of the public health, safety, morals, welfare, or resources, and must be reasonably necessary and not unduly oppressive (all cases supra). When a statute is justifiable under the police power, the officer acting under it is protected, that is, no action is maintainable against him (Lawton v. Steele, supra). The sovereignty, of course, cannot be sued without its consent whether the apparent wrong be justified under the police power or not (Sec. 23 supra). But if the case be one of a proper exercise of the police power, as may be seen from the Lawton case, the officer through whom the state acted in committing the apparent wrong is protected, although if the act be not a proper exercise of the police power he is liable (Sec. 23, supra). The police power differs from the right of the state to condemn private property to its own use, in that, in the exercise of the latter right (called eminent domain), the state must compensate for the property taken. The modern tendency, however, is toward

granting compensation even in cases where private rights are affected by proper exercises of the police power; for example, under the Farms and Markets Law of New York diseased animals may be taken from their owners, but the state must compensate such owners. This is as an incentive to farmers to co-operate with the law. Even if no compensation were provided for, the enactment would be a valid exercise of the police power, for it has for its purpose the safeguarding of the public welfare, and is necessary to protect healthy animals. Such an instance must not be confused with the exercise of eminent domain. Eminent domain is distinguished by the fact that it authorizes the taking of private property for public use, and compensation is not optional but required.

Sec. 25. Inevitable Accident. When, even by exercise of reasonable care, an occurrence cannot be avoided, damages for the consequences of the occurrence, though such consequences be severe personal injuries, cannot be had. The harm in such a case is the result of an inevitable accident, and no legal duty having been breached, the plaintiff is without a remedy. The old rule was that a recovery could be had on the theory that when one of two innocent persons must suffer, the loss falls on him whose act occasioned the harm. But today the courts are more logical, and apply the test of whether or not a legal duty has been breached. So, if due care and all proper precautions have been used, there is no recovery for accidental harm (Brown v. Kendall, 6 Cush. (Mass.) 292, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 52; 

Burkes v. Lieberman, 218 App. Div. 600). Ultimately, by legislative and constitutional aid, we shall evolve a system under which, by the state or through compulsory insurance, all harms will be redressed. The Workmen's Compensation Law (infra, 

Ch. IV) was a major step in this direction. See also infra, Sec. 

150; cf. infra, Sec. 49.

Sec. 26. Defense of Self and Family. To justify harm com mitted upon the person of another in self-defense, the act must be in the honest and reasonable belief of immediate danger. The questions of whether the belief was honest and reasonable, and whether only necessary force was used, are for the jury. Only such force as is necessary to repel the attack of the other may be used. Force may be used if the fear of harm is reasonable, even though the apparent attacker has no intent to injure but

only the appearance of such intent. An equal right to use necessary force in defense of master, servant, husband, wife, child, parent, brother or sister exists (Scribner v. Beach, 4 

Denio 448, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 55). 

Sec. 27. Defense of Property. A similar right to use force to defend and retain property exists. Again no more force than is necessary may be used. But one may go to greater lengths in defending his home or his land against trespassers by force, or against invaders and burglars, than against mere peaceful, ordinary, or accidental trespassers. There is, however, no right of retaking property (as distinguished from retaining) by force (Scribner v. Beach, supra; Commonwealth v. Donahue, 148 

Mass. 529, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 58).

In the American Law Institute's Restatement of the Law of Torts the view is adopted that it is mere "retaining" for one to retake personal property by force at the end of an immediate pursuit.

Pursuit may still be "immediate" though the owner or custodian of the property taken took time to get help, if help was necessary and no time was wasted.

Sec. 28. Defense Against Trespassing Animals. The same principles apply to defense against trespassing animals. The rule of reason governs. Such animals cannot be killed because of their trespass alone; but if they are engaged in the destruction of property, certainly if that property be live-stock, (Matthews v. Fiestel, 2 E. D. Smith 90), or if they are known to be dangerous and of mischievous habits (Brill v. Flagler, 23 Wend. 

354), they may be killed.

Sec. 29. Conflicting Rights in General. The purpose of our law is to secure to every person a liberty of action as nearly absolute as possible. We in America speak of ourselves as free, and our law attempts to safeguard that freedom. Unnecessary restriction of a person's rights to use his property and his right to conduct himself as he may desire, is, in theory, prevented by constitutional guarantees. In practice, however, some restriction is unavoidable, but only to the extent necessary to protect the rights of others. A man has a right freely to go and come, wheresoever he pleases. But, if his going and coming endangers the right of personal security of other persons, his right is limited by theirs. For example, an insane person may

be restrained or incarcerated after a proper proceeding but, if the confinement of a sane person be maliciously procured, an action for malicious prosecution will lie (Reade v. Halpin, 193 

App. Div. 566, appeal dismissed 230 N. Y. 588). Each of us has a right of personal security, but if one assault another the aggressor's right of security is limited by the assaulted person's right to use reasonable force to defend himself (Penal Law, Sec. 

246, sub. 3) or those whom he is bound to protect (Penal Law 

Sec. 42; Scribner v. Beach, supra). The right freely to go and come is limited by the property rights of others; the unjustifiable entry into the premises of another is a tort (see the discussion of "Trespass," Ch. XIII, infra), and may be a crime (Penal 

Law Sec. 400, et seq.). All through our law we deal with conflicting rights. Indeed, most, if not all, tort cases present to the courts a problem requiring the drawing of the line between conflicting rights.

Sec. 30. Conflicting Rights Between Adjoining Land owners. A landowner has a right to erect a fence, cutting off light and air from his neighbor. At common law this right was absolute (Guest v. Reynolds, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 1). By statute in New York (Real Property Law Sec. 3) a fence may be adjudicated a nuisance if it exceeds ten feet in height, and wooden fences within the fire limits of the city cannot exceed twelve feet in height (Code of Ordinances, Chap. 5, Art. 22, Sec. 

477, sub. 4). A landowner may dig indefinitely downward and rear structures indefinitely upward, but he cannot withdraw lateral support from his neighbor, which means that he is bound to refrain from removing the natural support his land would give his neighbor's. But the right of lateral support extends only so far as to require support of land alone. If the land has been improved by buildings, their added weight is not entitled to support and in the absence of negligence no liability attaches to a withdrawal of support from land weighted by buildings, if the land itself, unencumbered by buildings, would not have been damaged by the withdrawal (Dorrity v. Rapp, 72 N. Y. 307; 

Finegan v. Eckerson, 32 App. Div. 233; Gilmore v. Driscoll, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 61). By ordinance in the City of

New York, a landowner who excavates to a depth of ten feet or more below the curb is absolutely required to seek permission from adjoining landowners to enter upon their land, and if such

permission be given, he must protect any wall, building or structure, the safety of which may be affected by said excavation 

(Code of Ordinances, Chap. 5, Art. 12, Sec. 230). But if the ex cavation is ten feet or less, the adjoining owner must protect his own property, and may enter upon the lands being excavated in order to do so. The duty is upon the landowner who is excavating or having excavating done. It does not extend to his contractor so as to render the contractor liable for failure to protect (Daly v. Margolies, 169 N. Y. Supp. 448). A landowner may not 'divert or unreasonably use a flowing stream or maintain any other nuisance (see discussion of "Nuisance," Ch. XVI, infra), and may not use pumps of sufficient force to draw underground water from the land of others (Forbell v. City of New York, 164 N. Y. 522,

Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 66). As we have seen (supra. Sec. 

18), a landowner may blast his own land and unless negligent is not liable for damages caused by the concussion. But if earth or stones be cast upon adjoining land, he is responsible, even though not negligent (Radcliff v. Mayor, etc., of Brooklyn, 4 

N. Y. 195). In the case of Booth v. Rome, etc., Co., supra, Sec. 

18, had there been proof of negligence, there would have been a recovery even though earth or stones were not cast.

For additional authorities dealing with adjoining landowners, and the duties and rights reciprocally existing, see Tiffany "Real Property," Sec. 335; Sutherland "Damages," Sec. 

364; Gerard "Real Property," Sec. 1695; Foley v. Wyeth, 2 Allen (Mass.) 131; Panton v. Holland, 17 Johns (N. Y.) 92; Dorrity v. Rapp, 72 N. Y. 307; Farrand v. Marshall, 21 Barb. 409; 

White v. Nassau Trust Co., 168 N. Y. 155; Berger v. Norton, etc., 95 Misc. 647; and Blanchard v. Savarise, 97 App. Div. 58.

Sec. 31. Conflicting Rights in Business and Industrial Com petition. It is axiomatic that all lawful means may be employed to increase trade, and consequently mere underselling is not tortious (Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, L. R. 23 Q. B. 598). 

The difficulty, however, is to determine what are lawful and what are unlawful means. As to these, the authorities differ in many respects, although they agree on the important general rules. Fraud in competition, resulting in the palming off of goods of one person as the goods of another, will not be tolerated (Parsons Trading Co. v. Hoffman, 107 Misc. 536, aff'd 200

App. Div. 900). The use by one person of a name identical with

that of another or so similar to it as to mislead the public is unlawful (Pansy Waist Co. v. Pansy Dress Co., 203 App. Div. 

585). But a man has the right to use his own name in business, not only as an individual but as part of a corporate name, even though others of the same name and of longer standing in the same business are injured; but the use must be honest and there must be no attempt to make the public think that the product of the newcomer is that of the others (Romeike v. 

Romeike and Co., 179 App. Div. 712, aff'd 227 N. Y. 561; B. Forman Co. v. Forman Mfg. Co., 119 Misc. 87). Formerly, in order for an action for unfair competition to lie, it was necessary for the plaintiff to show that he and the defendant were competitors. 

This is no longer necessary, if the defendant's conduct is such as to lead the public to believe that the plaintiff is interested in the defendant's business. Accordingly, an injunction was issued against a defendant motion picture company, at the request of the plaintiff, a jeweler, restraining the defendant from using the name "Tiffany," a gem for a trade-mark, and advertising phrases such as, "Seventy Gems From Tiffany," "Tiffany Presents," "Controlled by Tiffany," and "Tiffany, a World Wide Organization" (Tiffany and Co. v. Tiffany Productions, 147 Misc. 679, affirmed 272 App. Div. 801, affirmed 262 N. Y. -). 

It is, of course, not illegal to strike (Yates Hotel Co. v. 

Meyers, 195 N. Y. Supp. 558; Naisel v. Sigman, 123 Misc. 714) or to picket (Segenfeld v. Freedman, 117 Misc. 731), for collective bargaining short of monopoly is lawful. However, if force or intimidation be used, it is unlawful (Pre Catelan v. Int. 

Fed., 114 Misc. 662). But a union has the right to take the position that none of its members will work for an employer if some of his work is done by non-union workers or by strikebreakers (Maisel v. Sigman, supra). It is not unlawful, by peaceful persuasion, to induce workmen not under time-contract to cease work, or by any reasonable effort to influence trade; illegality steps in only in the event of force, violence, trespass, breach of contract, annoyance to the public, or intimidation (Yates Hotel Co. v. Meyers, supra). If the servants of a master are maliciously induced to breach their contract with him, an action lies against the enticer (Lamb v. Cheney and Son, 227 N. Y. 

418). The same principles apply to labor unions in inducing employees to breach an agreement with their employer not to join

such union (Third Ave. R. Co. v. Shea, 109 Misc. 18, aff'd 191

A. D. 949). The requirement of malice should be liberally applied (Lamb v. Cheney and Son, supra); to induce a breach with knowledge of the contract seems to be a sufficient showing of malice (Campbell v. Gates, 236 N. Y. 457). It would seem that the contract must be one for a definite period of time in order for an action for damages to be maintained (Posner v. Jackson, 223 N. Y. 325), but an injunction will issue to restrain. interference with a contract at will (Third Ave. R. Co. v. 

Shea, supra, citing Hitchman v. Mitchell, 245 U. S. 229 at p. 254 and Truax v. Raich, 239 U. S. 33 at p. 38). Combinations and monopolies are limited and regulated by both Federal and state legislatures and were looked upon with disfavor at common law. Although, as we have seen, underselling is not tortious, a combination to fix prices is unlawful and even criminal (People v. Amanna, 203 App. Div. 548). It has been said that monopolies and exclusive privileges are condemned whether accomplished by combinations of labor or of capital (Michaels v. Willamn, 112 Misc. 395), and, indeed, no other rule would be possible under our American theory of equal protection. Any combination which has for its purpose the destruction of another's business other than by fair methods is unlawful (Auburn Draying Co. v. Wardell, 178 App. Div. 270). Boycotts are generally held to be unlawful, not because unlawful in themselves, for they are not, but because they are generally used to further an unlawful purpose (Auburn Draying Co. v. Wardell, supra; Seubert v. Reiff, 98 Misc. 402). 

Sec. 32. Assent of Plaintiff. Inasmuch as the law does not require that a person stand upon all of his rights at all times but permits the waiver of some in advance, it is legally possible for the plaintiff in many cases to assent to the commission of a tort in advance of its happening. The assent, however, is legally effective only to the extent that it does not violate public policy. 

It is held in most states, including New York, that liability for negligent damage to property can be contracted away in advance (Gardiner v. N. Y. Central, 201 N. Y. 387; Tewes v. North 

German Lloyd, 186 N. Y. 151), and so, as in the above cases, contracts freeing a carrier from liability in whole or in part for damage to baggage are upheld.

Sec. 33. Assent of Plaintiff (continued). The various jurisdictions are not in accord, however, with respect to assent by contract, express or implied, to torts involving personal injuries.

In New York: 

(a) A famous case (Johnston v. Fargo, 184 N. Y. 379) established the rule that contracts between master and servant exempting the master in advance from liability for personal injuries to the servant growing out of breaches by the master of his legal duties will be looked upon with great disfavor, and that where the effect of the contract is to relieve the master from such duties as the important common law duty to provide a safe place to work, the contract will be held void as against public policy. (b) A carrier, on the other hand, may contract away its liability for personal injuries to a passenger in consideration of reduced rates of fare or a free pass (Anderson v. Erie, 171 App. 

Div. 687, Fish v. D. L. & W., 211 N. Y. 374), although not otherwise (Wilcox v. Erie, 162 App. Div. 94, app. dism. 220 N. Y. 

701). Such a contract inures to the benefit of connecting carriers who act on the free pass by extending accommodations (Wilder v. P. R. R., 245 N. Y. 36). (c) It would seem (from a dictum in the Anderson case) that although public policy permits contracts exempting one who owes a duty from breaches of that duty by his agents or servants resulting in personal injuries to the other contracting party, yet, if the contract is so worded as to excuse breaches of duty committed by the principal himself, the contract will not be upheld. For example, if a passenger in consideration of a free pass or of a reduced rate of fare has signed a waiver exempting a railroad from liability for personal injuries occasioned by the negligence of the railroad, its agents and servants, and an employee improperly constructs a rail as a result of which, before notice of the defect is had by the railroad, a wreck occurs in which the passenger who has waived is injured, the waiver is a defense. But if, before an accident occurs, the company receives notice of a danger and fails to repair it or to disclose it to the passenger, the waiver is of no avail as a defense. (d) In all cases public policy is the test, and each case will be decided on its own facts (Johnston v. Fargo, supra; Anderson v. Erie, supra).

(e) Even in those cases where public policy permits waiver in advance, the waiver must definitely and expressly say that the exemption is to include negligence. General terms are not enough, for the contract will be strictly construed, and unless liability for negligently caused personal injuries is expressly included in the exemption, it will not be read in by implication (Kenney v. N. Y. Central, 125 N. Y. 422).

Sec. 34. Assent of Plaintiff (continued). Thus far the discussion of "Assent" has dealt with waiver by contract in advance. Such contract may be express (oral or written) or implied from acts of the parties. But an actual contract is not necessary to spell out an assent. For example, the doctrine of "assumption of the risk" is based on assent, generally without consideration; and the tort defense of "leave and license," based on the legal maxim "volenti non fit injuria," is based on an assent without consideration. The maxim referred to, translated freely in order to obtain an exact statement of law, means "that to which a person assents is not deemed at law an injury," and applies to cases (not those involving negligence alone, but nuisance, and other torts, as well) where the harm to the plaintiff was occasioned by his voluntary placing of himself in the way of danger with knowledge of the facts (Magar v. Hammond, 171 N. Y. 377, again reported 183 N. Y. 387). For example, one who participates in sports assents to the ordinary harsh treatment of the game (Fitzgerald v. Cavin, 110 Mass. 

153), even such as results from non-malicious breaches of the rules of the game, according to the Restatement of the Law of Torts.

It was once thought that one who voluntarily engaged in a fight could recover for injuries received therein, because, his agreement having been unlawful, it was void (Barholt v. 

Wright, 45 Ohio State, 177, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 333).

This was probably the majority rule. 

The minority view was to the contrary and was expressed in Gilmore v. Fuller, 198 Ill. 230, 60 L. R. A. 286.

The Restatement of the Law of Torts follows the minority view, which is better law. However, even under this rule, the consent quite properly will fail to bar an action where the statute making the consent invalid had for its object the protection of the plaintiff. So, a girl who, not having reached the age of

lawful consent, voluntarily engages in sexual intercourse, may nevertheless recover from her seducer. 

The difference between this case and that of a person injured in a fight in which he voluntarily engaged is quite distinct. 

In the latter, the statutes against breach of the peace have for their purpose the protection of the public against undue clamor and accidental injury because of the fracas, rather than the protection of the combatants. 

Therefore, as between themselves the consent to fight is valid, no matter how entirely void it is as between the combatants and the state prosecuting them for the crime. 

In the case of the girl under the age of consent, however, the statute governing is intended primarily to protect her against seduction by making the offense the very serious one of rape, though she did actually consent. 

Her consent is void, not only between her seducer and the state in a criminal prosecution, but also between her seducer and herself as further protection to her. 

The Restatement of the Law of Torts also adopts the view that consents procured by trading upon youth and inexperience, or trust and confidence reposed, are void as between the parties, if, as a result of the overreaching or for some other cause, the person giving the consent was incapable of understanding or appreciating the consequences of the invasion of personality. 

Of course consents procured by fraud or duress are void, even as between the parties. In this connection, see Pratt v. 

Davis, 118 Ill. App. 161.

In the case of a consent to fight, we have seen that the Restatement justifies the rule that the consent bars an action between the parties, and upholds the Gilmore decision, though the act consented to was a crime. 

The Restatement applies the same reasoning to the case of a woman upon whom an abortion was committed with her consent. See also Milliken v. Heddlesheimer, 110 Ohio St. 381.

But if the operation is consented to by the woman because of false representations to her that it is necessary to save her life, the consent is not a bar.

For additional cases on assent of the plaintiff, see Murphy v. Steeplechase A. Co., 250 N. Y. 479; Lumsden v. Thompson, 130 App. Div. 209; Tantillo v. Goldstein, 248 N. Y. 286.

Sec. 35. Illegal Conduct of the Plaintiff. The illegal con duct of the plaintiff may deprive him of his cause of action based on the defendant's misconduct. For example, in New York and most other jurisdictions (following the common law rule), contributory negligence of the plaintiff is regarded as illegal conduct which will be an absolute bar to his recovery in an action based on the defendant's negligence (vide infra, Secs. 155159). However, illegal conduct does not usually make one an outlaw so as to justify assault upon him and murder of him without redress unless his illegal conduct was an act of aggression and the assault upon him was committed in defense. of self, family or property. Consequently, in order to bar a plaintiff's action, his illegal conduct must have been such as to enter into the occurrence complained of so that it modified defendant's duty toward him. Professor Burdick's classical example of the three Confederate soldiers is adequately illustrative of this proposition. Actions were brought by or on behalf of these three soldiers or their estates for injuries or death alleged to have been caused by the negligence of the railroad companies on whose trains they were riding when the accidents occurred. It appeared that one of these soldiers was, at the time of the accident, being carried to the front, there to take part in the war against the Union. No recovery was allowed in this case. In the case of the second soldier, defendant failed to show that he, the soldier, was going to the front, and a recovery was had by the plaintiff. Likewise, in the case of the third soldier the plaintiff recovered, because it affirmatively appeared that the soldier was returning to his home on a furlough (Burdick's Law or Torts, 4th Ed., p. 123; Turner v. N. C. Ry., N. C. 

522; Ireland v. N. C. Ry., 63 N. C. 526n; Clark v. Raleigh, etc., Ry., 63 N. C. 526n).

Participation of a plaintiff in an illegal enterprise, therefore, defeats his action for injuries sustained therein. A spectator at an illegal enterprise is a participant therein, and he cannot recover if injured by the illegal thing, as, by one of the cars in an illegal race he is watching (Johnson v. City of New York, 186 N. Y. 139; Bogart v. City of New York, 200 N. Y. 379, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 74). However, if he is injured, though while watching, by something not connected with the illegal thing, as, by an object dropping upon him from a building, or

perhaps even if he were assaulted for a cause apart from the exhibition by another participant, he probably could recover. At any rate, the cases cited are not authorities against this latter proposition. And, of course, no other breach than the illegality of the thing itself need be shown by a passer-by who is not a spectator (Bogart v. City, supra), to the extent, at least, that the illegality of the thing and resultant damages constitute a prima facie case. See Secs. 9 and 10, supra, and 152, infra. 

Sec. 36. Proximate and Remote Cause. In working out our practical definition of tort, we found that in order to be actionable the breach of legal duty must proximately result in damage (vide supra, Sec. 14). We come now to a discussion of the principles determining whether or not a given cause is the proximate cause of the damage, for the apparent cause of a harm may be legally so remote that the harm is not a tort by the author of the cause. See the following sections of this chapter, and compare with Sections 149-151, infra.

Sec. 37. Proximate Cause; General Rules. Different juris dictions apply different rules. The question of what is the proximate cause of an injury has always been a difficult one. But the following statements will be found to be a practical aid in solving the difficulty: (a) If the injury inflicted was intended by the originator of a cause, that cause will be held to be the proximate cause 

(Pollock's Law of Torts, 12th Ed. p. 334; Burdick's Law of 

Torts, 4th Ed., p. 29; Quinn v. Leatham, A. C. 495, 537), for what a man actually intends to bring about by an act he will be held responsible for, and no finely drawn rules of proximate cause ought to enter in to excuse him. But, of course, if what he did was lawful, though harm resulted, he is not responsible (Sec. 19, supra). See also (c), infra this section. (b) Where, independently of any actual intent to injure, a breach of legal duty is committed, the natural consequence of which is the effect complained of, and such effect follows without the intervention of another possible cause in the shape of a breach of duty by another person, the law will say that the damnum (the effect complained of) has followed as a proximate result (Cooley's Laws of Torts, Students' Ed., p. 30). This is because the law presumes a man to have intended the natural and foreseeable consequences of his acts, although the newer and

better way of stating the rule omits mention of foreseeability of harm, which enters in only as a test of whether the act or omission causing the harm to a given person was an injuria to that person in the first place. If it is, and harm actually follows to that person as a result, though remote and not foreseeable, a recovery will be had for it if the chain of causation can be shown. 

This has caused many conflicting decisions in cases involving an intervening culpable cause, for the reason that, in many cases in which the older rule would have operated to discharge the author of the first cause under the second sentence of subdivision (d) hereof, the application of the newer rule holds the authors of both as though the causes were concurrent, as in subdivision (e). (c) To be the proximate cause, and actionable, a given cause must be a culpable cause, i. e., a breach of legal duty (Stone v. Boston R. Co., 171 Mass. 536, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 96; Mitchell v. Rochester R. Co., 151 N. Y. 107, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 111; Rich v. N. Y. Central, supra, Sec. 4). (d) If there are two possible causes, the original of which is a breach of legal duty naturally and usually a source of legal harm, and harm occurs through the intervention of the second, which, however, is an innocent cause, the original and wrongful cause is the proximate cause (Cooley's Law on Torts, Students' 

Ed., p. 31 Rich v. N. Y. Central, supra; Phillips v. N. Y. Central, 127 N. Y. 657). However, if the intervening cause is not innocent, but is itself an injuria, and if the earlier cause, though an injuria, would not have occasioned the harm but for the intervention of the later, which, however, might have caused the harm without the aid of the earlier cause, the later will be held to be the proximate cause; the earlier, by virtue of the proximity of the later cause in time, being considered the more remote (Stone v. Boston R. Co., supra; Ross v. Western Union, 81 Fed. 676). For a further application of this doctrine, see Section 152, infra. 

But compare latter part of (b), supra, and see (e), infra. (e) When there are two causes, both of which are breaches of legal duty, neither of which would have caused the entire harm alone, but both of which together have caused it, the case is one in which two causes have united or concurred to produce a single injury. In such a case, the authors of both causes are liable, jointly and severally. Strictly speaking they are not joint

tort feasors, for there is no element of concert. Practically, however, they are joint tort feasors for they are jointly and severally liable just as joint tort feasors are. Both causes are thus considered proximate (vide infra, Sec. 64). The authors of the causes are referred to as "concurrent tort feasors," and the causes as "concurrent causes." (f) Where, of two causes, whether technically concurrent or not, the one is foreseeably induced by the other, the author of the inducing cause is liable as in Vandenburgh v. Truax, 4 

Denio 464, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 88. In that case, the plaintiff's servant was chased by defendant into plaintiff's store, in which, while taking refuge, the servant knocked over a cask of wine. Irrespective of whether the servant had been negligent, the defendant was held liable, he having wrongfully put the servant to flight. Similarly in the famous English squib case, Scott v. Shepherd, 2 W. Bl. 892, the defendant, the author of the inducing cause, was held liable. It ap- peared that he had thrown a lighted squib into a markethouse. It fell near one Yates, who picked it up and threw it in fear of it; it was twice again thrown from hand to hand by others near whom it fell; it then exploded, putting out the plaintiff's eye, which damage, the court held, was a proximate result of the defendant's original act which might have been foreseen to have induced the acts of the others. A third much quoted example is found in the case of Lowery v. Man. R. Co., 99 N. Y. 

158, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 92. In that case a defendant elevated railway negligently permitted burning coal to fall to the street upon a standing horse and wagon. The horse ran away, and the driver, to arrest the progress of the horse, drove him against the curb. This, however, failed to stop him, and he went upon the sidewalk, running over the plaintiff. The negligence of the railway, and not the act of the driver in heading toward the curb, was held to be the proximate cause. The driver, having met the test of what the ordinary person in a like emergency would have done, was excused. This test, then, determines whether he whose intervening causation is induced is liable with the inducer, who is always liable. The application of the test (as to whether also to hold liable the author of the induced and intervening cause) is for the jury, unless the conduct induced would not have been a breach of duty under ordin-

ary circumstances, in which case, as a matter of law, the induced causation is excused, under (c) supra. (g) The question of proximate cause is usually for the jury, upon a charge from the court as to the law. Each case stands upon its own facts (Milwaukee, etc., Ry. v. Kellogg, 94 

U. S. 469, at p. 474, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 83). Where the facts are undisputed and permit of only one inference, the court will decide the question (Mitchell v. Rochester Ry., supra). But where the facts are in dispute, or where conflicting inferences can be drawn where the facts are undisputed, the question is for the jury (Milwaukee, etc., Ry., v. Kellogg, supra; Lowery v. 

Manhattan R. Co., supra).

For additional cases on proximate cause, see Laidlaw v. 

Sage, 158 N. Y. 73, 44 L. R. A. 216; Machill v. N. Y. T. Co., 201 

N. Y. 221; Butler v. Jersey Coast News Co., N. J. L. 

160 Atl. 659; Perry v. Rochester Line Co., 219 N. Y. 60. 

Sec. 38. Proximate cause; New York Rule of Liability for

Negligent Fires. A peculiar and arbitrary rule exists in New 

York with respect to liability for negligent fires. It is here held that the liability of a person who negligently causes a fire to start, or who negligently permits a fire to get beyond his control so that it damages property of his neighbors, is only to adjoining landowners, and not to those whose lands do not touch his (Hoffman v. King, 160 N. Y. 618). This doctrine has been called arbitrary by Chief Judge Cardozo (Bird v. St. Paul, etc., Ins. Co., 224 N. Y. 47, at p. 53), but is based on a desire to protect against judgments for remote damages in cases where some limitation may well be imposed. The Federal rule is that no such artitrary limitation exists in the law, and in cases of negligent fire, the question of proximate cause is left to the jury upon the usual charge as to the law (Milwaukee, etc., Ry. v. Kellogg, supra). The Federal rule is the better one, applying, as it does, to cases such as this the logical and ordinary tests of proximateness set forth in the preceding section. Thus the limitation imposed upon liability is the intelligent one of actual causation, which serves well and is the modern one (supra, Sec. 37 [b]; infra, Sec. 39). New York, however, reluctantly adheres to its old test. But if, though there be lands of a third person intervening between the land of plaintiff and the land of the defendant, the fire was communicated to the plaintiff's property directly, as

by sparks from the defendant's building or the sweep of flames. in a high wind, and not through the medium of a "connecting" fire on the intervening land, the defendant is liable. (Homac 

Corporation v. Sun Oil Co., 258 N. Y. 462, Edgar's Supplemental 

Cases on Torts, p. 1). 

Sec. 39. Proximate Cause; Mental Suffering; Conduct Induced by Fear. If the defendant intends, either actually or by legal implication, to put the plaintiff in fear or cause him other mental suffering, as in cases of assault, where fear of bodily harm is the very basis of the action, such suffering is legal damage, and recoverable for, even though unattended by other damnum. But where there is no intent, or the equivalent of intent (Sec. 94, infra), mental suffering unattended by actual physical contact is injuria sine damno, and not even nominal damages will be awarded (Mitchell v. Rochester Ry., supra).

Even if the fear or other mental suffering produces severe physical harm, as where a pregnant woman is frightened by defendant's negligent driving of a team and as a result of her fright has a miscarriage, there is no recovery unless there was physical contact between the plaintiff and the defendant's team or vehicle, although even the slightest actual physical injury from contact will be sufficient to permit the plaintiff to recover for the whole chain of damages, including the fear and the miscarriage (Mitchell v. Rochester Ry., supra).

The real reason (though others such as public policy and the difficulty of ascertaining the truth are sometimes given) for denying a recovery is that the New York courts regard severe fright and its consequences as not reasonably foreseeable in negligence cases except as the result of contact, and hold that when fright unattended by contact is so severe as to cause physical harm or mental derangement the proximate cause is more likely to be the peculiarity of the individual than the negligence of the defendant. (This is out of accord with the rule of Sec. 37 [d], first sentence, supra). So in a well-known defamation case, where the slander was not of a class actionable per se but required proof of special damage, it was held that proof alone that the statement had so hurt the plaintiff's feelings and caused him to fear for his reputation that he became ill in bed was not proof of legal damage. However, if there had been damnum, as, if the slander had been in the per se class or if there had been

proof of the loss of social or business standing (analogous to the required physical contact in the Mitchell case), the injured feelings, the fear of lost prestige, and the physical illness probably all would have been proper items of damage (Terwilliger v. 

Wands, 17 N. Y. 54, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 103; see also 

Garrison v. Sun, etc., Ass'n, 207 N. Y. 1, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 368). See infra, Section 112.

The application of the "newer theory" mentioned in the latter part of subdivision (b) of Section 37, supra, would have led to a recovery on the facts of the Mitchell case (and possibly, also, in the Terwilliger case if repetition by third persons had not been involved in the latter).

In other words, whether what was suffered by Mrs. 

Mitchell in the one case and what was suffered by Mr. Terwilliger in the other were foreseeable consequences of the acts or omissions of the defendants is, under the newer theory, unimportant, if (1) the acts or omissions were injuriae and (2) the sufferings followed as actual consequences, though not foreseeable.

Foreseeability of harms of some sort as a result of the acts or omissions are important as an aid in determining whether or not the acts or omissions were breaches of legal duty in the first place.

Knowing, as we do, that the things of which the defendant in the Mitchell case was guilty, and the things of which the defendant in the Terwilliger case was guilty, are breaches of legal duty in certain cases (i. e., where certain kinds of damages occur), we can work out for ourselves the application of the test. 

They are breaches of legal duty in certain cases because it is foreseeable that harm of some sort will occur. 

Given, then, harms as an actual result, the newer theory would compensate for those harms, and so, under the newer theory, a contrary result would be reached today were the Mitchell case to come up now for the first time. The same is probably true of the Terwilliger case, or, at least, it would be but for the fact that the real damage in that case was done not by the defendant, the original defamer, but by repeaters. 

However, the old theory in vogue when those cases were decided did not admit of compensation for the harms suffered, because the harms suffered were not among the harms foresee-

able as results. It will probably require legislative aid to correct the rule of those antiquated decisions. 

Carroll v. N. Y. Pie Baking Co., 215 App. Div. 240, allowing a recovery for illness caused by the sight of cockroaches in a pie, is in conflict with the Mitchell case, in spite of the ingenious attempt in the opinion to differentiate it. It was correctly decided because it can be reasonably foreseen that the sight of cockroaches in food may make even a normal person ill. But so, in the Mitchell case, could it have been reasonably foreseen that Mrs. Mitchell would be frightened, and even normal persons may become ill from fright. Certainly it cannot be said that pregnancy is abnormal. At any rate, the question of whether a normal person can become ill from fright is a matter properly left to expert medical testimony, and not to be decided by a nonmedical court as a matter of law, if, indeed, it is important at all. The important question is not whether a normal person could, but whether the person in question, normal or not, did become ill as a practical and actual consequence. As hinted in the Mitchell case, the slightest physical contact is a sufficient peg from which to hang fright and all its consequences as proximate damages. So, when two cars "scraped," and the plaintiff's testatrix, a passenger in one, was frightened and got out and fell to the sidewalk in a faint, fracturing her skull, from which injury she died, it was held that the defendant, the negligent operator of the other vehicle, would be liable for wrongful death if the jury were to find that the fainting spell resulted from the fright caused by the accident (Comstock v. 

Wilson, 257 N. Y. 231, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 5).

The absurdity of the Mitchell decision can clearly be seen by comparing these two cases. Under the rule of the Comstock case, Mrs. Mitchell could have recovered for the entire chain of events if she had been even lightly touched, though not the touching but the fright were the cause of all her suffering and damages.

Sometimes fright induces conduct leading to violent physical injury. In such a case, if negligence causes the fright, and the frightened person acts as the ordinary person would act under like circumstances, recovery can be had. For example, where a passenger becomes frightened because of the imminence of a collision negligently brought about by the defendant carrier,

and jumps from defendant's car, sustaining physical harm, the passenger can recover (Kreuzer v. 42nd St. etc. R. Co., 13 N. Y.

Supp. 588). It has also been held that the passenger can recover when the blowing of a fuse causes him to jump and he is hurt (Poulsen v. Nassau Elec., 18 App. Div. 221, reported again 

30 App. Div. 246). In cases such as these, the passenger's contributory negligence (i. e., the question of whether he has acted as the ordinarily reasonable and prudent man would act under like circumstances) is for the jury (Robson v. Nassau Elec. 80 

App. Div. 301; see also notes 45 L. R. A. et seq.). The defendant, having created the dilemma, should be responsible for the results of the choice its negligence has made necessary. See also infra, Sec. 156.

Sec. 40. Proximate Cause; Repetition of Defamatory State ments. In New York it is held that the originator of a defamatory statement is liable only for the harm done by his original publication of the statement, and that if another person repeats the statement, the repeater only, and not the originator, is responsible for the harm done by the repetition (Terwilliger v. 

Wands, supra). Obviously this is in accordance with that rule of proximate cause discussed above in Section 37 sub. [d] second sentence. However, as stated above (Section 39), the "newer theory" we have examined might (were it to have been in vogue when the Terwilliger case was decided) have caused a different result to be reached in that case if the sufferings of the plaintiff had been caused by the defendant's defamation, that it, if repetition by other persons had not been involved.

CHAPTER III PARTIES TO TORT ACTIONS Sec. 41. The Federal and State Governments. The state government and the Federal government may sue for torts committed against them. Indeed, they are corporate persons as well as sovereignties (Cooley's Law of Torts, Student's Ed., p. 82). They can, therefore, be parties plaintiff in tort actions. But they cannot be made defendants without their consent (Supra, Sec. 23). The legislative body of the sovereignty may redress wrongs due. The method most frequently provided for redress is by means of a Court of Claims. The Federal government has such a court, and so has the State of New York. To the extent that the legislation creating the Court of Claims vests such court with jurisdiction, the sovereignty is consenting to be sued. But such consent extends only to claims within the jurisdiction of the court, and to claims prosecuted in that court. The consent does not permit proceedings in other courts than the Court of Claims. 

The State of New York, in a recent amendment to its Court of Claims Act, known as Section 12a, waived its freedom from liability for torts of its employees, committed in the scope and course of their employment. The enactment is retroactive. It requires that within 60 days of the accident, a verified claim or notice be served upon the Attorney General and the Superintendent of Public Works, and that the action on the claim be commenced within two years of the accident. 

Sec. 42. Representatives of the Federal and State Govern ments. As we have seen, in considering apparent wrongs which are not torts (vide supra, Chap. II), the officer through whom the state or Federal government acts in committing a wrong is liable for that wrong, and this is so even though the wrong be one for which there is no redress against the sovereignty by reason of its having failed to give consent to suit. We have also seen that if the act done be a valid exercise of the police power the officer committing it is as free from liability as the state itself, not, however, for lack of a remedy against the officer (for there is no lack, he being suable in the courts whenever he com-

mits a wrong), but because there is no wrong, the police power, based on the paramount necessity of public safety, having made rightful a destruction of private property which would otherwise be wrongful.

Sec. 43. Foreign Sovereigns and Their Representatives. A 

foreign government may be a plaintiff in our courts (King of Spain v. Oliver, Fed. Cas. No. 7814), but on principles of international comity, neither a foreign sovereign nor its representatives on the business of the sovereign may be made defendents in actions in our courts unless they consent to be sued, although such representatives (generally diplomatic officers) may sue as plaintiffs (Hall, Int. Law, 8th Ed., Part II, Ch. 3 and 4).

Sec. 44. Political Subdivisions of the State; Public Cor porations. The general rule is that political subdivisions of the state in acting as such by performing for the state a duty of the state toward its inhabitants are no more liable for their torts than the state is, because in so acting their function is governmental (Lefrois v. Monroe County, 162 N. Y. 563; Herman v. 

B'd of Ed., 234 N. Y. 196). However, frequently they engage in functions which are not governmental, and to that extent they are liable (cases infra). (a) Quasi municipal corporations (Counties, Towns, and School Districts): These are true subdivisions of the state, generally brought into existence without voluntary action on their part. In this they differ from true municipal corporations, which are generally, if not always, brought into existence by the voluntary act of their constituents in applying to the legislature for a charter. Some of the text-book writers make a distinction between quasi and true municipal corporations with respect to liability for torts upon this ground, but in New York there is little difference between the rules governing each. The test for both is whether or not the function is governmental (Lefrois v. Monroe County, supra). However, the functions of quasi municipal corporations are more frequently governmental than are those of true municipal corporations, and so there are more cases dealing with the liability of the latter class than there are dealing with that of quasi municipal corporations. But it may be that even in a governmental function, if a duty imposed upon the corporation is to be performed by its governing body instead of by the corporation through its agents, liability

attaches in the event of a breach of that duty. (See discussion of school districts and boards of education later in this section). (b) True municipal corporations (Cities, Villages and Specially Incorporated Towns): The rule governing these is as stated above, namely, that to the extent that their functioning is governmental (i. e. to the extent that they are truly acting as subdivisions of the state) they are not liable, but when their functioning is not governmental but private and corporate, they are liable (Van Dyke v. Utica, 203 App. Div. 36). Examples of governmental functions, in the exercise of which liability does not arise, are: The maintenance of a jail (Finkelstein v. N. Y. 183 

App. Div. 539); the maintenance of a court house (Boutet v. 

N. Y. 199 App. Div. 835; Schlegel v. N. Y., 90 Misc. 285); the maintenance of a fire department (Gaetjens v. N. Y., 132 App. 

Div. 394); and the maintenance of a police department (Wilcox v. Rochester, 190 N. Y., 137; Clayman v. N.. Y., 117 App.. Div. 

565). However, though the maintenance of public buildings such as court houses, jails, and police stations is a governmental function, it has been held that if one of these buildings encroaches on adjoining and privately owned property, an action will lie, for to hold otherwise would be to countenance a taking of private property for public use without compensation, which is prohibited by constitutional safeguards (Miles v. Worcester, 154 Mass. 511).

Examples of private functions, in the exercise of which liability may arise, are: The maintenance of highways (Bennett v. N. Y., 241 N. Y. 385), even though the part of the highway in question be the sidewalk in front of a governmental building (Boutet v. N. Y., supra); the maintenance of a sewer system (Wanamaker v. N. Y., 197 App. Div. 441, aff'd 233 N. Y. 652); and the maintenance of playgrounds (Van Dyke v. Utica, supra).

A municipal corporation is not liable for the torts of those of its municipal departments which are separate entities, such as boards of education (Titusville Iron Co. v. N. Y., 207 N. Y. 203; 

Herman v. Board of Ed., supra; Ham v. Mayor, 70 N. Y. 459), if specially created, or the Building Department of New York City (Metzroth v. N. Y., 212 App. Div. 253), which is a bureau specially created by statute. Such boards, being themselves entities, may themselves be sued in proper cases.

Special reference should be made here to school districts and boards of education. These entities are quasi municipal corporations, and not true municipal corporations. They are organized by the state to perform locally the state's governmental function of seeing to the education of its future voting citizens. 

They are not liable for the wrongful acts of a teacher if they used reasonable care in selecting the teacher and did not retain him after notice of his unfitness. But if the tort complained of is the tort of the body itself, as, if the body votes to install in a school a dangerous machine, the board or district is liable as such (Herman v. Board of Ed., supra). 

The Herman case indicates that there is a similar rule with respect to quasi and true municipal corporations, i. e., that there is no liability for the torts of their agents unless the function in pursuit of which the tort was committed is a private one (which is the law in New York beyond doubt), but that if the tort is a breach of a duty imposed by law not upon the agents of the body (in the manner that a policeman's duty is upon him), but upon the body itself, the body is liable whether the function be governmental or not. It will be interesting to observe the development of the last half of this rule and to note whether or not it is in future years applied to true municipal corporations in exercising governmental functions with the definiteness with which we know it applies to boards of education, school districts, and other specially created entities merely partaking of the nature of municipal corporations. 

On the whole, a close similarity exists between the rules governing liability of such bodies and the rules governing liability of charitable corporations. See infra, Section 45. 

Sec. 45. Charitable Corporations. Hospitals which are arms of the state come within the rules set forth in Section 41, supra.. The remedy is in the Court of Claims of the state. Hospitals and schools maintained even partly by state moneys and to which convicts, patients or wards of the state are sent, are not liable for torts committed by their agents and servants upon convicts, patients or wards of the state, for as to them such institutions are arms of the state (Corbett v. St. Vincent's Industrial School, 177 N. Y. 16). The remedy is in the Court of

Claims. 

Hospitals and educational institutions, not arms of the

state, but organized and operating as true charities, have not in this state been held liable to the recipients of their charity for torts committed by doctors, nurses and teachers. 

At first it was thought that this freedom from liability was based on the theory that the funds contributed were not intended by donors to be used to pay claims and also on the theory that to hold the charities liable would be to encourage the biting of the hand that feeds (Horden v. Salvation Army, 199 N. Y. 

233, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 114; Phillips v. Buffalo General 

Hospital, 239 N. Y. 188; Schloendorff v. N. Y. Hospital, 211 

N. Y. 125).

There was much to-do over the question of who is the recipient of charity, and the Schloendorff case and the case of Wilson v. N. Y., etc., College, 122 Misc. 453, held that even pay patients were charitable patients, since what they pay cannot be regarded as a quid pro quo, but is rather a donation. The Phillips case, however, definitely leaned the other way.

It is established now that the freedom from liability is based upon the non-applicability of the doctrine of respondent superior (Hamburger v. Cornell University, 240 N. Y. 

328; Wilson v. N. Y., etc., College, supra).

In other words, a charitable hospital does not itself agree to attempt through physicians and nurses as agents or servants of the hospital to cure patients. It merely furnishes a place where a cure may be sought at the hands of physicians, assisted by nurses, both classes being independent contractors, in the selection of whom the hospital represents no more than that it has used reasonable care and has not yet learned of their unfitness. 

So, too, an educational institution, organized as a charity, merely furnishes a place where instruction may be sought in the classes of teachers who are independent contractors. 

There is no doubt, however, of the liability of such corporations for the negligence of their employees toward nonpatients, as toward a workman on the premises of the charity who is injured through the negligence of the employees of the charity (Hordern v. Salvation Army, supra), and toward strangers on the street who may be injured by the negligence of the driver of an ambulance (Kellogg v. Church Charity Foun dation, 203 N. Y. 191; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 142, in which a recovery would have been allowed had the driver been an em-

ployee of the charity instead of the employee of an independent contractor). As to whether a charitable corporation may be liable to patients for the negligence of mere administrative agents (not physicians, nurses or teachers), the law in this state is said to be still in doubt (Hamburger v. Cornell Univ., supra, at p. 335). In the Phillips case, however, the charitable corporation defendant was held not liable to a charity patient for the negligence of an orderly, although that may have been because the orderly was performing a nurse's work. Of course, an institution organized for profit, but which has some charity cases, would be liable for negligence toward a pay patient, for as to him it would not be a charitable corporation at all (Case notes, 2 L. R. A., N. S., p. 556 et seq.). 

See also Robertson v. Charles B. Towns Hospital, 178 App.

Div. 285. Compare the rule of non-liability with the case of 

Wallace v. Casey, infra, Section 52. 

Sec. 46. Private Corporations. Private corporations are subject to liability for their torts, and there is little difference between their liability and that of natural persons (Clifford v. 

Press Pub. Co., 78 App. Div. 79). At one time the law did not permit a recovery against corporations for torts involving an intent or malice on the theory that a corporation, having no mind or soul, could not conceive an intent. Today, however, the rule is more logical. A corporation thinks, as well as acts, through its agents. At the present time, actions against corporations are permitted as a consequence of this more reasonable view for even such torts as fraud, malicious prosecution, false imprisonment, and defamation, and the law also recognizes that a corporation may be a conspirator (Corrigan v. Bobbs Merrill, 228 

N. Y. 58; Fishkill Savings Inst. v. Bank, 80 N. Y. 162; Buffalo, etc., Co., v. Standard Oil, 106 N. Y. 669; Lynch v. Met. El. Ry., 90 N. Y. 77; Garrison v. Sun, 207 N. Y. 1, reported in part in 

Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 368; Samuels v. Evening Mail, 75 

N. Y. 604; Willard v. Holmes, 142 N. Y. 492). A corporation will even be held guilty under present day law for express malice (i. e., hatred, ill-will, or vindictiveness leading to an express intent to commit injury upon the plaintiff) so that punitive damages may be awarded against it, if the plaintiff can show that the act complained of was explicitly authorized 

(Macauley v. Starr, 194 App. Div. 643).

Sec. 47. Married Women; Husband and Wife. Although at common law the husband had to join with his wife as a party plaintiff in order for her to sue (because the recovery, when obtained, belonged to him), and had to be joined with her as a defendant when she was sued (because he, being vested with all her property, was responsible for her torts), the law in New York by statute has been changed so that the married woman now has control of her own property and estate, her earnings are hers and lost earnings may be recovered by her, and she may sue and be sued without the joinder with her of her husband 

(Dom. Rel. Law Sec. 57; C. P. A. Sec. 200). The husband is not liable for his wife's torts unless they are instigated or ratified by him or unless he coerces her, and instigation and coercion are not presumed (Dom. Rel. Law, Sec. 57; Tanzer v. Read, 160 

App. Div. 584; Mason v. Mason, 66 Hun 386; Duffy v. Ascher, 191 App. Div. 918), except that under Section 59 of the Vehicle and Traffic Law the husband is responsible for his wife's negligent driving of his motor vehicle with his consent. 

A married woman has no cause of action for loss of services, support or consortium if the husband be tortiously injured (Goldman v. Cohen, 30 Misc. 336), but in view of the fact that she can sue for the alienation of his affections (Bennett v. Bennett, 116 N. Y. 584; Edgar's Cases on Torts p. 358) and for criminal conversation with him (Oppenheim v. Kridel, 236 N. Y. 156; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 346), undoubtedly the rule will some day be extended to give her a cause of action for loss of consortium when he suffers tortious personal injuries.

When personal injuries are tortiously inflicted upon the wife, in addition to her action for the tort to her, there is another action, this one accruing to the husband, for loss of her domestic services and of consortium and the expense to which he is put to cure her (Schaupp v. Turner, 188 App. Div. 338). The husband has a cause of action for loss of consortium, wounded feelings and honor, the defilement of the marriage bed, and for the doubt thrown upon the legitimacy of children (Tinker v. Colwell, 193 

U. S. 473), against one guilty of criminal conversation with the wife, and for loss of consortium and services when the affections of the wife are alienated (Weston v. Weston, 86 App. Div. 159.; 

Heermance v. James, Barb. 120), or when she is abducted, enticed away, or harbored (3 Black Comm., 139, 140). See infra, Ch.

XI, "Disturbance of Family Relations." Neither at common law nor at the present day is any action maintainable by husband against wife or by wife against husband for personal torts (Abbe v. Abbe, 22 App. Div. 483; Schultz v. Schultz, 89 N. Y. 644;

Thompson v. Thompson, 218 U. S. 611; Abbott v. Abbott, 67 

Main 604, Longendyke v. Longendyke, 44 Barb. 336, Perlman v. B'klyn City R. Co., 117 Misc. 353 Aff'd 202 App. Div. 822; 

Newton v. Weber, 119 Misc. 240, Freetly v. Freetly, 42 Barb. 

641, Minier v. Minier, 4 Lans. 421), although the husband may sue his wife for torts to his property (Berdell v. Parkhurst, 19 

Hun. 358; Mason v. Mason, 21 Supp. 306, 66 Hun. 386) and the wife may sue her husband for torts to her separate estate (Dom. 

Rel. Law, Sec. 57; Ryerson v. Ryerson, 8 Supp. 738, 55 Hun 

611 mem.).

If a married woman suffers personal injuries through the negligence of her husband committed in the scope and course of his duties as an employee, the master cannot escape because his servant, the plaintiff's husband, is not liable. 

Of course, however, the husband would not in such a case have a cause of action for loss of consortium against his master. 

In connection with all of this, see Schubert v. Schubert 

Wagon Co., 249 N. Y. 253. 

Sec. 48. Infants; Parent and Child. When an infant sustains personal injuries through the tort of another person, two actions arise, the one accruing to the infant (who sues through a guardian ad litem), the other accruing to the parent for loss of services (Cooley's Law of Torts Sec. 135; Tidd v. Skinner, 

225 N. Y. 422). The parent also has a cause of action against one who seduces his female child or abducts, entices or harbors his child of either sex (Cooley Sec. 135; infra, Ch. XI, "Disturbance of Family Relations"). When the daughter is seduced, if the father be dead, the mother may maintain the action (C. P. A. 

Sec. 89). There is in this state no liability to an infant for pre-natal harm (i. e., injury inflicted upon him while in ventre sa mere) even though the act be a tort to the mother 

(Drobner v. Peters, 232 N. Y. 220, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 138). When a parent has a cause of action for loss of services, he may recover the expenses to which he has been put to cure the child (Cuming v. B'klyn Ry., 109 N. Y. 95). If the father be dead, the mother or anyone standing in loco parentis

has this right of action (Gray v. Durland, 50 Barb. 424, aff'd 51 

N. Y. 424; Furman v. Van Sise, 56 N. Y. 435; Tidd v. Skinner, supra). A child has no cause of action against his parent for personal torts (Smith v. Morrison, 31 A. L. R. 1135 and annotation, 20 R. C. L. 631; 29 Cyc. 1663; Mannion v. Mannion, 129 

Atl. 431; Foley v. Foley, 61 Ill. App. 577; Roller v. Roller, 37 

Wash. 242; Smith v. Smith, 81 Ind. App. 566, Editorial, N. Y. 

Law Journal, May 23 and 24, 1927). A child may, however, maintain an action against his parent to redress property torts, as conversion (Dunn v. Beamann, 26 N. C. 771; 36 S. E. 172, Smith v. Morrison, supra, dis. op. pp. 1145 and 1146). Many authorities feel that in view of the fact that it has been held that a child may sue for a personal tort one who stands to him in loco parentis, actions by children against their parents for personal torts should be permitted (Editorial, N. Y. Law Journal, May 23 and 24, 1927). A parent is not liable for the torts of his child unless the parent constitutes the child his agent or servant, by authorizing or ratifying the tort (Weiner v. Weinberg, 125 

Misc. 393), or except under Vehicle and Traffic Law Sec. 59, whereby the owner of a motor vehicle is made liable for the negligence of any person driving the motor vehicle with his consent. A child is responsible for his own torts (Moore v. Eastman, 1 Hun 578, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 119). However, since an infant is not liable on his contracts unless he elects to be, he is not responsible for torts growing out of contracts. The test to be applied is whether or not the injury consists merely in non-performance of a contract; if the answer to this question is in the affirmative, no action lies (Moore v. Eastman, supra). 

In Moore v. Eastman, supra, it was held that an infant who had hired a horse from the plaintiff was not liable to the plaintiff for the death of the horse, occasioned by the infant defendant's overdriving of him. The theory of the decision was that the overdriving was a breach of contract, and though it was also the tort of negligence, to hold an infant responsible for it would be to hold him liable on his contract by indirection. However, if there has been a wilful and positive act amounting to an intention to disaffirm the contract, the infant will be liable; for example, if he lends the horse to a friend who drives it over a fence (the owner having stipulated that the horse would not be taken from the highway) and impales it on a stake, the infant will

be liable for conversion (Burnard v. Haggis, 14 C. B. [U. S.] 

45, cited in Moore v. Eastman, supra), or if he drives the horse further than the stipulated journey or on a different one, he will be held liable (Towne v. Wiley, 23 Vt. 355; 56 Am. Dec. 85; 

Homer v. Throing, 3 Pick. 492, Lucas v. Trumbull, 15 Gray 307; 

Fish v. Ferris, 5 Duer 49, all cited in Moore v. Eastman, supra).

Clearly in such cases there is an evident intention to disaffirm which mere negligent overdriving does not comprehend. An infant's liability for negligence depends upon whether or not he has acted as the ordinary child the same age would have acted under like circumstances; and the test of his liability for torts involving malice and of his liability for punitive damages (which is dependent upon whether or not he has been guilty of express malice) is whether "his age and capacity may justify imputing malice to him" or "preclude the idea of his indulging it" 

(Cooley's Law of Torts, Stud. Ed., Secs. 22 and 352). 

The question of whether or not an infant is liable for deceit in misrepresenting his age is unsettled at present in New York.

Very early cases were nearly all agreed that such liability would nullify the protection the law gives infants against the consequences of their contracts. In short, it was felt that to hold an infant liable for such deceit would be to hold him liable on his contracts by indirection.

The Appellate Division in the Second Department held an infant liable under these circumstances (Falk v. McMasters, 197 

App. Div. 357), however.

The First Department Appellate Division has stated a contrary rule (Sternlieb v. Normandie Nat'l Corp., 238 App. Div. 

349, N. Y. Law Journal of June 14, 1933).

In that decision a rule is clearly contemplated which will (1) permit the infant to disaffirm on the ground of his infancy in spite of his fraud and (2) refuse the right to the adult to disaffirm or sue for damages because of his fraud. 

The Court of Appeals has not resolved the conflict, but that court has held in a case of an infant's misrepresentation of his age that he is not estopped from setting up the defense of infancy when the adult sues him on the contract (International 

Text Book Co. v. Connelly, 208 N. Y. 188), which is an indication of the attitude of that Court.

The authors incline toward the early view, now restated in the Sternlieb case.

The Massachusetts rule is that the infant is not liable for such deceit (Slayton v. Barry, 175 Mass. 513).

Sec. 49. Insane Persons and Drunkards. Lunatics are gen erally held liable for torts (McIntyre v. Sholtz, 121 I11. 660, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 70), unless they involve malice (Cooley on Torts, Sec. 21). So, since punitive damages is never awarded unless there was express malice, insane persons are never held liable to pay these enhanced, exemplary damages. 

It is with reluctance that the courts hold insane persons liable in any case (Williams v. Hays, 157 N. Y. 541, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 78). They are not held liable for defamation, even when malice in fact is not an element. The fictional malice in law (Cooley, Sec. 116; infra, this outline, Section 111) may have been designed to prevent such liability. 

Probably a change in the rule can be expected, with the result that torts of lunatics will be treated as inevitable accidents (supra, Section 25); compare the present rule with the discussion in Sec. 150, infra.

That day may be far off, however, and its dawning may require legislative aid, for nothing could be clearer than the understanding of a general rule of liability which the Court of Appeals accepted in deciding the Williams case to be exceptional.

Committees of the property of insane persons are certainly responsible for harms resulting from improper management thereof. Those having the custody of a dangerous lunatic's person are responsible for harm done by him during a period of freedom allowed him through their carelessness.

Drunkenness is no defense to an action in tort (Cooley, Section 24).

N. Y. 248). They are joint tort feasors (Schultz v. U. S. F. G., 201 N. Y. 230; Suydam v. Moore, 8 Barb. 358) in practical result.

The same rule governs tort liability of principal and agent. !..

Sec. 52. Master and Servant; Independent Contractors;

Loan of a Servant; Gifts Delivered Through a Servant. The above rule becomes important in determining whether a given relationship is that of master and servant or one involving an independent contractor, for whose acts there is no liability. The question is one of right to control. If the hirer of services retains the right to control the manner of performing the services (evidenced generally by the right to hire and discharge the servants of the party who is to perform the work, although this is not an unfailing indication), then the hirer is the master of the other party and of the servants of the other party, and responsible for their torts, while if he has not retained this right of control he is not, for in the latter case the person hired is an independent contractor (Berg v. Parsons, 156 

N. Y. 109, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 122; Kellogg v. Church 

Charity Foundation, 203 N. Y. 191, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

142). The latter case evidences the rule that a liveryman is usually an independent contractor, and he alone, and not the hirer, is liable for the torts of drivers furnished by him when a vehicle also is furnished. The owner of premises who has engaged a general contractor to erect or repair the building is not liable for negligence of the general contractor's servants or the servants of a sub-contractor to a third person or to the servants of another sub-contractor (cases cited Smith v. Matthews, 241 

N. Y. Supp. 689). Sometimes, however, the person for whom the work is being done is liable, even though the person doing the work is an independent contractor. Examples of such liability are found (1) when the person for whom the work is. being done interferes and directs the act causing the damage or..... orders the work done in such a manner that harm results; (2). when the thing contracted to be done is unlawful or creates a nuisance; or (3) where a statute places a duty on the owner and the injury is the result of the commission or omission (by the independent contractor) of an act which the statute made it the duty of the owner to refrain from doing or to do, for in such case

the owner having delegated his duty must see that it is properly performed. See Berg v. Parsons, supra; Cooley, Stud. Ed. Sec. 

260; Burdick, 4th Ed. pp. 168; 171-178. In a case under (3) above, where the contractor fails to observe the duty the statute has put upon the owner, the contractor is not liable to a person injured unless the statute has placed the duty upon him as well; it is the owner only who is liable (Gordon v. Auto Club, 101 

Misc. 724, aff'd 180 App. Div. 927; Daly v. Margolies, 169 Supp. 448).

When a master loans his servant to another, and the servant commits a tort, the test for determining which of the two possible masters is liable is again the test of "right of control." If the general master is still in control of the manner of doing the work and the person to whom the servant is loaned has no right to control him (as to the manner of doing the work, for merely pointing out the work to be done is no control, otherwise every passenger in a taxi would be liable for the torts of the driver), the general master is still liable (Kellogg v. Church Charity 

Foundation, supra). But if control is parted with, and vested in the borrower of the servant, no matter how temporarily, the borrower is liable (cases infra). As to which of the two is in control, the question is usually for the jury (Ryciak v. N. Y. 

Oversea Co., 206 App. Div. 781), but the court will decide the question as a matter of law when the facts are not in dispute and conflicting inferences cannot be drawn. (For cases showing the rule, and holding the borrower responsible, see Bassi v. 

Orth, 58 Misc. 372; Higgins v. W. U. Tel. Co. 1, supra; Olive v. 

Whitney Marble Co. 103 N. Y. 292; Wyllie v. Palmer, supra; 

Bonwit, Teller v. Hosford, 107 Misc. 490, aff'd 186 N. Y. Supp. 

934). See also St. John's Law Review Vol. II, p. 72. 

Wallace v. Casey, 132 App. Div. 35, is of peculiar interest.

In that case, the defendant, a factory owner, was in the habit of giving away barrels as fire wood. The plaintiff, a child six years of age, a seeker after the free wood, was struck by a barrel negligently thrown to him by one Murtha, defendant's employee. 

The Court held that an action could not be maintained, because the plaintiff, having been in the act of receiving something for nothing, must be held to have assumed the risk of any negligence defendant's servant might commit in giving it to him.

This rule, while arguable if the analogy with the giving of a gratuitous ride to a guest passenger (infra, Sec. 155) or of the hospitality of the home to a guest (infra, Sec. 149)) is to be preserved, is a doubtful one. Cf., also, with the rule governing charitable corporations, supra, Section 45. 

While, however, similarity to gratuitous benefits extended to guests exists, it must be remembered that the host's freedom from liability extends only to results of defects not known to the host. The risk of them is assumed by the guest, but not the risk of careless driving. (Sec. 155, infra.)

That reasoning applied here would result in a rule that the beneficiary assumes the risk of dangers, not known to the donor, in the thing given, but does not assume the risk of carelessness in the manner of giving. The donor, having undertaken to give it, is bound not to deliver it in so careless a manner as to hurt the donee.

Sec. 53. Master and Servant; Incidents of Their Liability. 

When a servant commits a tort for which the master is liable, the servant is also liable; indeed, to the person injured they are joint tort feasors, and can be sued together or separately (supra, Sec. 50). If, however, through no participating guilt of the master, but merely because of the relationship, he is made liable for the tort of his servant and judgment against him is taken, and by him paid, he can recover from his servant the whole amount, on the equitable principle of indemnification (infra, Sec. 66).

Sec. 54. Master and Servant; Liability of Master for Acts of Servant; Conclusion. It is elementary that if a master expressly authorizes a tort, he is liable together with the servant who commits it in obedience to the master's orders. He is also liable when by accepting the benefits of the transaction in which the tort was committed he ratifies or adopts torts committed by the servant (Dempsey v. Chambers, 154 Mass. 330, Edgar's

Cases on Torts, p. 157). But by far the greatest number of instances of liability on the part of a master for acts of his servant are cases where the act done was not authorized expressly or ratified, but was within the scope and course of the master's business and within the scope of the servant's duties. 

The question of whether the act of the servant is within the scope of the master's business is, as every question of fact

is, a question for the jury if the evidence is in conflict or if conflicting inferences can reasonably be drawn when the facts are not in conflict (Craven v. Bloomingdale, 171 N. Y. 

439, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 148; Rounds v. D. L. & W., 64 

N. Y. 129, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 131). As to what acts are within the scope of the master's business, let us consider one or two cases so that we may arrive at the important rules: (a) Where a gate-keeper refused to let a passenger who had lost his ticket pass out of the station, and caused his arrest, the employer, a railroad, was held liable, even though the gatekeeper had exceeded the powers given him by his master, the railroad, for the steps were taken in an effort to collect the fare due his master (Lynch v. Met. etc., Ry. Co., 90 N. Y. 77, Edgar's

Cases on Torts, p. 126). Likewise, if the act be done in the implied line of duty, or pertain to the service within the duty of the servant, the master is liable although the doing of the act be the disobedience of an express prohibition by the master (Higgins v. W. P. R. Co., 46 N. Y. 23; Trimble v. N. Y. Central, 162 N. Y. 84). (b) If a brakeman kicks a trespasser off a train so that he is hurt, the master of the brakeman is liable, for to eject trespassers being within the scope of his duties, the servant makes the master liable if he uses excessive force in performing his duties (Rounds v. D. L. & W., supra). 

(c) If, however, an injury is wantonly inflicted, for a purpose not connected with the master, the master is not liable, for then the wrong is done to serve the purpose, personal to the servant, of sport, revenge, or what not (Rounds v. D. L. & W., supra; Cohen v. Dry Dock, 69 N. Y. 170). as to (d) Similarly, any act done by the servant to serve his own purposes is not within the scope of his duties so make the master liable. For example, if an arrest be wrongfully procured by a servant, not for the purpose of aiding the master but to aid the public in bringing a suspected criminal to justice, the master is not liable (Mulligan v. N. Y. Central, 129 N. Y. 

506), though the alleged crime concerned the master's affairs. (e) Inasmuch as the master is not liable for wanton acts of the servant under (c) above, he is not liable for punitive damages when an act within the scope of his business is maliciously done, although he will be liable for compensatory dam-

ages if the malice was not the inducing cause of the act in the first place, but merely led to or consisted in the use of excessive force in the doing of it, as in (b) above; the master is liable for punitive damages only where in pari delicto, as by expressly authorizing or participating in an act involving express malice (Craven v. Bloomingdale, supra; Sec. 46, supra). 

An interesting case, Riley v. Standard Oil, 231 N. Y. 101, holds (reaffirming an old and well known rule) that where a servant goes on an independent journey of his own, the master is not liable for torts committed on the way. But it also holds that where the journey is partly to serve his own purposes and partly to serve those of his master (as if he had to go from his master's place of business to another place and back, and did so, but on the way back deviated from the course for his own purposes, in the end returning), the question of whose business was being served is for the jury, on the ground that conflicting inferences can be reasonably drawn from the facts.

Under Section 59 of the Vehicle and Traffic Law (until recently Section 282e of the Highway Law), the owner of a motor vehicle is liable in damages for harms done with it by any person driving it with the owner's consent, express or implied. 

This statute was first adopted in 1924. 

So many and varied are the ramifications of this rule, and so frequently are new aspects of it adjudicated upon, that it cannot satisfactorily be dealt with in an outline. 

For that reason, careful consideration is urged of Fleugel v. 

Coudert, 244 N. Y. 393, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 70; 

Psota v. Long Island R. Co., 246 N. Y. 388, Edgar's Suppl. 

Cases on Torts, p. 72; Cohen v. Neustadter, 247 N. Y. 207, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 77; and Gochee v. Wagner, 257 

N. Y. 344, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 80.

For articles on the subject, see St. John's Law Review, Vol.

CHAPTER IV MASTER AND SERVANT; WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION LAW Sec. 55. Duties of Master Toward Servant; Repairs of Machinery; Assumption of Risk and Contributory Negligence. 

The master owes the servant the duty of (a) furnishing adequate and suitable tools and implements for his use, (b) furnishing a safe and proper place in which to work, (c) employing skillful and competent workmen not only to assist and work with him but also to direct his labor, (d) warning the servant of danger, and (e) promulgating proper rules when made necessary by the complexity of the work and the size of the establishment (Pantzar v. Tilly Foster Mining Co., 99 N. Y. 368, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 165; McGovern v. Ry., 123 N. Y. 280). The measure of all these duties is that ordinary diligence is required; and if, in any case, these duties or a part of them are delegated to another, the master is nevertheless liable for failure of that other to use due care (Pantzar v. Tilly Foster Mining Co., supra; 

Baily v. Ry., 139 N. Y. 302; Carlson v. Phoenix, etc., Co., 132

N. Y. 273). It is, of course, elementary that the question of what is due care is for the jury, unless but one inference can be drawn from the proven facts. 

Where a servant who uses a machine has the duty of repairing it and failing to do so is hurt, he cannot, of course, recover, for the negligence is his own; a fellow servant who uses the machine with him who has the duty to repair it can recover for breach of that duty against his fellow servant only and not against the master; but if the servant in whom the duty to repair is vested is not a co-user of the machine with him who is injured, the latter can recover from either or both, for this is a clear case of delegated duty, the delegee being the alter ego of the master (Cregan v. Marston, 126 N. Y. 568 and cases cited).

These are complete defenses to actions in tort by a servant against his master for a breach of any or all of the above duties. For previous discussion of assumption of risk see Sec. 

34, supra; for discussion of contributory negligence in general, see infra, Sections 155-159. Assumption of risk is a matter

of assent, while contributory negligence is a matter of conduct. 

A servant who fails to leave an employment after notice of a danger will be held to have assumed the risk thereof; but if the inducement to stay was a promise by the master to repair, the master is liable, for the risk has not been assumed (Hough v. Ry., 100 U. S. 213; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 169).

Sec. 56. Assumption of Risk; Fellow Servant Rule. If a master has used due care to select competent fellow workers, his duty is at an end with regard thereto, and he is not liable for the negligence of one fellow servant toward another, for the law considers that servants have agreed to assume the risk of such negligence (Randall v. Ry., 109 U. S. 478). It is not every employee of the common master, however, who is a fellow servant within the rule. The test as to whether or not a servant is a fellow servant (in New York and the Federal courts) is as to whether he is employed to do any of the acts within the five duties of the master considered above, or whether he is employed to do some other acts; if the former, the rule does not apply, they are not fellow servants, and the master is liable; but if the latter then the contrary is the result (Crispin v. Babbitt, 81 N. Y. 516; 

Hankins v. Ry., 142 N. Y. 416; Baltimore Ry. v. Baugh, 149 

U. S. 368). There are other jurisdictions, however, whose test is different. It is also necessary (a) that there be a common master (Hallett v. N. Y. Central, 167 N. Y. 543), (b) that the employee be injured while engaged in his master's work, and not after hours or when he is otherwise out of his master's business, the question of whether or not he is at the time engaged in his master's work being for the jury if conflicting inferences can be drawn (Russell v. Ry., 17 N. Y. 134), and (c), that they be engaged in a common employment (Slater v. Jewett, 85 N. Y. 

61), or they are not fellow servants and the master is liable.

But they may be fellow servants, though they exercise different functions, if they contribute to a common object. So it has been held that a chauffeur, whose duty is to transport persons to and from his master's place of abode, is a fellow servant of a maid, whose duties lie within the maintenance of the place of abode 

(Fay v. De Camp, 257 N. Y. 407, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 14).

Sec. 58. The Workmen's Compensation Law. This enact ment is one of the greatest pieces of paternal legislation of all time. Inasmuch as our harsh common law rules giving the master such defenses as the fellow servant rule and others furnished just ground for the charge of class selfishness, the Workmen's Compensation idea sprang up. The first enactment (Laws 

1910, Ch. 647) was held unconstitutional (Ives v. So. Buffalo R. 

Co., 201 N. Y. 271), and a constitutional amendment was adopted 

(Const. Art. 1, Sec. 19) permitting this legislation. The present law (Laws 1922, Ch. 615) is derived from the laws (Laws 1914, Ch. 41) passed pursuant to the amendment. The validity of the present law has been passed upon by the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court of the United States (See McKinney, Book 64, notes p. 10 et seq.), but it is unconstitutional so far as it attempts to regulate matters within the admiralty jurisdiction of the Federal courts (Newham v. Chile Exploration Co., 232 N. Y. 37, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 178; Washington v. 

Dawson, 264 U. S. 219). Federal statutes in this field are considered in the courses in Federal Practice and Admiralty. 

In the main, the law provides that in certain hazardous employments which are set forth, and in all employments in which four or more operatives are engaged (Europe v. Addison Amusements, 231 N. Y. 105, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 184), and in all other employments in which the employer desires to bring those of his help (who do not object in writing) within the statute (Secs. 2 and 3), certain things shall be done, the most noteworthy of which is that the employer shall arrange for insurance to cover illness, accident, or death (of all employees, both those who are operatives, and those who are not) arising out of the employment (Sec. 10). See St. John's Law Review, Vol. III, p. 144.

Payments are made to injured or ill employees or the dependents of deceased employees where the illness, accident, or death arose out of the employment (Scholtzhauer v. C. and L. 

Lunch Co., 233 N. Y. 12, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 175; Heide-

mann v. A. D. T. Co. 230 N. Y. 305, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

182, Andrews v. L. and S. Amusement Corp., 253 N. Y. 97, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 17), according to definite schedules set forth in the law, irrespective of negligence of the master, or contributory negligence of, or assumption of the risk by, the servant. Even though the injuries are received in another state, they are compensable under the New York statute, if the employment is here and the sojourn in the other state was temporary, and connected with the employment (Smith v. Aerovane 

Utilities Corp., 259 N. Y. 126, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 25).

The remedy provided thereby is an exclusive one in all cases covered by the law. That is, the employee cannot sue the master (Sec. 11). However, if the case is one where the employer is bound to make arrangements for compensation and he fails to do so, the employee has an election (which, once made is irrevocable) either to file a claim or to sue in a court of law (if he can prove a breach of duty by the master). If he elects to sue, the defenses of assumption of risk and contributory negligence cannot be interposed (Lindebauer v. Weiner, 94

Misc. 612; Pavia v. Petroleum Iron Works, 178 App. Div. 

345; Bocchetti v. Schuler, 33 St. Dept. 380). See Sections 

11 and 52 of the law. The action is a method of additional penalization of a master who fails in his duty under the statute.

The State Industrial Board, the commissioner, and deputies, in passing on claims, are not bound by technical rules of evidence (Sec. 118).

The employee or some one in his behalf must, within thirty days after an accident, give a written notice of injury to the employer and the commissioner, but the failure to give notice may be excused (a) if it could not be given, (b) if the master knew of the injury, (c) if the master has not been prejudiced by failure to receive the notice, or (d) if the master waives the provision, as by a failure to raise the matter before the board (Sec. 18). See Twonko v. Rome Brass and Copper Co., 224 

N. Y. 263, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 188).

A claim (whether for illness, injury, or death) must be filed with the commissioner within one year (Sec. 28). See Twonko v. Rome Brass and Copper Co., supra).

Appeals from determinations of the board are taken to the 

Appellate Division, Third Department (Sec. 23). 

The only acts of the servant which can defeat the provisions of the act are (a) intoxication as the sole cause of the injury (b) wilful intention to bring about injury or death of himself or another (Sec. 10). But there are presumptions (a) that the claim comes within the act, (b) that sufficient notice was given, (c) that intoxication of the servant was not the sole cause of the injury, and (d) that the injury was not occasioned by wilful intent to bring about injury or death of himself or another (Sec. 21).

The employee cannot (a) agree to waive the act, (b) contribute to the cost of the insurance, (c) assign, release or commute compensation, or (d) waive the exemption (given by the law) from claims of creditors (Secs. 31, 32, 33).

The failure of an employer to insure in a case under the act, in addition to giving the employee an election between compensation and an action, is a misdemeanor (Sec. 52). 

It can thus be seen that although this statute is not part of the law of torts, it is of importance in our course because in the cases to which it applies it abolishes much of the law of torts, for it accomplishes (in the case to which it applies) two radical results: (a) It eliminates considerations of the employer's negligence, except in the event of the election permitted when the employer fails to provide compensation. (b) It eliminates consideration of the employee's contributory negligence and of assumption of the risk by him. 

When an employee is injured in the course of his master's business, but negligence of a third person caused the injuries, the employee may either (a) claim compensation and refrain from suing the third person, or (b), file a notice that he elects to sue the third person, the effect of which is to preserve his rights to compensation in the event that he loses his suit or recovers less than the compensation award would have been. If

the election is made to take compensation, the employer is subrogated to the employee's rights against the third party

Sec. 59. In General. Most actions to redress breaches of legal duty are for money damages. In many cases, however, the law gives a right to self-help (without resort to the courts) or to sue for an injunction (judicial help) so that the wrong may be more effectively righted than by the mere payment of money. 

Sec. 60. Injunction. A much more thorough consideration of the principles governing the granting of injunctions will be had in the course on equity than can be attempted here. Suffice it to say that an injunction will not be granted where money alone would suffice or where harm is not threatened or continuing. Injunctions are frequently granted in nuisance actions (indeed, this is the class of cases in which injunctions are most frequently sought and granted) for it is mainly in such cases that the commission of the wrong is not a matter of a mere instant but a continuing affair, and that money damages are not adequate redress (Campbell v. Seaman, 63 N. Y. 568, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 539). We have previously seen that in labor disputes and to restrain unfair competition and practices in industrial relationships injunctions are frequently granted (supra, Sec. 31). Under Civil Rights Law 

Secs. 50 and 51, the publication of a person's name or picture for advertising or commercial purposes may be enjoined. Injunctions are also granted, where the injuries are continuing or where inadequately reparable mischief is threatened in cases of waste and trespass (Forbell v. City of New York, 164 N. Y. 552, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 66). Injunctions will not be granted to restrain libel (Marlin v. Shields, 171 N. Y. 384). These matters will be touched upon further in considering particular torts. 

Sec. 61. Self-help. The two most important examples of self-help are the abatement of nuisances, and defense of self, family and property. The latter has been previously considered in Chapter II (Supra, Secs. 26-28). As to the right to abate nuisances: the remedy of injunction is a form of abatement, but only abatement accomplished by self unaided by judicial decree can be considered "self help." Injunction is an example of

"judicial help." An exclusively public nuisance cannot be abated. It is only private nuisances, or public nuisances which as to the person abating them are private nuisances, that may be abated (Moody v. Supervisors 46 Barb. 659; Brown v. De 

Groff, 50 N. J. L. 409). Due care must be taken, in using this remedy, to do no more harm than is necessary; the remedy should, therefore, be availed of, with caution, only when the emergency requires such speedy relief that recourse to the courts is not feasible (People ex. rel. Copcutt v. Board of Health, 140 

N. Y. 1; Mark v. Bridge Co., 103 N. Y. 28; Meeker v. Van Rensaalaer, 15 Wend. 397). Notice of intention to abate is not necessary, unless human life will be endangered, but it is better. to give it if time permits (Estes v. Kelsey, 8 Wend. 555; Bull v. Flagler, 23 Wend. 354). 

Sec. 62. Damages. As was said above, most actions sounding in tort are for money damages. Damages in tort actions are generally compensatory (i. e., for fair and just recompense). 

Sometimes, however, as in cases of injuria sine damno (supra, Chapter II), even though breach of legal duty be shown, the plaintiff cannot recover because of his having failed to prove damage (damnum, i. e., legal damage). Frequently, however, in tort actions proof of damage is not required, damage being inferred, as in cases of malicious prosecution, where the institution of a criminal proceeding, though no arrest is made, is sufficient, the other elements being present, to sustain an action (infra, Sec. 86). However, there are cases where proof of damage is required, as in cases of defamation not per se (q. v.) before even nominal damages can be awarded. *Nominal damages, nevertheless, are generally awarded even where compensatory damages are denied by the jury in wrongs to the person (i. e., defamation per se, assault, etc.), with a few exceptions such as defamation not per se and negligence. As to whether nominal damages or compensatory damages will be awarded in a case which is permitted to go to a jury in which actual damages have not been proved, but may be inferred, the question is for the jury. Punitive damages are permitted to be awarded by the jury where express malice (i. e., hatred, ill-will, or intentional inflicting of injury) are shown.

Lack of time in this course, which is primarily a course of rights and duties rather than of remedies, prevents full discus-

sion of these principles, which will be more thoroughly considered in the course in the law of damages. For authorities, see the standard works on damages; Chicago Ry. v. Rend, 6 Ill. 

App. 243, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 195; Burdick's Law of 

Torts, 4th Ed., pp. 238-242; Cooley's Law of Torts, Stud. Ed., pp. 124-126; supra, this work, Secs. 18, 46, 48, 49 and 54(e). Mention will again be made of the classes of damages in the discussion of the various particular torts. 

Damages are also divided into "general damages" (which necessarily arise from the wrong complained of, as pain in a personal injury case, and which need not be specially pleaded), and "special damages" (such as loss of earnings and cost of medical attendance, which must be pleaded specially to be recovered).

Sec. 63. Interest. In tort actions, the rules governing the allowance of interest are as follows: (a) In actions to redress torts to personal property which diminish the value of the property the allowance of interest is discretionary with the jury (Wilson v. City of Troy, 135 N. Y. 96, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 209); (b) in actions to redress all other property torts, the right to interest is absolute (Wilson v. City of Troy, supra); (c) in actions to redress personal torts, interest may not be had (Wilson v. City of Troy, supra); and (d) in actions for wrongful death, which partakes of the nature of a property tort, the clerk adds to the amount of the verdict interest thereon from the date of death (Dec. Est. Law Sec. 132). 

Sec. 64. Joint, Several, and Concurrent Tort Feasors.

When wrongdoers act together, with some element of concert, they are "joint tort feasors," and their liability is joint and several, i. e., all, or any number less than all, may be sued in one action, or the plaintiff may sue them separately, or sue part of them in one action, part of those remaining in another, and so on.

The judgment when obtained will be for the full amount against all (Barrett v. Third Ave. Ry., 45 N. Y. 628; Keegan v. 

Hayden, 14 R. I. 175, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 203; Matthews v. D. L. & W., 56 N. J. L. 34). The same rules of liability have been applied to tort feasors whose acts concur without concert to cause a single injury, and, although strictly speaking they are not joint tort feasors, they are so called by many courts; a better name, and one used by more careful judges and writers,

is "concurrent tort feasors" (Matthews v. D. L. & W., supra;

Kirby v. Pres. of D. & H., 90 Hun. 588, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 226; Colegrave v. N. Y., N. H., & H., 20 N. Y. 492; Barrett v. Third Ave. R. Co., 45 N. Y. 628; Slater v. Mersereau, 64 N. Y. 

138; Wabash, etc., Ry. Co. v. Schacklett, 105 Ill. 364; Transit Co. v. Schacklett, 119 Ill. 232; Carterville v. Cook, 129 III. 152; Cuddy v. Horn 46 Mich. 596. Contra, in Pennsylvania; cf. Penn. cases cited in Matthews v. D. L. & W., supra). The feature which distinguishes joint tort feasors from concurrent tort feasors is concerted action, the wrongdoers belonging to the former class if they acted in concert. The matter is not important, however, for the rules of liability are the same. They may be sued together or alone. In Matthews v. D. L. & W., supra, which was an action brought against plaintiff's carrier and a railroad whose tracks were crossed on the journey, for personal injuries received in an accident alleged to have been the result of negligence of both of them, it was held that, if each defendant owed the plaintiff a duty, whether each owes the same or each a different duty, and if each is guilty of a breach which concurred with that of the other to cause a single injury, they may be sued together or separately; it was also held that if there be no element of concert, the question of whether both or either, and if only one, which, should be held liable, is properly left to the jury upon a charge that if each was guilty of a breach and their breaches concurred to cause a single injury, they should both be held. 

See also Kirby v. Pres. of D. & H., supra, which holds, further, that the jury may find against one defendant and not against the other if it believes only one of the two to have been at fault. 

In Barrett v. Third Ave. R. Co., supra, the court said, "If both were negligent in a manner and to a degree contributing to the result, they are liable jointly and severally." A similar statement may be found in Covey v. Havener, 182 Mass. 250. 

Hanrahan v. Cochran, 12 App. Div. 91 and Simons v. Everson, 124 N. Y. 319, more closely resemble cases of joint tort feasors than of merely concurrent tort feasors, and yet even in these cases the same element of joint and concerted action is not present which made Keegan v. Hayden, supra, in which three constables accomplished a joint false arrest, a typical case of joint tort feasors. In the Hanrahan case, two persons were illegally racing, and the vehicle of one injured the plaintiff. Both defendants

were held. In the Simmons case, three defendants had maintained adjoining buildings, which were destroyed by fire, leaving the walls tilting dangerously. The plaintiff was injured by the fall of the walls, and all three defendants were held liable to him, although, as to one of them, proof that his wall struck plaintiff was lacking. The best known and clearest example of concurrent tort feasors is found in the case of Slater v. Mersereau, supra, in which one contractor negligently omitted to cut a recess in a roof so that water could run into a pipe and be drained. As a consequence the water ran into the cellar, and there joined with water permitted to accumulate by another contractor. The whole was sufficient to overflow and seep into the adjoining premises, injuring the plaintiff's goods. The plaintiff sued one of the two and the one sued was held liable for all the damage. The other, however, would also have been held liable had he been joined as a defendant or sued in a second action, for the cases cited and followed were the cases of railroad collisions in which both railroads were held, and the court said: "There is no good reason why each should not be held liable for the damages caused by the different acts of all." 

It has, however, been held that where many persons whose lands abut on a stream are draining into it matter which pollutes it, they may not be sued jointly in an action for damages, for each acts entirely independently and on his own land. However, they may be joined in an action for injunctive relief. If damages be sought, however, each is not liable for the whole. damage, but only for the part caused by him (Chipman v. Palmer, 77 N. Y. 51). The reason for this is that there would still be pollution if one of the wrongdoers were to cease committing his nuisance, while in the Slater case it took the combined quantities of water to accomplish the seepage.

Likewise owners of noxious chimneys cannot be joined in actions for damages, although the creator and the maintainer of a nuisance may be (Burkick, 4th Ed., Par. 439) where both are liable. See infra, Sec. 147. 

Sec. 65. Conspiracy. In New York, conspiracy is not a separate tort. The only advantage (it is, however, a substantial one) of pleading and proving it is to hold all the conspirators liable for the overt acts of the others, committed pursuant to the conspiracy. If, therefore, one or more of the conspirators com-

mits an overt act amounting to a tort in pursuance of the conspiracy, the liability of all is joint and several, for they are joint tort feasors, the element of concert consisting in the common plan (Brackett v. Griswold, 112 N. Y. 454; Place v. Minster, 65 

N. Y. 89). See also Van Horn v. Van Horn, 52 N. J. L. 284, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 375.

Sec. 66. Contribution and Indemnity. In the absence of stat ute, joint wrongdoers are not entitled to contribution or indemnity as between themselves, which means that if one of them pays a judgment for a tort he is not entitled to reimbursement in whole or part from the others, for the reason that the right to contribution and indemnity rests on equitable principles, and a court of equity will not lend its aid to a wrongdoer, whether wilfully or merely carelessly at fault (Weidman v. 

Sibley, 16 App. Div. 616; Keegan v. Hayden, supra; Andrews v. 

Murray, 33 Barb. 354). An important exception to this rule. and reasoning, however, exists where the parties are not in pari delicto, i. e., not equally at fault. In such a case, the less guilty, if he pays a judgment based on a tort committed by the other but for which he is at law responsible, may have complete indemnity from that other (Trustees, etc., v. Foster, 147, Edgar's

Cases on Torts, p. 205). The cases of joint tort feasors in which this remedy is most frequently granted are those of master and servant (for they are joint tort feasors; see supra Sec. 50) in which masters recover from servants the amounts of judgments obtained against and paid by masters for torts of the servants in the scope of their employment, but in which the masters have not participated (Moore v. Appleton, 26 Ala. 633). See also 

Smith v. Forman, 43 Conn. 244, Fulton County v. Hudson R. T. 

Co., 130 App. Div. 343, and Hudson, etc., Ry. Co., v. M., etc., Co., 180 App. Div. 86.

When parties are jointly engaged in a legal enterprise in furtherance of which an unintentional tort is committed (as in the case of partners), and one of them pays a judgment obtained against both or against him alone, he is entitled to contribution, i. e., a pro rata share of the amount, from the other 

(Cooley's Law of Torts, Stud. Ed., Sec. 44). 

Since 1928, even joint tort feasors in pari delicto have had contribution to some extent. Where some or all of them are joined as defendants in a single action, a right to contribution exists

between those joined (Civil Practice Act, Section 211a; 

Deuscher v. Cammerano, 256 N. Y. 328, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on 

Torts, p. 33; Price v. Ryan, 255 N. Y. 16).

But under the section it has been held that one of several joint tort feasors, sued alone, may not bring in the others as parties, to make sure of contribution (Fox v. Western N. Y. 

Motor Lines, 257 N. Y. 305, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 36).

An amendment of the Civil Practice Act is expected in the near future, to overrule the Fox case and supply the missing need. Otherwise there is grave question as to whether or not 

Section 211a violates the equal protection of laws clause in the .Constitution. See Professor Rothschild's article (and headnote), St. John's Law Review, Vol. VI, p. 1. His views upon

Section 211a were contrary to the rule of the Fox case but his article is an excellent "expose of the background of the subject and a review of its history and problems." 

Sec. 67. Local and Transitory Actions; Conflict of Laws. 

In the absence of statute, actions for personal torts and for wrongful death are transitory actions (i. e., they may be brought in any forum which can obtain jurisdiction of the person), and the law of the place where the tort was committed will be the test of whether an action lies, while the law of the forum will govern the matters pertaining to the remedy (Nelson's Admr. v. Ry. Co., 88 Va. 971, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 197; Wooden v. Ry Co., 126 N. Y. 10, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 247). In the Wooden case, however, it was held that although the lex fori governs the remedy, the officer who is permitted by the lex loci ("place") to bring the action there may bring it here, even though he be not the executor or administrator who, under our law, is the one who brings the action. Actions for wrongful death caused in other states may be brought here, and the foreign statutes will apply in toto, unless contrary to our public policy (Wikoff v. Hirschel, 258 N. Y. 28, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 30).

The rule that freedom from contributory negligence must be proved by the plaintiff is a rule of substance and is not merely procedural. Therefore the lex loci (the law of the place where the accident occurred) governs it and not the lex fori (the law of the place of trial). So if a case is tried here involving an ac-

cident which occurred in a place in which the law does not require the plaintiff to prove his freedom from contributory negligence, he will not be required to prove it here (Fitzpatrick v. 

Intern. Ry. Co., 252 N. Y. 127, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 112).

Sec. 68.

DISCHARGE OF TORTS CHAPTER VI 

Discharge in General. Torts may be discharged by act of the parties or by operation of law. Discharge by act of the parties is accomplished in any of four ways: 

By (a) contract, express or implied, in advance, (b) waiver of the injured party after the tort is committed. (c) satisfaction of judgment, (d) release.

Discharge by operation of law may be accomplished in various ways, of which the following three only will be considered, they being the important methods:

By (a) death of either or both parties, which discharges only some torts, however, (b) operation of the statute of limitations, (c) bankruptcy of the tort feasor, which discharges some, but not all, torts.

Sec. 69. By Contract in Advance of the Tort. The matter under this head has been previously considered, under the topic 

"Assent of Plaintiff," in Chapter II hereof, Sections 32-34. 

Sec. 70. By Waiver. An injured party may have more than one remedy to redress a given harm. The ground of complaint may, as we have more than once seen, be both a breach of contract and a tort. If so, there may be inconsistency between the remedies. For example, if goods of a person are wrongfully taken, the owner may treat the taking as an implied contract of sale, obligating the wrongful taker to pay for them as if he had bought them, upon the theory that by taking them he promised impliedly to pay for them, or the owner may treat the taking as a breach of the duty of the taker to respect his (the owner's) rights in the property. But since an action for the price is on the theory of sale, and since ownership passes in a sale, this remedy is inconsistent with an action for damages for conversion, based, as the latter is, upon the theory that ownership did not pass (the very essence of the action being that the defendant took what he did not own). Where such an in-

consistency exists, the commencement of an action on the theory of contract is an irrevocable election of that remedy to the exclusion of the other, and is, therefore a waiver of the tort (Terry v. Munger, 121 N. Y. 161, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 19; Zurlick v. Stankus, 125 Misc. 457; Sec. 16, supra). This follows the general rule governing election of remedies, which is that when inconsistent remedies exist, and one of them is chosen with knowledge of the existence of the other, the commencement of the action for the one remedy is a bar to a subsequent choice of the other (Kline v. Myriad Pictures Co., 211 A. D. 550, aff'd 

240 N. Y. 667).

Sec. 71. By Satisfaction of Judgment. If the plaintiff in a tort action obtains a judgment against one of a number of joint or concurrent tort feasors or a joint judgment against more than one or against all, or separate judgments against individual defendants, all joint wrong-doers, full satisfaction of any one judgment wipes out the tort and the right to all further remedies, either by action or execution, except for costs of pending actions 

(Inter. R. Co. v. Pickarski, 114 Misc. 349, aff'd 199 App. Div. 

953; Livingston v. Bishop, 1 Johns, 290; Knickerbacker v. Colver, 8 Cow. 111; Breslin v. Peck, 38 Hun. 623; Lord v. Tiffany, 98 N. Y. 412). Of course part may be obtained from one or more without discharging the tort, for it must be a full satisfaction of one judgment to discharge all (Livingston v. Bishop, supra).

Sec. 72. By Release. Consistently with the rules govern ing discharge by satisfaction of the judgment against one or several joint wrongdoers, the courts have steadily held that release of one of them will operate to discharge them all (Gilbert v. Finch, 173 N. Y. 455; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 216). However, where the right to sue the others is reserved, or where it otherwise appears that there was no intention to release the others, the release will be treated as a mere covenant not to sue, which does not operate as a blanket discharge of all (German- 

American Coffee Co. v. O'Neill, 102 Misc. 165; Gilbert v. Finch, supra).

A uniform statute proposed for adoption by all the states took effect in New York on April 6, 1928. It is found in the 

Debtor and Creditor Law. Its provisions apply to tort liability 

(Section 231), and all laws inconsistent with it, except pro-

visions of the Civil Practice Act and of the Partnership Law are repealed (Section 250). Section 234 is declaratory of the 

New York rule found in the cases. It provides that a release to one obligor, when there are more than one, does not discharge the other co-obligors against whom the obligee in writing and as part of the same transaction expressly reserves his rights. 

It also provides that in the absence of such a reservation, a discharge of the other obligors takes place only to the extent provided in Section 235.

Section 235 reads as follows: 

"Discharge of one obligor, without reservations. (a) If an obligee releasing or discharging an obligor without express reservation of rights against a co-obligor, then knows or has reason to know that the obligor released or discharged did not pay so much of the claim as he was bound by his contract or relation with the co-obligor to pay, the obligee's claim against that co-obligor shall be satisfied to the amount which the obligee knew or had reason to know that the released or discharged obligor was bound to such a co-obligor to pay. "(b) If an obligee so releasing or discharging an obligor has not then such knowledge or reason to know, the obligee's claim against the co-obligor shall be satisfied to the extent of the lesser of two amounts, namely (1) the amount of the fractional share of the obligor released or discharged, or (2) the amount that such obligor was bound by his contract or relation with the coobligor to pay."

Sec. 73. Abatement of Tort Actions by Death of a Party; 

Assignability of Torts; Actions for Wrongful Death. The death of either party before verdict, report, or decision serves to discharge a personal tort, although a tort to property survives the death of either or both and an action to redress a property tort may be maintained and continued by or against an executor or administrator. Death of either or both parties to a personal tort does not discharge it, however, if the death occurs after verdict report or decision (C. P. A. Secs. 82, 89, 1131; Dec. Est. 

Law 120; Gorlitzer v. Wollberg, 208 N. Y. 475). "Personal tort" as here used does not mean physical injuries alone, but also such torts as libel, slander, assault, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, etc., for although the statutes use the phrase "personal injuries," personal injuries are listed in General

Construction Law Sec. 37a, and include nearly all personal torts. 

It is of interest to note that the test of assignability of tort causes of action is similar, if not identical, with the test of sur vivability. Section 41 of the Personal Property Law provides, in effect, that the only causes of action for torts which cannot be transferred or assigned are those for personal injuries, which, read in the light of General Construction Law Sec. 37a, means personal torts without limitation to physical injuries alone. 

Judgments for money damages upon tort causes of action, however, are assignable under Personal Property Law Sec. 41, and this applies to judgments for personal torts as well as to judgments for torts to property. 

Although at common law when a wrongful act resulted in the death of a person his right to damages abated so that his estate had no remedy, a statute (Dec. Est. Law, Secs. 130-134) has created a remedy for the benefit of the surviving spouse and next of kin, when death is occasioned by the tort of another. 

The deceased person's right of action abates upon his death still, as at common law, so that his estate, as such, cannot sue for injuries. But the death brings into being a new action, brought by the executor or administrator of the deceased, the proceeds of which, however, do not belong to the estate, but to the surviving spouse and next of kin, free from the claims of creditors. The death of the tort feasor before verdict wipes out the cause of action (Hegerich v. Keddie, 99 N. Y. 258). Constitutional prohibition exists against enactment of statutory abrogation of this remedy, and against statutory limitation of the amount to be recovered (N. Y. Const., Art. I, Sec. 18). If, before his death, the injured person had obtained judgment for the personal injury which later caused his death, no action for wrongful death can be maintained (Littlewood v. Mayor, 89 

N. Y. 24, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 227). Although the period of limitations upon actions for wrongful death is two years from the date of death, yet, when the statute of limitations upon the original cause of action for injuries has run before death ensues, no action for wrongful death can be maintained (Kelliher v. 

N. Y. Central, 212 N. Y. 207, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 23.).

Although, as we have seen, the action abates and the cause of action dies with the death of the wrongdoer, it survives the death of the sole administrator or executor of the deceased and

of one or more beneficiaries, if there is another of the next of kin of the deceased still alive (Meekin v. B'klyn H. R. Co., 164 

N. Y. 145, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 239). In (negligence) death actions, contributory negligence of the deceased must be both pleaded and proved by the defendant (Dec. Est. Law, Sec. 131).

The damages when recovered are distributed among the bene ficiaries according to Dec. Est. Law, Sec. 133. The measure of damages is the "fair and just compensation for the pecuniary injuries" caused by the death (Dec. Est. Law, Sec. 132). The outrage to the deceased and his pain and suffering may not be considered (Quinn v. Moore, 15 N. Y. 432), nor may sorrow and loss of companionship be included in the damages (Costello v. 

Buffalo G. E. Co., 183 App. Div. 48; Grimm v. Maurocordato, 191 App. Div. 550). Funeral expenses are a proper item of damage (Murphy v. N. Y. Central, 88 N. Y. 445), certainly if paid, under a duty of payment, by the surviving relatives who will receive the proceeds of the action for wrongful death. The income of the deceased is very important (Brougham v. State, 104 Misc. 

281; Hiser v. Davis, 201 App. Div. 213, aff'd 234 N. Y. 300;

Lewis v. State, 112 Misc. 667), as are his habits, health, sex, intelligence, and age (Hiser v. Davis, supra; Voorhaar v. Whitney and Co., 170 Supp. 674). The mortality tables may be used to show the expectancy of life in determining its value. (Lewis v. State supra). The age of the next of kin may be taken into consideration (Fleahman v. Ry. Co., 206 App. Div. 542, app. dism. 237 N. Y. 566). If the decedent leaves a father and mother surviving, the death of the father prior to the verdict will not be permitted to affect the amount of damages (Dec. Est. Law, Sec. 132). When the verdict is returned, the clerk, before entering judgment, must add interest on the amount of the verdict from the date of death (Dec. Est. Law, Sec. 132).

Sec 74. Statute of Limitations. The law imposes certain limitations of time within which actions must be started. In the main, these periods begin to run when the cause of action accrues (i. e., when the tort is committed), except that (1) in cases of fraud, the time does not begin to run until the discovery of the fraud (C. P. A. Sec. 48 sub. 5), and (2) infancy, insanity, and imprisonment for a term less than life will suspend the running of the statute for a time (C. P. A. Sec. 60). The technical problems presented by the suspension, and the limitations upon the

suspension, of the running of the time will be thoroughly considered in the course on the law of pleading and practice. 

The periods of limitation in the important toṛt actions are: 

Two years: 

Libel, slander, assault, battery, seduction, criminal conversation, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, malpractice (C. P. A. Sec. 50). 

Three years: Personal injuries resulting from negligence; actions against executors, administrators, receivers, and trustees of insolvent debtors to recover a chattel or damages for taking, detaining or injuring personal property by the defendant or the person whom he represents (C. P. A. Sec. 49). 

Six years: Actions to recover a chattel; actions to recover damages for fraud (deceit); actions to recover for other torts to person or property not otherwise provided for (C. P. A. Sec. 48).

It must not be forgotten that the period of limitation upon actions for wrongful death is two years from the date of death. 

As we have seen, except as above noted, the period begins to run when the tort is committed. In cases of conversion, where wrongful taking is relied on, the period begins to run at the time of the taking, for it is then that the tort is committed; where wrongful detaining is relied on, however, in a case where possession was lawful to begin with so that demand is necessary, the tort is not committed until after demand and refusal, and the period starts to run at that time (Burdick's Law on Torts, 4th 

Ed., par. 251). See infra, Section 130. 

Sec. 75. Bankruptcy. If the injured person is the bankrupt, and the tort is a personal one, bankruptcy has no effect on the cause of action and after his discharge in bankruptcy, the injured person may bring his action and the proceeds will belong to him (Colwell v. Tinker, 196 N. Y. 531). But if the cause of action be a tort to property, it passes to the trustee in bankruptcy (Bankruptcy Act of 1898, Sec. 70a). Claims under the

Workmen's Compensation Law are not subject to the demands of creditors (Workmen's Comp. Law, Sec. 33), and so come within the class of "exempt" property under Sec. 70a of the 

Bankruptcy Act.

If the tort feasor is the bankrupt, his liability for the torts of deceit, seduction, criminal conversation and all other wilful and malicious injuries to person or property survives bank-

ruptcy, (Bankruptcy Act of 1898, Sec. 17). This is true whether the "malicious" injuries have been reduced to judgment or not. 

They survive in any event. Judgments for other torts (i. e., those not malicious; e. g., negligently inflicted personal injuries or property damages) are not discharged in bankruptcy unless they arise out of contract or are reduced to judgment before the adjudication of bankruptcy. But if they arise out of contract, they are discharged in bankruptcy whether reduced to judgment or not. (Collier on "Bankruptcy," 13th Ed.), p. 622, note 136; p. 

1428, note 291).

But if a tort not arising out of contract is committed by the bankrupt after the adjudication, or if, though committed prior thereto, it was not reduced to judgment until after the adjudication, it will not be discharged by the bankruptcy whether the tort was wilful or not (Collier, pp. 1430, 1431, note 295).

FALSE IMPRISONMENT CHAPTER VII Sec. 76. Elements of the Tort. The essential elements of the tort of false imprisonment are (1) 

Detention 

(a) by force (actual or constructive), (b) against will be plaintiff, (c) must be entire. (2) Unlawfulness of detention (a) without valid process; or (b) without other legal justification. 

(3) Damages 

(a) general damages will be inferred, (b) special damages may be proved.

Actually, detention and damages are the only elements of this tort which must be proved to show a prima facie case. 

Damages are inferred by law; therefore, it is not necessary expressly to prove them, although such proof is always given, if it exists, otherwise an adequate sum often would not be awarded to the plaintiff. Unlawfulness is also inferred. 

This tort consists in the unlawful deprivation of the plaintiff's right of personal liberty. The tort is sometimes called "false arrest," although "false imprisonment" and "unlawful detention" are better names, since nothing so formal as the lay conception of arrest is necessary to constitute the tort. In other words, conduct may be actionable as false imprisonment though there be no intention to put the plaintiff into custody of the law or to charge him with an offense. 

Sec. 77. What Constitutes Detention. It is generally said that there must be an entire restraint of liberty in order to constitute an imprisonment, so that, if the plaintiff be permitted to go over one way although another or even all others are closed to him, there is no imprisonment (Bird v. Jones, 7 Q. B. 742).

If freedom of motion is restrained by force, that is a detention (Lynch v. Met. El. Ry. Co., 90 N. Y. 77, Edgar's Cases on

Torts, p. 126); but it is not necessary that force be used, for if the arrested person understands that he is under restraint or in

custody, that is enough (Pike v. Hanson, 9 N. H. 91, Edgar's

Cases on Torts, p. 253). If the person making the arrest says "You are my prisoner," and the plaintiff submits, arrest is accomplished (Genner v. Sparks, 1 Salk. 79, cited in Pike v. Hanson, supra), for there is no duty to resist a detention, void though it be (Pike v. Hanson, supra). When, however, plaintiff voluntarily accompanies the defendant, formal words of arrest and fear to resist being absent, there is no imprisonment, for the will of the arrested person has not been overcome, as it must be, to constitute detention (Foulke v. N. Y. etc. R. Co., 180 App. 

Div. 848, aff'd 228 N. Y. 269). Bare words, of course, will not constitute an arrest, for if, after being told that he is in custody, the plaintiff flees, no arrest has been accomplished; there must be a taking into possession of the body of the plaintiff by touching it, or "what is tantamount, a power of taking immediate possession of the body, and the party's submission thereto" (Pike v. Hanson, supra). Since an insane person has no will but is subject to the will of his guardian or committee, an action for false imprisonment in behalf of the insane person can be maintained for taking the insane person out of the custody of the committee without the consent of the latter (Barker v. Washbush, 200 N. Y. 280, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 264).

For a complete discussion of the ramifications of these principles, see the American Law Institute Restatement of the Law of Torts, particularly on the question of whether detention is complete. Under its views, the overcoming of one's will may be accomplished by trading upon his mere moral compunctions. 

For example, not only is there a complete detention though a way be left open if it leads only through a place where there may (known to the plaintiff) be a danger, such as a poisonous snake or wild animal, but there is also complete detention if the plaintiff has been deprived of his clothes and the only way out is through a place where he may reasonably expect to come upon persons of the opposite sex.

There must, however, always be consciousness of the restraint, or there is no overcoming of the will. So, if the plaintiff does not know of the dangers lurking on the way out, he has not been detained; his failure to obtain freedom is voluntary; so, too, if, unknown to him, he has been locked in a room and freed before his discovery of the incarceration.

69. 

Sec. 78. Unlawfulness. The plaintiff proves a prima facie case by showing the detention; he need not prove the element of unlawfulness as part of his prima facie case, for the burden of showing justification for the arrest is on the defendant (Snead v. Bonnoil, 166 N. Y. 325). 

Sec. 79. Justification. Detention may be justified in many ways. As we have seen many times before, every person, under our American theories, has a right of absolute freedom of action, limited only by the rights of others. As to whether or not a given state of facts will justify the deprivation of any of this freedom, the tendency is (and the true test always has been) to balance the right of the one against the right of the other or others, and render judgment in favor of him or them whose right outweighs the right of the other or others. Detention, then, is justified when the rights of other persons or of the state are involved to the extent that the conservation of those rights is of greater moment than the right of the plaintiff to absolute freedom of motion.

Let us consider some of the more important instances of justification: (a) Justification under valid warrant or judgment: A valid warrant or other process calling for the detention of the plaintiff protects him who executes it; all that is necessary is that the process be valid on its face, although, of course, the arrested person may have a cause of action for malicious prosecution against the person who procures the issuance of the warrant (Peo. v. Warren, 5 Hill 440; Marks v. Townsend, 97 N. Y. 590). 

Process void on its face does not protect either the magistrate who issues it or the officer who executes it (Burdick's Law of 

Torts, 4th Ed., par. 255; Blodgett v. Race, 18 Hun. 132, Edgar's

Cases on Torts, p. 255). Even a valid process may be so utilized as to cease to be a protection, as, if used for a purpose for which it was not intended (i. e., to collect a debt, etc.), which constitutes the tort "Malicious abuse of process" (q. v., Ch. VIII, B, infra). When a valid arrest is abused, it becomes void ab initio so that from the very beginning the detention is considered unlawful (Hendrix v. Manhattan, etc., R. Co., 181 App.

Div. 111, in which the accused was not taken before a magistrate without unnecessary delay, as he has a right to require). 

A magistrate who issues a warrant without evidence to support

it is liable (Blodgett v. Race, 18 Hun. 132, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 255), but if the evidence, though slight, be sufficient to raise an inference of guilt the magistrate is justified in issuing a warrant (Swart v. Richard, 148 N. Y. 264, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 261). A judge who erroneously decides as a matter of law that a certain statute permits the imprisonment of a person who later procures his discharge and sues the judge, is not liable (Lange v. Benedict, 73 N. Y. 12), and it has been held that a judge who erroneously but in good faith decides that he has jurisdiction over a proceeding and hears it, the hearing resulting in the incarceration of the defendant, is not liable for false imprisonment (Grove v. Van Duyn, 44 N. J. Law 564).

But the general rule is that to constitute justification the judge must have jurisdiction of the subject matter and of the person of the defendant (Lange v. Benedict, supra; Austin v. Vrooman, 128 N. Y. 229; Grove v. Van Duyn, supra). (b) Arrest without a warrant : Under certain circumstances, arrest without a warrant is justified. In New York a peace officer, and even a private person, may arrest without a warrant a person who commits or attempts to commit a crime (whether a felony or a misdemeanor) in the presence of the officer or private person making the arrest; a peace officer also may arrest when a felony has actually been committed (whether in his presence of not) and he has reasonable cause for believing that the person arrested is the one who committed it; a private person, however, may not arrest for a felony not committed or attempted in his presence unless the person arrested has actually committed it (Code of Criminal Procedure, Secs. 

177 and 183). It can thus be seen that neither a peace officer nor a private person can without a warrant arrest for a misdemeanor not committed or attempted in his presence, and that, when a felony has been committed, although not in the presence of the arresting officer or private person, a peace officer may arrest if he has reasonable cause to believe the arrested person guilty, but a private person may not arrest without absolute certainty that his prisoner committed the felony. As to what cause to believe the arrested person guilty of felony is reasonable and probable cause (so as to excuse a peace officer who has made an arrest though the commission of the felony was not in his presence), the question is for the jury, as questions of

reasonableness generally are, which we shall see in considering malicious prosecution (q. v. infra, Ch. VIII; Snead v. Bonnoil, 166 N. Y. 325). (c) Restraint of dangerous incompetents: If, by reason of illness, insanity of drunkenness, a person is in such condition that because of violence his freedom endangers his own safety or that of others, detention of him while the danger lasts is damnum absque injuria (Penal Law, Sec. 246, sub. 6; Porter v. 

Rich, 70 Conn. 235). 

Sec. 80. Damages. It is axiomatic that general damages (i. e., those which always follow the invasion of the right, as, in false imprisonment cases, the duration of the loss of freedom) may be proved though not pleaded, if detention was, as it had to be, pleaded generally. But special damages, which though naturally flowing from the injuria, do not necessarily or always follow it, must be both pleaded and proved, as, illness caused by a false imprisonment (Atchison, etc., Ry. Co. v. Rice, 36 Kan. 593). 

Sec. 81. Persons Liable. As we have seen, magistrates, peace officers, persons executing warrants, private persons, in short, anyone, may in certain cases be guilty of the tort of false imprisonment, and the circumstances under which their liability may arise has been considered. In this connection, a private person, without actually making an arrest or otherwise restraining a plaintiff, may be held liable if he expressly orders an arrest, or shows undue zeal or is active and officious in bringing about an unlawful arrest (Regan v. Morgan, 211 App. Div. 443), and a person who engages an attorney to enforce a right is liable if the attorney procures a false imprisonment in attempting to serve his client (Guillaume v. Rowe, 94 N. Y. 268, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 259).

CHAPTER VIII MALICIOUS PROSECUTION, MALICIOUS ABUSE OF PROCESS, AND SIMILAR TORTS. 

A. MALICIOUS PROSECUTION Sec. 82. Elements of Malicious Prosecution. If a person maliciously and without reasonable grounds therefor institutes criminal prosecution against another, he does him an injury, and an action lies (cases infra). Such wrong is known as "malicious prosecution." The elements of this tort (Miller v. Milligan, 48 

Barb. 30, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 270) are: (1) Prior termination of the maliciously instituted proceeding in favor of the person complaining (i. e., the defendant in the maliciously instituted proceeding, but who is now the plaintiff in the action for damages therefor). As to the necessity for this element see Wachter v. Silber, 190 N. Y. Supp. 863, and cases infra, (2) A want or lack on the part of the prosecuting party (i. e., the defendant in the malicious prosecution action) of reasonable and probable cause to believe the prosecuted person guilty of the offense charged (Ahrens v. Lefstein, 186 App. Div. 

953; Day v. Levine, 181 App. Div. 261; Ruhle v. White, 198 

App. Div. 388), (3) Malice on the part of the prosecuting party in instituting the proceeding of which plaintiff complains (Baumstein v. 

Rothkrug, 202 App. Div. 342), and 

(4) Damages. 

This tort usually but not necessarily results in a deprivation of personal liberty and in that respect is similar to false imprisonment. But the action for false imprisonment does not lie when arrest is accomplished under valid process, as we have seen before (supra, Sec. 79 [a]); however, where valid process is illegally procured an action for malicious prosecution lies (Hobbs v. Ray, 18 R. I. 84, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 275). The plaintiff has the burden of proof as to all the elements (Schultz v. Greenwood Cemetery, 190 N. Y. 276, 278), except that, when the prosecution was a criminal one, damages will be inferred (infra, Sec.

86). Where special damages are pleaded, as they must be to be recovered, the plaintiff has the burden of proving them, whether the proceeding maliciously prosecuted was a civil one or a criminal one (infra, Sections 86 and 88).

Sec. 83. Favorable Prior Termination. The test of whether or not there has been a termination is whether or not the prosecution has ended in such a way that the particular proceeding cannot be revived, although the fact that a new proceeding for the same offense can be started does not matter (Woodman v. 

Prescott, 66 N. H. 375, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 296). The termination must have been in favor of the defendant in the proceeding maliciously undertaken, that is, of him who is now the plaintiff (Woodman v. Prescott, supra). Examples of such termination are: An acquittal by a jury (Cardival v. Smith, supra); a withdrawal of the complaint by the complainant (Cardival v. Smith, supra); the failure of the complainant to appear, or other fact because of which the judge or magistrate orders the discharge of the prisoner (Clark v. Cleveland, 6 Hill. 

344; Cardival v. Smith, supra); a nolle prosequi, which means a quashing of the indictment on motion of the district attorney (Woodman v. Prescott, supra), although some courts disagree (Cardival v. Smith, supra). It can thus be seen that in order to constitute termination, a decision on the merits is not necessary. 

Indeed, the running of a period within which a proceeding, once undertaken, must be completed, may be a sufficient termination, although if the reason for the running of the statute is that the accused fled the jurisdiction, there is not suffi cient termination (Halberstadt v. New York Life Ins. Co., 194 

N. Y. 1, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 308). However, if the reason for the prisoner's flight is that the charge against him is a trumped up on and if he knows and can now prove that there was a conspiracy to convict him, the passing of the statutory time limit might be held to be a termination (Coffey v. Myers, 84 Ind. 

105, in which, however, it appears that the charges were dismissed because the originators thereof "became satisfied that they could not maintain the prosecution"). In Robbins v. Robbins, 133 N. Y. 597, “it appeared that the accused had been discharged in the criminal proceeding after a hearing by a police justice, and the only question was whether she had been discharged because there was no sufficient evidence against her or

whether she was erroneously discharged as a matter of sympathy upon her promise of good behavior." The quoted statement is drawn from the opinion in the Halberstadt case (supra), and the court continued: "This question was one of fact for the jury, which presumably resolved it in favor of the plaintiff. But, even if the justice under the circumstances was actuated by erroneous or improper motives in discharging her, it, nevertheless, beyond any question, was a sufficient termination of the proceeding under all of the authorities bearing upon that subject, and on either theory the basis was laid for an action of malicious prosecution."

Sec. 84. Want of Probable Cause. Probable cause (the lack of which is a necessary element of this tort) is a reasonable ground of suspicion supported by circumstances reasonably strong in themselves to warrant a reasonable man in his belief that the accused is guilty of the offense charged (Day v. Levine, supra), that is, to constitute probable cause (so that no action for malicious prosecution lies), the facts must be such as would lead a reasonably prudent man under like circumstances to believe the accused guilty (Hyman v. N. Y. Central, 240 N. Y. 

137). Conviction (which is not reversed) is, of course conclusive of the presence of probable cause, for, as we have seen (supra, Sec 83), prior favorable determination is a necessary element of the tort, but if the judgment of conviction is reversed and the indictment dismissed, or if upon a new trial. an acquittal is had, there is no such favorable termination (MacDonald v. Shroder, 214 Pa. 411). The indictment of the accused and even the holding of the accused by a magistrate so that the grand jury can act are prima facie evidence of probable cause (Dachs v. De Lite Realty Co., 210 App. Div. 

230, and cases cited). The defendant in a malicious prosecution case is entitled to state that when he prosecuted the plaintiff he believed that he had probable cause (Conner v. Wetmore, 110 App. Div. 440; Dachs v. De Lite, supra). Such testimony ought, on principle, be admissible only to show freedom from malice and not possession of probable cause, although in Conner v. Wetmore, supra, a dictum would seem to indicate that in the opinion of the court the evidence is admissible for both purposes. Advice of counsel as to the guilt of the accused does not constitute a defense, and is admissible only to show freedom

from malice and not to show that probable cause existed (Hazzard v. Flury, 120 N. Y. 123, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 290).

Both advice of counsel and belief of guilt-relate to the subjective state of mind of the defendant. Probable cause is objective. So advice of counsel and belief of guilt relate only to the absence of malice, which, too, is a subjective state of mind in its purer sense (see infra, next section), and they do not relate to the objective existence of probable cause and are not properly admissible to prove its presence, though admissible for their slight, self-serving weight to disprove malice. The question of whether or not probable cause existed may be for the court or for the jury. It is generally a mixed question of law and fact, so that the judge charges the jury as to the definition of probable cause, and the jury then finds the facts and applies to them the law charged by the court (Halsey v. 

N. Y. Society, 234 N. Y. 1; Anderson v. Dyer, 188 App. Div. 

707; Ruhle v. White, supra). Where, however, the facts are undisputed, both facts and law are for the court (Foulke v. N. Y. etc., R. Co., 180 App. Div. 848; Day v. Levine, supra; Hazzard v. Flury, supra; Ahrens v. Lefstein, supra). Although, even if the facts be undisputed, if conflicting inferences might be drawn from them by reasonable men, the question is for the jury (Halsey v. N. Y. Society, supra; Galley v. Brennan, 216 N. Y. 118).

The lack of probable cause may not be inferred from the presence of malice (Hall v. Suydam, 6 Barb. 83; Dachs v. De

Lite, supra), although malice may be inferred by the jury from the absence of probable cause (Hall v. Suydam, supra; 

Dachs v. De Lite, supra; Anderson v. Dyer, supra; Sidorsky, v. 

Lemkin, 188 Supp. 401; Miller v. Milligan, supra). If it appears as a fact that the defendant's prosecution of the plaintiff was due to a misapprehension as to the law governing the offense with which the defendant accused the plaintiff so that the plaintiff was acquitted or discharged on the law, there is a want of probable cause as a matter of law; in other words, the prosecutor may not escape liability for an error of law in arriving at his conclusion as to the guilt of the accused (Hazzard v. Flury, supra), even though the prosecutor was following the erroneous advice of counsel, if there was malice nevertheless.

Sec. 85. Malice. The malice required to be shown in prov ing a case of malicious prosecution is "malice in fact," i. e., wilfulness in commencing an unfounded, or poorly founded, prosecution of the plaintiff, with knowledge of its lack of proper basis, and either without regard to whether or not it injures the plaintiff or with intent to injure him (Vanderbilt v. Mathis, 5 

Duer 559). The question of malice is always for the jury (Harpsham v. Whitney, 77 Ill. 32; Stewart v. Connebom, 98

U. S. 187). To rebut a showing of malice a defendant is entitled to testify that he believed he had cause to think the plaintiff guilty (supra, Sec. 84), and that he was advised by counsel that the plaintiff was guilty or that probable cause to believe him guilty existed (supra, Sec. 84). The jury is entitled to infer malice from the absence of reasonable and probable cause (supra, Sec. 84), but "the deduction is not a necessary one" (Cooley's Law of Torts, Stud. Ed., p. 179 and cases cited).

Sec. 86. Damages. When the proceeding maliciously prosecuted was a criminal proceeding, damages need not be proved, for general damages (the harm to reputation, the rendering of the accused subject to imprisonment), are enough. 

As we shall see, however, in Sec. 88, infra, in order to recover for the malicious prosecution of a civil action, actual damages must be proved. Punitive damages may also be recovered upon a showing of express malice, i. e., hatred or ill will, which is a more sinister and aggravated class of malice than the malice in fact required to be proved as an element of the tort. See Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed. pars. 269-275; Smith v. Michigan 

Buggy Co., 175 Ill. 619, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 298. 

Special damages (actual imprisonment, if any, lost earnings, if any, and the like) may, of course, be proved. Obviously, to be compensated for, they must be proved. 

Sec. 87. Persons Liable. It is not the actual prosecutor alone who may be held liable for malicious prosecution; all who are shown to have had a part, even though slight, in originating or instigating it, or in interfering and engaging in it after its commencement, are liable jointly and severally (Dann v. Wormser, 38 App. Div. 460). But if the only complicity shown is a complaint to the police or to a magistrate stating the facts known to the complainant, as a consequence of which the arrest is made without active participation by complainant, there is no

liability Macauley v. Starr, Inc., 194 App. Div. 643 and cases cited therein). A corporation may be held liable for malicious prosecution (Willard v. Holmes, 142 N. Y. 492).

Sec. 88. Malicious Prosecution of a Civil Action. The prosecution, maliciously and without probable cause, of a civil action does not give rise to legal liability unless the person or property of the defendant in the action maliciously prosecuted has been interfered with as by arrest, attachment, receivership or replevin, for theoretically, although in practice inadequate, the costs allowed to the successful party in a civil action are his compensation for the inconvenience (Smith v. Michigan Buggy 

Co., supra; Ferguson v. Arnow, 142 N. Y. 580; Willard v. 

Holmes, supra; Weber v. Leventhal Co., 103 Misc. 80). The malicious institution of bankruptcy proceedings is actionable on the same theory, for bankruptcy proceedings render the estates of alleged bankrupts subject to receiverships (Sachs v. Weinstein, 208 App. Div. 360). B.

MALICIOUS ABUSE OF PROCESS; EXCESSIVE ATTACHMENT. 

Sec. 89. In General. The use of lawful, valid process for a purpose for which it was not intended, so that a result not lawfully or properly attainable under it is secured, is the tort "malicious abuse of process" (Lyons v. Scriber, 174 Supp 332). 

The elements of the tort are: (1) Lawful process, (2) Malice (in its use for an illegal purpose), and, 

(3) Damages. 

Examples of this tort are to be found in what is known as "excessive attachment," as, where a plaintiff attached property of the defendant to the value of $40,000 in suing upon a claim of $4500 (Zinn v. Rice, 154 Mass. 1, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

306). Another example is the use of a subpoena to collect a debt (Dishaw v. Wadleigh, 15 App. Div. 205). Abuse of process renders its use void ab initio, so that, if lawful process calls for an arrest, and the arrest is made, but following it an abuse of process takes place, the hitherto proper imprisonment is void so far as the wrongdoer is concerned, and he can be sued for false imprisonment (Holley v. Mix, 3 Wend. 350). Want of

reasonable and probable cause has no place in this tort; malice is inferred from the fact of the abuse or illegal use. (Zinn v. Rice, supra; Foy v. Barry, 87 App. Div. 291).

C. MAINTENANCE; BRINGING SUIT IN THE NAME OF ANOTHER. 

Sec. 90. In General. Maintenance (the aiding, with or with out malice, of litigation in which one has no interest) is said to be a tort unless the litigant aided is a kinsman, servant or poor neighbor (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 279). The bringing of an action (even though a perfectly good claim exists in favor of him whose name is used) in the name of another without authority from him is a tort to the defendant (Burdick's Law of 

Torts, 4th Ed., par. 278).

CHAPTER IX INVASION OF THE SO-CALLED RIGHT OF PRIVACY. 

Sec. 91. In General. At common law no such right existed (Roberson v. Rochester Folding Box Co., 171 N. Y. 538), but by statute (Civil Rights Law, Secs. 50 and 51) the right now exists in this state. If the likeness or name of a living person be used without his or her written consent for commercial or advertising purposes, three remedies now exist: (a) An action for money damages, (b) An action for an injunction, (c) A criminal proceeding to punish the offense as a misdemeanor.

This enactment is constitutional (Rhodes v. Sperry- 

Hutchinson Co., 193 N. Y. 223, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 318).

A partnership, not being a person or entity, cannot claim the benefit of these sections (Rosenwasser v. Ogoglia, 172 App. 

Div. 107), though the individual partners can, and, because it is not a living person, a corporation is outside of the protection of the statute. A corporation has no remedy if its seal and name are used for advertising purposes, unless, perhaps, they are libelously used (Vassar College v. Loose-Wiles Biscuit Co., 197 Fed. 

982) or the use amounts to unfair competition (supra, Sec. 31; infra, Sec. 140).

CHAPTER X. 

ASSAULT AND BATTERY 

Sec. 92. In General. The right invaded by the torts of assault and battery is the right of personal security. Although some writers and many judges use the two terms interchangeably, assault technically differs from battery in that, as we shall see, no physical injury or even contact is necessary to constitute assault if reasonable fear is induced by actual or apparent hostility, while to battery, physical contact is essential, and fear is not. Assault resembles an attempted battery, although an actual intention to commit battery is not necessary to assault if there be an apparent attempt or apparent intention. Battery is generally a completed or successful assault. See generally Chapman v. 

State, 78 Ala. 463, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 325, and cases cited infra.

The Restatement of the Law of Torts devotes three chapters to a fuller consideration of these principles than is contained in this brief outline. 

Sec. 93. Elements of Assault. The gravamen of assault is fear, i. e., reasonable fear of bodily harm. The elements of the tort, therefore, are: (1) Apparent hostile intent, wherein this tort differs from mere negligence (Brodsky v. Reiser, 195 App. Div. 557, and Sec. 

94, infra), evidenced by an apparent attempt to do bodily harm, (2) Producing reasonable fear (Sec. 95, infra), and (3) Damages (Sec. 96, infra). 

Sec. 94. Apparent Hostile Intent; Attempt to Do Bodily

Harm. The intention of the wrongdoer to do harm is not important, provided he gives the appearance of intending and attempting bodily harm (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 

280). Therefore, one who points even an empty pistol at another, provided the person at whom it is pointed does not know that it is not loaded, is guilty of assault (Beach v. Hancock, 27 

N. H. 223; State v. Church, 53 N. C. 15, Chapman v. State, supra). One who, at a heated meeting, advances on the chairman with fists clenched and a threat upon his lips, may be guilty of assault, though he is stopped before coming within striking dis-

tance (Stephens v. Myers, 4 Carr & P. 349, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 327).

However, if circumstances exist which, to the minds of reasonable men, preclude or rebut the inference that harm is intended, there is no assault. For example, words alone do not constitute assault (Scott v. Fleming, 16 Ill. App. 539; 

French v. Ware, 65 Vt. 338); and if the appearance of a present intention to commit an assault is detracted from, as, where defendant's words were, as he raised his whip "Were you not an old man I would knock you down," it was held that these words were to be taken into consideration by the jury as tending to qualify defendant's act and show that he had no intention to strike (State v. Crow, 23 N. C. 375, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 328). 

Sec. 95. Reasonable Fear. The fear produced must be reasonable (Beach v. Hancock, supra). As we have seen, it is not unreasonable for a man to experience fear at the sight of a gun aimed toward him, though it is not loaded, if he be in ignorance of the fact that it is not loaded (Sec. 94, supra). Further, it is not unreasonable for a woman to suffer fear if pursued against her will in an effort to induce her to sexual intercourse, or if her consent to sexual intercourse is solicited roughly or while she is helpless (State v. Meeley, 74 N. C. 425; Newell v. 

Whitcher, 53 Vt. 584). However, it is elementary that unless the alleged wrongdoer have or appear to have at his immediate command the means to commit bodily harm, fear of injury is unreasonable, and if the apparent assault be accompanied by qualifying words showing a lack of present intent to harm, the jury may find that the fear was likewise unreasonable (Sec. 94, supra). 

Sec. 96. Damages. In actions for assault, general and compensatory damages, without special proof, are allowed upon proof of the other elements; in accordance with the general rule, special or indirect injuries alone are required to be pleaded and proved if they are suffered (O'Leary v. Rowan, 31 Mo. 117).

Injured feelings of the assaulted person are to be considered by the jury (Maisenbacker v. Concordia Society, 71 Conn. 369). 

Upon proof of wilful intent, punitive damages may be recovered (Conners v. Walsh, 131 N. Y. 590). 

131 N. Y. 590). Although threatening language as provocation for an assault is not a de-

fense, it may be considered in mitigation of damages (Daniel v. 

Giles, 108 Tenn. 242), and in at least some of the cases of a tort assented to by a void consent (of which there are a few), it is arguable that the jury may consider even the void consent in mitigation of damages. See Section 34, supra.

Sec. 97. Elements of Battery. The gravamen of this tort is unjustifiable force applied to the person of another. Unlike assault, there is no necessity in proving this tort to show fear, for the elements are: (1) A hostile intent, (2) Application of force to the person of another (infra, Sec. 99), and (3) Damages (supra, Sec. 96).

Sec. 98. Hostile Intent. Hostile intent is an element of this tort (Brodsky v. Reiser, 195 App. Div. 557). However, it must be remembered that since fear is not an element of this tort, the facts constituting hostility need not appear to the injured person; he need not even see his assailant. Battery is usually consummated assault, and so, where there is an assault ending in harm inflicted as actually or apparently intended, there is, ordinarily, a battery. In Englehardt v. State, 88 Ala. 100, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 330, the Court quoting from 1 Am. & Eng. 

Encyc. of Law 783, said, "A battery is not necessarily a forcible striking with the hand or stick or the like but includes every touching or laying hold (however trifling) of another's person or his clothes, in an angry, revengeful, rude, insolent, or hostile manner." If the lesser be battery, the greater, namely, "forcible striking with the hand or stick, or the like," is certainly battery on the theory that what is consummated assault is battery, even though a touching may be battery under such circumstances that (because of the absence of fear) it would not be assault. 

Any intentional violence is battery (Collins v. Butler, 179 N. Y. 

156; Brodsky v. Reiser, supra). A tradesman who without consent enters the plaintiff's sleeping quarters and wakes him by grasping him so as to present a bill commits battery (Richmond v. Fisk, 160 Mass. 34). It is not necessary that the person of the defendant touch the plaintiff. The force may be applied through a weapon or other medium, as a stick, a gun, a whip, an animal or a missile (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 281; Bullock v. Babcock, 3 Wend. 391), or, in this later day, gas, electricity

and possibly x-rays and the like under certain circumstances, and surely if potentially or actually lethal. 

Sec. 99. Application of Force to the Person of Another. 

As we have seen (supra, Sec. 98), the least touching, if accomplished with the required hostility, may constitute the application of force. It is required that this force be applied “to the person" of the plaintiff. Person, however, is here used in a broad sense, and is not limited to the body alone. For example, the clothing of the plaintiff (Geraty v. Stern, 30 Hun 426), articles he is carrying (Dyke v. Young, 35 Ill. App. 138), or a horse he is riding or driving (Marentille v. Oliver, 20 N. J. L. 

379; Clark v. Downing, 55 Vt. 259), are all parts of his person as here understood.

Sec. 100. Defenses; Justification. Defense of self or fam ily (supra, Sec. 26), defense of property (supra, Sec. 27), and assent of plaintiff (supra, Sec. 34) may justify assault and battery. Only reasonable force may be used in defense, however (supra, Secs. 26, 27). Where assent of plaintiff is relied on, it must be obtained without fraud or force (supra, Sec. 34). It is a defense that plaintiff consented to engage in a fight and so was injured (supra, Sec. 34), but not that plaintiff provoked defendant to assault him by insult or threat, although this fact may be shown in mitigation of damages (supra, Sec. 96).

Enforcement of discipline by parents, of order by carriers, the making of an arrest, and the prevention of harm by lunatics, all justify the use of reasonably necessary force (Penal Law, Sec. 246; Montgomery v. Ry., 165 N. Y. 139).

CHAPTER XI. 

DISTURBANCE OF FAMILY RELATIONS. 

Sec. 101. In General. Much of the subject matter properly considered in discussing wrongful interference with domestic relations has already been covered (supra, Secs. 47, 48). Family relations are jealously guarded by the law, and as truly protected now as under the common law. Indeed, the present day trend toward liberation of woman and toward giving her equal rights with man has led to great changes, accomplished by both statutes and decisions, the net result of which has been to give to married women a degree of protection such as they did not have under the common law. As we have seen (Sec. 47, supra), they can now sue in their own behalf, a radical departure from the common law theory that the husband was not only the head of the house but also the only important legal entity therein, for it was he who alone could enforce the rights of the others and who was responsible for wrongs done by his wife and his servants. We have seen, too, how the married women's enabling acts resulted in a judicial decision upholding the right of a married woman to sue for criminal conversation. (Oppenheim v. 

Kridel, 236 N. Y. 156, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 346, cited supra, Sec. 47). We have seen that New York early gave what some states still deny, namely, the right to sue for the alienation of the husband's affections (Bennett v. Bennett, 116 N. Y. 584,

Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 358). In considering conflicting rights in trade relations, we found that maliciously to induce a breach of contract (Sec. 31, supra) was a tort, and that the only malice required to be shown was that inferred from the fact that the breach was induced with knowledge of the contract. This tort is called "interference," and will be mentioned again. There is a similarity between torts to family relations and torts to contract relations, and, indeed, some authorities refer to the wrongful disturbance of family relations as "interference" therewith. 

This similarity arises chiefly out of the fact that in these torts at least three persons (the wrongdoer, and the two persons between whom the relationship interfered with exists) are involved, and that there is, in both interference with domestic rela-

tions and interference with contract relations, a disturbance or deprivation of rights growing out of a status or relationship guarded by the law.

At common law the husband and father was not only husband and father but master as well. Upon him devolved, as it still devolves, the duty to support his wife and minor children. 

In return he had a right to their services, and in the case of the wife, to her comfort, society and chastity, and to conjugal aid (in all of the rights, duties and privileges of wedlock) from her. 

This right still continues in the main (except as to the earnings. of a wife employed by a person other than the husband) and, as of old, a breach of legal duty resulting in the temporary or permanent loss to the head of the family of any of these rights is actionable. However, those dependent on the husband and father have no cause of action for harm to him which results in their loss of support by him (with the exception of actions for wrongful death and a wife's right to sue for alienation and criminal conversation) for the remedy of the head of the family theoretically brings him damages out of which he may continue their support (3 Blackstone Comm. 142).

Ses. 102. Elements of the Tort. It is difficult and all but unnecessary to analyze this tort so nicely as to arrive at an exact list of elements, but the elements can be roughly stated to be the following: (1) A subsisting family relationship, (2) An unlawful act of disturbance thereof, and 

(3) Damages. 

Sec. 103. Family Relationships. These relationships are three: (a) Husband and wife, (b) Parent and child, (c) Master and servant.

The husband has a right to the society, affection and conjugal aid of his wife (called consortium), and to her chastity and domestic services (Clark's Law of Torts, Sec. 202). It is, of course, not necessary that she be rendering services other than the intimate ones of comradeship and conjugal fellowship (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 291; Bigaouette v. Paulet, 134 

Mass. 125, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 355; Bennett v. Bennett, 116 N. Y. 584, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 358. Indeed, it has

been said that he need not be living with her in order to maintain an action, for unless he has renounced his marital rights, they still continue though their enjoyment be suspended 

Bigaouette v. Paulet, supra).

A father has a right to the services of his minor child unless he emancipates him or her, but for an act which would otherwise be an actionable disturbance no action can be maintained if the child has been emancipated (Dean v. Peel, 5 East. 

45; Whitbourne v. Williams, 2 K. B. 722; Dain v. Wycoff, 7 

N. Y. 191; Neckelson v. Stryker, 10 Johns. 115). The test of emancipation is whether or not the father has parted with the right to require his child's services (Mulvehall v. Millward, 11 

N. Y. 343, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 372; Dean v. Peel, supra; 

Clark v. Fitch, 2 Wend. 457). If he or she has left home and entered the service of another, there being no intention to return, the action is not maintainable (Dean v. Peel, supra). In cases of seduction, if she is living at home, the action is maintainable although the degree to which she performs services be so slight as to be non-existent (Martin v. Payne, 9 Johns. 387; 

Lipe v. Eisenhard, 32 N. Y. 229), for the fiction of services is but the method by which substantial damages are awarded for family shame. If she is over the age of twenty-one, an action for her seduction is nevertheless maintainable if she is living at home; only the very slightest giving of services need be shown (Lipe v. Eisenhard, supra, in which there is a dictum that a father may recover for the seduction of even a married daughter who has returned to the parental roof).

At common law the servants of a family were in a measure members of the family. The head of the family at common law had and no doubt still has (where the servant resides in his household and damages by way of support continued during illness and the expenditure of money to bring about a cure are proved), an action for torts to his servants (Cooley's Law of 

Torts, Stud. Ed., p. 270; dictum, Lipe v. Eisenhard, supra).

Sec. 104. Disturbance. The act of disturbance must be in and of itself a tort to the wife, child or servant, or it must be an act such as would be a tort to the wife, child, or servant but for his or her illegal participation, as in cases of a wife's voluntary adultery or the seduction of a daughter over the age of consent, where to permit the wife or daughter to recover would enable

her to profit by her own wrong. If the act is a tort to the person for the loss of whose services the head of the family sues, or if it would be a tort to such a person if the element of that person's unlawful participation were lacking, it is a tort to the head of the family (Clark's Law of Torts, p. 326).

The husband, therefore, has a cause of action for criminal conversation with his wife though she consented (Bigaouette v. 

Paulet, 134 Mass. 123, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 355), for beating and abusing her (3 Black Comm. 139, 140), for abducting her or enticing her away (3 Black. Comm. 139, 140), for alienation of her affections whether he continues to live with her or not (Rinehart v. Bills, 82 Mo. 534, Webster v. Weston, 86 App. Div. 159, Heermance v. James, 47 Barb. 20), for negligently caused personal injuries to her (Schampp v. Turner, 188 

App. Div. 338), for malicious prosecution of her (Rogers v.

Smith, 17 Ind. 323), for libel of her where physical illness follows in a case where it is a proper item of damage so that she herself can recover for it (Garrison v. Sun, etc., Co., 207 N. Y. 1, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 368), and so on.

J. 

The father has a cause of action for seduction of his daughter (Martin v. Payne, 9 Johns. 387), for enticement or abduction of a child of either sex (Caughey v. Smith, 47 N. Y. 244, Lawyer v. Fritcher, 130 N. Y. 239; Magee v. Holland, 27 N. 

Law 86; Kreay v. Anthus, 2 Ind. App. 482), for negligently caused personal injuries (Tidd v. Shinner, 225 N. Y. 422) and for intentionally caused personal injuries (Cowden v. Wright, 24 Wend. 429; Whitney v. Hitchcock, 4 Denio 461) to a child of either sex. 

Sec. 105. Damages. As is frequently the case, general damages are inferred from the disturbance of the relationship. 

Facts in aggravation may be shown so that punitive damages may be recovered (Lipe v. Eisehard, supra). Special damages may be proved, if pleaded, so that the expense to which the head of the family has been put to accomplish a cure (Cunning v. 

B'klyn Ry., 109 N. Y. 95; McGarr v. Mills, 27 R. I. 447) is recoverable.

The general damages for which recovery may be had without other than general proof are loss of services, (Mulvehall v. Millward, 1 N. Y. 343, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 372), injured feelings and shame in cases of criminal conversation and

seduction, and all harm suffered by the plaintiff in the relation of parent or husband (Middleton v. Nicholas, 62 N. J. Law 636). 

Actually lost earnings of a child, of course, have to be proved to be recovered.

The strength of the bond between the head of the family and the wife or child is of course, important on the question of damages, particularly in seduction cases. The relationship must be a subsisting one, although, as we have seen above, the husband's rights in his wife continue, unless repudiated, although they live apart (Supra, Sec. 103).

Sec. 106. The Head of the Family. Anyone in loco parentis may recover for harms to the child which are family wrongs (Lipe v. Eisenhard, supra; Gray v. Durland, 50 Barb. 100, aff'd 

51 N. Y. 224; Furman v. Van Sise, 56 N. Y. 435; Tidd v. Skinner, 225 N. Y. 422). The action is maintainable by the head of the family, who stands in the place of the father; it may be a brother (Murray v. Fitzgerald, 2 Ir. R. 254), or a mother (C. P. A. Sec. 89).

CHAPTER XII. 

DEFAMATION 

Sec. 107. In General. The right invaded by the tort of defamation is the right to enjoy a good reputation. This right continues until a person's character has become such that in. justice and as a consequence of bad conduct he is no longer entitled to bear a good reputation. Defamation is of two kinds, slander, the expression of which is temporary, as by the spoken word or a gesture, and libel, which is more lasting, and is accomplished by print, or writing, or pictures and effigies (Clark's 

Law of Torts, par. 353). Defamation is also divided into libei. and slander per se and libel and slander not per se, according to whether or not proof of actual damage is required.

Sec. 108. Elements. The elements of the tort which it is necessary to prove in order to make out a prima facie case are : (1) Defamatory communication, (2) Publication, (3) Malice (sometimes), and (4) Damages (sometimes). 

Sec. 109. Defamatory Communication; Defamation per se and Not per se; Innuendo. Any communication which injures the reputation may be technical defamation. If it is in one of the classes of defamation per se, it is actionable without specific proof that it was hurtful to reputation; otherwise proof of damage must be given. The classes of defamation per se are: (1) Imputing a crime involving moral turpitude or pun ishable by infamous punishment; in other words, imputing guilt of an indictable offense, i. e., practically speaking, accusing one of guilt of a felony (Young v. Miller, 3 Hill. 21, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 380). The offense charged need not be a felony in the place where the action is brought if it was a felony in the place where it was supposed to have been committed (Van Ankin v. Westfall, 14 Johns. 233, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 384).

It is not important that the statute of limitations has run (Van Ankin v. Westfall, supra), or that the charge includes a statement that punishment has been suffered (Clark, p. 275).

(2) Imputing a loathsome disease. The disease must be one which would tend to cause society to shun the person defamed, and so it must be a venereal disease (Williams v. Holdredge, 22 Barb. 396), or one as loathsome, such as leprosy (Simpson v. Press Pub. Co., 33 Misc. 228, 67 Supp. 401); but tuberculosis is not within the class (Rade v. Press Pub Co., 37 

Misc. 254, 75 N. Y. Supp. 298). The imputation must be of present infection, for to charge merely that one has had such a disease is not actionable per se (Burdick, par. 369). (3) Imputing unfitness for office, provided the injured person was incumbent at the time of the charge, (Bennett v. 

Comm., etc., Assn., 230 N. Y. 125, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

438; Forward v. Adams, 7 Wend. 204, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 387), or unfitness for his profession (Cruikshank v. Gordon, 118 N. Y. 178; Bornmann v. Star Co., 174 N. Y. 212, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 397; Secor v. Harris, 18 Barb. 425, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 390), trade (Fowles v. Bowen, 30 N. Y. 20), or business (Moore v. Francis, 121 N. Y. 199, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 403), or otherwise defaming one in his office or vocation. (4) Imputing unchastity to a female (Rules Civ. Pr., Rule 

97; Sidney v. MacFadden Newspaper Pub. Co., 242 N. Y. 208, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 42). (5) Ridiculing or holding one up to public contempt by libel (Peck v. Tribune Co., 214 U. S. 185), although if this is accomplished by slander it is not actionable per se (D'Altamonte v. N. Y. Herald, 154 App. Div. 453. See also Bennett v. 

Comm., etc., Assn., supra, and Sydney v. MacFadden, supra).

Where the defamation is latent, that is, where it becomes apparent only by explanation to the court or jury of facts known to those to whom it was published, but not apparent on the face of the charge to others, the case is one of "defamation by innuendo," and though it is actionable, the explanation must be pleaded and proved (Hemmens v. Nelson, 138 N. Y. 157; Gorham v. Ives, 2 Wend. 534, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 385; Crasley v. Press Pub. Co., 179 N. Y. 27; Sydney v. MacFadden, supra). 

Sec. 110. Publication; Repetition. No action arises unless and until the defamatory thought has been communicated to the mind of a third person. If those in whose hearing a slander is

uttered do not understand it (Sullivan v. Sullivan, 48 Ill. App. 

435), as, if words are spoken in a foreign language not understood by the third person (Wormonth v. Cremer, 3 Wend. 394), there is no publication. If a statement is made or letter sent to the plaintiff alone, there is, of course, no publication, but if it is opened and read by a third person and if this could have reasonably been foreseen by the defendant an action arises (Rumney v. Worthley, 186 Mass. 144). So if the libel is written on the back of a postcard, there is publication (Clark's Law of Torts, Sec. 170); and if the letter is negligently sent to another than plaintiff and read by that other, there is publication (Clark's Law of Torts, Sec. 170). Where a defendant writes a defamatory statement and reads it to a third person, there is publication, and what is more, the tort is libel and not slander even though it was only the voice of the defamer which the senses of the person to whom it was published perceived, for even though he did not see the writing, it existed as a matter of greater permanence than the mere spoken word (Snyder v. 

Andrews, 6 Barb. 43). The sending of a defamatory telegram is publication (Peterson v. Western Union Tel. Co., 65 Minn. 

18). If a third person overhears the slander by accident that is enough, for the intent of the wrongdoer is not material (Desmond v. Brown, 33 Iowa 13 at. p. 15). If another person than plaintiff is in the mind of the defamer, there may, nevertheless, be defamation of the plaintiff (Griebel v. Rochester Printing 

Co., 60 Hun. 319; Morey v. Morning Journal Assn., 123 N. Y. 

207; Warner v. Press Pub. Co., 132 N. Y. 181). It would seem that a communication from husband to wife or vice versa is privileged (Lesler v. Montgomery, 78 Cal. 486; Trumbull v. 

Gibbons, 3 City H. Record, N. Y. 97), although the utterance by an outsider to one spouse of a defamatory statement concerning the other spouse is a publication (Linck v. Driscoll, 13 Ind. App. 

270; Schenck v. Schenck, 20 N. J. Law 208; Wilcox v. Moon, 64

Vt. 450). It has been held that since a corporation must act through agents, dictation by one employee to another, a stenographer, of a defamatory letter is not publication (Owen v. Ogilvie Pub. Co., 32 App. Div. 465). Whether this case would have been affirmed in the Court of Appeals is doubtful (Kennedy v. 

Butler, Inc., 245 N. Y. 204). Where the master is not a corporation, dictation to a stenographer has been held to be publication

(Ostrowe v. Lee, 256 N. Y. 36, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 39). What is more, the tort there is libel and not mere slander, for the stenographer makes a writing of the spoken words. The Ostrowe case so held, citing various cases. (Cf. 

Snyder v. Andrews, supra). Even if Owen v. Ogilvie Pub. Co., supra, had been affirmed by the Court of Appeals, that court would nevertheless have decided the Kennedy v. Butler, Inc., case, supra, as it did. The Kennedy case holds that when a corporation circularizes its employees with a defamatory letter, publication is made. The opinion points out a possible distinction between cases in which publication is accomplished by dictating to a stenographer and cases in which the publication is made to other employees, such as store managers, whose duties "are distinct and independent of the process by which the libel was produced," i. e., typing the letter, so that although dictation to the former may not be a publication when the defendant is a corporation, communication to the latter is. 

The repeater of a defamation is alone responsible for the harm done by the repetition; the original defamer is responsible only for the harm done by the original statement (Terwilliger v. Wands, 17 N. Y. 54, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 103). This is consistent with a rule of proximate cause set forth in the second sentence of subdivision (d) of Section 37, supra. See also Section 40, supra. 

Sec. 111. Malice. Malice in law is an element of this tort, but since it is inferred from falsity and is at best a fiction, it is of no practical importance, for it need not enter into the plaintiff's proof (Bromage v. Prosser, 4 Barn. & C. 247, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 394; Byam v. Collins, 111 N. Y. 143, Edgar's

Cases on Torts, p. 417). Malice in fact is important, as we shall see, only in cases of qualified privilege (infra, Sec. 117).

Sec. 112. Damages. 

Damages. General damages are inferred from the fact of publication of defamation per se. Special damages may be shown in cases of defamation per se, as in all torts, and damages must be proved expressly to obtain a recovery in cases of defamation not per se (Terwilliger v. Wands, supra; Beach v. 

Ranney, 2 Hill 309; Reporters' Assn. v. Sun, 186 N. Y. 437).

Mental suffering is not a sufficient showing of special damages in cases of defamation not per se unless accompanied by direct damages. These "direct damages" sufficient to constitute a peg

upon which to hang mental suffering as a compensable damage would, in a case of defamation not per se, have to be provable and proved special damages causing the mental suffering (e. g., if Terwilliger had proved that he lost customers of his business and that this fact made him worry himself into a sick-bed). In the case of defamation per se, mental suffering is compensable without proof of special damages to act as a peg for it, because general damages are inferred. For example, where unchastity is imputed to a woman, the law infers that she lost caste with her neighbors. It is natural to suffer in spirit because of such a thing. If, therefore, the defamed woman did worry and become ill over the fear of losing caste, she could recover for the mental suffering and consequent physical injury without producing evidence that she fell in the opinion of any particular person (Terwilliger v. Wands, supra). Punitive damages may be recovered upon proof of express malice (Corrigan v. Bobbs, 228 N. Y. 58, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 431). In mitigation of damages, good faith, provocation and bad reputation of the plaintiff may be shown (Dole v. Lyon, 10 Johns 447; Walmsley v. Kopczynski, 202 App. Div. 104; C. P. A. Secs. 338, 338a, 339).

Reasonable ground of belief that the charge was true may also be shown in mitigation of damages, though belief is not a defense except where it constitutes a lack of malice where malice is necessary under Section 117, infra, and so, also, testimony of what investigation was made before publication is also admissible when a necessity for the existence of malice is involved under Section 117 (Kehoe v. N. Y. Tribune, 241 N. Y. 

Suppl. 676).

Retraction is not admissible in mitigation of compensatory damages, but only of punitive damages where express malice (ill-will or hatred) is proved. So a verdict of six cents for a gross libel against a person of fine reputation was set aside in the Kehoe case, although there had been a retraction, for the retraction was inadmissible, there having been no showing of hatred, and punitive damages, therefore, not having been involved so as to be mitigated. 

Sec 113. Parties. Masters are liable for defamation com mitted by their servants, in proper cases, as for other torts (for examples, see cases supra in which corporations have been held liable). A corporation may recover for defamation, however,

only when the defamation charged will injure its business, as by affecting its credit, for a corporation has no personal reputation, but only a business reputation (Bank v. Winters, 225 N. Y. 47, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 473).

Sec. 114. Defenses; In General. There are three main de fenses to actions for defamation, namely: (a) Truth, (b) Privilege (absolute or qualified), (c) Fair comment. 

Sec. 115. Truth. The defense that the charge made is true is an absolute and complete defense to a civil action for defamation, the qualification being only that the justification proved must be as broad as the charge (Young v. Adams, 113 Mich. 

199, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 409). For example, defamation of a man by calling him a thief, which conveys the idea that he has committed larceny, is not justified by proof that he was guilty of fraud and cheating under circumstances not amounting to larceny in the jurisdiction in which the charge was made (Young v. Adams, supra). The defense must be pleaded and proved by the defendant, for plaintiff makes out a prima facie case by proving the defamatory words and publication; he has not the burden of proving falsity; it is the defendant who must justify (Ashcroft v. Hammond, 132 App. Div. 3). 

Sec. 116. Absolute Privilege. As we have seen (supra, Secs. 21 and 22), an absolute privilege to defame exists in favor of legislators and judges while acting officially, and in favor of counsel, parties and witnesses (Sec. 117, infra) in making statements material to the issue before the court. This means that even though malice is proved, no action for defamation can be brought against such persons while the privilege continues (Andrews v. Gardiner, 224 N. Y. 440; Edgar's Cases on

Torts, p. 448). However, in making statements as to a matter not material to an issue before the court, counsel, parties and witnesses have only a qualified privilege (Sec. 117, infra). 

Sec. 117. Qualified Privilege. Under certain circumstances, a conditional or qualified privilege to defame exists. To overcome this privilege, proof of malice in fact is necessary, or an action does not lie (Fresh v. Cutter, 73 Md. 87, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 411; Moore v. Bank, 123 N. Y. 420, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 426, Pecue v. West, 233 N. Y. 316, Edgar's Cases on

Torts, p. 443; Andrews v. Gardiner, supra). Examples of such privilege are found in cases where counsel, in arguing before a court, make statements not strictly material to the issue; the test of materiality governs the determination of whether the privilege is absolute or qualified in the case of parties, witnesses, and jurors, as well as of counsel (Youmans v. Smith, 153 N. Y. 

214; Moore v. Bank, supra, citing Hastings v. Lusk, 22 Wend. 

410, Klinck v. Colby, 46 N. Y. 427, and Marsh v. Ellsworth, 50

N. Y. 309). Wherever possible, statements will be construed to be material so as to be protected by absolute, and not merely qualified privilege (People ex, rel. Bensky v. Warden, 258 N. Y. 

55, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 50). Another example is found in the publication of fair reports of judicial proceedings 

(C. P. A. Sec. 337; Campbell v. Evening Post, 219 App. Div. 

169) and of public meetings (Barrows v. Bell, 73 Mass. 301).

However, to be protected, the report of a proceeding or meeting must be free from distortion (Schaffer v. Press Publishing Co., 258 N. Y. 207, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 47). Still other examples of conditional or qualified privilege may be found in cases where the defamer has a duty to speak and the hearer has an interest to listen, or vice versa. This duty may be a duty to the public, as where a citizen gives information to the district attorney concerning the alleged commission of a crime by another (Pecue v. West, supra). The privilege is lost, however, when the defamation is published to others than those who have a duty or interest to hear, as, where a complaint referred to a proper quarter is also given to the newspapers (Bingham v. 

Gaynor, 203 N. Y. 27), in which case an action lies. A private duty or interest also is sufficient to support the privilege. For instance, a conditional privilege exists in favor of one who previously employed a servant in giving a new employer information concerning the servant (Fresh v. Cutter, supra). It also exists in favor of a relative of a woman in giving her information concerning a suitor, although no privilege exists (the duty not being great enough) in the case of one who is not a relative (Byam v. Collins, supra; Krebs v. Oliver, 12 Gray 239), nor does it exist though the defamer be the woman's close friend and former pastor (Count Joannes v. Bennett, 5 Allen 169). As is the case with a privilege based on duty to the public (Bingham v. Gaynor, supra), privilege based on a private duty or in-

terest is lost if communication is made to one who is not under a duty or possessed of an interest to be informed (Moore v. 

Bank, supra). A qualified privilege exists in favor of credit agencies in giving information when requested by a subscriber concerning one with whom he is about to deal, but not when all their subscribers are circularized (Ormsly v. Douglass, 37 N. Y. 

477; Sunderlin v. Bradstreet, 46 N. Y. 188).

Sec. 118. Fair Comment. Public men may be fairly commented upon as to conduct and motive without liability to those who discuss them. Such is one of the attributes of free speech in America. The rule applies alike to public officers and candidates for public office (Mattice v. Wilcox, 147 N. Y. 624; King v. Root, 4 Wend. 113; Hamilton v. Eno, 81 N. Y. 116), to writers (Cooper v. Stone, 24 Wend. 434), public performers (Fry v. Bennett, 28 N. Y. 324), and so on. What is fair comment is ordinarily for the jury, but when it clearly transcends fairness or amounts to defamation of private character rather than a statement of qualifications and matters in which the public is interested (Cooper v. Stone, supra), the court will dispose of the matter as a question of law (App. Div. opinion, reported 

141 App. Div. 301, in Bingham v. Gaynor, supra, aff'd 203 N. Y. 

27). An excellent dissertation upon fair comment is found in the opinion by Judge Crane in Hoeppner v. Dunkirk Printing 

Co., 254 N. Y. 95, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 54. `

Sec. 119. Miscellaneous. No action lies for defamation of the dead (Wellman v. Sun, 66 Hun. 331; Sorenson v. Balaban, 11 App. Div. 164). Equity will not enjoin defamation of person (De Wick v. Dobson, 18 App. Div. 399), although when it affects property it may in a proper case be enjoined (Marlin v. 

Shields, 68 App. Div. 88).

CHAPTER XIII TRESPASS TO PROPERTY 

Sec. 120. In General. We are now dealing with a tort to property. The right here invaded is the right to enjoy undisturbed possession of one's property, real and personal. In its broader sense it includes every unlawful taking of possession of chattels (Clark's Law of Torts, p. 26), as well as of land, but except for a few general observations upon trespass to chattels our discussion will be limited to trespass upon lands as the more important of the two. With respect to trespass to personalty, however, it must be remembered that an action for the wrongful disturbance of possession of that class of property lies only when the property thus trespassed upon is tangible property (Clark's 

Law of Torts, p. 26, note 1), and that the plaintiff must show (and we shall see that these elements are identical with those involved in trespass to realty) a right to possession and an unlawful disturbance of that possession (Putnam v. Wyley, 8 

Johns 432), with or without valuable loss of use (Bull v. Colton, 22 Barb. 94), for damages need not be particularly shown, being inferred from the invasion of the right (Salmond's Law of Torts, 2nd Ed., p. 340; Bull v. Colton, supra).

Sec. 121. Elements of Trespass to Realty. The elements of this tort, called trespass quare clausum fregit, are: (1) A right to possession in the plaintiff, (2) An unlawful disturbance thereof, and (3) Damages.

Sec. 122. Right of Possession. It is not necessary that the plaintiff own the fee to the realty or have a freehold estate, for even a lessee may bring the action (Clark's Law of Torts, p. 23, note 1). It is not necessary that the plaintiff be actually in possession, for constructive possession (4 Kent's Comm. 120) of even unenclosed lands is sufficient (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 355).

One who does not own the fee may recover for the damage to whatever interest he has (Stewart v. Doughty, 9 Johns 108), and if the harm done be of a permanent nature, the owner of the fee may recover for the injury to the fee (Develin v. Smell-

inburg, 132 Pa. 186; Gidney v. Earl, 12 Wend. 98, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 456). In Gidney v. Earl, plaintiff had the fee to land over which the public had a highway easement; the defendant appropriated soil from the highway, and it was held that the plaintiff could recover for trespass since the damage was permanent and therefore to the fee. 

In the tort of tres- 

Sec. 123. Disturbance of Possession. 

pass to lands, the disturbance of possession is generally an unlawful entry into the close of another, and hence the ancient name of the tort, "trespass quare clausum fregit." This unlawful entry is ordinarily accomplished by the physical intrusion of the defendant upon land of the plaintiff, but trespass is also committed in throwing missiles upon it (Hay v. Cohoes Co., 2 N. Y. 

159), turning water into it (Byrnes v. City of Cohoes, 67 N. Y. 

204; Mairs v. Real Estate Ass'n, 89 N. Y. 498), using powerful pumps to suck subterranean water from it (Forbell v. City, 164 

N. Y. 522, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 66), removing a fence from it (Newkirk v. Sabler, 9 Barb. 652, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 458), and so on. Trespass by animals was actionable at commọn law (Van Leuven v. Lyke, 1 N. Y. 515, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 469), and their owner or custodian was bound to keep them on his own lands (Wells v. Howell, 19 Johns 385; Malone v. Knowlton, 15 Supp. 506), whether he had knowledge of their vicious or mischievous propensities or not (Van Leuven v. Lyke, supra; Malone v. Knowlton, supra), and in this state the owner of an animal is liable for harm done by it while trespassing, whether the owner had knowledge of its vicious propensity or not (Buchanan v. Stout, 139 App. Div. 204), although he is not liable for vicious harm done by domestic animals which are not trespassing unless he has had notice (scienter) of their vicious nature 

(Van Leuven v. Lyke, supra). By statute in New York (Town 

Law, Secs. 360-369), the common law duty of the owner of cattle to fence them in or otherwise keep them on his own land has been changed in a way the practical effect of which is to require him who would prevent the trespasses of his neighbor's cattle to fence against them (each to bear the cost of the fence), so that, if there is no fence, there is no liability for trespasses by cattle. 

Neither is there any liability, unless the herdsman is guilty of negligence, when cattle driven along a highway trespass on abutting land (Tillet v. Ward, 10 Q. B. D. 17; Moynahan v.

Wheeler, 117 N. Y. 285), but if they go further in from the road and upon the land (of another) which does not abut on the road, liability arises (Wood v. Snider, 187 N. Y. 28).

Theoretically, any invasion, serious or slight, of the plaintiff's close is a trespass, no matter how far above or below the surface, for ownership of land includes the air and space above the land infinitely upward, and the soil beneath it to the center of the earth. Consequently any culpable invasion entitles an owner to at least nominal damages. But a mere accidental invasion by stones carried upon the lands of A from the lands of B by a storm is not actionable, for there is no human involvement (Burkick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par 351; Knight v. Dunbar, 83 

Me. 359). However, intent plays no part in trespass, and so the accidental landing of an aeroplane in a field is actionable (Guille v. Swan, 19 Johns. 381, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 453), and so are the inundation of premises by a defective sewer (Seifert v. Brooklyn, 101 N. Y. 136), and the accidental throwing of stones and debris on the lands of another in blasting (Radcliff v. Mayor, 4 N. Y. 95; Hay v. Cohoes Co., 2 N. Y. 159; supra, Sec. 30; Booth v. Rome, 140 N. Y. 267, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 41, at p. 49). Yet, on the ground that there is no culpability (i. e.; breach of legal duty), the casting of particles of a boiler upon adjacent buildings, there being no proof of negligence, has been held to be not a trespass (Losee v. Buchanan, 51 N. Y. 

476). For a discussion of the apparent conflict between this case and the case in which the accidental throwing of debris in blasting was held to constitute trespass, see Sec. 150, infra.

Though it has always been thought that even the very slightest invasion is a trespass, no matter how high in the air or deep below the surface, a qualification (best explained on the theory that the law is not concerned with trifles-"de minimis non curat lex”-particularly when the demands of progress justify overlooking them) undoubtedly exists today in the case of aeroplanes which pass over property without landing (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 351, and note 12 p. 406), so that no liability arises from such a trespass, although if the machine lands (Guille v. Swan, supra), or if articles are dropped (Burdick, p. 406, supra), there is undoubted liability.

The rules of proximate and concurrent causes do not relate to negligence alone, but to all torts, and so, if the falling of a

balloon attracts a crowd which trespasses on plaintiff's land, the aeronaut and the crowd are jointly and severally liable as the authors of concurrent causes (Guille v. Swan, supra), and if a person is forcibly carried upon the lands of another, although the former is not liable to the latter because guilty of no breach of legal duty, yet those who carried him are liable, for their culpability is the proximate cause (Clark's Law of Torts, p. 23, note 2). Yet if a person strays by accident upon another's land he is liable, for intent is not an element of the tort (Newson v. 

Anderson, 37 Am. Dec. 406; De Camp v. Bullard, 159 N. Y. 450).

Airplane travel has caused serious changes in the timehonored views as to ownership and the right to undisturbed possession to the very heavens. This branch of the law is the subject matter of a special course in most institutions, including our own, and will not be dealt with here at length. But see General Business Law, Secs. 240-246. 

Sec. 124. Damages. Damages are inferred from the fact of trespass upon land (Dixon v. Clow, 24 Wend. 188). Good faith of the trespasser may reduce the recovery to a nominal sum, while express malice will enhance it by way of punitive or exemplary damages (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 354).

Sec. 125. Defenses. Consent is a complete defense to an action for trespass (Adams v. Freeman, 12 Johns 408), but after the license is withdrawn, as it may be, for licenses are revocable at will, entry is a trespass (Giles v. Simmonds, 15 Gray, 441, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 462). In the case last cited the defense was that plaintiff had orally sold defendant some trees standing on plaintiff's land, but upon proof that the plaintiff had thereafter revoked the license to remove the trees, defendant's entry upon the land was held to be tortious, his true remedy being an action for breach of contract. Trespass necessarily committed by a public officer in performance of his duty is justifiable although, if the outer door of a house is locked, an officer serving civil process has no right to break in. (Hager v. Danforth, 20 Barb. 16, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 251). When one who has a license afforded by the law, as in the case of a public officer, abuses it, everything done thereunder is as to him void ab initio (although perhaps not void as to the state, for he is still an officer, and public duties performed by him while trespassing are, so far as the public is concerned, validly performed), and an

action can be maintained against him for trespass as though his original entry was wrongful (Adams v. Rivers, 11 Barb. 

390). But where the authority or license was given not by law but as the voluntary act of the plaintiff, the abuse does not render conduct under the license void ab initio, but only from the time of the abuse, for plaintiff having selected whom he would trust cannot reap profit from his bad judgment (Allen v. 

Crofoot, 5 Wend. 506). There is no implied license of law to enter upon the land of another for the purpose of regaining property left there wrongfully, as, if the original entry was a trespass. (Newkirk v. Sabler, 9 Barb. 652, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 458). A license is, however, given by the law to go upon land abutting on a highway for the purpose of getting around an obstruction in the road (Campbell v. Race, 7 Cush. 

408, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 465).

CHAPTER XIV CONVERSION Sec. 126. In General. 

The tort of conversion is the unauthorized assumption and exercise of the rights of ownership or dominion over personal property belonging to another to the exclusion of the owner's rights (Erwin's Summary of Torts, p. 

165). The action to redress conversion is an action for money damages for the value of the thing converted. At common law this action was called trover (Frome v. Dennis, 45 N. J. Law 

515, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 486). The distinction between trespass to chattels and conversion is that the latter has not been committed unless there has been some usurpation of rights of ownership consisting in or evidenced by some repudiation of, or some act inconsistent with, the owner's right of ownership (Frome v. Dennis, supra, and cases cited therein including 

Woodside v. Adams, 40 N. J. Law 417; Spooner v. Manchester, 133 Mass. 270; Laverty v. Snethen, 68 N. Y. 522, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 476), while any unwarranted interference with possession is a trespass. Intangible personal property may be the subject of conversion, as well as tangible personal property (Laverty v. Snethen, supra; Murray v. Burling, 10 Johns. 172, Lawatsch v. Cooney, 86 Hun. 546). Tangible property only, real or personal, can be the subject of trespass (Sec. 120, supra). 

Judgment in an action for conversion does not pass title to the defendant until satisfaction (Miller v. Hyde, 161 Mass. 473, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 496; Russell v. McCall, 141 N. Y. 437), although, as we have seen (supra, Secs. 16 and 70), when property is converted and the owner elects to sue on the theory of implied sale, mere starting of the action is an irrevocable election of that remedy to the exclusion of the inconsistent one, conversion. 

Sec. 127. Elements. The elements of the tort conversion are: 

(1) A special or general property in the plaintiff, (2) Possession or a right to immediate possession in the plaintiff, (3) An act of unlawful dominion by the defendant, and 

(4) Damages. 

See generally Moore v. Walker, 24 Ala. 199, at p. 202.

Sec. 128. Property in the Plaintiff. As the student will find in taking up the law of sales, ownership of personal property is not called "title" as in the case of real property. Instead, the term "general property" is used to denote ownership, while "special property" is the name applied to the interest of bailees and others who have rights in personalty short of ownership. 

As a special property in the plaintiff is sufficient to enable him to maintain this action (the other elements being present), it is not necessary that he be the absolute owner (Smith v. James, 7 

Cow. 328). When one who does not have the general property in personalty sues for conversion, he recovers the full value of the thing converted, and is liable to account to the general owner (Nat. Sur. Co. v. U. S., 129 Fed. 70; Cooley's Law of 

Torts, Stud. Ed., par. 234). It is no defense to the action that the plaintiff is not the true owner if he has a right to possession and the defendant has none; the defendant must go farther and show a right to possession or otherwise connect himself with the actual owner (Wheeler v. Lawson, 103 N. Y. 40). See also the preceding section.

Sec. 129. Possession or Right to Immediate Possession. 

Possession or the right to immediate possession is a necessary element of the tort (Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 234 and cases cited note 67).

Sec. 130. Unlawful Dominion. It is necessary, as we have seen in Sec. 126, that the act of dominion be a repudiation of the owner's right or a usurpation by the converter of rights incident to ownership. If plaintiff unlawfully acquires possession as by larceny or fraud (Thompson v. Rose, 116 Conn. 71; Powell v. 

Powell, 71 N. Y. 71; Green v. Russell, 5 Hill 183), or, in the case of a public officer, by unlawful levy (Russell v. Cole, 167 

Mass. 6; Sanborn v. Hamilton, 18 Vt. 590), the unlawful taking is itself conversion, and no demand by the owner for the return of the property is necessary to enable him to maintain an action. 

In every case, however, some act of unlawful dominion is necessary, and unless possession was acquired in such a way as amounted to unlawful dominion, something more must be shown. For example, where one merely takes a plow for a few days, uses and returns it, there is no conversion, though there may be trespass (Frome v. Dennis, supra). But if he locks it up 

Leonard v. Gelknap, 47 Vt. 602) or conceals it (Burdick, par.

366) that is conversion. Where property of the plaintiff is intentionally destroyed or its character is changed in a manufacturing process or otherwise so that it loses its identity or previous nature, there is conversion (Pease v. Smith, 61 N. Y. 477,

Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 492). The intention need not be to do a wrongful act (Boyce v. Brockway, 31 N. Y. 490), for the destruction may be in the belief that it is rightful (as, if defendant believes that he owns the goods) and still be conversion if destruction was intentional (Pease v. Smith, supra) rather than merely negligent, for negligent destruction is not conversion 

Wamsley v. Atlas, 168 N. Y. 533, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 480; 

Bank v. Wheeler, 48 N. Y. 492, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 489).

On the other hand, some overt act of the defendant with respect to the property is necessary to constitute conversion; mere threats or claims by one not in possession of the goods are not sufficient (Burdick, par. 367; Hamlin v. Patton, 118 App. 

Div. 796). But it is not necessary that the act be physically and literally with respect to the property, for it is possible for a wrongful taking to be constructive, as where one wrongfully negotiates a bill of lading and appropriates the proceeds (Proctor & Gamble v. Peters, etc., Co., 187 App. Div. 376). Wrongful use may constitute conversion, as where a bailee uses property for another purpose than the one called for by the terms of his possession (Disbrow v. Tenbroeck, 5 E. D. Smith, 397; King v. Machellar, 109 N. Y. 215), or where an agent violates his instructions as to the delivery of property by accepting a mere promise to pay in lieu of payment (Laverty v. Snethen, supra), or where an agent or bailee delivers to the wrong person (Furman v. Ry., 106 N. Y. 579). Any unauthorized sale of personal property is conversion (Griggs v. Day, 136 N. Y. 152), even if the seller believes himself to be the owner, having unknowingly purchased it from a thief (Harris v. Saunders, 2 Strobh. Eq. 370, cited at p. 495 of Edgar's Cases on Torts in the opinion in Pease v. Smith, supra), although, of course, purchase from a thief is not itself conversion, unless it was with knowledge that the goods were stolen. Unlawful detention is conversion (McCormick v. 

Penn. Ry., 49 N. Y. 303). Indeed, actions for conversion based on unlawful detention are very common, for coming without fault into possession of the personal property of another (as, if one unknowingly and in good faith buys from a thief) is not tortious,

and an act of unlawful dominion is necessary to constitute conversion. In such cases liability is created by demand and refusal (i. e., demand by the true owner upon the person in posses- sion that the latter turn the property over to the owner); such demand is necessary in every case where the original possession is lawful (Branch v. Latham, 186 App. Div. 437), unless conversion is otherwise shown (Cooley, par. 237), as by wrongful taking or wrongful use (cases supra). Refusal does not always create liability, however, for if the property has been destroyed before demand under such circumstances that liability for negligence only and not for conversion arose, demand and failure to comply, through inability so to do, do not change the wrong into conversion (Bank v. Wheeler, supra). The refusal must be unqualified (Terwilliger v. Browning, King & Co., 193 App. Div. 

428), for the possessor is entitled to a reasonable time in which to investigate plaintiff's claim of ownership (Burdick, par. 379). 

Sec. 131. Damages; Replevin. The measure of damages for conversion is the value of the property at the time of the conversion (Powell v. Powell, 71 N. Y. 71; Cooley, par. 240; 

Burdick, par. 201). If, however, the property is of a fluctuating value, the measure of the recovery is the highest market value between the time of conversion and a reasonable time after the owner learns of it to enable him to replace it (Wright v. Bank, 110 N. Y. 237). Punitive damages may be recovered upon proof of express malice (Burdick, par. 201). In the case of conversion, the plaintiff is not limited to an action for damages for the value of the property. He may instead sue for the return of the property itself, with or without damages for its detention. 

This is called an action of replevin (C. P. A., Sec. 1089-1131; 

R. C. P. 270-273). In such a case possession is not delayed until final judgment, but a provisional remedy exists by which the plaintiff, upon the giving of a bond, may obtain quick possession, unless the defendant replevins the property back by also giving a bond, all of which will be considered in the course on pleading and practice.

CHAPTER XV DECEIT AND COMMERCIAL WRONGS 

A. DECEIT 

Sec. 132. In General. Fraud, or intentional misrepresentation calculated to, and which does, mislead a person and induce him to act to his prejudice, is relieved by the law in several ways. In the course on contracts, the student has learned that fraud entitles a party deceived to avoid the contract. In the course on equity, various remedies for fraud (notably actions for judgments either reforming a contract or rescinding it, i. e., declaring it a nullity and thereby accomplishing by judicial decree what a person does without aid of the courts in "avoiding" a contract), will be taken up. There is another remedy known to the law, generally available only when avoidance and equitable relief have not been elected, and it is that remedy in which we are interested, namely, an action for money damages to redress the fraud, that is, to compensate the deceived person for having been inveigled into a situation into which he would not have entered had he not been deceived. The equitable remedy of rescission and the right to avoid considered in the course on contracts put an end to a contract induced by fraud; and when these remedies are availed of, the parties are in theory at least placed in the position in which they were before the fraud was committed, so that there is no damage and consequently seldom any occasion for an action for money damages. But see Sec. 

138, infra. For a list of the remedies for fraud, see Merry Realty 

Co. v. Shamokin, 230 N. Y. 316.

Fraud, when made the basis of an action for money damages, is the tort called "deceit," and it is so referred to in the 

Merry Realty Co. case.

Sec. 133. Elements of Deceit. The elements of fraud are the same throughout the law. While it is true that equity will redress situations on the ground of constructive fraud or other illegality such as innocent misrepresentation falling short of legal fraud, that is a matter of equitable conscience (in re 

Dorrity, 118 Misc. 725), and the illegality or breach of legal duty in those cases ought not to be called fraud at all, and is not so

called except for lack of a better name. True fraud, whether it be relied on as a basis of an action for deceit or availed of as justifying one of the other remedies, has for its elements: (1) The making of a false statement of a material fact, (2) Knowledge of its falsity by its maker, (3) Intent that it be acted upon, (4) Belief and reliance by the deceived person, who thereupon does act, and 

(5) Damages. 

The courts have repeatedly refrained from defining or otherwise limiting fraud, for the reason that some species of deceit might be ingeniously devised which would fall outside of definition or limitation, and yet be in justice a wrong. No definition of fraud will be attempted here for the added reason that since the courts will not define it, a text-book definition is valueless. To be the basis of an action for deceit (i. e., at law for money damages), a misrepresentation must, however, contain the above ele ments (Ressler v. Samphimor Holding Corp., App. Div. 344; 

Hobaica v. Byrne, 123 Misc. 107; Rose v. Goodale, 169 N. Y. 

Supp. 446, Hoabeeb v. Daas, 111 Misc. 437).

Sec. 134. False Statement of a Material Fact. Obviously the statement must be false, or there is no misrepresentation and consequently no fraud or deceit. The discussion of this element will be confined to a consideration of what are statements of material fact. Although it is true that merely promissory statements can, if broken, be the basis only of an action for breach of contract and not for deceit (Adams v. Clark, 239 N. Y. 

403), yet a statement of intention is a statement of existing fact, and if important enough to induce a contract or other change of position on the part of the promisee or intended hearer, it is a statement of material fact (Moore v. Abbey, 213 App. Div. 787; 

Dwelle-Kaiser Co. v. Aetna, etc., Co., 211 App. Div. 369). Mere matters of opinion are not statements of fact (Porter v. Thoens, 180 Supp. 733). For example, expressions of value are matters of opinion merely (People ex rel. Greene v. Swassey, 122 Misc. 

388) unless the buyer lacks fair means for knowing the value 

(Merry Realty Co. v. Martin, 103 Misc. 9). Statements of price paid, however, are statements of fact (Names v. Peck, 181 

App. Div. 760). A representation that a warehouse is fireproof is also a matter of fact and not of opinion (Hickey v. Morrell, 4

102 N. Y. 454, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 515), although a representation that a safe is thief-proof is a mere expression of opinion (Walker v. Milner, 4 Fost. & F. 745, cited in Hickey v. Morrell, supra). Stating matters of mere opinion to induce a transaction is called "puffing," and a tradesman has a right to do a certain amount of this in order to sell his product (Deming v. 

Darling, 148 Mass. 504; Burdick's Law of Torts, 4th Ed., par. 

388). Statements that a person or concern is solvent, made in an application for credit, may or may not be statements of existing fact. In Upton v. Vail, 6 Johns. 81, it was held that such statements were of fact, although in Hickey v. Morrell, supra, citing Upton v. Vail, there is a statement which indicates that under some circumstances statements of solvency are mere matters of opinion. A great deal depends on the wording of the statement and on the surrounding circumstances, for, if it clearly appears that it was given as a belief, no court will hold it to be a statement of fact. Misrepresentations as to the law (Bernhan v. Ship-A-Hoy, 200 App. Div. 309, modified 234 N. Y. 563, aff'd on reargument 234 N. Y. 605) or as to the legal effect of an instrument (Bodenstein v. Singer, 209 N. Y. Supp. 748) cannot be the basis of an action for deceit (though in certain cases equity will relieve against them), for ignorance of the law excuses none, so it is said. This rule seems to be a strained application of the dogma. But since the law of another state or country is a matter of fact, to be proved like every other fact, misrepresentation as to foreign law is of a material fact (Bernhan v. Ship-A- 

Hoy, supra). Silence may be the equivalent of a false statement where the inference from silence would be of a thing not true, but only when there is a duty to speak (Dwelle-Kaiser Co. v. 

Aetna, etc., Co., supra). In the case of defects in personal property which is the subject of a prospective sale, there is no obligation on the part of the vendor to disclose them if they are patent (i. e., reasonably discoverably upon inspection), but only if they are latent, for the rule of caveat emptor applies (Grigsby v. 

Stapleton, 94 Mo. 423, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 511). However, if the seller makes representations as to even discoverable matters, caveat emptor does not apply, for the buyer is then entitled to rely (infra, Sec. 137). It has been held that a banker (between whom and depositors a quasi confidential relationship exists) is guilty of fraud in accepting deposits with knowledge

of his insolvency (Cassidy v. Uhlman, 170 N. Y. 505; Anonymous, 67 N. Y. 598), although between people dealing at arm's length in ordinary business transactions silence is not a representation of solvency (Dwelle-Kaiser Co. v. Aetna, etc., Co. supra). However, one who does business intending not to pay is guilty of deceit though no express statement of an intention to pay was made, for merely doing business is the equivalent of a representation of intention to pay (Dwelle-Kaiser Co., v. Aetna, etc., Co., supra).

The modern tendency is to require fairness in all commercial dealings to an increasing degree. The rules of the common. law governing the tort deceit, however, have not been greatly broadened. It is rather in equity jurisprudence that the student of commercial fraud sees the operation of the requirement of entire fairness.

Common law deceit remains, then, the most difficult thing to prove in the law, for it involves as a basic element a state of mind, as will be seen in the next two sections, and state of mind is not capable of proof by direct evidence of any sort except the testimony of him whose mind it is. The defendant in an action. for deceit thus comes to the trial of the action with a tremendous advantage. Equitable and contractual remedies for fraud or breach of warranty are therefore sought more frequently than damages for the fraud as the tort of deceit.

Sec. 135. Knowledge of Falsity or Its Equivalent. Knowl edge of the falsity of the representation is an essential element of this tort (Rose Co. v. Goodale, 169 Supp. 446; Crossways 

Apartments Corp. v. Amante, 213 App. Div. 430, Reno v. Bull, 226 N. Y. 546). 

Sec. 136. Intent That Plaintiff Act. The intent to deceive plaintiff so that he acts on the false statement is another necessary element of this tort (Habeeb v. Daas, 111 Misc. 437; Ressler v. Samiphon Holding Corp., supra). Even if the person to whom the statement is made is a different person from the one intended to be deceived, if the person intended learns and was intended by the defendant to learn of the making of the statement and acts upon it to his damage, he may recover, as where the defendant gave false information concerning his financial status to a mercantile agency known as Dun, Barlow & Co., knowing that commercial people learning of the Dun rating and report

thereupon given would act upon it, and the plaintiff was given the report and did act (Eaton v. Avery, 83 N. Y. 31, Edgar's 

Cases on Torts, p. 528). From this it can be seen that the defendant need have no specific person in mind; if plaintiff is deceived it is enough if he is one of a class whom defendant intended to deceive (Eaton v. Avery, supra). So also corporations or promoters who issue a prospectus containing false and material statements are liable, for in these cases no particular persons, but any into whose hands it comes, are intended (Derry v. Peek, L. R. 14 App. Cas. 337, House of Lords, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 502).

Where accountants are responsible for the issuance of a, certificate upon which they know their employer intends to get credit, they obviously are within this reasoning. (Ultramares 

Corporation v. Touche, 255 N. Y. 170, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on 

Torts, p. 96). They were not held liable on the theory of negligence, because privity of contract was missing (infra, Section 149).

Sec. 137. Reliance and Change of Position by the Deceived 

Person. The statement fraudulently made need not be the sole inducement, if it is a proximate or techhnically concurrent inducing cause (Morgan v. Skiddy, 62 N. Y. 319). It is necessary, however, that the statement be not so palpably false that even a simpleton would not be deceived, although it need not be so shrewd, keen and probable as to mislead a clever person (Hirschberg Optical Co. v. Michaelson, 95 N. W. 461; Barndt v. 

Frederick, 47 N. W. 6).

It is also necessary that the plaintiff rely on the representation. He cannot recover if he makes an investigation himself (Humphrey v. Merriam, 32 Minn. 197, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 535; Long v. Warren, 68 N. Y. 426; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 522). He is not, however, bound to make an investigation, for he is entitled to rely on the representations of the defendant (Eufemia v. Moan, 206 N. Y. Supp. 185; Bank v. Burdick, 87 

N. Y. 40).

Sec. 138. Damages. Damages are a necessary element of this tort (Mahon v. Equitable Trust Co., 81 App. Div. 335), and must be pleaded (Mahon v. Equitable Trust Co., supra) and proved (Weiner v. Fine, 189 N. Y. Supp. 164). The general measure of damages for deceit is the difference between the value of what

the plaintiff paid and the value of what he received, together with interest on that difference from the time of the payment (Reno v. Bull, 226 N. Y. 546). Prospective profits are excluded from consideration, for the purpose of the action is indemnity for the actual pecuniary loss sustained as a direct result of the wrong (Reno v. Bull, supra). In addition to the difference between the value of the subject matter received and the actual value, if money is paid out by the plaintiff as a direct consequence of the deception, it is properly included in the recovery (Merry Realty Co. v. Martin, 103 Misc. 9).

Deceit is usually a property tort, and has evolved as such. 

But where the injuria has been committed, though it result in a damnum which makes it a personal tort, recovery will be allowed. So a plaintiff was permitted to recover who had been induced to marry a woman, then with child by the defendant, by the defendant's representation that she was chaste (Kujek v. 

Goldman, 150 N. Y. 176, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 8). A recovery was also allowed by a woman against the relatives of her former husband (from whom she had obtained an annulment on the same ground) who had induced her to marry him by representations that he had never been ill, when, in fact, they knew that he had tuberculosis (Leventhal v. Liberman, N. Y. —, N. Y. Law Journal, July 24, 1933). In this case, in the annulment, there was repudiation, which usually eliminates the propriety and necessity for a recovery of damages, because of the restoration to status quo. Here, however, there could not, even through the annulment of the marriage, be a complete restoration to status quo because the marriage did take place and did endure for a time. Damages were proper to compensate for that.

B. SLANDER OF TITLE Sec. 139. In General; Elements. Professor Burdick says that this tort differs from deceit in that the defendant here intends to deceive not the plaintiff, but third persons in order to induce them to refrain from buying the plaintiff's goods or patronizing his business; that it is not limited to the plaintiff's title to property, and that it might better be called "Disparagement of Property." Professor Cooley divides it into "Slander of

Property" and "Slander of Title." Misrepresentation of the

character and quality of goods or other property sold or facilities afforded by the plaintiff (Maglio v. Herald Co., 93 App. 

Div., 546; Paull v. Halferty, 63 Penn. 46; Kendall v. Stone, 5 

N. Y. 14 at p. 18; Wier v. Allen, 51 N. H. 177), as well as false statements to the effect that plaintiff's title to property is faulty or non-existent (Andres v. Deshler, 45 N. J. Law 167; Cooley's 

Law of Torts, Third Ed., par. 166, 167) are actionable if the other elements are present. The elements of slander of title are: (1) Unfair disparagement of plaintiff's property, property interests, or title, which we have just considered, (2) Intended to induce third persons to refrain from patronizing plaintiff, or which result in the loss to plaintiff of their patronage, (3) Malice in fact (Cooley par. 126, 127; Cardon v. Mc- 

Connoll, 120 N. C. 461) and, (4) Damages, which must be pleaded and proved (Maglio v. Harold Co., supra; Marlin v. Shields, 171 N. Y. 384; 

Cooley, par. 126, 127).

If the disparagement consists in a claim of title made in good faith by the defendant it is not actionable (Lovell v. 

Houghton, 116 N. Y. 520), and neither is a mere claim that one's goods are superior to those of the plaintiff (Tobias v. Harland, 4 Wend. 537 at p. 541; Johnson v. Hitchcock, 15 Johns. 185).

Unquestionably the presence of malice is inferable from the intention and the disparagement, but rebuttable by evidence of honesty of purpose.

C. UNFAIR COMPETITION 

Sec. 140. In General; Elements; Conspiracy. Unfair competition requires redress by way of injunction (Salamy v. Sorgeous, 213 App. Div. 160) more often than merely by way of money damages, and so cases involving this tort come up more often in equity than at law. It has been considered in some detail in Sec. 31, supra, to which attention is again directed. The elements of this tort are similar to those of slander of title, except that instead of disparagement of plaintiff's goods or his title thereto, any oppressive or unlawful (see Sec. 31, supra) means or device by which successful attempt is made to deprive plaintiff of the good will of his business, or to impede him in carry-

ing it on, is the first element (Cooley, par. 195), and except that damage need not be proved to have been suffered, so long as it is threatened, in which case, however, only an injunction, and not a money judgment, can be had (William P. Goldman & Bros., v. Goldstein, 211 N. Y. Supp. 872, 125 Misc. 737). The tort may consist of deception or oppression practiced on the public, so that it is induced to refrain from trading with the plaintiff, and generally to trade with the defendant instead (Ford Motor Co. v. Cady Co., 124 Misc. 678; 

Buffalo T. E. v. McGail, 240 N. Y. 113), or prevented by fear of violence or by other unfair means from trading with the plaintiff (Sec. 31, supra); or it may consist of force, fraud, or fear utilized or practiced against the plaintiff so that permanently or temporarily he is prevented wholly or partially from carrying on his business with freedom (cases cited Sec. 31, supra). The unfair duplication or near-duplication of trade marks is one of the commonest forms of unfair competition, but it may take other forms as well, as boycotting or picketing with intimidation as an incident (Sec. 31, supra), or unlawful combinations (Sec. 31, supra).

Conspiracies or combinations are not of themselves actionable, however, unless in pursuance of them an overt act be committed which is in itself a wrong (Brackett v. Griswold, 112 N. Y. 454).

In such cases all the conspirators, whether they participated in the act or not, are liable (Sec. 65, supra).

We have seen that a man may use his own name in business though it enables him to compete with a longer established concern of the same name; this rule, however, does not extend to initials, and the user of G. G. as a monogram will be enjoined (though those letters are his initials) upon application of a prior user of G. G. G. (William P. Goldman & Bros. v. Goldstein, 211 

N. Y. Supp. 872, 125 Misc. 737).

Geographical names are not protected at the common law, and there can be no common law copyright of them by user (even though it be possible to copyright such names under the statute); as a consequence, it has been recently held that if there has been no statutory copyright obtained the used of the word "Normandy" will not be protected (Fred Butterfield & Co. v. 

Abraham & Strauss, 212 App. Div. 384).

The true test of actionable or enjoinable infringement is not identity of the imitating name or device with that imitated, but

misleading similarity between them (Luxor Cab Mfg. Corp. v. 

Leading Cab Co., 125 Misc. 764, aff'd 215 App. Div. 798).

The right to a trade name, mark or device acquired by user is not lost by mere non-user; accordingly it has been held that a defendant who has without protest from plaintiff infringed a trade device for six years will nevertheless be enjoined from further infringement even though the plaintiff's laches may defeat his right to an accounting and to damages (William P. 

Goldman & Bros. v. Goldstein, supra). Non-user coupled with other facts, however, may constitute abandonment, which is a question for the jury (Burt v. Tucker, 178 Mass. 493). See also

Lehrenkrauss v. Universal Tours, 262 N. Y. 332. 

D. INTERFERENCE WITH CONTRACT RELAȚIONS 

Sec. 141. In General. The successful and malicious persuasion by one person to induce another to break a contract existing between the latter and a third person is a breach of legal duty and gives to the third person a cause of action in tort against him who induced the breach. Malice in fact is an element of the tort, but it may be inferred from the fact that the defendant induced the breach with knowledge of the existence of the contract (Sec. 31, supra).

CHAPTER XVI NUISANCE Sec. 142. In General; Elements; Nuisance Per Se; Public and Private Nuisance. Nuisance is a term applied to wrongs arising from an unreasonable or unwarrantable and therefore unlawful use by a person of his property, real or personal, and resulting in actual or threatened material discomfort or hurt to another or others (McNulty v. Ludwig & Co., 153 App. Div. 

206; Congreve v. Smith, 18 N. Y. 79; Heeg v. Licht, 80 N. Y. 

579, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 587; Clifford v. Dam, 81 N. Y. 

52, Babbage v. Powers, 130 N. Y. 281). The right of action in nuisance cases exists whether or not there was negligence, i. e., a lack of due care (Heeg v. Licht, supra), although very frequently the same acts or omissions constitute both negligence and nuisance (McNulty v. Ludwig Co., supra; Lamming v. 

Galusha, 135 N. Y. 239; Uggla v. Brokaw, 117 App. Div. 586). "For time out of mind the term 'nuisance' has been regarded as incapable of definition so as to fit all cases, because the controlling facts are seldom alike, and each case stands on its own footing. We are not aided by the classification into public and private nuisances, because the difference between them does not depend on the nature of the thing done, but on the fact that one affects the public at large and the other a limited number only. 

The primary meaning of the word, suggested by its derivation, is that which injures, or, in the quaint phrase of ancient times, 'that which worketh hurt.' The injury may be to person or property, to health, comfort, safety or morality. It may be a crime. Courts of high standing have held that a nuisance at law, or a nuisance per se, exists only when the act done is a nuisance at all times and under any circumstances, regardless of location or surroundings. Other courts make fitness of locality the standard and give controlling effect to surrounding circumstances, holding certain acts not permissible as matter of law under some circumstances, but permissible under others and under others still not permissible if the jury find them nuisances as matter of fact. The weight of authority in this state and elsewhere is in accordance with the latter view; except when

the act is malum in se, when the surrounding circumstances have no bearing upon the question. "We think that each case must depend on its own facts for classification as a nuisance at law, or in fact, or neither. Without attempting a general definition, we are of the opinion that as applied to the facts of the case before us, if the natural tendency of the act complained of is to create danger and inflict injury upon person or property, it may properly be found to be a nuisance as matter of fact; but if the act in its inherent nature is so hazardous as to make the danger extreme and serious injury so probable as to be almost a certainty, it should be held a nuisance as matter of law. Locality, surroundings, methods, the degree of danger, and the custom of the country are the important factors. The firing of a cannon loaded with grapeshot, if in a city or village, would be a nuisance as matter of law; if in a remote place far from the habitations of man, it might be a nuisance as matter of fact, and if against the face of a precipice, no nuisance at all. "An exhibition of fireworks is not malum in se, but is evil or innocent according to circumstances. Unless malum in se, like a disorderly house, for instance, a nuisance is a matter of degree. Thus we have held that the storage of gunpowder is not a nuisance per se and that it 'depends upon the locality, the quantity and the surrounding circumstances,' which are for the consideration of the jury. Heeg v. Licht, 80 N. Y. 579). A nuisance does not rest upon the degree of care used, for that presents a question of negligence, but on the degree of danger existing even with the best of care. Degree implies gradation, and gradation depends on circumstances. When the degree of danger is obvious and so extreme as to invite calamity, a nuisance per se exists, but when the danger is so secret in nature that the cause of an accident cannot be discovered and according to all experience is neither imminent nor extreme, it is not a nuisance per se, although the jury may find it a nuisance in fact." (From the opinion by Judge Vann, in Melker v. City of 

New York, 190 N. Y. 481).

From this it can be seen that the elements of the tort of nuisance are:

(1) A right of "person, property, health, comfort, safety or morality," (2) An invasion of that right, and 

(3) Damages. 

The basis of this tort is the maxim "sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas," and the matters involved in considering problems of the law of nuisance are matters of degree. In Sec. 30, supra, it was said that the question of what use of a flowing stream constituted a nuisance to those down the stream, whose land abutted on it, depended upon the test of reasonableness, and the cases of Strobel v. Kerr Salt Co., 164 N. Y. 303, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 572 and Whalen v. Union, etc., Co., 208 N. Y. 1; Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 584, illustrate this rule. The Melker case clearly holds that an act or omission is not a nuisance per se (that is, a nuisance as a matter of law), unless beyond doubt it materially invades one of the rights of the plaintiff listed in element (1) of the tort, or is so hazardous that from the very nature of it material invasion of some such right of the plaintiff is all but inevitable. A thing may, however, said Judge Vann, be a nuisance without being a nuisance per se; such cases present questions for the jury; in deciding questions of nuisance (per se and not per se) courts and juries are controlled by the locality, surrounding circumstances, methods, the degree of danger, and the custom of the country. A given state of affairs may be a public nuisance, i. e., a nuisance to the community; and criminal law makes such nuis ances crimes (Penal Law, Secs. 1530-1534). No action by a person for damages based on a public nuisance can be maintained, however, unless as to him the public nuisance is a private nuisance, in that it invades one or more of his private rights (Buckholz v. R. Co., 148 N. Y. 640, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 554).

People v. Rubenfeld, 254 N. Y. 245, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on 

Torts, p. 61, is an example of a conviction for the crime of maintaining a public nuisance. The objectionable establishment was a boisterous, all-night dance hall in a residence neighborhood. 

This particular public nuisance was also a private nuisance to every neighbor, for in addition to violating the criminal law by disturbing the peace of the locality, it was a personal disturbance to every individual in the locality.

Sec. 143. Rights of Person, Property, Health, Comfort, 

Safety, and Morality. The enumeration here given is drawn directly from the Melker case, and is not intended to limit nuisance to violations of rights in the list, which is suggestive merely. Instances of nuisance actually litigated are so numerous that to attempt to set them forth would be impossible. 

Illustrative cases are sufficient for present purposes. 

It is not necessary that health be actually endangered; it is sufficient that a plaintiff has been rendered more uncomfortable than the ordinary incidents of civilized life render unavoidable (Campbell v. Seaman, 63 N. Y. 568, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

539), as by a hot frankfurter stand, with its consequent odors, and noise late at night, in a beautiful residential district (Andrews v. Perry, 127 Misc. 320). Thus it can be seen that a business may be lawful, and yet, because of the place in which it is carried on, be a nuisance. See also Campbell v. Seaman, 63 N. Y. 568, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 539; Heeg v. Licht, supra. Thus the maintenance of an open air arena in a residential district is a nuisance where it brings together large crowds and causes traffic increase at least once a week (Russell v. Nostrand A. C., 212 App. Div. 543, mod. 240 N. Y. 681). On the other hand, the nuisance, if mere noise or odor, must bẹ unreasonable, i. e., sufficiently annoying to normal people; accordingly it has been held that the ringing of a church bell is not a nuisance, although it causes convulsions in an invalid (Rogers v. 

Elliott, 146 Mass. 349, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 566). How ever, this requirement that the nuisance be annoying to normal persons is elastic, and since locality is one of the considerations, the ringing of a bell near enough to a hospital to annoy the patients will be held to be a nuisance (People v. Hess, 110 Misc. 76).

The location of a cemetery within six feet of a water supply has been held to be a nuisance (Cheektowaga v. St. Peter, etc., Church, 123 Misc. 458). Under Sec. 3 of the Real Property Law, a fence over 10 feet in height may be adjudicated a nuisance (supra, Sec. 30).

The Curb Market, before it erected its own building, had been adjudicated a nuisance because it lowered the rental value of surrounding property (Broad Exch. Co. v. Curb Market, 117 

Misc. 82).

Unlawful enterprises, such as houses of prostitution, gambling houses, opium dens, lying-in hospitals where unlawful abortions are performed, etc., are generally held to be nuisances 

(Penal Law, Secs. 1530-1534; People v. Schenk, 142 Supp. 1081; 

Barnesciotta v. People, 10 Hun. 137, aff'd 69 N. Y. 612; People v. Curtis, 152 App. Div. 372; aff'd 206 N. Y. 747; People v. Hoffman, 118 App. Div. 862, aff'd 189 N. Y. 561; Penal Law, Secs. 

971, 972).

The pollution of the air may be a nuisance (People v. International Nickel Co., 168 App. Div. 245), particularly if, as by destroying verdure, it results in special damages (Campbell v. 

Seeman, 63 N. Y. 568, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 539). Pollution of running water is a nuisance (Whalen v. Union, etc., Co., supra; Strobel v. Kerr Salt Co., supra).

It is a nuisance to divert a flowing stream (Strobel v. Kerr 

Salt Co., supra), or to dam it up through a whole season or for any appreciable period, depending on circumstances, although to dam it for a short period during a drought so as to increase power is permissible (Clinton v. Myers, supra).

It is a nuisance to change the natural condition of one's land so that water drains from it to that of another person where none did before the change, or so that it now drains in greater quantity than it did before the change (Barkley v. Wilcox, 86 

N. Y. 140, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 549). 

Sec. 144. The Invasion; Damages; Injunction; Abatement.

In nuisance actions, the plaintiff is generally entitled at least to nominal damages (Watson v. New Milford Water Co., 71 Conn. 

442; Newark v. Chestnut Hill, 77 N. J. Eq. 23), upon showing the invasion of the right. In many cases, proof of the right and of its invasion is proof of facts enough to justify a recovery of substantial damages of a general nature, as, where plaintiff proves pollution of the atmosphere over a considerable period without proving specific damages other than continued discomfort; indeed, it has been said, although not with strict accuracy, that from proof of a nuisance the law will import damages (Farley v. 

Gas. Co., 105 Ga. 323).

If, however, the invasion of the right (and we will not take time here to consider specific instances of invasion, for in discussing the rights which may be invaded we have already sufficiently considered what is necessary to be shown to constitute

this element) consists rather of threatening danger than of accomplished harm, or if the nuisance is a continuing one, remedy by injunction may be had, even without proof of damage (Campbell v. Seaman, supra; Clinton v. Myers, supra; Strobel v. Kerr Salt Co., supra; Whalen v. Union, etc., Co., supra). In order, however, to obtain injunctive relief, the plaintiff must be able to show either that harm which money will not adequately recompense will probably result if the nuisance is continued, or, since in an action for damages for nuisance a plaintiff can recover only for the damage sustained to the time of trial, that injunction is required to avoid a multiplicity of suits (Campbell v. 

Seaman, supra; Clinton v. Myers, supra; Whalen v. Union, etc., Co., supra; Strobel v. Kerr Salt Co., supra).

An injunction cannot be had, however, when the nuisance is causing or likely to cause comparatively slight harm and when to enjoin it would cause the defendant great harm; in such a case the plaintiff is left to his remedy at law for damages (Mc- 

Cann v. Chasm Power Co., 211 N. Y. 301, Holahan's Cases on 

Equity, p. 541). Nor can an injunction be ordinarily had when the nuisance complained of amounts to a taking of property or rights in property by a public or quasi public corporation which has the right (eminent domain) to condemn that property; in such a case the plaintiff is required to take compensation by way of damages (Buckholtz v. R. Co., supra) more often than not.

Abatement, a necessary but somewhat dangerous remedy (self-help), has already been considered (supra, Sec. 61). It must be remembered that the burden is on the person who abates a nuisance to show a right to abate, that he must use care to do only what is necessary to abate, and that he must not disturb the public peace or put in peril innocent third persons or their property (see cases cited Sec. 61, supra; Roxbury Light & 

Power Co. v. Dimmick, 196 N. Y. Supp. 320).

Sec. 145. Contributory Negligence. As a defense to nuis ance actions, where the cause of action is based upon a nuisance which is also negligence, contributory negligence is now available (McFarlane v. City of Niagara Falls, 247 N. Y. 340).

Sec. 146. Coming to a Nuisance; Legalizing Nuisances. It does not matter that the existence of the nuisance antedates the settling of the plaintiff in the locality; the voluntary coming of the plaintiff to that place is not an assumption of the risk, the

doctrine of "volenti non fit injuria" does not apply, and therefore it is no defense (Campbell v. Seaman, supra; Andrews v. 

Perry, supra). "Legalizing nuisances" by statutes or ordinances is possible, but the authorization must not violate constitutional safeguards; furthermore, the statutory sanction must be so clear that it is evident the legislature contemplated the very result accomplished (Morton v. City of N. Y., 140 N. Y. 207; Coggwell v. N. Y., etc., R. Co., 103 N. Y. 10; Bates v. Holbrook, 171 N. Y. 460). 

Sec. 147. Persons Liable. The general rule is that both the creator and maintainer of a nuisance are liable for it if they are separate persons (Holland v. Levy, 189 N. Y. Supp. 77). The creator is liable whether it is his own land or whether he has set it up elsewhere (Uggla v. Brokaw, 117 App. Div. 586; Deming v. R. R., 169 N. Y. 1).

One who creates or has been maintaining a nuisance is liable though he leases the premises containing it, and until recently it was thought that the same principles operated to continue the liability of a grantor of such property (Wilks v. N. Y. 

Tel. Co., 208 App. Div. 542; Aherne v. Steele, 115 N. Y. 203, at p. 209; Timlin v. S. O., 126 N. Y. 514; Swords v. Edgar, 59 N. Y. 

28, at p. 34). If the lease entirely divests the lessor of control for the period of the lease, the duties and liabilities of the lessor may be only those of a grantor, as set forth in the next paragraph. .

It is now known that the grantor of land containing a nuisance is not liable for harm caused after the grantee has taken possession, unless he, knowing of the nuisance, failed to disclose it to his grantee, who does not know of it and the grantor has reason to believe that the grantee will not discover the condition or realize the risk (Kilmer v. White, 254 N. Y. 

64, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 64).

The grantee or lessee of premises containing a nuisance is liable if he continues the nuisance by suffering it to remain after notice of it or after the lapse of such time as is sufficient to enable him to discover the nuisance by the exercise of reasonable care (Moose v. Board of Health, 116 Misc. 459; Zolezzi v. Mac- 

Canlis, 210 App. Div. 368).

For joinder of parties defendant in nuisance actions, see supra, Section 64.

CHAPTER XVII NEGLIGENCE Sec. 148. In General; Elements. The elements of the tort of negligence are (Erwin's Summary of Torts, p. 190): 

(1) A legal duty to use care, (2) A breach of that duty, and 

(3) Damages. 

Purpose, or intent, plays no part in negligence; it is not an element of the tort, which consists solely in the failure to use care when a duty to use care exists (Gardner v. Leartt, 3 Denio 232).

Sec. 149. Legal Duty to Use Care; In General; Duties To ward Trespassers, Licensees and Invitees; Duties of Master to

Servant; Duty of Carriers. Professor Cooley (Sec. 337) says: 

"Negligence is a relative term, and its application depends on the situation of the parties and the degree of care and vigilance which the circumstances reasonably impose." There are no degrees of negligence, for negligence in every case is failure to observe the duty of care (Burdick, par. 446). To every person with whom one comes in contact a duty to use care is owed, but that duty of care differs in different cases, depending on the relationship of the parties, the degree of risk or danger, and the other circumstances (Burdick, para. 446; Larmore v. Crown Point 

Iron Co., 101 N. Y. 391, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 161). The question of what degree of care is owed is for the court; the question of whether, with respect to that duty, the defendant has acted as a reasonably prudent man would act under the circumstances is for the jury; when the law fixes a standard of care (as in the case of trespassers, licensees and invitees) the question of whether the defendant's conduct falls short of the standard is for the court if the facts of the defendant's conduct are not in doubt and reasonable men could not disagree about the reasonableness of that conduct; if the facts are in doubt, or if there is no prescribed standard (as in the ordinary every-day case, in which the question of due care "rests on the particular facts of the case and is to be settled for the occasion by the exercise of human judgment upon these facts"), the question is of

fact and for the jury upon a proper charge from the court as to the law (Burdick, par. 453, quoting from the head-note of Farrell v. Ry., 60 Conn. 239).

Examples of standardization of the requirements as to care owed are found in comparing duties toward trespassers, licensees and invitees. Toward a trespasser, the duty of a landowner is merely negative, and requires him to refrain from inflicting upon the trespasser wilful or malicious injury and from setting traps for him without warning (Magar v. Hammond, 171 N. Y. 377; 

Quigley v. Clough, 173 Mass. 429).

Many jurisdictions have recognized an exception to this rule. The exception deals with the attraction of infants to places and situations which are alluring and dangerous. It is known as the "attractive nuisance" doctrine.

Infants thus attracted are, of course, none the less trespassers, but the theory is that since the attractive thing is alluring, the owner and the maintainer of the location and the owner and the maintainer of the thing alike owe the infants who may be attracted all the duties ordinarily owed to invitees. 

The duty exists either to prevent entrance of such children by insurmountable walls or fences, or by watchmen, or, in default thereof, to make both the premises and the attractive thing safe.

England, and what was once probably a majority of our American states, held fast to the doctrine, which had its origin in the former jurisdiction, and was followed by the United 

States Supreme Court (Railway Co. v. Stout, 17 Wall. 657; 

Hayes v. Railroad Co., 111 U. S. 228).

New York was the most vehement opponent of the rule, and the protagonist of the contrary and minority view.

For a discussion of the above and New York's attitude, see 

Walsh v. Fitchburg Ry., 145 N. Y. 301, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 644; also Flaherty v. Metro, 202 App. Div. 583 and Jaffy v. 

R. Co., 118 Misc. 147.

A strong reaction against this rule set in, and finally the weight of authority was heavy on the other side, so that the minority view represented by New York became the majority view (Burdick, Secs. 567-569).

In spite of the hostility to the rule which was frequently expressed in New York, there were influences at work tending to

cause New York to recede from its position. Numerous decisions imperilled New York's aloofness.

Finally, by a four to three decision of the Court of Appeals, in the very recent case of Parnell v. Holland Furnace Co., 260 

N. Y. 604, the attractive nuisance rule, in effect, was embraced. 

Strangely enough, this has occurred at a time when the tide in states which formerly supported the doctrine is running strongly against it.

It is significant that there was no prevailing opinion. Judge 

Crane, however, wrote for the minority, and in his opinion discussed the developments in New York and the line of cases constituting the theretofore existing rule from which the decision departed.

One month previously, in a unanimous decision, the same court, in Long v. City of Dunkirk, 260 N. Y. 599, without any opinion at all, allowed a recovery by an infant who, when injured, was trespassing, not on real property as in the Parnell case, but upon a truck left on a highway. 

With this exception, the rules governing the duties owed trespassers are as stated eleven paragraphs above. 

Inasmuch as we have been over the subject matter of the tort trespass, no discussion of who are trespassers need be had. 

However, it is well to remember that permission to enter upon lands need not always be expressly given; e. g., where a sale of standing timber is made, the law implies a license to enter upon the land and cut the timber (Giles v. Simonds, 15 Gray 441, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 462). Where a property owner, by not objecting, acquiesces in long use of his property, the law, if it does not actually imply a license, at least holds that the landowner ought to have anticipated the presence of the plaintiff and that toward him, therefore, is owed a greater degree of care than that owed the ordinary trespasser, so that for all practical purposes such a plaintiff is a licensee (Swift v. Ry., 123 

N. Y. 645). See also supra, pp. 14, 15, 100, 101.

Toward a licensee is owed no affirmative duty, but only the duty to refrain from doing anything active to hurt him; a landowner, therefore, would be liable to a licensee for all for which he would be liable to a trespasser, and, in addition, for negligent acts of commission, although not for negligent omissions (Larmore Crown Point Iron Co., supra; Morrison v. Hotel,

200 App. Div. 636; Rosenholz v. Frank G. Shattuck Co., 112 

Misc. 468; Stacy v. Shapiro, 212 App. Div. 723; Parkes v. N. Y. 

Tel. Co., 120 Misc. 459). Such cases as Klippel v. Weil, 204 

App. Div. 323; Dorsey v. Chautauqua Inst., 203 App. Div. 251, and Fox v. Warner-Qlinlan, 204 N. Y. 240, so far as they contain statements that the duty toward a licensee and the duty toward a trespasser are the same or that toward a licensee is owed only the duty to refrain from wilful or wanton injury, are not entirely accurate. For example, although a landowner need not give notice of repair work in plain sight (Fox v. Warner- 

Quinlan, supra), he is liable (as for an act of commission) if he creates dangers on his premises without warning licensees (Beck v. Carter, 68 N. Y. 283), or if he runs over a licensee with a train without having given notice of its approach (Swift v. Ry. supra; 

Byrne v. N. Y. Central, 104 N. Y. 362), although such acts are not wilful or wanton and would not make him liable to a trespasser.

Toward an invitee the ordinary duty of reasonable care to avoid injuring him by mere omissions (Dorsey v. Chautauqua, supra) as well as by affirmative acts is owed. 

As to the distinctions between licensees and invitees, in the main they are apparent from the meanings of the terms. An invitee, of course, is one invited, while a licensee is one who is merely suffered or permitted. Customers at a store (Tryon v. 

Chalmers, 205 App. Div. 816; Ford v. Wanamaker, 224 N. Y. 

655), those who come to premises as a matter of right, or on business in which the occupant is interested, or who come on an invitation arising out of a common interest or mutual advantage, and the employees of such persons (Lyman v. Putnam, 182 App. 

Div. 705, aff'd 230 N. Y. 548; Heffron v. N. Y. Central, 223 

N. Y. 473; Quinn v. S. I. R. T., 224 N. Y. 493), theater patrons (Brister v. Flatbush Leasing Corp., 202 App. Div. 294), policemen and firemen on public business (Meiers v. Koch Brewery, 229 N. Y. 10), and the like, are invitees. Canvassers, on the other hand, are mere licensees (Stacy v. Shapiro, 212 App. Div. 

723). See, also, the following cases: Mendelowitz v. Neisner, 258 N. Y. 181, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 11; Flynn v. 

Central R. Co., 142 N. Y. 439; Sterger v. Van Sicklen, 132 N. Y. 

499; Cusick v. Adams, 115 N. Y. 55; Ansteth v. Buffalo R. Co.,

145 N. Y. 210; Beck v. Carter, 68 N. Y. 292; Potter v. N. Y., etc., Ry., 261 N. Y. 489.

However, according to the Restatement of the Law of 

Torts, the principles of the case of Higgins v. Mason, 255 N. Y. 

104, Edgar's Suppl. Cases, p. 121, dealing with guests in automobiles, apply equally to guests in homes. See Section 155, infra, and the Higgins case, for a discussion of those principles. 

Invited social guests are undoubtedly invitees (Green v. 

Green, 212 App. Div. 381; Loucks v. Dolan, 211 N. Y. 237), although it has been held that if the visit be for the benefit of the plaintiff, as where a mother is injured at the home of a neighbor while in the act of calling for her children who have been cared for by the neighbor (Sterger v. Van Sicklen, 132 N. Y. 499), the caller is a mere licensee.

Persons invited to ride on vehicles are invitees (Bolton v. 

Madsen, 205 App. Div. 180; Clark v. Traver, 205 App. Div. 206, aff'd 237 N. Y. 544). See also Lowell v. Williams, 183 App. Div. 

701, aff'd 228 N. Y. 592. See also infra, Section 155.

The duties owed by master to servant have been dealt with in another place (supra, Ch. IV).

The duty owed by carriers to their passengers is to use the highest degree of care practicable to protect against accidents of travel (Stierele v. Union Ry. Co., 156 N. Y. 70; Erwin's Summary of Torts, pp. 195-199). A carrier also undertakes absolutely to protect its passengers from injury, abuse and insult at the hands of its employees, and, so far as it can prevent them, at the hands of fellow passengers and other persons (Gillespie v. 

R. Co., 178 N. Y. 343, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 620, and authorities cited and quoted therein). 

Professor Bohlen's "Essays on the Law of Torts" expounds in various of its articles the theories underlying the duty to use care.

He holds that no duty exists toward those who may be only possibly hurt by an act or omission. The duty extends only to those to whom harm as a consequence of an act or omission is reasonably to be foreseen.

Consequently no recovery was allowed against a defendant railroad whose guard, in pushing passengers into a train, caused a package containing fireworks to fall from the arms of one of the passengers; they exploded upon striking the ground,

and the concussion dislodged a shingle from a building some distance down the platform; the plaintiff, standing at the building, was struck by the shingle (Palsgraf v. Long Island R. Co., 248 N. Y. 339, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 83). Cf. Sec. 

151, infra.

When the duty claimed to have been broken is one which would not have existed had it not been for a contract a recovery ex delicto depends upon privity almost as truly as would a recovery ex contractu. So, accountants who are engaged by a firm to go over its books and make a certificate as to the condition of the firm are not responsible for their negligence to any person except their employer. They are not, for example, liable on the theory of negligence to third persons who extended credit to the employer on the strength of the report 

Ultramares Corp. v. Touche, 255 N. Y. 170, Edgar's Suppl.

Cases, p. 96). The effect of possible fraud has already been considered (Section 136, supra). However, even on the theory of negligence they would have been held liable if the reaching of the third person had been the "end and aim of the contract" (Glanzer v. Shepard, 233 N. Y. 236, distinguished by Chief Judge Cardozo in the Ultramares case). In the Glanzer case, public weighers were held liable to purchasers of beans from a seller who had engaged the defendants to weigh the beans, the defendants knowing that their certification of weight in order that buyers would buy was the "end and aim” of the contract. This is in accord with the newer theory (see comparison of Kalinowski and Palsgraf cases, infra, next section). 

Sec. 150. Legal Duty to Use Care; Theory of "No Liability 

Without Fault." A famous English case, Rylands v. Fletcher, L. R. 1. Ech. 265, 35 L. J. Ex. 154, approved L. R. 3 H. L. 330,

L. J. Ex. 161, held that one who brings upon his land and keeps or collects there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes does so at his peril, and if it escapes is liable for harm done by it. This doctrine was rejected in New York in 1873 (says 

Dean Pound, in Lecture IV of his "Introduction to the Philosophy of Law"). The decision rejecting it was Losee v. 

Buchanan, 51 N. Y. 476, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 608. In it the Court of Appeals (which Dean Pounds refers to as "the

leading exponent of no liability without fault") were unanimous. 

See also Sec. 25, supra; cf. Sec. 49, supra.

The doctrine is a much discussed one. In another lecture course in book form ("The Spirit of the Common Law," Lecture VII), Dean Pound says, "There is a tendency to revive the idea of liability without fault," and again, "There is a strong and growing tendency, where there is no blame on either side, to ask in view of the exigencies of social justice, who can best bear the loss." (See supra, Sec. 25. The ultimate answer, so modern. tendencies indicate, will be compulsory insurance against the consequences of inevitable accident, or the assumption of the financial burden by the state, as the one "who, in the interests of social justice, can best bear the loss." This will, however, not be a matter of case law development, but of legislation.) 

In New York such tendency has not found favor with the courts. Indeed, Dean Pound's rating of our Court of Appeals as the leading exponent of the opposite theory is well founded. 

While it is true that in Hay v. Cohoes, 2 N. Y. 159, that court decided in favor of the existence of a cause of action where, without proof of negligence, a defendant, in blasting, caused rocks and earth to be thrown upon the land of the plaintiff, the decision rested on the theory of trespass (see Booth v. 

Rome, 140 N. Y. 267, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 41) rather than on the theory of negligence. It is somewhat difficult to see why 

Losee v. Buchanan (the case of a boiler's exploding and throwing pieces of metal through the plaintiff's roof) was not decided on the same theory (i. e., of trespass), and in favor of the plaintiff rather than of the defendant, nor are the reasons given in the opinion at all satisfying. It is possible although not probable (but it would be a logical solution) that eventually the Court of Appeals will accept one of the several alternatives suggested by Dean Pound, and say that the throwing of matter in blasting is a proper case for the application of the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur (which is, nevertheless, not "liability without fault" but a doctrine of inference of fault). See Section 153, supra.

Cases of exploding boilers have not been dealt with as res ipsa loquitur (Losee v. Buchanan, supra). If, however, the process should be reversed and the rule of the Losee case should be applied to cases like Hay v. Cohoes, our Court of Appeals would preserve its position at the head of the list of those

opposed to Rylands v.

Fletcher, under the rule of which 

Losee v. 

Buchanan would doubtless have been decided for the plaintiff. To be consistent, however, our Court of Appeals would then have to hold that extraordinary circumstances by way of an excuse are not necessary to absolve insane persons from liability for their torts. See Section 49, supra, and compare with this discussion and with Section 25, supra. 

All in all, it is likely that New York will not for many years forsake the list of those generally supporting the doctrine of no liability without fault. The tendency indicated by the decision of Ives v. R. Co., 201 N. Y. 271, holding the first Workmen's

Compensation Law unconstitutional, is still too recent. If the doctrine is ever materially abrogated in this state it will probably be through the aid of the legislature. 

In view, therefore, of New York's attitude in favor of the doctrine, there is in this state no liability for harm done by fire, electricity, or explosives unless there is negligence or unless the use or storage of those things is under such circumstances as to constitute nuisance (vide supra, Ch. XVI; Heeg v. Licht, supra; 

Braun v. Buffalo, etc., Co., 220 N. Y. 484), although, of course, the care required is commensurate with the risk (Clark v. Foot, 8 Johns. 421; Torgeson v. Schultz, 192 N. Y. 156; O'Brien v. R. 

Co., 185 App. Div. 867), as we have seen, Section 149, supra.

Sec. 151. Legal Duty to Use Care; Duties With Respect to Poisons and Other Dangerous Articles. A person who manu factures, sells or lends dangerous articles is not liable in the absence of negligence (Rosenbusch v. Ambrosia, 181 App. 

Div. 97). But it is negligence for a druggist to label a poison. with the name of a harmless substance, and he is liable to anyone who is injured thereby (Thomas v. Winchester, 6 N. Y. 

397). If, therefore, a person sells or rents an article which he knows or ought to know is inherently dangerous, he must give notice of all the dangers he created or of which he knows or which he could have discovered upon reasonable inspection, and if he fails in his duty therein in any respect he is liable to any one who is injured thereby whether in privity with him (i. e., when the one who purchased or borrowed did so directly from him) or not (i. e., where it passed through several hands). See

MacPherson v. Buick Co., 217 N. Y. 282, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 650; Statler v. Ray Mfg. Co., 195 N. Y. 478; Henry v.

Crook, 202 App. Div. 19; Rosebrock v. Gen. Elec., 236 N. Y. 227 ; 

Sider v. Gen. Elec., 203 App. Div. 443, aff'd 238 N. Y. 64; Cahill v. Iniecto, 208 App. Div. 191; Rosenbusch v. Ambrosia, supra.

However, he is not liable if the defect is not discoverable, even though he made no inspection (Licari v. Markotos, 110 Misc. 334).

A late and very important case on this subject (Kalinowski vs. Truck Equipment Co. and Rock Asphalt & Construction 

Company, 237 App. Div. 472) was a case in which it was held that the rule of MacPherson v. Buick Motor Company, 217 N. Y. 

382, and Smith v. Peerless Glass Company, 259 N. Y. 292, apply even where the person injured was a stranger to the use of the article, which in this case was a truck, the plaintiff being not connected with the truck or its operation in any way, but being a bystander injured when a rear axle broke and she (plaintiff) was struck by a wheel which came off as a consequence, and which ran up over the curb and upon the sidewalk. 

It was also held that the jury should be permitted to say whether or not the injury to the plaintiff was an immediate consequence of the breaking of the axle and whether the hazard that ensued to the plaintiff was a hazard to be avoided by the defendant truck company.

The defendants were the owner of the truck and a company which had rebuilt the truck installing the new axle and wheels.

In speaking of the MacPherson and Smith cases, the court said: "The trend of these opinions was furthermore to the effect that the same principle applied as well to manufacturers of component parts of an article of commerce as to the manufacturer of the complete article. The defendant Truck Equipment Co., upon the alleged facts, is without doubt in the same situation as was the defendant Buick Motor Company in the cited case. 

(See American Institute of Law, Restatement of the Law of Torts, Tentative Draft, No. 5, Section 274). The MacPherson and Smith opinions have announced an extension of the applicability of proximate causation beyond those having contract relations with the offender to those whose use of the article causing injury is fairly to be foreseen. We are asked to go furtherto say that it is a fair jury question whether this truck repairing

company was bound to appreciate that a broken truck axle resulting from the company's failure to use proper material or to do proper work or to make proper inspection was reasonably likely to cause injury to lawful users of the streets, those whose presence in the vicinity of the proper use of the truck was a matter of reasonable anticipation—and whether the repairer can be held liable for injuring such person in the vicinity. An affirmative answer is indicated in Section 265 of the Restatement, supra. (Rosebrock v. General Electric Co., 236 N. Y. 227; Hopper v. Cooper & Co., 104 N. J. L. 93; McLeod v. Linde-Air Products Co., 318 Mo. 397; 1 S. W. [2d] 122). "This step beyond the literal application of the MacPherson and Smith opinions takes us no deeper into a region of doubt as to tort liability than do many cases in which recovery in tort has been granted to those whose liability to injury was held reasonably to be anticipated, e. g., Parnell v. Holland Furnace Co. (234 

App. Div. 567; affd. 260 N. Y. 604); Burrows v. Livingston- 

Niagara Power Co. (244 id. 548); De Haen v. Rockwood Sprinkler Co. (258 id. 350). The situations of this plaintiff and the truck were neither strange nor remote from reasonable expectation-the girl walking along a public sidewalk, the truck being driven along a public street. Negligence (under the pleading) caused the truck to break down. The sequel was something unusual, but was of a type which might be expected. And that is the test."

This is in accord with modern theories as expressed, supra, in Sections 37-40. It is not in conflict with the rule of the Palsgraf case, supra, Section 149, because in that case harm to the plaintiff was not reasonably foreseeable, and so whatever duty was broken was a duty to the passengers who were pushed and not to the plaintiff. It is a ratification of the view expressed above that the duty exists even to persons who are not in privity with the defendant. It is consistent with the rule which is the sum total of the Ultramares and Glanzer cases, supra, Sec. 149.

The Kalinowski case might be likened to the Glanzer case and the Palsgraf case to the Ultramares case. Under both rules, where it can be foreseen that persons such as the plaintiff will be affected, a duty of care to the plaintiff exists. Where it cannot be foreseen that the plaintiff will be affected, though there may be a duty to others, a breach of which would be a tort to

those others, there is no duty to the plaintiff, and if a breach of the duty to the others is committed and incidentally the plaintiff is injured, he cannot recover.

Another case involving an application of the rule of the MacPherson case is Smith v. Peerless Glass Company, 259 N. Y. 

262, cited in the Kalinowski case. 

In the Smith case a bottler was held liable for injuries resulting from the explosion of a pop bottle, there being evidence that there was a defect in the bottle discernible upon proper in spection. 

Sec. 152. The Breach; Presumption from Violation of Stat ute. It is hardly necessary to say that the duty to use care has been breached when the defendant has used less care than the ordinarily reasonable and prudent man would have used under the same circumstances. (Stierele v. Ry. Co., 156 N. Y. 70). The circumstances are the criterion of duty (supra, Sec. 149; O'Brien v. 

R. Co., 185 App. Div. 867). The question of what care, with regard to the duty owed, a reasonably prudent man would have used is for the jury (supra, Sec. 149).

It is, for example, not a breach of the duty (i. e., it is not negligence), for a mechanic, about to get down on his knees in a store to look at mechanism upon which he is working in plain sight, to fail to take the extraordinary precaution of giving notice that he is about to change his position from an upright one to a crouching one, as a result of which a customer falls over the mechanic's legs and is injured (Greene v. Sibley, Lindsay & 

Curr Co., 257 N. Y. 190, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 130). Only ordinary, and not extraordinary care is required in ordinary situations. 

The courts (in cases like the line established by MacPherson v. Buick Co., supra) and the legislature vie with one another in seeing that our law keeps abreast with economic, social and scientific progress.

An example of the legislative contribution is a 1933 amendment to Section 15 of the Vehicle and Traffic Law (Laws 1933, Ch. 765).

It requires that on and after January 1, 1934, all public conveyances be equipped with safety glass; and that private vehicles assembled on and after January 1, 1935, be so equipped.

In an action for personal injuries consisting of cuts from glass in an accident involving a vehicle whose equipment does not conform to the statute after the time set, it will be at least prima facie evidence of negligence that the vehicle was not so equipped. 

The statute does not say so, but an elementary, general rule is that proof of failure to obey a statute or ordinance is prima facie proof of tortiousness; if the damages which occurred were proximate, a case is made out (Orr. v. B. & O. R. R. Co., 168 

App. Div. 548). See also supra, Secs. 9, 10 and 35.

We have seen (in Section 37, supra), that frequently an injuria will be held not to be the proximate cause of a harm if there intervened in the chain of causation another injuria. This is particularly true if the first cause could not have produced the harm had the second not intervened. It is almost absolutely true if the second cause could have produced the harm without the aid of the first. 

Where the second cause is not only tortious but criminal, the application of the doctrine is almost automatic. 

So, in Benenson v. National Surety Co., 260 N. Y. 299, the negligence of the defendant was held not to be the proximate cause of the harm sustained by the plaintiff in accepting a surety bond, apparently executed by the defendant, but actually forged by a person who, through the negligence complained of, was enabled to steal the bond form.

Sec. 153. Burden of Proof; Res Ipsa Loquitur. The bur den of proof as to negligence is on the plaintiff (Ford v. McAdoo, 231 N. Y. 155; Brianzi v. Crane, 196 App. Div. 58; Spinneweber v. Every, 189 App. Div. 35). This is true even though the facts of the case be such as to raise an inference of negligence against the defendant (Spinneneber v. Every, supra) and even though the case be a clear one for the application of the doctrine “res ipsa liquitur” (Plumb v. Richmond L. & R. Co., 233 N. Y. 285), for the existence of a presumption of negligence merely throws upon the defendant the duty of coming forward with evidence to rebut the presumption or inference of negligence; when he has done this, the burden of coming forward with evidence is thrown upon the plaintiff, and if he then fails to give evidence of negligence, the judgment must be for the defendant; if, on the other hand, the plaintiff does meet the defendant's rebuttal of the in-

ference or presumption of negligence by giving evidence of negligence, the jury must decide upon the whole case whether or not the plaintiff's evidence preponderates over the defendant's; this is because the presumption of negligence is rebuttable and not conclusive and the ultimate burden of proof is upon the plaintiff even though the burden of coming forward with the evidence may shift from time to time (Plumb v. Richmond L. & R. Co., supra).

The doctrine of “res ipsa loquitur" (meaning "the thing speaks for itself") is a rule applied when from the manner and circumstances of an occurrence no other inference can be drawn than that the defendant was negligent (McKinney v. R. Co., 179 N. Y. Supp. 535). In a proper case for the application of the doctrine, the plaintiff proves merely the accident and its circumstances, and the defendant must then explain it, to show that he was not negligent (McKinney v. R. Co., supra; Courtney v. Gainsborough Studios, 186 App. Div. 820; 

Plumb v. Richmond L. & R. Co., supra). Examples of the application of the rule are found in cases in which the plaintiff falls through a sidewalk into a coal chute (Bender v. Dazian, 180 N. Y. Supp. 461), or through a grating in a sidewalk (Herman v. Josephsohn, 188 App. Div. 163), or where he is struck by a pane of glass which drops out of a window (Goldfarb v. New York, 108 Misc. 505), or is injured by the falling of a building (Mullen v. St. John, 57 N. Y. 567). In such cases, the fact that no fair inference can be drawn (without some explanation by the defendant) other than that the defendant was negligent calls for the application of the doctrine, and the result is that without direct proof of negligence plaintiff makes out a prima facie case (Courtney v. Gainsborough Studios, supra;

Plumb v. Richmond L. & R. Co., supra). It is not, however, the mere fact of the accident but its circumstances which raise the presumption (Kinney v. R. Co., supra; Courtney v. Gainsbor-ough Studios, supra). The doctrine applies only when the instrumentality through which the accident happens is entirely in the control of the defendant (King v. I. R. T., 197 App. Div. 

15, mod. 233 N. Y. 330; Plumb v. Richmond L. & R. Co., supra;

Courtney v. Gainsborough Studios, supra). The doctrine does not apply when there is any reasonable room for the inference that defendant was not negligent or that the fault was that of a 1

third person, for the test is as to whether the accident could, in the ordinary course of events, have happened without negligence of the defendant (Courtney v. Gainsborough Studios, supra; 

Plumb v. Richmond L. & R. Co., supra). The doctrine cannot, for these reasons, be applied to the bursting of a fly-wheel (Piehl v. Albany Ry., 162 N. Y. 617) or the explosion of a boiler (Losee v. Buchanan, 51 N. Y. 476, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

680; cf. supra, Sec. 150), or the falling of a ceiling, Slater v. 

Barnes, 241 N. Y. 284, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 132), for in such cases the defect may not have been discoverable by the defendant by the use of reasonable care; but the fall of an elevator is within the rule (Griffin v. Manice, 166 N. Y. 188), because such an incident could hardly occur except as the result of improper equipment or operation. [Quaere: does not the same reasoning justify the case of Hay v. City of Cohoes, supra, Section 150? But that would be holding the tort in the Hay case to be negligence, and not the trespass it was decided to be. 

However, by holding it to be res ipsa negligence, and not trespass, we can both justify the decision of the Hay case and hold to the "no liability without fault" doctrine of the Losee case. 

Further, there is less likelihood of culpability in the bursting of a boiler than in the hurling of material in a blasting operation.] 

It is typical res ipsa when a passenger turns on a hot water tap in a bath on a steamship, for boiling water to come out and scald him (Watson v. Compagnie General Transatlantique, 147 

Misc. 697). See also St. Johns Law Review, Vol. III, p. 247.

There is an inference of negligence (similar to but not strictly an example of the rule of res ipsa loquitur) in favor of a passenger against a carrier from the fact of the happening and circumstances of an accident in which the passenger is injured or killed while being carried (Plumb v. Richmond, supra); but if from the surrounding circumstances any fair inference inconsistent with the theory of the defendant's negligence can be drawn, as, if in a collision case the plaintiff's testimony shows that a third party, and not the plaintiff's carrier, was at fault, the doctrine does not apply (Norris v. Nat. B. Co., 216 App. Div. 

462), although the rule is otherwise if the testimony as to the third party's fault may, nevertheless, leave room for concurring fault of the carrier (Loudoun v. Eighth Ave. R. R. Co., 162 N. Y. 

380). When there is doubt as to whether the plaintiff was a pay

passenger, this doubt should be left to the jury to resolve (Salomone v. Yellow Taxi Corp., 242 N. Y. 251), although upon the question of negligence, if the case is such as would ordinarily give rise to an inference of negligence were the plaintiff a pay passenger, the jury will be thoroughly instructed as to the inference, and told to draw it if they find that the plaintiff was a pay passenger, but to render a verdict for the defendant if the plaintiff was not a pay passenger and there is no negligence in the case unless the inference is to be drawn. 

This rule (respecting the inference of negligence to be drawn from the happening of an accident to a conveyance in which the plaintiff is riding as a passenger of defendant carrier) differs from the rule of res ipsa in this: that the rule governing carriers applies even to cases of collisions with the vehicles and trains of other persons, so that there is, in the possible negligence of the other person, a possible, reasonable explanation other than that the defendant was negligent. The rule is based upon the fact that it is the business of the carrier to know how an accident occurred, while its passengers, not being involved in the operation of the conveyance, ordinarily are not watching and therefore cannot know the facts. For any other agency to be involved than those entirely within the defendant's control, as we have seen, will prevent the operation of the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur in its pure form. 

Sec. 154. Damages. Damages, to be recovered, must be proved (Chicago, etc., Ry. v. Rend, 6 Ill. App. 243 Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 195) and must be proximate (supra, Secs. 36-39).

Sec. 155. Defenses; In General. Assent of plaintiff, as sumption of the risk and illegal conduct have already been considered (supra, Secs. 32-35).

Two cases involving questions of assumption of the risk require mention here. 

In Heim v. Mitchell, 262 N. Y. 71, the Court of Appeals sustained a jury's verdict for the plaintiff in a case in which the defendant claimed that the plaintiff had assumed the risk of being hit by a baseball bat which, when swung, flew out of a player's hands. In other words, to watch such a game is not to assume the risk of such a happening as a matter of law. 

The other case is Higgins v. Mason, 255 N. Y. 104, Edgar's 

Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 121.

It holds that, although the driver of a car owes to his guest the duty of using ordinary care (supra, Sec. 149), yet the guest assumes the risk of defects in the vehicle unknown to the owner. 

The owner's duty is to drive carefully, but the guest "takes the vehicle as he finds it, and as it pleases the owner to maintain it.” 

Of course, however, defects of which the owner knows should be disclosed.

Patnode v. Foote, 153 App. Div. 494, was therefore not overruled by the cases in Section 149, supra. Those cases are correct in holding the passenger an invitee to whom ordinary care is owed. The Higgins and Patnode cases are correct in holding, however, that the guest assumes the risk of dangers not known to the host or of which the guest knows. 

The remainder of our discussion of defenses will be upon contributory negligence.

The rule as to who has the burden of proving contributory negligence or freedom from contributory negligence is a rule of substance and not of procedure merely. Consequently the lex loci governs, and not the lex fori (supra, Section 67). 

Sec. 156. Defenses; Contributory Negligence; Rule When 

Induced by Defendant. The negligence of the injured person is a complete defense to an action for damages for negligence; it need not be the main but it must be a concurrent cause (Rider v. Syracuse R. T. Co., 171 N. Y. 139, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

591) in order to constitute a defense.

The burden of proof as to contributory negligence is upon the plaintiff to prove his freedom therefrom (Rider v. Syracuse 

R. T. Co., supra, at p. 594 in Edgar's Cases on Torts), except in death cases, where the defendant has the burden of showing the deceased guilty of negligence contributing to the accident (Keosayian v. Geiger, 188 App. Div. 829).

The question of whether the plaintiff has been guilty of negligence is for the jury (Graham v. Ry. Co., 149 N. Y. 336, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 657) unless such negligence is established by uncontroverted evidence from which reasonable men could not draw conflicting inferences, in which case it is for the court, as where a pedestrian about to cross a city street fails to look to see if vehicles are coming (Knapp v. Barrett, 216 N. Y. 

226, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 662).

It is the duty of a guest or passenger in a vehicle to object when the vehicle is not being safely driven and to get out if a demand for safe driving is not met. But New York does not follow the Massachusetts rule that for a guest to go to sleep in the vehicle is contributory negligence as a matter of law. In New 

York, it is a question for the jury (Nelson v. Nygen, 259 N. Y. 

71, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 126).

We have seen (Sec. 39, supra) that when negligent conduct of the defendant creates a danger between which and another danger plaintiff must choose, the question of whether plaintiff has been contributorily negligent is for the jury, i. e., the court will not as a matter of law rule in such a case that his voluntary seeking of a danger is contributory negligence, for, when the defendant's negligence is the inducing cause, he ought in reason to bear the result of the choice his negligence has made necessary (Twomley v. Railroad, 69 N. Y. 158, Edgar's Cases on 

Torts, p. 618; other cases supra Sec. 39). In making his choice, the plaintiff need not use the best judgment; it is enough if he acts as others would act in like circumstances, for the law considers the emergency (Healy v. Warwick, 174 Supp. 632; Twomley v. Ry., supra).

Sec. 157. Defenses; Contributory Negligence; Saving Hu man Life. A similar rule exists with respect to cases in which the plaintiff's voluntary going into danger was for the purpose of saving human life. "The law has so high a regard for human life that it will not impute negligence to an effort to preserve it, unless made under such circumstances as to constitute rashness in the judgment of prudent persons" (Eckert v. Railroad, 43 

N. Y. 502, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 604). The question of contributory negligence in such cases is for the jury, and the test to be applied by them is as to whether or not the chances of escape were so small that the seeking of danger was rashness. 

No such broad rule as to leave every case to the jury exists, however, where the effort was to save property; it is for the protection of human life alone that the rule exists. (Eckert v. Railroad, supra). However, even to save property, reasonable risk may be incurred (Wardrop v. Santi, 233 N. Y. 227).

Sec. 158. Defenses; Contributory Negligence; Last Clear 

Chance. Where a plaintiff puts himself in danger, though negligently, and the defendant having full notice of the fact can

nevertheless avoid the accident and does not, then the contributory negligence of the plaintiff in thus putting himself in the way of danger does not defeat his recovery (Goldman v. Lanigan, 185 App. Div. 742). For a case discussing this rule and citing and distinguishing cases illustrating the rule and cases to which it does not apply, see Rider v. Syracuse R. T. Co., 171 

N. Y. 139, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 591. This doctrine is called by some authorities the doctrine of "last clear chance.”

Under the rule of the Rider case, it does not apply when a plaintiff, crossing a street in front of an approaching vehicle, continues across, though the driver of the vehicle sees him and fails to stop; but it does apply if the plaintiff is standing on the track or sitting in a vehicle which is parked upon the track, and the defendant, though seeing plaintiff or his vehicle, nevertheless collides with him or it. The test is really one of proximateness of the contributory negligence, and is for the jury. 

The doctrine will not be applied to a case in which it is not shown that the defendant knew of the plaintiff's peril and did not prevent harm to him. It is not enough that the defendant, by the exercise of reasonable care, could have known, and so ought to have known of the peril. It must be brought home to the defendant. The defendant may have been negligent in not knowing of it, but the doctrine of last clear chance does not apply. Further, when the plaintiff can at any moment step out of peril, the defendant is ordinarily justified in believing that he will (Panarese v. Union Railway Co., 261 N. Y. 233; Woloszynowski v. N. Y. Central, 254 N. Y. 206, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on 

Torts, p. 135).

Sec. 159. Defenses; Contributory Negligence; Imputed 

Negligence. Sometimes the negligence of one person is imputed to the other by reason of the relationship between them. In considering master and servant as parties to tort actions (supra, Ch. 

III) we found that the negligence of the servant while in the scope of his employment is imputed to the master so as to make the latter liable for that negligence to one injured by it. So also is it imputed to the master when he is plaintiff and the negligence of his servant contributed to the occurrence for which he sues (Smith v. N. Y. Central, 4 App. Div. 493). Likewise, when two persons are engaged in a joint entérprise and each has an equal right to direct the movement of the vehicle, the negligence

of one is imputed to the other (Donnelly v. Ry., 109 N. Y. 16; 

Cooley, Sec. 355).

The rule does not extend, however, so as to impute to a hirer the negligence of an independent contractor, as a hackman 

(Little v. Hackett, 116 U. S. 366, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 

640). Nor is the negligence of one driving a vehicle imputed to a relative riding with him (McCrossen v. Moorhead, 205 App. 

Div. 497, aff'd 236 N. Y. 644), nor to any passenger who has no right to control him (Fraser v. State, 112 Misc. 19; Hardin v. 

N. Y. C. R. Co., 205 App. Div. 157). However, whether or not the driver of a car is actually the agent or the servant of the owner, the negligence of the driver, in possession of the car with the consent of the owner, will be imputed to the owner in an action for personal injuries brought by a third person injured by the car (Vehicle and Traffic Law, Sec. 59). So, where property damage to the car is sustained in a collision between it and another car, caused by the concurrent negligence of the operators of both cars, the negligence of the plaintiff's driver, if he was driving with plaintiff's consent, will be imputed to the plaintiff and he will be denied a recovery because of the imputed contributory negligence. And if he is injured while riding in his own car while it is being driven by someone other than himself the negligence of his driver, whether his servant or not, will be imputed to him if he sues the negligent driver of the other colliding car (Gochee v. Wagner, 257 N. Y. 344, Edgar's Suppl. Cases on Torts, p. 80. This rule is an indirect development of Vehicle and Traffic Law, Sec. 59).

Inasmuch as the law requires of a child only that degree of care which an ordinary child of the same age, judgment, intelligence and experience would use in similar circumstances (Karpeles v. Heine, 227 N. Y. 74), a rule has been evolved according to which the negligence of a parent in permitting a child of such tender years that he cannot take care of himself to stray or otherwise be present in a place of danger is imputed to the child (Mangam v. Bklyn. R. R. Co., 38 N. Y. 455, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 630). Such a parent is not, however, contributorily negligent as a matter of law; the question is for the jury (Mangam v. Bklyn. City, 38 N. Y. 455; Regan v. R. Co., 205 App. Div. 

425). A child who is using at the time of the accident the care an adult would use is not, however, defeated by the negligence

of his parent in permitting him to escape or stray (McGarry v. 

Loomis, 63 N. Y. 104, Edgar's Cases on Torts, p. 637; Regan v. 

R. Co., supra). If the child escapes into a place of danger without fault of the parent or other custodian the rule does not apply (Mangam v. Bklyn. City, supra). The question of whether an infant is non sui juris (i. e., of such tender years and slight experience that his custodian's negligence will be imputed to him because he cannot take care of himself) is for the jury unless he is so clearly competent or incompetent that there is no doubt of his responsibility or the lack of it (Kenner v. Rader, 170 

N. Y. Supp. 957). An infant three years and seven months of age is non sui juris as a matter of law; however, the courts will not fix an arbitrary age (Mangam v. Bklyn. City, supra).
