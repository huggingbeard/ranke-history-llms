REAL PROPERTY. 

Incidents of Ownership. 

About the time of the Revolution, the colonies, throught their individual legislatures, passed a series of laws to settle the condition of real estate titles on an "as is" basis. This was the only practical thing to do at the time, since the boys were fighting the Red Skins on the West 

Front and the Red Coats on the East Front. King 

George was thus declared illegal, but all deeds or grants of real estate to the colonists from him or any of his mob were declared to be held by "free and common socage". Sort of as the Pigeon said to the Sugar Daddy when he tried to put on the squeeze, "Scram, Bum! I'm changing the locks on the flat on 54th St., to say nothing of the family locker at Murphy's Baths in Coney 

Island"; a girl can't be too careful these days!"

So, Feudal Estates were abolished around 1764, and in 1785, all Royal Grants were declared to be held in fee simple with power of sale or disposal (an idea to which the King had been strongly opposed.)

Of course, the colonists didn't get used to the new order of things right away and it took them some time (about 50 years, to be exact) to free their real estate from the other restrictions against free and unrestricted use that the King had doped out.

But finally the colonists authorized themselves, by Statute and judicial decision, to do the following things with the P. Q.:

1. Make valid deeds of future or contingent estates, or even expectancies. (Note: It would take a Yankee in any event to sell an expectancy for real dough). 

2. Sell without getting the tenant upstairs to accept the new landlord as his social equal. 

3. Pass the title by the best English they knew, including Brooklynese, it making no difference that the words "his heirs forever" were omitted. 

4. Include in the conveyance all buildings and improvements (and whatever is nailed to the dump), together with ways, roads, woods, waters, water courses, rights, privileges and appurtenances, by a deed that merely describes the real estate. 

5. By a conveyance to Phizzlestick and Whistlestop, have Phizzlestick and Whistlestop each receive an equal share outright (tho undivided) instead of the whole thing after the other guy died and nothing in the unpleasant interim, except the joint use thereof.

In most states they also passed laws giving to friendly aliens the same rights and liabilities as citizens.

DEEDS. 

A conveyance is the act of transferring title to real estate and is accomplished by drawing up and delivering a written instrument called a deed. The deed, like many legal documents, is usually written in reverse English, thereby becoming all

but unintelligible, but says in effect that "I, Whitney Whistlestop, transfer title to my lot known as number 23 Sourpuss Lane to Phineas 

Phizzlestick for $178.30 in coin of the realm."

The deed has to be signed by the owner of the fee (though at the common law this was unnecessary, because the owner of the fee was usually unable to write).

Formalities. 

As you may have guessed the deed must be written (or printed) and should be on paper or something similar like the back of an envelop or the front of a dress shirt. The idea is that it be legible though it need not be intelligible since then it would lose its character as a legal document. 

There must be a grantor and grantee and they must be named with sufficient certainty so that they can be identified. If Ed Ashcan is deeding his corner dump to Joe Charleyhorse, he can't do it by first names. "Id certum est quod certum reddi potest" is a good thing to remember. Who knows, it may even help to fix the leak in the bathroom.

On the other hand, a general reference to the parties is valid if a person of reasonable intelligence can tell who is meant. Thus a grant from 

Joe Aroma and the mob is plenty good, Joe being a W.K. gent and the mob being members in good standing of the Joe Aroma Social Club. 

It is also usual, and in fact even customary to add the place of residence of the parties in order further to pin them down, though where this would embarrass characters like Joe, the street

4 and number may be omitted, a mere statement of city, county and state being enough. It's a nice touch. Consideration.

Though this is desirable and even advisable in most human relationships, consideration is no longer essential for a deed. A conveyance today can be for free; an idea so inexplicable that it took a couple of hundred years for the law to recognize such a transaction as valid. Still and all, the usual form of deed says "$1 and other valuable consideration," when it comes to saying what the purchase price is. And since the purchase price is a precise and definite number of shills, the phrase is an understatement of ambiguity. It must be the heat. No one gets the buck, and what the grantor gets remains a deep secret. 

The only time the matter is important is when some creditor of the grantor tries to get the conveyance set aside on the ground that Joe was merely putting his property in someone else's name to save it from being sold to pay his debts. 

I don't suppose you can really blame the guy for trying, but if this is what he was up to, the creditors win. But if the grantee really paid something, even though not moola, the conveyance stands up and the creditors get stood up. 

I don't know why it is, but the question is always coming up as to whether or not love is a valid consideration; and a close reading of the cases leaves one in a quandary. To some courts it seems to be a matter of degree of relationship, and while some persons might try to estimate its

value with a slide-rule, one hour with Nellie G.

String should, in all impartiality, be worth Joe's corner lot.

Signature. 

The deed nowadays must be signed by the grantor, if he can write, and if not by someone acting for him, though he must make an "X".

More important than the signing, perhaps, is the seal. And why? Well, because most states hold a deed invalid unless it's duly sealed regardless of the signing. And what is a seal? Just a red waifer stuck to the paper where the grantor signs, or merely the letters L. S. printed at the end of the line where he signs. Am I kidding? I am not.

This hocus-pocus was started at the Common Law in the age of illiteracy. Deeds were signed or executed with a seal because Louie the Duke couldn't write. Later on when the girls and boys learned to write, and it seemed to make good sense to have them put their John Hancocks on other places than the backhouse wall, it became the custom to require all legal documents to be signed by the party who was to be charged. 

But they also liked the seal idea, (why, I don't know) and so now in our age of literacy and superintelligence a deed is both signed and sealed. So now you know.

Description. 

The property must be described so that a person of average intelligence can tell where it is and what it is. But since everyone you meet is

in all probability either a genius or a chowderhead, which seems to be the usual experience, see if you can figure it out yourself. it. 

Maybe you are an Average Man and don't know

Following are some of the more customary ways of describing property (The description to be preceded in any event by the name of the municipality, county and state of location): 

By metes and bounds 

By links and chains 

By lot and block number 

By street number.

There is also the stumble-bum method, to wit: "Beginning at the head of Southwest Harbor, the corner bound on the shore between said lot and Isaac's lot, and follows the above to the corner of 

Jonathan's lot and thence follows Allen's lines westerly to the head of said lot; and which is called 'Cockle's lot'." This was held sufficient in 

Carter v. Clark, 42A 398, 92 Me. 225 so paste it in your hat, if you ever want to know the situs of 

Cockle's lot.

Execution and Delivery (or Closing Title). 

The execution and delivery of a deed usually takes place at a little ceremony in the nature of a huddle (muddle would be a better word) and causes the layman to blink his eyes with astonishment at the law's facility for bewilderment. 

All that Joe Aroma thought he had to do was to sign the deed and hand it over for the stipulated moola, pick up his hat and go home.

But, alas, no. It must always be done the hard way, and by the time the ceremony is over, three to four hours will have elapsed and Joe's lot will have long since been lost in a sea of legal confusion, to say nothing of Joe.

He has had to sign the deed, all right, and his lawyer has witnessed his signature and taken his oath or acknowledgment on the back, but after that everything went blank and men were deducting from the cash consideration rightfully his the following: 

The broker's commission 

A sum to pay off the mortgage A stipend in the nature of a penalty for paying the mortgage so soon 

Another stipend for taxes

A couple of bucks for a proportionate share of the water bill $12.50 for documentary stamps for the deed and 50¢ as the cost of cancelling the mortgage 

An honorarium for his counsel.

If there is anything left, he gets it, but he won't need an armored truck to carry it home. 

Furthermore, besides signing the deed he will have signed two or three other papers to prove that he doesn't owe any more debts than they already know about, that he is an American citizen, that he lives at 23 Sourpuss Lane, without benefit of marriage ties, that he is not an escapist but just lucky, that he has never been through bankruptcy or insolvency nor in jail, that he is free, white and twenty-one, parts his hair in the middle and has a mole behind his right ear.

The final and effective incident in the passage of title, is the delivery of the deed, but Joe will be unconscious by then and this last act will be mercifully performed by his attorney. 

Resquiat in pace, Joey boy!

Validity. 

The validity of a deed depends upon the fact that the grantor knew what he was doing, and did it voluntarily and whether he could read or write is beside the issue and quite irrelevant.

As the books say, "One possessing the mental capacity to execute a deed may execute a valid deed although mentally weak or infirm, at least in the absence of attendant circumstances of an inequitable character, although one lacking such capacity may not execute a valid deed even though he is not totally bereft of understanding and although general incompetence is not a requisite." 

See Wooten v. Sullivan, 171 Ark. 1189, and I hope this makes a simple thing simpler, or does it leave us where we stood? By the way, where did we stood, or stand? Who cares!

A deed may also be invalidated because of mistake, fraud, duress, undue influence or illegality of consideration. To illustrate: 

Mistake-Where Joe was selling Mickey the Lug his gin mill, but retaining his doughnut shoppe, a deed conveying the doughnut shoppe to Mickey would be more than an error; it would be sure death.

Fraud-If Mickey gave Joe the heat treatment to make him sign, Joe could get a court to set it aside easy enough-if he could get to court. 

Undue Influence-This could come from 

Mickey's moll, Lulu Belle, and what an influence! Maybe a nice clean doughnut shoppe wouldn't be so bad after all.

Illegality of Consideration 

Yes, Lulu 

Belle, again. A deed to Mickey in exchange for Lulu Belle wouldn't be counteranced. In fact, it wouldn't even be legal. Recording.

A conveyance is valid and binding although the deed is not recorded in the County Clerk's Office, but, by recording the deed, subsequent purchasers, mortgagees and anyone else who cares, are put on notice that the property has changed hands. 

This has the practical effect of preventing Mr. 

Aroma from making a quick parlay on the Old 

Homestead to Jil, Mabel, and Jane in the order named and in succession, and only to make a profit of a mere 300%. Mr. Aroma will have to think of another way to get the dough. 

Estates Created. 

Deeds are construed in accordance with the law of the place where the land is situated. This rule is fundamental when discussing just what it is you get under a deed. Thus, if Joe Aroma signs a deed in the usual form for property in New York State, the grantee will doubtless get just what

Joe appears to be giving him. But if the property is in Hot Tomale, Mexico, where conveyances are governed by the law of grab and hold, whether the grantee gets much more than a thorough dusting off from Joe is all a question of Mexican law. 

Another profound rule of construction is that where the intention of the parties can be determined from the face of the instrument, this will control. Does this help you, brother? It doesn't do anything for me and I can read too.

The interest conveyed may be in fee, which means the works; a life estate, which means the grantee owns the property only so long as he lives; a term of years, which amounts to a lease; or an estate conditional on the happening of some event.

Also the conveyance may be of the Grantor's whole interest or of any fractional part, thereof, and may be made subject to conditions, restrictions, reservations, and exceptions. Isn't it just too horrible for words? It is. I guess we're just a bunch of Indian givers.

Thus, a clause requiring the grantee to construct a driveway with the provision that if the driveway shall cease to be used as such it shall revert to the grantor, creates a condition. 

A deed providing that the property cannot be used as a driveway creates a restriction. 

A deed establishing a mutual driveway on part of the land conveyed, creates a reservation; and 

A deed excepting a driveway from the conveyance is just what you thought it was, an exception.

These things all mean something different at the Common Law, but between you and me and the guy under Kelly's table, today, what one court calls a condition another calls a restriction, a reservation or an exception. Like Omar Khayam, you come out the same hole no matter the path "that in you went." Some fun!

Types of Deeds. 

The usual form of deed contains the following covenants:

Covenant of seizin-that grantor is in possession

Covenant as to right to convey-that grantor owns the joint

Covenant as to quiet possession-that the buyer won't get the bum's rush from some third party

Covenant against encumbrances that a dozen other guys don't already have a bite on the place

Covenant as to the grantor's acts—that the seller won't give the buyer the bum's rush himself 

Covenant as to further assurances-that the seller will sign more papers at any time if it will make the buyer any happier 

Covenant as to general warranty-that everything is hokay and if it ain't, the buyer can start suit

Covenant as to special warranty-that some particular aspect of the premises is O.K. 

There are several different types of deeds and these are:

Quitclaim Deed-where Whistlestop doubts that he really owns the property or any interest therein, but signs a deed releasing to the party of the second part whatever interest he may have in order to clear the title. 

Bargain and Sale Deed-where Whistlestop conveys whatever title he has to the premises in question to Phizzlestick for a consideration, but makes no guarantee that he actually owns the title. 

Warranty Deed-where Whistlestop guarantees that he has title to the premises free and clear of all liens and furthermore agrees to make it up with Phizzlestick if Phizzlestick should be tossed off the premises later by someone with a better title than Whistlestop's.

MORTGAGES. 

In General.

A mortgage of realty is an instrument in writing whereby Whistlestop and Phizzlestick put their real estate in hock with the Full Faith and 

Credit Association ("Uncle" to you) as security for a loan to tide them over. A mortgage reads like a conveyance, except that it says that when, as and if the loan (plus interest) is paid off, then the title conveyed (as security) "shall cease and determine and be of nothing holden." Of course in this happy event the record of the mortgage as filed in the County Register's Office should be cancelled. And if it's not paid off, the holder of the mortgage must foreclose on the property and

have the Sheriff sell it at Public Auction in order to realize anything on the loan. The mortgagee, himself, may buy in the property at the sale, and thus get good title, but the mortgage as such cannot transfer a valid title despite what it says.

A chattel mortgage is an instrument in writing whereby Whistlestop and Phizzlestick put everything in hock which their creditors can see, and which they can move. Yes, the love of money is the root of all evil, but the want of it is the mother of every mortgage. And whereas Uncle will keep possession of your watch, affording you the pleasure of gazing at his receipt for the time, the usual chattel mortgage, if duly recorded, is notice to the world, and all subsequent creditors, that your chattels are in hock but you are allowed to retain and use the chattels themselves, such chattels usually consisting of machinery, motor vehicles, household furniture and the like. And so although you may have to go to Uncle's to see the time, you can still park your weary bones in your own arm-chair.

So, to reiterate, a mortgage is a conditional conveyance of land or chattels given as security for the payment of money or the performance of some act, said conveyance to be void upon such payment or performance.

The usual real estate mortgage contains the following covenants or agreements of the unhappy owner:

Covenant of seizin (that he has possession) General Warranty (that he will defend the title until kingdom come)

That the taxes are paid (Of course, the buyer makes his own search at the tax office anyway)

That he will pay back the loan 

That he will keep up the insurance

That he may or may not pay the loan in advance That he will not claim any offset against the mortgage debt at some future time. 

Nature of Mortgages.

Assuming that you know what a mortgage is, in a vague sort of way, let us list a few of the principal attributes or characteristics thereof: 

1. In the first place, everything which is capable of being assigned, can be mortgaged, including future advances, and the hope that springs eternal. After all, when old Phizzlestick mortgaged his future to a Battle-Axe, called Lulu 

Belle, he was no no worse off than Whistlestop hitching his wagon to a star. They both hit all the higher bumps. 

2. The power to sell the security in case of default is sometimes expressly set forth in the mortgage, but it doesn't add much, since the law gives the right to sell the property on foreclosure anyway. 

3. Where the owner delivers an absolute deed to the other party, but the idea is that the property be held merely as security for a loan, then the courts will hold it to be an "equitable" mortgage and enforceable, the same as any other mortgage. In fact, any old arrangement under which title is transferred, but is to be held merely as

security, will be considered a mortgage by the courts. Well then, why bother with all this other hocus-pocus? It's just an old custom, I guess.

And this rule applies even where the "defeasance clause" (i. e. the agreement to reconvey the property) or the right to redeem, appears in an entirely separate instrument. But if it only exists in the jerk's mind, it's of no legal significance. 

You gotta draw the line some place, seem's if.

Execution and Delivery. 

The mortgage becomes effective and binding at a little ceremony usually held in some lawyer's office which ends with the mortgagee holding the mortgage and the mortgagor holding the dough. 

The mortgagor signs the mortgage by writing his name at the bottom and seals it by writing "L. S." thereafter; but if "L. S." is already printed on the document, he is presumed to adopt this as his seal by his signature. (This whole business of a seal is a hangover from the days of the Common Law, when the Lord of the Manor couldn't write but signed papers by putting his seal at the bottom. Since the ability to write (at least a name) has been characteristic of the average Homo Americanus for a hundred years, it would seem to be about time for the law to bring the matter up to date. But no, the law doesn't move that fast, as you doubtless are aware if you have ever been innocent enough to seek legal redress. Time may march on in some places, but in a court-room it proceeds at a slow belly roll-fortissimo to the fore followed by obligato to the rear.

Though the cases frequently hold, after much puffing and huffing, that the absence of a seal does not affect the validity of the instrument, the entire matter is much like getting chewing-gum in your hair. (Once it gets in, try to get it out.) 

The signature of the mortgagor must also be witnessed and an acknowledgment taken by a notary public or some similar officer (for the purpose of recording, as to which see post). In some jurisdictions an affidavit by the mortgagee as to the consideration is required for certain types of mortgages.

The effective moment of a mortgage is that of delivery. And since a mortgage is usually security for a bond or a note, the bond or note must also be handed over to the mortgagee, before the transaction can emit its first squeal, or squawk, as the case may be. Validity.

The mortgage is void and the gurgling squeal just a horse laugh if at its inception there was fraud, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, mutual mistake, or plain illegality.

Thus the mortgage would be n. g. for fraud if 

Phizzlestick executed it because Lulu Belle said she'd had a vision and that it portended that she was soon to bear a scion, and furthermore that her scion was going to have a phiz like Phizzlestick's-but she'd had neither vision nor hopes of scion. 

It would be misrepresentation if Lulu Belle had held out the hopes of holy matrimony to Mr. Phizzlestick, although she were already duly wedded.

It would be duress if she gave Phizzlestick the needle, and undue influence if she pulled a rod. 

And the whole thing would be just plain illegal anyhow.

Property Included. 

Despite the fact that the property mortgaged is described only as premises measuring so many feet this way by so many feet that way, this description, in most jurisdictions includes all trees, ways, streams and buildings, backhouses as well as outhouses, and those in existence as well as those to be built. A mortgage now of premises to be later acquired is valid, as well as a mortgage of premises described by reference to a map that can be identified.

Priority of Lien. 

In general, the priority or order of precedence of liens, whether of mortgages, judgments or what have you, is governed exclusively by their date of acquisition. It's a question of who puts the bite on first. 

Therefore, and to wit, it follows that: 

1. A judgment filed prior to the recording of the mortgage is a prior lien. 

2. A first mortgage takes precedence over all mortgages and liens later put on record, unless the holder of the first mortgage consents thereto. 

3. When it comes to liens for labor and materials supplied for construction purposes, you gotta be careful if you are the mortgagee to be, since you are now dealing with the Labor Bosses who

will be pleased to let you win second money any day of the week. Your position (as mortgagee) is in a puddle somewhere between the Supreme Court and the Legislature. However, the best bet is that if your money was actually used for the purchase of the land and construction of the building, you come in ahead of the plumber and the dealer in 

Church seats. 

4. Where a mortgage is put on the books but the money is paid out in instalments as services are rendered, a judgment takes precedence over all payments to be made after its entry. And the moral of this, Lulu Belle, is "Don't toy with a jerk who lays out his dough on the instalment plan". 

5. Where the buyer borrows the purchase price from a third person to whom he gives a mortgage, and then doesn't pay off the seller and the mortgagee knows all about it, the seller is said to have a vendor's lien prior to the lien of the mortgage regardless of recording. 

6. Where the mortgagee does not record his mortgage, which is dumb to start with, he may lose out as against judgments entered thereafter depending on the state in which the land is situated; though a mere attachment of the property in an action against the mortgagor is pretty generally held to be second fiddle to a prior unrecorded mortgage. The reasoning, if any, is that the attaching creditor stands in the mortgagor's shoes and hence must know what goes on under his hat, but any other type of creditor who gets a judgment against the mortgagor may or may not be using the mortgagor's galoshes, depending

on which side of the state line the land happens to be.

Rights and Liabilities as Between Mortgagor and Mortgagee.

The rights of the parties as between themselves are usually set down in writing so far as is humanly possible, neither one trusting the other, naturally. But when they overlook something, the law does the best it can.

Thus in the absence of an agreement the mortgagee is entitled to possession, both before and after default. In other words, the mortgagor gets the dough or loan, and the mortgagee gets the security or property and if the mortgagor fails to pay when due, the mortgagee simply keeps what he has, which is possession (but not the title), in case you're interested.

Where the land mortgaged is woodland, any timber cut thereon belongs to the mortgagee since the timber in this case constitutes a factor in the value of the land as security. Buildings are in the same class and cannot be removed by the mortgagor, but the crops and rents belong to him alone, and so do the taxes and municipal liens. 

But when the taxes, liens, upkeep, mortgage payments and Lulu Belle together prove too much for poor Phizzlestick, and the mortgagee gets possession of the premises, then he collects the rents, if any, but must charge them off against 

Phizzlestick's debt. On the other hand he charges 

Phizzlestick for any taxes or municipal liens which he may pay and for any repairs which he had to make.

If the mortgagee undertakes to pay for insurance, that's entirely his own business; but if the mortgagor commits any act tending to waste or destroy an integral part of the premises (such as keeping hogs in the sun parlor, or chickens in the bed-room) that's also his business and he can have the mortgagor enjoined. After all Phizzlestick is going too far when he moves chickens into the bed-room-or is he? H'mm!

When the day of judgment finally comes and 

Phizzlestick is about to be put through the wringer, he can, in most jurisdictions, compel the 

Sheriff to offer his watch and stickpin to the mortgagee in appeasement. In other words, most states have laws directing the holder of a defaulted mortgage to foreclose on the premises, or security, before permitting him to put the bite on what the mortgagor has in his pocket, which can only be done to make up any deficiency after the proceeds from the foreclosure sale have been credited on the obligation. 

Assignment of Mortgage.

It should be remembered that a mortgage is merely security for a loan, the loan being evidenced by a bond or promissory note, so that, according to the rule of law, an assignment of the bond or note operates as on assignment of the mortgage too. The theory is that as the tail follows the dog, the can follows the tail, the security follows the debt, and Lulu Belle follows Phizzlestick.

The usual procedure is for the mortgagee to execute a written assignment of the bond (or

note) and mortgage, the assignment of the mortgage then being recorded by the assignee, thus giving notice to all the world, in theory, that the assignee is now the owner of the obligation and the one to be paid. If thereafter the mortgagor pays the mortgagee, he's paid the wrong guy and has to pay the assignee all over again; and if the mortgagee makes another assignment of the same mortgage, the second assignee gets his lick on the taffy only after the first assignee has had his, after which there won't be any taffy left to lick. 

There's another fancy rule I might throw in to wit: "The assignee stands in the assignor's shoes and has the same rights he had, and the assignment is subject to all equities outstanding against the assignor unless the assignment were made before the due date to a person without notice". All that this means in United States 

English is that you can't sell what you ain't got, with the substatement that if some jerk has a piece of the mortgage, though it ain't supposed to be known, he can't be cut out by a mere transfer of said mortgage, unless it's done before the bite got set, and then the buyer has gotta be so dumb he don't know from nothing.

When the mortgage is finally paid, the guy is entitled to a statement to that effect from the mortgagee which is called a discharge, and which he records in order to wipe the thing off the books. 

There's a neat swindle in this connection, an understanding of which might come in handy sometime. It goes like this: Phizzlestick mortgages his apartment house in Canarsie, title to which is held by a dummy corporation, first to

Whistlestop and then to Simon Swivelchair, Whistlestop and Swivelchair holding first and second mortgages respectively, a not uncommon financial set-up for apartment houses. Phizzlestick then lets enough time go by so that the place gets a little run down (which is easy) and since he hasn't had any repairing done he's accumulated enough out of the rents paid by the tenants to pay off the first mortgage. But here he's gotta be careful.

He contrives (which is the right word since he has no right to demand it) to have the first mortgage assigned to his secretary or other stooge, instead of accepting a discharge of same. His stooge then forecloses the first mortgage and the second mortgage disappears like a swallow of Napoleon '47, same being cut off by the decree of foreclosure. 

Of course, Swivelchair has the right to protect his second mortgage by redeeming the first himself, but the chances are 100 to 1 against his putting any more chips into the game at this stage. It is assumed that title to the apartment house was held by a dummy corporation and that there was no personal liability on the bond. 

In fact, Phizzlestick, operating thru his dummy corporation, can even hold the reassignment himself despite the fact that he already holds the legal title, and thus hold a threat over Swivelchair's mortgage since the law says that where one party holds both legal title and lien, there is no merger of these interests if the party intended otherwise.

Sale of Mortgaged Property. 

This is the customary thing these days since practically everyone who owns any property, has

it decorated with the great American mortgage. 

It's all part of "Our Way of Life" you hear so much about, and shakes down to a scheme for spending $5000 per year on a $3500 income. The difference is the mortgage. And what a difference! Once you got it, it's all yours whether you sell the premises or not; and when the premises are sold "subject to the mortgage", nothing is added and nothing is taken away. In fact, the words mean nothing (no novelty in the law), since the premises are subject to the mortgage anyway. 

But when the grantee "assumes the mortgage" by the insertion in the deed of a covenant to that effect, then you have company in your liability. 

To the mortgagee you are still No. 1 man but to the Equity Court you are No. 2 man, all of which means that when the mortgagee sues you on your obligation, you can get a court order that he first sue your grantee who assumed the mortgage after which, and if he can't get satisfaction out of the grantee (and he probably won't since he already has foreclosed on the property,) he can and will return to you. 

But if in the course of human uncertainty and procrastination the mortgagee makes the mistake of giving more time to a grantee who has assumed the mortgage, this does let the mortgagor out, since the law considers it unfair to postpone the due date of the mortgagor's obligation. It's like the case of where the hanging was postponed to the afternoon; the prisoner escaped the death penalty on the ground that he had a right to be hung at 6:00 A. M., according to the order of the court, and not later.

Of course, the proper way for a person to sell mortgaged property, is to have the buyer pay off the old mortgage with funds raised from a new one on which the buyer alone is liable. 

Release and Satisfaction.

This is the pay-off. You might think it would be simple and easy; but alas, no. The payment may be in whole or in part, the release may be for money or services, and the mortgage may or may not emerge with the legal title, but just give me 

Lulu Belle for a consolation prize and let the liens fall where they may.

But laying Lulu Belle aside for the nonce (but no longer), leave us get rid of the mortgage first. 

Well, these thoughts occur to me: 

1. Part of the mortgaged premises may be sold free and clear by obtaining the mortgagee's release for that portion, but where the premises are sold in parcels without obtaining releases, the parcels are chargeable for the satisfaction of the mortgage in the inverse order of alienation. If you like this mathematical twist, come back after

Baton Rouge. 

2. A mortgage may be paid off by rendering personal services, where such are called for, and even sometimes where they are not called for. 

Forsooth, did not Simon Legree tear up the mortgage in exchange for a piece of the pie like mother used to make? He did?—not! 

3. If the mortgagee goes about it nice and civilized and buys up the mortgaged premises, what

happens to the mortgage? Answer:-it's still his'n and alive and kicking, but who cares. The point is he may need the mortgage to save the property from subsequent lienors. Get the idea? 

No. Well then we'll go on with No. 4. 

4. A mortgagee need not accept payment before the due date, unless this is specifically provided for since he is entitled to get his interest out of you down to the last decimal. You can't spoil the guy's investment; he only has $2,000,000 so far. Foreclosure.

This is the business, and you get it in one of three ways: 

1. The mortgagee may walk in and say "Scram" in which case the legal title is his after 

20 years, this being the time you get to redeem. 

Isn't it long enough? 

2. The mortgagee may sell the property himself in accordance with the terms of the mortgage. 

3. The mortgagee may hire a lawyer to start the usual foreclosure action, which, ending in a public sale by the Sheriff, affords as effective a dusting-off of yourself, et al, as can be reasonably expected for the money involved. The rules affecting foreclosures are largely statutory. Some of the more general incidents are: (a) All subsequent lienors must be made parties defendant, so that they may know that their liens are about to be cut off. The technical reason is to give the second mortgagee, or what have you, an opportunity to protect his lien by paying off

the first lien himself, something that happens once in a thousand times. (b) The mortgagee is usually compelled to foreclose on the property before suing on the bond or note, on the theory of realizing on what he's got anyway (i. e. the premises) and leaving something for the other fellow. Very charitable, indeed, in theory, but in fact he usually buys in the land for nothing at the Sheriff's sale (which he controls as the moving party) and sues the mortgagor too. Some states, though, permit a decree for the deficiency to be entered at the same time the sale under foreclosure is confirmed. (c) The mortgagee doesn't have to wait with his action until the principal is in default, but can foreclose if the interest alone is past due. The theory here is that nothing is too small to be overlooked. (d) A foreclosure action is usually barred unless brought within 20 years after the default and where the mortgagor is in possession and has not recognized the mortgage in any way. This should certainly be long enough. (e) A receiver will be appointed to collect rents, where there are tenants, at the instance of anyone of the mortgagees, in order to grab a few more cents to put in the pot. Of course, the mortgagor gets credit for what is collected (less a lawyer's fee for the fun of collecting). (f) It is the usual thing to provide that if the mortgagor fails to pay taxes, water rents and insurance, then the principal becomes due immediately.

(g) A purchaser who has "assumed the mortgage" is on the spot too, and can be held for any balance still due after the foreclosure sale. (h) Any surplus (ha, ha!) realized at a foreclosure sale would go to the next junior lienor. (i) The mortgagor's title is sometimes referred to as a right of redemption, which means his right to pay off the mortgage. The right exists until cut off by the court's final decree confirming a sale of the premises. 

A "strict foreclosure" is another way of giving the mortgagor the business. Under this method, there is no sale, but all liens subsequent to the foreclosing one, and including the mortgagor and his right of redemption, are all given the bum's rush by order of the court.

LEASES (OR THE RELATIONSHIP OF LANDLORD AND TENANT). 

A lease is a conveyance of real estate for a limited period usually made on the consideration of the payment of rent to the lesser. It may be oral or in writing, but most states require that it be in writing if the period is for more than three years. It is also called, by the law, a letting or a demise, and sometimes other things too, by the lessee when the spring showers drip into his coffee and when some of Nature's multi-legged insects. come to share his downy couch.

Different Species of Tenancies. 

Lease for life—No, fair reader, this is neither a jail sentence nor a marriage, but a demise of premises for the life (usually) of the lessee. 

Lease for years, or from year to year-This is the usual tenure of the great American nomad. 

Here today-gone tomorrow.

Lease at will-This is a name given to screwy arrangements, whereby or whereunder as the case may be, the landlord can give the tenant the bounce any old time. A typical situation is one where the landlord lets the tenant occupy the premises rent free, but where the disposition has not attained the dignity of an actual lease. 

Tenancy at sufferance-This is where the tenant holds over after the termination of his lease. 

The landlord can elect to hold him for a new lease or to treat him as a trespasser and give him the shoe.

Demise of lodgings-The letting of rooms does not usually acquire the status of a lease, since the tenant or lodger acquires no interest in real estate, but only a contractual right to use certain premises. A lodger is the lowest form of tenant.

Who May Make a Lease. 

A lease of an infant (an infant being anyone under the age of twenty-one) is voidable by him. 

Ah, sweet indulgence to youth! But the fun of being indiscreet ends at twenty-one and if the lad continues with the arrangement thereafter, he is bound.

A lease of a person of unsound mind is void or voidable depending on how unsound his mind actually was at the time. If the person is completely blotto, his acts naturally have no legal consequences; but, if he only wears his Napoleon hat once in a while, he may choose to avoid his obligations, though he may actually be smarter than you or I. It doesn't seem quite fair.

A married woman is bound by her lease and no longer gets any special consideration. The married woman nowadays is just another citizen who happened to take part in a ceremony at which she didn't promise to honor and obey, but just promote some guy for a while. All very unimportant in our world of push and grab.

The ordinary business categories, as to who may make a lease, may be thus stated: 

A tenant may sublet, unless his prime lease forbids it; one partner may lease partnership property for all the partners; a mortagor's lease is valid but is subject to the mortgage; a corporation lease must be approved by the directors; a trustee's authority depends on the terms of his appointment and an executor's on the provisions of the will; the lease of an administrator or guardian must be approved by a court order; an agent's lease must be within the powers contained in his appointment; and the lease of an alien is generally recognized without statutory enactment. 

Formal Parts of Lease. 

According to the cases, a lease, being a demise of real estate for a limited period, is personal property, and may be oral or written. The fact

that a lease is legally considered personal property is of prime importance from the standpoint of taxation and distribution of property on death, but just why an estate for ten years is personalty when an estate forever is realty, is just another one of those things. Snap your G-string, Millie, and let's proceed to the next hitch. 

An oral lease takes effect on the lessee's taking possession of the premises and a written lease on the lessee's accepting delivery of the instrument. 

A lease, usually containing covenants to be performed by the lessee, is ordinarily signed by both parties, though the lessee is bound by his promises on his acceptance of delivery, even if he did not sign.

The term commences with the date of the lease, but if the date is omitted the term starts from the time of delivery.

The names of the parties need not be exact and though consideration is necessary it need not be rent. But if neither consideration nor rent is specified, a reasonable rent will be implied. 

And whereas no particular or technical words are necessary the parties and the premises must be sufficiently identified so that the average person can tell who is leasing what to whom. 

And whereas the lessee may never have graduated from P. S. 8, he must understand that after all he only gets what the lease gives him. Thus if the lease merely refers to the third floor rear of the premises located at 462 Madison Avenue, he can't complain if he doesn't get a view of the park; and though the dump were jokingly de-

scribed as equipped with all modern improvements, including running water, he may have to settle for murals in the lobby depicting "Twilight in the Ozarks" and nightly concerts on a playerpiano by the maestro upstairs. And hot water and janitor service he can write off under the caption of "C'est la guerre".

An easement over premises leased is sometimes reserved by the lessor, as, for instance, a way to be used by his cattle to go to pasture or a path for himself to the backhouse, though it was held in the early case of Sand vs. Kingscate, 6 M. and W. 

189, that the lessor could only use the path across the lessee's land to the backhouse at reasonable times and upon request, a condition that might prove embarrassing under certain circumstances.

To summarize, and lest you think that a lease is something that can be scribbled on the back of an envelope, permit me to state that the better practice (outside of hiring an attorney) requires that the lease contain a date, the names of the parties, an accurate description of the premises let, the term (giving the dates of commencement and termination), the amount of rent, the terms of payment and the signatures of both parties. So now you know! Legality.

The cases say that a lease is void if the lessor knows in advance that the property is to be put to an illegal use, such as the establishment of a place d'etre ("joint" to you) where Millie G. String and her co-workers can busy themselves at what

Kipling defined as "the oldest profession known to man"; but you and I and the desk Sargeant at 

Headquarters know that these places operate in all our better cities and not rent-free either. So, the landlord and tenant in this situation (to say nothing of the Sargeant at the desk at Headquarters) are just realists, seems if. 

Then, too, a lease can be cancelled for fraud, duress or mutual mistake, and the remedy for the lessee is to apply to the equity court for recission. But the fraud must be substantial. Thus it may be O. K. for the landlord to lie about little things such as bed-bugs and cock-roaches, but if the place is infested with ten foot snakes that didn't come out of a bottle, the landlord can't get away with it.

The same rule applies to duress and mutual mistakes. Duress doesn't mean a slight nudgeit means the business. And mutual mistake is, for example, where both parties assumed the building on the premises to be a jive dive when in fact it was a pickleworks. If they thought it was a sausage factory but it turned out to be a boloney plant, the difference wouldn't seem to be material. 

Incidents of a Lease. 

A lease can be assigned and the leasehold can be mortgaged, but the lessee remains bound by his original obligations; and a provision for removal means just what it seems to mean, which might surprise some people.

A holdover tenant is one who doesn't know when to get out. Since he failed to vacate the

landlord's property when the lease ran out, he's in the frying-pan as far the landlord is concerned. 

The landlord can either toss him out or turn him over on the other side, in which event the tenant is in for a second (and similar) term. But in this latter sad turn of events, the tenant is entitled to his second sizzle in toto, and can justly complain if the landlord tries to toss him out when he's only half done.

Both landlord and tenant can sue to prevent damage to their respective estates, but in a suit by the landlord versus the tenant, the tenant cannot question the landlord's title to the premises, since the tenancy is presumed to be still in existence.

The landlord may assign the rents or sell the premises, but the purchaser takes subject to the lease.

If the tenant takes a powder on the rent, the landlord can seize the tenant's furniture and hold it as security, and, by statute in most states, can also sell the furniture and apply the proceeds to the debt.

The relationship, in fact, is a status characterized by relative indestructability. The incident of being at one end of the landlord-tenant axis rather than the other is a fact pungent with implications social as well as legal. 

Option to purchase.

This should be insisted upon by every tenant, since it prevents a sale of the premises to some third party who might refuse to renew the lease

on its expiration. It is customary in many forms of business leases, and is favored by lessees who don't think enough of the location or neighborhood to buy property outright, but want the right to do so if their first guess turns out to have been wrong. It is better practice if the price be set forth in the option, but since so many people are hypersensitive on the subject of telling the world (and their competitors) what they pay for things and therefore leave it out, a failure to state a price will usually be construed to mean a reasonable price. And how do you tell what is reasonable? 

Come around next year, Bub; I take that up in the advanced course.

Termination. 

Most leases are not terminated-they just expire. Comes a certain day in September, the tenant moves out his furniture, his mother-in-law and his rubber plant, puts his hat on his head and leaves the key in the lock, and that is that. He neither gives nor gets any notice unless the lease specifically calls for it, in which case it must be given in writing. The lease just ends according to its own provisions.

But there are instances in which a lease is terminated, to wit: 

1. The parties thereto just tear it up the roughnecks. 

2. The landlord, being a dirty crook, destroys the lease, if it is unrecorded, by selling the premises to a third party who has no knowledge of the lease.

3. If the tenant is evicted by court action, the lease is kaput. 

4. If the state condemns part of the premises, whether or not the lease is terminated depends on the relative importance of what the state took. 

Thus, if the state put a super highway through the middle of Phizzlestick's Pickle Works so that the landlord got the pickles and the tenant the works, the lease would be a house divided against itself and not worth a pretzel to the trade. 

5. The landlord can usually elect to terminate the lease for: (a) Illegal use of premises. So long Lulu Gal! (b) Tenant's failure to repair, when he agreed to do so. No, Joe, you can't store your spuds in the vestibule when you got a bath-tub you can use. (c) Tenant's failure to pay taxes, when this is his obligation under the lease. (d) Non-payment of rent. However, it should be noted in this connection, that this is a case of dealer's choice and that the dealer is the landlord. 

Enjoyment and Use of Leased Premises.

Everything that is reasonably essential to the enjoyment of leased premises, passes under a lease as an incident to them. Thus, the lease of a room with a window (a not unheard of demise in our democracy) implies the right to look through the window and the landlord cannot block it up; nor can he close up an alley onto which leased premises open and a lease of part of the premises implies a means of entrance and exit over the remaining or other parts of the premises, if there

is no other way in and out. It's the rule of ne plus ultra, or don't fence me in. Also, if you lease a building, the land is thrown in for the building to rest upon, and if you lease the land the building goes with it in accordance with the well known receipt for serving poached eggs.

On the other hand, the tenant takes the premises "as is", there being no warranty implied that the premises are fit for habitation, nor even that they are suitable for the purposes of the lease. By the lease the tenant gets the premises located at 123 Sourpuss Lane but the fact that said premises have no more fixity than Chief Featherduster's tent, which is little, and any more modern plumbing than Noah's Ark, which is none, is quite unimportant. And if he can't use the dump for the

Cafe de Luxe as he intended, maybe he can use it to keep chickens in instead-who cares.

In addition to worrying about what he didn't get, the tenant must also shed a tear for something he usually does get, namely and to wit, a list of restrictions as to what he can't do with the property all neatly set forth in the lease and all absolutely binding. This is the law and illustrates what fun it is to be a landlord.

Repairs, Improvements, etc. 

As you can probably guess from the foregoing, the landlord is under no duty to make repairs, in the absence of an express provision in the lease (and, of course, there almost never is). Furthermore, a promise made by the landlord after the signing of the lease, to fix this, that and the other thing, is a nudum pactum, as we lawyers say, in

describing a jumble of words that add up to nothing. 

On the other hand, if the tenant puts a few nails in the barn door, installs a new holder for the Sears Roebuck catalogue in the backhouse, or even builds a new chickencoop, these improvements are just works of love and belong to the landlord and all for free.

The tenant can even be stuck with the taxes and insurance, if the lease so provides, though otherwise they are the landlord's obligation. 

About the only break the tenant gets is that upon termination of the tenancy, he is accorded the right of ingress, egress and regress to remove his personal property, meaning his Sears Roebuck catalogue.

Injuries from Defective Conditions. 

Strange as it may seem, there is one obligation that belongs to the landlord alone, and that is to keep in safe condition those portions of the premises retained for the common use of the tenants. 

This applies to any part of the premises under the landlord's control such as common hallways, stairways, piping, and even the roof. But for the landlord to be liable for defective conditions, he must have known of the conditions, or they must have existed sufficiently long so that he should have known. Since the only thing a landlord will ever admit knowing is the date the rent is due, the injured party is usually forced to fall back on proof of the length of time the condition existed. But proof of this sort is extremely difficult to obtain with the result that many an unworthy

landlord escapes his just deserts by hiding between the comma and the semi-colon of the legal rule of "whereas, but and if".

On the other hand, if the landlord, in an unguarded moment, undertakes to do some repairing, he becomes liable for injuries resulting from a failure to repair properly, although he was under no obligation to repair in the first place. The tenant is liable for rent so long as he occupies the landlord's premises even in the absence of an express undertaking. It is compensation for the use of land but the obligation to pay it ceases when: 

1. The tenant is evicted by court action. 

2. The tenant moves out voluntarily but because the landlord has failed to comply with a covenant to furnish heat or to make some major repair.

This latter is called constructive eviction, but it should be emphasized that the premises must actually be in an uninhabitable condition and the tenant must actually move out. He can't decide to stay and make the best of it and also not pay the rent.

4. The premises are destroyed by an act of

ACKNOWLEDGMENTS AND PROOFS. 

These are touches of ceremonial hotsy-totsy accompanying the simple function of signing an instrument and are required by statute in most states before the instrument can be recorded. 

An acknowledgment is a statement in writing made before an officer of the state authorized to take oaths, that Whitney Whistlestop, the signer, is really Whitney Whistlestop, the signer, after all. Surprise! It is like tying a granny-knot twice, and, of course, its function is to prevent a slip.

A proof is usually the form of identification of signatures used for corporations, and is a statement under oath that the proper officers did the signing for the corporation.

As above stated this rigamaroll is required only for recording purposes and adds nothing to the validity of the instrument.

The recording of instruments is a function of the state, through its county offices, whereby a record of the instrument is made, after which all the world is supposed to know that Whitney 

Whistlestop is no longer owner of the property on Sourpuss Lane. The effect of recording is to prevent Whistlestop, in one of his off moments, from committing fraud by making a second sale of the same property to another sucker. One swindle is all right, it appears; but after that it's illegal.

The rules and procedure with respect to acknowledgments, proofs and recording apply to every instrument of every sort having to do with real estate.
