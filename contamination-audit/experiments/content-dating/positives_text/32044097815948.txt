STATUTORY RECODIFICATION IN MASSACHUSETTS History of Recodification 

Statute law in Massachusetts is among the oldest in the country. 

For many years before the adoption of the Massachusetts Constitution in 1780, laws were enacted at successive legislative sessions. When the new Constitution was adopted it did not eliminate these laws; instead, it expressly provided that both the statutes and the common law in effect prior to 1780, which were not repugnant to the Constitution, should remain in force until altered or repealed by the Legislature. (Mass. Const., Part II, c. VI, Art. VI.) 

In the succeeding 170 years up to the present, an increasing volume of statutes has been enacted annually. As early as 1832, however, the mass of statutory laws had become bulky, scattered and obscure. A commission was therefore then appointed to revise, collate and arrange the general statutes. Its work resulted in the Revised Statutes which were enacted and published in 1836. 

The commission's most singular accomplishment was the general scheme of classification and arrangement which it devised for the presentation of the Revised Statutes. The division of our statute law into parts, titles, chapters and sections dates from the single volume of Revised Statutes which was published at that time.

Inevitably, subsequent revisions became necessary periodically.

The incomplete work of the commission would be continued by a new law revision office, according to current Senate, No. 125, proposed by Senators

This bill states that an office of law revision counsel is essential to the modern legislative process, and proposes appointment of a law revision counsel and an assistant law revision counsel, skilled in this technical field, by the Rules Committees, acting concurrently.

Procedure of Official Revision Commissions of revision.

The resolves which led to the creation of all past revision commissions, are similar in content. A generally uniform approach has evolved in the task 

Broadly, the first step has been collating all subsequent statutes with the last officially enacted revision. The second step is to check collated laws to date to discover and correct errors, inconsistencies, ambiguities, and to supply omissions and remove obsolete provisions. The last step is to edit and arrange the entire body of statute for publication. 

During this comparatively lengthy process the commission submits to the General Court preliminary annual reports in the form of bills, with annotations, containing all recommended changes of a substantive nature. (The only occasion upon which a state legislator may receive compensation for service upon a recess

committee is in connection with revision of the general statutes.) (Mass.

Const. Amendments, Art. LXV.) 

The final report of the commission usually contains a general body of revised statutory law which is recommended for enactment, as well as a table of specific repeals of those existing laws which are being merged in the recommended revision. This final report is referred to a legislative recess committee and then takes the course of an ordinary bill-hearing, committee report, debate and action by the two legislative branches, and approval by the 

Governor. 

With the exception of the work of the first commission of 1832-1846, statutory revision in Massachusetts has been generally conservative in nature in order to avoid any infringement of the legislative prerogative to create new law. Only where compelling reasons dictated the need for changes in statutory provisions have changes been recommended.

Revision Commission of 1948 (a) Authorization of Commission Work 

The resolve of 1948 creating this commission (c.94) specifically directed that the laws from December 22, 1920 to the date of the commission's final report be revised, recodified, consolidated and arranged. Notwithstanding these broad powers, the commissioners only recommended substantive changes in the General Laws when such changes were deemed necessary to remedy defects in the existing law or when the commission believed repeal of provisions of laws to be necessary to correct unconstitutional, obsolete or redundant statutes. 

Any substantive improvement of particular laws were judged to be exclusively a matter of policy and a legislative function.

Commissioners (c) Work Method of Commission 

This 1948 commission adopted a two-pronged approach for its work as follows: 

1. Locating those places in the statutes where substantive changes must be made to eliminate defects arising from inconsistencies, ambiguities, mistakes and omissions.

Such work was the basis of its preliminary report.

2. Detailed editing of the entire body of statutory law in a final report to serve as the basis for enactment and publication of all existing statutes. 

The commission wrote each executive agency, department, or division of the state to request notification of any defects in the laws relating to administration of their departments. Similar requests were made of judges and clerks of courts, registers of deeds and registers of probate, and bar associations and legal societies.

Many of the recommendations received were beyond the scope of the commission. 

Others were incorporated in the final report after consultation and discussion with persons responsible for enforcement and administration of the laws. (d) Reports Issued by Commission 

1. Preliminary Report. This three-part report (of legislative document size) contained the substantive changes in the statute law proposed by the commission. Parts I (1950; 49 pp.) and II (1951; 118 pp.) described the history and methods of the previous and current commissions. Part III (1953; 342 pp.) contained further recommended changes in substantive law, and summarized commission accomplishments. 2. 

Special Reports. The commission published a 15-page report in 1952 which explained the adverse effects of the spasmodic financial support which had been extended to the commission. It distinguished in detail between the revision originally called for and the mere compilation subsequently required (under a 1951 resolve, c. 33). A 16-page report in 1954 reviewed the early efforts of the commission, stated its accomplishments and outlined its remaining work. 

3. Final Report. In February 1953, the commission completed and delivered to the Joint Committee on the Judiciary five-hundred copies of its revision of the General Laws in 4-volume sets, compiled through Jan. 1, 1953. A supplement

including all changes since Jan. 1, 1953 was also prepared separately. (1) 

The commission has listed its accomplishments aside from the four above volumes as follows: 

(1) Completion of a statutory history of every section in the General Laws throughout the Acts of 1953. (2) Listing all citations since 1932 of cases which interpret each section of the General Laws through page 728 of the 1953 Advance 

Sheets of the Massachusetts Reports. 

(3) Completion on cards of a cross index of all references within the General Laws to other sections or chapters. 

The commission emphasized that a revision project requires the sustained efforts of a trained staff and should not be subject to interruptions or changes It suggested two alternative plans for extending the life of the in policy. commission. 

1. Provide that Senate and House Counsel assume the work (under G. L. c. 3, s. 53, as amended, which provides that the Concurrent Committees on Rules can direct statutory revision in whole, chapter by chapter, or otherwise). 

2. Provide for appointment of a single Revisor of Statutes either on a temporary or a permanent basis. (1) The Final Report was prepared in this manner: Amended Laws were separately extracted from the annual session laws (known as the "blue books"). General 

Laws were extracted from a copy of the 1932 Tercentenary edition. The annual laws were pasted in proper place and order among the General Laws with inked citations written in the margins. These pasted extracts were then reproduced by an economical "Xerox" process which produced a final product making all revisions readily apparent and thus easy to examine for approval or disapproval without prior expensive setting of type. The Report was given "next annual session" by the Committee on the Judiciary and was accepted in concurrence May 27, 1954.

Alternative Means of Recodification 

While the practice in the past has been to create a special revision commission to bring the General Laws of the Commonwealth up-to-date, alternative means of accomplishing this task have been urged by persons whose work requires frequent reference to the General Laws. They argue that official revisions are so outdated that a hopeless situation would exist if it were not for the fact that a continuous, annotated revision of the General Laws, printed and distributed by a private publisher, is available for consultation; this latter revision however, does not have the official certification of the Legislature. 

To bring about an official, current revision of the General Laws, at least four methods have been suggested at one time or another: (1) contracting with a private law publisher who would prepare a revised officially certified edition of the General Laws and keep it current on an annual basis; (2) directing House or Senate Counsel to make a continual revision as provided in G. L., c. 3, s. 53 as amended; (3) establishing a permanent Division of Law Revision in the Department of the Secretary of State; (4) creating a permanent office of Law Revision 

Counsel directly responsible to the General Court. 

(a) Private Law Publishing Firm 

An annotated set of the Massachusetts General Laws, is currently being revised, printed and distributed by the Lawyers Cooperative Publishing Company on a subscription basis for members of the bench and bar and others. This company is one of two major law publishing firms in the country, the other being the West Publishing Company of St. Paul, Minnesota. The Lawyers Cooperative publication, "Annotated Laws of Massachusetts" contains the full text of the General Laws of Massachusetts as embodied in the Tercentenary Edition, together with all subsequent amendments and additions thereto. Annotations add the interpretations of the Statutes presented in judicial opinions arising from litigation.

Opinions of the Attorney General and federal cases construing Massachusetts statutes are also included. Each volume contains an index of its contents; in addition, a General Index is printed with cross references. A cumulative pocket supplement to each volume is printed annually and occasional pamphlet supplements are printed in the interim to keep abreast of the latest amendments to the General Laws and the latest judicial interpretation thereof. 

This invaluable legal tool has the disadvantage of not being recognized for judicial purposes or for certifications. Thus when the 1948 revision commission was authorized, the Lawyers Cooperative offered to cooperate in the task of revision but this offer was refused, partly because it was the intent of the Legislature to distribute the work among Massachusetts lawyers. Those familiar with the labors of the 1948 commission contend that this decision to give the work to local lawyers resulted in the burden of revision falling upon the shoulders of two or three individuals, since most of the assistants employed by the commission were young lawyers, inexperienced with the technical work of law revision. On the other hand, the Lawyers Cooperative of Rochester, New York, is said to have a trained staff of Massachusetts lawyers, long familiar with the techniques of Massachusetts law revision.

Another factor not to be overlooked is the cost of printing. Lawyer s experienced in this field of law revision estimate that a revision by an experienced law publisher could eliminate as much as 30% of the current text of the "blue books" of annual Massachusetts statutes. A private publisher undertaking this task would print the final product; he would provide, at little or no cost to the Commonwealth, a sufficient number of sets to take care of official distribution.

The Lawyers Cooperative, considered to be equally competent to the West Publishing Company, its major competitor, nevertheless makes smaller charges

for its service than the West Company. Thus, Lawyers Cooperative publication, Annotated Laws of Massachusetts, which consists of 21 books, can now be purchased for $128.00; whereas it is estimated that a similar publication by the 

West Company would probably cost an additional $100. 

Revision of the General Laws by private companies would not be an innovation. This arrangement is carried on in other states. Moreover, in New York 

State two competing private publishers sell subscriptions and provide certified, annotated sets of printed New York Revised Statutes. The Lawyers Cooperative recently completed revisions of both New York Statutes and New Hampshire Statutes, working with respective state revision commissions; the basic revisions will be supplemented with cumulative annotations annually or as needed. 

If a private publishing firm takes over the printing and distribution of revised editions of the General Laws, Wright and Potter Printing Company would probably be eliminated from the picture. 

Distribution of public documents, by the State Secretary, would then be limited in this case to supplying state officials and state agencies. (In this connection it is of interest that an unpublished survey made by the "Baby Hoover" Commission in 1951 showed that the State Secretary still had on sale 

2,073 sets of Volumes 1 and 2 of the 1932 Tercentenary Edition of the General 

Laws, at $13.00 per set; and 2,081 Volumes of the Tercentenary Index at $7.00 per volume.

This large residue reflects the lack of public demand for long outdated volumes.) 

(b) House or Senate Counsel 

The General Laws of the Commonwealth (c. 3, s. 53) provide that revision of the General Laws may be accomplished by either House or Senate Counsel under the direction of the Committees on Rules, or of the General Court. The nature of

the duties imposed upon the offices of Senate and House Counsel in the past, however, has made it virtually impossible to assume this task on a continuing basis. (c) Department of the Secretary of State

There has been outspoken opposition by the State Secretary to the past methods of periodically creating a revision commission to consolidate statute law. 

The Secretary has characterized this method as "archaic, cumbersome, extravagant, inefficient and unsatisfactory", adding, "The expense is great and frequently the ..1 finished product is obsolete in large part within a year of its compilation. 

The Secretary contends that consolidation, if done currently by a division of compilation within his department, would result in considerable economy. Such a division would be headed by an attorney who is expert in the field of Statute Law and judicial interpretation thereof, and who would be aided by a trained assistant and a small secretarial staff. This division could prepare pocket or supplementary annual volumes, as is done with the present private and unofficial Annotated Laws of Massachusetts. The Secretary estimated in a 1954 report (House, No. 75, p. 6) that this division could operate, with cooperation from House and 

Senate Counsel, at a maximum cost of $18,000 per year. As indicated above, the recent Revision Commission has thus far spent $505,224 in six years.
