PREFACE 

There appears to be no extant compendium on the general subject of the History of Building Trades Labor in 

Massachusetts. The building crafts are so incorporated or allied with other labor movements so that their identity is lost, or there is an actual paucity of information due to miscellaneous growth and gradual accumulation of steps forward and battles won.

The purpose of this book is, through research of existing records, documents, conversations with labor leaders and commissioners, actual experience in the employment and direction of labor, and from current press and periodical, to accumulate and herewith present a His tory of Building Trades Labor in Massachusetts.

INTRODUCTION 

The development of building trades labor organizations and employers associations cannot be of other than interest to those associated directly or indirectly with industry, and, perhaps, particularly to one who tries to be its historian. Prior to 1836 there was little, if any, building trades unionism in Massachusetts as it now exists. Certainly there was no effect on the building trades industry of Boston and upon the individual members of that trade then, or about that time, as there is today. Neither is the action of those times tangent to the outer or inner circles of industrial and labor organizations as it is today. 

So far as the building trades unions are concerned they present splendid examples of the policies of organizations of skilled workmen attaining results. Perhaps in the past twenty-five years machinery has made tremendous inroads into the province of the hand worker, yet less to the building trades artisan than to any similar workman, such as the automobile factory worker. In elaboration of that statement, there is still no method of laying bricks that differs materially from that used in Babylon more than two thousand years ago. Within those same years construction has led up to a personal relationship between employer and employee that I shall show later is of special significance. In all disputes covering wages, hours, piece-rates, and safety factors, the 

Building Trades Employers Association of Boston and the 

United Building Trades Council stand to the forefront.

The Building Trades Employers Association is an organization of of associations of employers, consisting of general contractors and subcontractors. The 

United Building Trades Council is composed of unions or councils of the various crafts in the building trades, com-

prising representatives from thirty-eight local unions which, in turn, represent twenty-eight distinct groups. 

The Association is composed of fourteen bodies of employers of the various crafts and one organization of general contractors. Nevertheless, those two organizations, important as they are, do not compose the whole picture. Schisms exist. 

Particular unions pursue their own activities so that the Council does not always find it easy to represent itself to the opposition as the spokesman of the industry as a whole. For example, the carpenters are not members of the Council, preferring to deal directly with the Employers Association in their own behalf. Similarly, some members of the Building Trades Employers Association are apparently not in full accord with the activities of their parent body, and in many cases make it a point to take care of their own labor problems. Then there are both associations of employers and associations of employees which are not connected with any central body whatsoever.

The interrelations between these various bodies sometimes lead to confusion, especially so in view of marked changes which have taken place in the actual performance of labor in the field as well as in the shop, and in the determination of what class of workmen shall perform any given operation of labor and with what material. 

This is the great weakness of unionism as is admitted by most professional union men, and by "professional" I mean men who devote their full time to the cause of labor. 

This is the so-called "jurisdictional" dispute. For instance, both tile setters and carpenters claim the right to erect metal tile. Metal tile is a facsimile of ceramic tile consisting of pressed steel plates in tile form with baked-on enamel to produce the simulated surface of true tile. Be-

The net result is nobody puts them up. If one trade persists, the other trade goes on strike, which eventually will, in all probability, cause cessation of construction at the particular site.

The process for the amelioration of such a dispute is weak and, indeed, has its authority questioned by some of the trade organizations and denied by others. Even when jurisdiction is granted, by the time the decision for the particular construction job in question is handed down, a delay, costly both to labor and capital, has been experienced, or the material has been rejected by the architect and owner, and a substitution enforced. This in turn, of course, passed on grief to some industry which is usually a "baby" industry. It is a "baby" industry in part because its material is not sufficiently well known to have acquired a trade agreement as to its erection. 

Building is the most highly organized industry in Massachusetts so far as the local union branches are concerned. In 1922 there was a total of 354 unions with a membership of 43,240. In Massachusetts the most important members of the building trades consists of the carpenters, who have 124 unions and a membership of 

19,190, with the painters second with 60 local unions and a membership of 6,745. The carpenters have 20 district councils in this state, while the painters have six. Third come the plumbers with 48 locals and 4,080 members.

Obviously these figures are correct only for some given time, and as presented here are for 1922, according to the 

Massachusetts Labor Bulletin. 

In 1919 when the General Agreement first went into effect there were about 23,000 men in the various building trades unions in Boston representing almost one-sixth of

xii 

the total organized wage earners in the entire city. While in Boston there is substantially a "closed shop," nevertheless it is limited to the construction operations of State and Municipal entities and those of the Public Utilities and great corporations serving the public in some degree, such as the great department stores. On small residential work, repair work in general, and work where other unions of organized labor are not represented, the "open shop" maintains. The main difference between "closed shop" and "open shop" is that the first is all union, and the second is non or part union. 

The building trades industry, in comparison with other industries in this nation, may be said to have a very stable character in its organization. It has been subject to much less change than other industries by the advent of labor-saving machinery and labor-saving construction methods. However, these two factors have to a great degree influenced the technology of the industry both as regard to methods and materials, so far as the worker himself is concerned, and, almost equally, in the case of the employer.

Years ago the employer, or contractor, was practically always a skilled artisan, probably a carpenter or mason, graduated from a daily workman into an employer. There are many contractors today who have never had a tool in their hands, for pay. They are brokers. 

Laws have even been passed in Massachusetts stipulating that a contractor, to qualify for various kinds of public work, must actually perform a given percentage of the whole with his own organization. This provision calls for 30 per cent of the work to be done by the general contractor, but it is honored more in the breach than in the observance. The policing of it is practically nil.

While the machine may displace the individual from a particular job or sequence of jobs, in the long run it will provide more work for more men because of the reduction in the time required to complete a job and in the cost of construction, and because in its own being men have to be employed to manufacture it. Consider, for example, the steam shovel. For a basic heavy industry of the nation there is still more hand work performed in the building trades than probably in any other industry of any nature approximating equal importance. This has served to react upon labor relations in the building trades industry and upon the disputes which have arisen between worker and employer in the matter of hours, rules of work, apprenticeship, jurisdiction, and so on. 

A weakness of the building trades unionism would seem to be shown by their inattention to the apprentice problem. The number of apprentices to any given shop per trade is so restricted that the normal increment in new blood in any given craft is insufficient. Of course, the union viewpoint on this matter is: the fewer men the greater the opportunity for work per man. 

A strength of unionism has been exemplified by electricians and plumbers who have a board set up in many states, including Massachusetts, through which licenses are issued to workers in their particular rank: the helper, the journeyman, and perhaps the master mechanic.

This automatically gives them a status, and the public has an opportunity to judge the value, and pay accordingly for the performance. In other trades, as carpentry or masonry, if the man is not an accredited union man, he is to be taken upon representation of his own rating until the difference is known.

Many years ago the mason and carpenter were, and

Either was a trade that had to be learned through years of application and study and presupposed a grounding in mensuration, beyond common education, and an ability to think and visualize not necessary in other trades.

About 1900 structural steel began in common practice to displace masonry and wood, and upon its heels trod, comparatively recently, reinforced concrete in its march into general use. This made possible the building of structures for which the old materials were not suitable and gave a more daring aspect to structural design. 

This has meant the introduction of new methods and equipment, and has changed labors of the building workers to a great degree. A splendid example of these two possibilities in the change by design influencing materials and labor are shown in Boston by the Ames Building and the Berkeley Building respectively. The 

Ames Building is the last of the big office buildings where the stone work in the exterior walls is a structural necessity, hence the fenestration is limited, as against the Berkeley Building which still stands as one of the buildings with less outside masonry walls than most others.

This had a further application to the pneumatic hammer in riveting and cutting away old masonry and concrete in addition and alteration work. Other examples possible to cite are

the hoisting engine, the steam shovel, Brown hoist, the long-boom derrick, freight elevators, concrete towers, caisson work, and work under varying atmospheres. This introduction of machinery went further back than the erection work of the building itself. It affected, also, the preliminary work done in factories and mills supplying materials used in construction. Many operations formerly done at the job itself are now completed and shipped ready to erect from some far-away mill, even though they arrive "knocked down" and require the last steps in erection.

Wood, stone, and brick, the old basic materials, are still in modern use, but even this use is specialized to meet the competition of new materials. Metal trim for base, chair rail, and picture moulding, has displaced wood. 

Hollow tile backing-up has displaced brick. This has resulted in the growth of large-scale production at the factory with the resultant application of factory methods and the displacement, to a certain degree which may be expected to increase rather than decrease of the hand worker on the job itself. Even the laborer, other than the skilled artisan in building, has felt the effect of this, but there is still no possible way that a mason can be served, an excavation can be trimmed, a plasterer can get his plaster, other than by the manual effort of the helper or laborer. There is substantially no difference between the helper and the laborer except in nomenclature, other than that perhaps the helper is associated largely with plumbing, electricity, or heating.

These various developments have meant changes in the relationship of the worker to his work. The large projects will require special hand work at all times. There is a definite limit to the standardization of plans and the manufacture of parts or units for the various trades. The

xvi 

greatest change is that all-round skill does not fit in with modern methods. There is specialization in performance. There is an increased number in the kinds of work to be performed, and this subjects the whole membership of unions and the requirements for union admission to new thoughts. A minor causative factor of ill will between trades lies in the seasonal character of the industry. Further, great construction companies must send men from one city to another which makes inroads upon the solid front unionism may present in any given city. This makes clear the need for national co-ordination among the unions in order to meet the operations of big construction companies as well as national employers associations.

The large employer of labor, the great general contractor, is neither a leader in industry's pace nor a menace to the growth and success of unions. In fact, it is the other way, so far as the menace is concerned. On small house erection requiring little technical training or capital the field is full of small contractors operating on the proverbial shoe string who have not sufficient stability, or enough to lose, so that it is practically impossible to keep them in line on the observance of union rules. 

Out of this welter of change and variety of conditions comes diversity of interest that makes it very difficult to secure co-operation in the building industry. As a result, difficulties arise over trade agreements, and upon the insistence of "local autonomy." Further, there is ground for jealousy among the various groups, and this presents difficulties when proposals for General Agreements are brought up. Each craft holds jealously the advantage of bargaining for terms for itself. The building trades unions are similar to others in that the common interest of the members is concerned mostly with the conditions of

xvii their own work. Unions are the means by which this interest is promulgated and fostered. The distinction between employer and employee has been drawn clearly, but the condition that the worker should retain control of his working tools, which to some extent separated the construction workers from those of other industries, is becoming less and less observed. The bricklayer still has his trowel and the carpenter his saw, but the separation from the ownership of the tools of production is becoming as complete as the divorce from the control of the sale and price of the product. This has limited the effectiveness of the local unions.

Co-operation with other unions of various crafts in the same locality is important in order to meet competition, and, as a result, there is a tendency in the construction business towards expanding those organizations which unite the unions in a given locality. There is also a marked tendency toward broadening the area of craft organization, one example being that of the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners. This organization has been active in its attempts to control all workers who handle wood or any materials that take the place of wood. In this way organization according to product has been accomplished as contrasted to organization according to process, as it was in the beginning. 

This foregoing introduction briefly outlines the background of the building trades industry among which the trades unions and the various employers groups move. 

There is the national desire for unity on the part of both employer and employee, and this finds expression in the building congresses which attempt to represent all the various elements in the industry. It finds expression also, in the almost constant agitation over trade agreements, hours and rates of wages, jurisdictional au-

xviii 

thority, and kindred points. It is the aim of this book to set forth the history of some of these various organizations which have participated in the construction industry and to comment on the effects on the worker which the growth of trade unionism in Massachusetts has exerted.

The degree of satisfaction, great or small, which members of the building trades industries find in their work today is due, first, to the fight that unionism has made for better working conditions, and, second, less tangibility, perhaps, but equally definitely, to the change in attitude between employer and employee. In direct contrast to the old "sweat shop" idea, labor is no longer looked upon as a chattel by any organization that prides itself upon its name. The days of the padrone are over. 

Padrone may be Anglicized as "protector," but what it really meant was an individual of some enterprise, usually an Italian, who gathered, in quantity, his immigrant compatriots and herded them into battalions of labor. 

He housed these men, fed them, and looked after all their necessities. In return he took their wages and allocated back to them such parts of it as he saw fit after he had deducted the expense, real or alleged, which they had incurred during the week previous. This resulted in great detriment to the men. The padrone never lost.

The decrease in the hours of labor with the accompanying increase in the time available for cultural possibilities cannot but effect society as a whole, and the remaining contemplated steps, now partially realized, of the five day week at the same basic wage rate will be another step in the right direction.

Building trades labor is seriously affected by the lack of continuity of employment caused either by the peculiar weather conditions which exist here, or by the difficulties

xix encountered by the employer in securing one contract after another so that he may keep satisfactory men constantly at work. It may be true that a skilled artisan in the building trades will get one dollar and fifty cents an hour, or twelve dollars a day, or sixty dollars a week, for every day or week he is actually employed, but it is equally true that if he is steadily occupied for thirty-six weeks in a year, he is doing a splendid year's work. The other weeks in the year are spent waiting for construction of the building to arrive at the stage of development when the services of his craft will be needed, for resumption of work held up because of strikes for any causes, lockouts, delay in delivery of materials to the site due to such catastrophes as floods, rain, or extreme cold, or to seeking employment upon the termination of satisfactory service on a completed job. It is only within the past ten years that it has been fairly well demonstrated that outside of heavy falls of snow and the erection under certain conditions of some parts of exterior walls that work may be satisfactorily performed, within given limits and under given conditions, in a temperature heretofore prohibitory. This, of course, reduces the seasonal hazard.

CHAPTER I HISTORY OF THE BUILDING TRADES UNIONS OF BOSTON-1880 

Records till late in the nineteenth century are few, scattered, and far between, in so far as they deal with the history of building trades in Massachusetts and particularly in Boston. Either the daily press did not find it worth while to write more than the dramatic sides of labor disputes, or labor did not find it worth while to issue brochures for the benefit of the public, or historians did not think it worthy of their efforts to make a chronology. 

Occasional references to certain unions and strikes are found, but it is practically impossible to develop a complete and satisfactory picture of the growth of building trade unions as distinct from other labor movements due to this paucity of record. However, the activities of these earlier days have such an important bearing upon later and present developments and conditions, that research has been made into the past, and reliance has been placed upon available and authentic records, and, to a lesser extent, upon the reminiscences of certain hoary-bearded individuals whose memories, I trust, are good. 

Of the general trades that may be considered in any way a part of building, shipbuilders were the first group of workers to organize. Even today there is a marked parallelism between ship-building and large edifice building, both as to its architecture and its craft. These were followed shortly by house carpenters, but the first organization of workers in Massachusetts, banded together in more or less permanent form, were a group of ship-

wrights and caulkers. The purpose of this organization, first and foremost, was to form a union for the support of the workers against domination by two forces, one, capitalists or merchants, who financed the building of ships, and two, the master mechanics who, strange to say, were the immediate employers. Most of the animus was directed, however, against the first named. 

In 1832 there was a strike. A society which called itself the "Society formed by Shipwrights and Caulkers for

Their Mutual Benefit and to Regulate in the Day's 

Work the Number of Hours Constituting Them" backed this strike. (1) In this Society with the long name no person was admitted to membership unless he had at least three years' experience as a shipwright or a caulker. Commensurate with the general policy of all unions, as still holds, it required a three-fourths favorable vote of the members of the existing society before the new member would be admitted. This neophyte paid an initiation fee of five dollars and monthly dues of fifty cents. Today many of the unions have an initiation fee of $250 upon which terms of payment are granted commensurate with occupation. Sickness and death benefits were among the features of this society, and assessments were levied upon the members to make up deficits or as the funds ran low. 

This insurance feature is now noticeably absent from unions due partly to the stringency of insurance laws and some lamentable experiences of those exercising mortality tables of an insufficient spread.

In 1825 there was a strike of the Boston House Carpenters, and this, as well as can be found out, is the first strike of the building trades in Boston. The men struck for shorter hours and higher wages, and this agitation was the forerunner of a movement which occupied building trades union officials at fairly close intervals during

3 the next seventy years. The strike was defeated due largely to the activities of the merchant owners, or the second group previously mentioned, and perhaps to the general inexperience of organized labor in securing their wants. (2) At this time there was extant the twelve-hour day, or the general idea that labor should work from sunup to sundown. This strike mentioned immediately above developed arguments for and against the ten-hour day which became common in the ten-hour movement for a period of about fifty years more. 

This strike is of interest, also, in that it sets forth clearly the economic positions and interests of the three factions involved, namely, the merchants or capitalists, the master builders or contractors, and the journeymen or wage earners. The first, of course, supplied the raw materials and financed the expense of production and, perhaps what is even more important, they marketed the the finished product. The second was subservient in some degree to the first, and the third fellow got along as best he could. could. These merchant contractors and allied owners were firmly against any reduction in hours and put forth bitter opposition to the demands of the workers. 

The number of hours constituting the working day was the general bone of contention. This strike and other strikes of 1832, 1833, 1834, and 1835, were defeated although one slight gain resulted, namely, that two hours were allowed the men at the noon period. Although it is difficult to find, except in legendary form, it is rumored that a gallon of rum per man per week was added as a further inducement. It had an intangible value that was not appreciated for some time, and that was the suggestion to the general public that sunup-sundown hours of labor were unfair even for a horse. 

In February, 1834, a convention was called in Boston

to form the Trades Union of Boston. This was one of thirteen central labor organizations that were formed between the ten-year period of 1830 to 1840. (3) There were fourteen crafts represented, three of which applied to building, namely: masons, painters, and house carpenters. This particular organization, the Trades Union of 

Boston, was short-lived, being organized, perhaps, around no distinct economic interest. It is interesting to note that employers were admitted as well as journeymen. For the next five or six years after 1849 there is little evidence of any important part being played by the Building Trades organization for the shorter hour movement. 

In 1837 there were fifteen years of hard times following the panic of that year. Unemployment was widespread, immigration was at its peak, and labor, therefore, was cheap, giving little active trade unionism in any field of industry. From 1898 to 1907 more than seven milion immigrants came to the United States. Of this number a major fraction cleared through the port of Boston. 

Many stayed in that city. Comparison with this and restrictive laws enacted in 1924 show less than one hundred and seventy-five thousand persons a year are admitted.

A decade before the Civil War the birth of laborsaving machines was accomplished, and the infant began to take strides forward very fast. Such unions as were then active devoted their efforts towards protecting the interests of their members against this seeming menace. Business began to recover in 1850, and what is strange to add is that the activities of the unions seemed to abate in the face of improved conditions. Living costs showed a steady rise, and the unions devoted themselves towards improving conditions in their own individual trades. Agitation sprang up again at this time for higher

5 wages and shorter hours. Agreements between employers and unions at that time were almost unknown, and the strike, always the immediate and economic fallacy, was the sole weapon of the unions. Together with the agitation for shorter hours was the equally aggressive agitation for higher pay. The unions seemed to lessen their activities toward the securing of legislation for the improvement of conditions for their members but remained with the strike. Apparently these were the days when men with spirit and education were not as prevalent in unionism as they are today.

On February 12, 1867, a mass meeting was held in Boston by the master mechanics for the purpose of drawing up a statement of their stand on the matter of working hours. About all the building trades were represented on the committee formed, and the chief result of the meeting was this: "We shall be persistent in exacting ten hours of labor for a six-day week's work." (4)

Organization of unions during this period of ten years from 1857 to 1867 seems to have been either overwhelmed by the difficulties presenting themselves or by the lack of good business organization because a fair parade of unions sprang up only to disappear almost as rapidly as they came into being. lasted until 1916. The Carpenters' Society of Amalgamated Carpenters and Joiners expired in 1914.

One point, perhaps, that has a bearing upon the decease of these sporadic trade unions of the Civil War period was that they were formed for trivial or local reasons or for the support of some particular point of misunderstanding or contention. They often did more than expire: they expired and came to life again when the same points in dispute, or some similar point, came up. However, the original Shipwrights and Caulkers were closely knit and an aggressive group, and their activities influenced the house carpenters in no small degree. (6)

At this time there seems to have come the first vigorous trial for adoption of the eight-hour day. In spots this was successful, but as soon as the union lessened its activities wages were reduced again and hours were lengthened. In 1877 and until the end of the 80's, the Shipwrights' and Caulkers' Union, though short-lived, was one of the most successful in Massachusetts, and organized labor in the building trade owes much to this Union. 

It might be construed from the above statements that trade unions took a long time to get firmly implanted in this state and were common only while the fight was on, success killing them as well as defeat. Prominent among the local unions which had a state organization were the tin plate, copper and sheet iron workers, carpenters and joiners, masons and painters.

In 1865-1866 there was another large strike, resulting in the strengthening of the position of the journeyman who improved, almost in spite of himself, with each new movement and each agitation. A further result of this strike was the establishment of the Master Builders Association and the Boston Building Trades Council. Both

Employers seemed to be able to defeat the unions by transportation of men from the outside. In this importation of labor to break a strike there was neither the idea of “scab” nor the idea of professional strike breaker.

The "scab" is the workman who will have nothing to do with organized labor or the general principles of collective bargaining, who prefers to fend entirely for himself, and who will at any time deal with his own interests with no regard for his fellow workmen. In Massachusetts today it is a crime by statute to call a man a "scab." The sole interests of the strikebreaker, hired usually by some renegade union official, is to get as high pay as possible, due to the emergency condition, and to turn out just as little work as he possibly can. He has as his chief basis of being the fact that he is one of a group of organized thugs who are quite willing to indulge in a brawl at little or no notice. Besides his getting a wage not commensurate with his efforts, a fat fee is paid to the organizer of this group. Strikebreakers are not used so much now as formerly, perhaps because of an innate sense of character or decency, influenced by public opinion.

It was not at all above special employees of a strikebreaker organization to foment strikes among otherwise contented workers so that they could be called into the plants. This type of person, regardless of who hires him or his purpose of action, is known as an "agent provocateur." The Building Trades Council of Boston was founded in 1866 shortly after the formation of the Master Builders Council. By this time, the ten-hour day was a fact; and the aim of the Building Trades Council was to aid the nine-hour movement. It might be interesting to interpolate today's accomplishment of a fairly general eighthour day on a five-day week, and the marked and growing tendency to make this six-hour day on a five-day week. 

It seems totally reasonable that this will come to pass within twenty years, and economists rather disagree with certain premises granted as to whether or not this would be beneficial for society as a whole. 

The interests of trade unionism until 1920 and on could hardly be said to have much thought other than the selfish interests involved or to act upon a philanthropic plan. When a man has only enough for bare existence he is hardly likely to beam upon mankind. The United 

Brotherhood of Painters, Decorators and Paper Hangers came into being in 1887, following the Brotherhood of 

Carpenters and Joiners in 1881. Building trades in

Boston were busy. It was a time for organization. Even though the number of crafts represented in the Council was small, and many kept drifting in and out, nevertheless some accomplishment was made. The men were organization-minded from a selfish point of view. In other words, craft separatism and exclusiveness, portrayed even today by the Bricklayers, showed itself prominently. A combination was effected, with the efforts of the Master

Builders Association, which resulted in an agreement between subsidiaries, namely, the bricklayers, stone masons, and building trades laborers. This was a severe blow to the strength of the Buildings Trades Council as unified action was difficult, if not impossible, without the support of these basic masons trades. Consequently, from 

1886 to 1903, when the bricklayers repudiated their agreement with the Master Builders Association, many of the activities of the Building Trades Council were definitely of a minor nature.

Additional obstacles to the successful operation of the Council were encountered when the Carpenters District affiliated with the subsidiary of the Master Builders 

Association, namely the Master Carpenters Association.

This affiliation lasted until 1908. There is a question how much of this was on the old principle of divide and conquer. As a result of these secessions, membership in the Council fluctuated badly. This was shown in the 

Labor Day parade in 1904 which made the poorest showing in years as only four distinct organizations were represented (1). Labor Day had been first suggested in 

1882 by the New York City Central Labor Union and was made a legal holiday in all states. 

In 1904 there was formed, only to fall apart almost immediately, a number of rival organizations affiliated with the National Alliance. Some years subsequent to 

1904 there was another reorganization with membership consisting of most of the building trades, but not all by any means, in a new organization. Some, such as the 

Plasterers' Tenders Union withdrew from the Building

Trades Council to form a new order, while still others tried to straddle the fence by retaining membership in both old and new organizations. 

This new alliance was formed in July, 1905, and was

known as the Boston Structual Building Trades Alliance and had the following seven unions on its membership rolls:

Bridge and Structual Iron Workers No. 7 Housesmiths and Architectural Iron Workers No. 59 Electrical Workers No. 103 Plumbers No. 12 Sheet Metal Workers No. 17 Plasterers' Tenders No. 1 Elevator Constructors No. 4 (2) 

A membership of approximately 4000 was claimed, representing now eight different crafts, the Building Laborers Union No. 15 also having been admitted. However, two very important groups, the Painters Union No. 

11 and the Carpenters District Council, refused to join the new Alliance.

The Building Trades Council naturally protested both to the local unions and to the international unions about the formation of this new Alliance, claiming rather obviously that it was dual to its own organization. The 

Council proffered a plan whereby a Building Trades Section would be formed made up from delegates of each union in the industry to be attached to the Central Labor Unions. Like the earlier organizations mentioned above the new Alliance was short-lived. Many unions dropped out and the activity of the organization petered out from sheer inanition.

Meanwhile the relationship between the Building Trades Council and the Central Labor Union were becoming strained. The Council was forced into disputes with a group that desired to establish a Building Trades Section of the Central Labor Union entailing the virtual exclusion of some of the crafts in the old Council. Another group took the opposite view: they advocated the amalga-

mation of the Building Trades Council with the Central Labor Union, but this project was distinctly rejected by the Council. However, the same old proposition was brought up year after year in the form of a movement to organize a Building Trades Section attached to the Central Labor Union. The latter body passed annual resolutions favoring such amalgamation. 

The objects of the two organizations, the Building Trades Council and the Central Labor Union, were identical in many respects, but the activities of the bodies differed greatly. The Council spent much time on municipal and state legislative matters and the securing of appointments, where possible, for representatives of organized labor who could in any way have a claim to preferred conditions because of their affiliations. The 

Central Labor Union occupied itself with matters of broader interest, such as amendments to the State Insurance Laws affecting unions, the hours and conditions of labor of municipal employees, and safety laws for crafts in the field.

In 1906 an agreement was entered into between the Council and the Central Labor Union, approved by the American Federation of Labor. (The American Federation of Labor was founded around Novembor 15, 1881, in 

Pittsburgh, Pa., under a definite sentiment, namely: "to protect trade union members and impress the necessity of protective trade and labor organizations." In addition, there was an object to "elevate trade unionism and obtain for the wage earners respect for their rights and that reward for their services to which they are justly entitled." 

Then for the first time in a declaration they went into sociology as it never had been touched by the local unions in Boston until their affiliation with the American Federation of Labor. This declaration was "we believe the gain-

ing of higher wages and the shorter work day to be preliminary steps toward great accompanying improvements in the condition of the working people." (3) Through this agreement a temporary organization was formed and later in the year made permanent, known as the Building 

Trades Section. Seven unions, members of the Council, ignored this new Section, but in April, 1907, the new body was regarded as definitely established, and it was compulsory for all local building trades unions which were affiliated with the Central Labor Union to join the Building Trades Section. As a result, all unions which had been affiliated with the Council were forced to withdraw from it and join the Building Trades Section. On May 18, 1907, the Building Trades Council disbanded after a continuous existence of twenty-two years.

CHAPTER III THE  ORGANIZATION AND ACTIVITIES 

The Building Trades Council is composed of delegates from various local unions in good standing with the international unions which are affiliated with the building trades. The regulations make it necessary for such international unions to secure the affiliation of local unions with the Council and also with the Boston Central Labor Union. When there are two or more unions in the same craft it is required that they form a District Council. 

This District Council is represented in the Trades Council instead of the local unions. (1) If any body has a hundred members or less, it is entitled to two delegates to the Building Trades Council, and those of two hundred or more are entitled to four, and so on in regular arithmetical progression up to those having over twelve thousand. These are premitted to send ten delegates to the

Council, the maximum number allowed or permitted for any one trade or craft. Emphasis, therefore, is plainly placed on the representation of craft interests, small unions being free from the danger of being submerged by the representation of the large unions. 

Funds for the operation of the Council are derived from such things as sub-renting of halls, and kindred incidentals, as well as from a quarterly per capita tax on affiliated unions. As a return for this tax, working cards are issued to the members of the local unions. Any organization which falls six months in arrears in its payments is automatically suspended. The Council does not main-

tain any health benefits or financial or philanthropic aids of any kind for its members. The funds are used entirely for the payment of its current and legitimate expenses. 

The jurisdiction of the Council covers building industry in all matters dealing with erection, alteration, and repairs. Territorially it embraces Boston, Milton, Brookline, Cambridge, Belmont, Somerville, Arlington, Malden, Medford, Everett, Chelsea, Revere, Winthrop, and the islands of Boston Harbor, or approximately two-thirds of Metropolitan Boston. It is the object of the Council to build up the building trades unions of this section and to see, so far as posible, that each man employed in the construction of buildings is a member of a union and carries a card or button of the Council. Where non-union men are found working on a job in the field, it is a special duty of the visiting agent of the union to compel them to conform to the rules of the trade to which they belong and to join the union. Failing this, they will either ask for their discharge or remove their particular craft from the site and, perhaps if they have—as it quite possible these days-a private agreement with the contractor, by the strength of that contractual agreement they may fine him. 

The Council itself has very few trade rules, the most important being that no member of any trade affiliated with the Council may work under police protection nor use or handle any material that is a product of convict labor. 

The Council holds regular meetings every two weeks, and special meetings may be called upon application of any three affiliated trades. The delegates are required to attend, and there is a suspension clause for any members absent for three consecutive meetings without being excused. One-third of the trade organizations in good standing attending constitutes a quorm for any meeting.

20 

The officers of the Council consist of the President, Vice-president, Executive Secretary, Sergeant-at-Arms, three Trustees, three Auditors, and the Business Agent.

Elections for office are held yearly in June, and tested politicians seeking the approval of the electorate could well learn from these elections. 

The Executive Secretary is the most important and active officer of the Council. One of his duties is to act as a central office of information and despatch in conjunction with the covering of construction jobs in the district, to see that union conditions are observed, and to remedy complaints and act as an executive between meetings. 

He receives the assistance and cooperation of the business agents of the local unions. The success of the Council in dealing with grievances which cut across craft lines calling for "sympathetic action" or with checking up other points with the large employer of labor depends upon the energy and tact of the Secretary and of the Business Agent of the Council, as differentiated from the business agents of the local unions.

The Organization Committee of the Council has as its duty the investigation of applications for membership, the seeking out of workers in every unorganized branch of the industry, and utilizing every effort to instruct and educate member employees on matters pertaining to the advancement of the working man. (2) In further addition it does everything possible to increase membership of all the different organizations affiliated with it. 

There is an Adjustment Committee which enjoys extensive power. It has complete control over the issuance of the working cards, the buttons, or the insignia of the craftsmen in good standing. These are customarily issued each calendar month. This Committee seeks to adjust troubles between crafts. It has the power to order

the withdrawal of any work which is being done by the contractor having charge of the operation where the trouble exists. When a job has been ordered stopped by the Adjustment Committee, the agents of the various crafts involved go to the site at a given time and also notify all other members of the action that has taken place. Thus the authority of the separate crafts is recognized; but if it should happen that any business agent does not appear on the job at the given time to carry out the orders of the Adjustment Committee, then the agent of the Council may be empowered to call out on strike the members of any affiliated union. 

However, neither the Adjustment Committee nor the Council has within itself the power to call a general strike of one craft, of several, or of all the crafts. That action can only take place when the matter has been put to a referendum vote of the members of the union or crafts affected. Then, if the vote be in the affirmative, and if the matter be one that calls for the action of the central body, a strike committee is appointed by the Council. If a union or a group of unions desire to undertake a strike, the proposal must first be approved by the National Union, and then a bill of grievances must be filed with the Adjustment Committee of the Council for endorsement. 

The rules of the building trades provide that "under no circumstances shall a local union or unions of any organization affiliated be allowed to inaugurate strikes without consent of the local council. (3)"

The above rule, however, has been diregarded by some local unions when they thought that a strike could be won without the support of the Council, or when they thought the Council's hand would be forced to support the strike anyway. Nevertheless, this has worked both ways. The 

Council is prevented by this rule from calling a strike

against a contractor who is fair to union labor and on their fair list, because of some grievances of a jurisdictional nature. Such a matter must be submitted to the international unions involved before any authoritative action can be taken by the local Council. Often in practice compromises are affected. (4) The local unions in these jurisdictional matters do not seem to be easily restrained by any fear that they cannot secure the support of the Council.

Until the establishment in 1916 of the Building Trades 

Employers Association and the General Agreement of 

1919, the Building Trades Councils had nothing to do with General Agreements except as their support or endorsement might be called for by some separate trade. Consequently in the structure of the Council, the negotiations for agreements being in the hands of others, there has been and is yet, no reflection of the need for the assumption of responsibility in these matters by the Building 

Trades Employers Association. Nevertheless, the United

Building Trades Council was called into being by the need for a united body which could represent the crafts in negotiation with the Employers Association. Since that time the Employers Association has had to assume, increasingly, the duties connected with negotiations for structural arrangements to the benefit of the worker and the employer. "Sympathetic action" was the desire of the Building

Trades Council in the earlier days of its existence, but little was accomplished. During the 1890's it was crippled because it could not count upon the masons. From 1900 on there has been a succession of secessions. Unions went on their own. If "sympathetic action" was necessary, a union was just as likely to secure it through its own efforts as through those of the Council, even granting that the latter might have been of assistance. The en-

dorsement of strikes already called seems to have more the function of the Council than the engineering of "sympathetic strikes." Just how valuable the endorsement of the Council in this case really is it is difficult to say. Undoubtedly, however public sentiment always a necessary assistance to a successful strike, was known by the Council to some extent and depended further upon the pleasure of the press.

In November, 1897, it was voted that "whenever local unions are about to enter into an agreement with employers the business agent of the Council shall first be consulted (5).” This ruling was not often lived up to. 

Once in a while the Council was helpful in enforcing a boycott or a lockout, as in that of 1894 against the Seth W. Fuller Company, but reference to such matters is rare. 

During the first decade of this present century, the Council was busy trying to force owners to give contracts to employers of union labor, in an endeavor to avoid the situation which so often arises, in which a general contractor who had promised to employ union men gave out parts of the work to subcontractors who did not employ union men. The efforts of the Council along these particular lines were effectual in a great many instances. 

A great deal of strenuous effort has been expended by the Council in preserving the closed shop. At all possible times it has opposed the open shops of the Master Builders Association. In 1913 it stoutly opposed the proposal of the masters that unions be incorporated. In 

1893 it put into effect a working card system which it enforced with varying degrees of vigor from that time on. 

This move secured the support of the Central Labor Union which endorsed its working card, and, as a result, a splendid increase in membership was secured. (6)

The Organizing Committee of the Council managed

from time to time to form new unions as well as to assist in increasing the membership of the then existing unions. 

In these ways it aided the lathers and painters, and formed, in 1895, a new union of floor layers, and another of artificial stone masons and cement workers, in 1898 tile layers, and in the same year bricklayers. It worked in close harmony with the craft unions intimately concerned. 

At all times it had the assistance of the organization brains of the American Federation of Labor.

During the first twenty years of its existence, one of the main activities of the Building Trades Council was agitation for shorter hours. It had its birth in that idea after the defeat of the nine-hour strike of 1885-1886. Its agitation, however, seemed to be sporadic. From time to time resolutions would be adopted which pledged certain unions to agitate for a reduction in working time. 

The Council advised all regarding strikes for this purpose, often endorsing them, and at times invoking "sympathetic action." It even resorted to the boycott to bring the employer into line (7). Considerable stress was put upon widespread publicity to further its efforts. Meetings in 

Faneuil Hall were called in 1900 which all affiliated unions were expected to attend. In 1902 there were demands for the eight-hour day for city and state employees, and in 1905 the Council started its campaign for Saturday half-holiday throughout the entire year without loss of pay for all workers in all building trades. All this was conducive toward an increase in membership in the unions and of the Council. Agitation and publicity were directly reflected in the growth of membership.

CHAPTER IV LOCAL UNIONS 

In Massachusetts, which for big union operations in building means substantially Greater Boston, the local unions of the building trades industry have preserved an independence of action that is rare among unions of dissimilar industries at the present time. Features contributing to this condition include: (a) the local character of the building industry, as there has not been any great degree of competition between general contractors in one city with those in another. Perhaps it may be otherwise expressed, that there are in Boston at the present time only about five contractors that are competent on a bonded financial guarantee basis to be signatory to a contract, to the satisfaction of both parties, that would exceed on its face two million dollars; outside of Boston there probably are not two in the rest of the state; (b) the persistence of craft skill and craft standards demanding insularity. There is a backwardness of mechanical facility in building trades as differentiated, for example, from textiles. Each year, nevertheless, sees more and more splitting up of craft processes, but there is still an indispensable craft knowledge which can only be acquired either through schooling, in a very few specialized schools, plus actual practice in the field, or from an apprenticeship basis. Specialization seems to effect the building trades unions most seriously in an indirect ratio, that is, through its effect upon employment; (c) the state of disorganization existing among em-

27 ployers. This comes in part from the localization or insularity of the industry, and the ease with which small contracting can be undertaken, owing to the meagre capital necessary, the lack of uniform standards, and so forth. 

As the population of the country, in the main, is housed by the small builder, small housing is one of the major heavy industries of the nation, and the only one at the present time, so far as construction is concerned, that may reasonably be called improving. Further, competition between local small contractors as well as large is keen. Nation-wide business as it might affect great public projects is in the hands of probably less than twenty great contracting firms. Out of such a contrast between the small and the large arises the question of organizing employers of building trades in any staple manner; (d) last, but perhaps most important, the weak position of the individual consumer be he personal or corporate. Owing to inexperience in matters of construction on the part of the ordinary individual he cannot determine where the standards of quality and workmanship are being maintained. Of course, he should at this point rely upon the services of an architect, but usually that is a service dispensed with in small house construction because of false but prevelant opinion that it is luxurious, whereas it properly should be considered at least as insurance. Consideration of price and not longevity of materials or workmanship is the chief attraction to the small consumer, and in all pertinent matters he is easily misled. 

These peculiarities put building trade unionism in a class by itself. For this reason, workers have strong local organizations. Most of the workers are skilled mechanics. The small employer is unable or unwilling to offer much resistance to the formation of unions. He ignores them as much as he can. The big contractors, in

general, are disposed to be fair to union labor, perhaps making necessity a virtue.

A feature against unionism is that the employees of the building trades are usually not so situated as to be able to stand a long period of lack of work, as has been witnessed between 1928 and 1930 when building money was misguided into the stock market, and in 1930 to 1933 when the cessation of industrial activity in general precluded any addition to plants or extensions of construction.

Aside from the great disadvantage of the "jurisdictional" dispute, there have been good reasons why local unions in construction have been able to maintain their strength without much aid from national unions. The chief reason for the development of national unions in the building trade has been the mobility of labor. Even in long established communities like Boston, the movement of building workers into and out of the city is considerable. 

The formation of national unions is made possible by standardization of apprentice rules, and regulations of unions in different areas in the competition for jobs. In some degree, local unions have been influenced by the action of national unions in matters where two or more unions overlap. This may be seen in the years of varying relationship between the Bricklayers, Masons, and Plasterers International Union, and the Operative Plasterers 

International Association. Much of this action has been dependent upon the personality of International Union officials.

Agreements in 1911 to 1913 had much significance for the local unions where, for example two national unions provided for full recognition of each union by the other, and for the adoption of uniform hours, wages, and working rules. The control of the national union does not run

29 counter to the long standing claim of "local autonomy." 

The bricklayers are exclusive and highly skilled, with the control resting on that skill which is of hand labor, and with control on the regulation of the conditions of entrance to the trade. Consequently, they are highly localized, and their relationship with the national union is based on that fact. 

Carpenters unions, on the other hand, are very inclusive. The necessity for skill has been greatly reduced in some branches of that craft, with the resultant feeling it would be very unwise to leave any sort of carpenter out of the union; hence, they have a nationally knit organization in many points strikingly in contrast with that of the bricklayers.

National claim of authority over local may be expressed in two ways: first, that the local "can exercise local autonomy only in matters in which the International Constitution and By-Laws are silent;" second, “to reserve as among the powers of the national body all legislative rights not definitely delegated to subordinate associations." These two statements amount to about the same thing and do not mean very much, but they do open up the way for an extention of the power of the national body The conception of craft self-sufficiency, with tendencies rooted in the past and the policies of unions, as summed up in the phase "local autonomy," are growing more and more chimerical. In some groups this unreality has become so evident that a compensatory type of co-operative action through the Building Trades Council is made welcome. However, these are restrained from a full measure of co-operation by the idea of separatism of unions to which local autonomy still offers advantages. 

Unions like plumbers and steamfitters, (it is interesting to note that in 1936 steamfitters' helpers officially, by union

agreement became steamfitters), approach the bricklayers, stone masons, and plasterers on both skill and control, but do not equal them. The electrical workers are interested in local autonomy not so much on account of their local independence as in their interest in collective bargaining under national auspices. The powerful carpenters must be put on another side altogether. Apart from the metal trim dispute they tend to stand alone, because they find the methods of co-operation characteristic of the Council disadvantageous, and because they feel they can do better for themselves. They await the development of real power in the Council. Their separatism is tentative. Like the Council itself, their form of organization, with its wide variety of particular callings, is an approximation to industrial unionism. The paths of the carpenters and the 

Council will be close together in the future. 

All these unions seem to be willing to use the Council as a means of action in relation to sympathetic support or general measures of jurisdictional disputes, but as regards the larger aims of the Council (such as those having to do with general agreement relations) such occasional voluntary co-operation is not enough.

For the present, about all the Council can do is to ascertain the will of the majority and do its utmost to make that will prevail. In recent years, this has meant the use of local autonomy by all, to offset the stubborn refusal of a few to limit their right to separate action. Mere selfish separatism, unsupported by technological advantages, will find it difficult to prevail because of changes in the industry, which are reflected in a great interdependence of the crafts and an increasing solidarity of the employers. 

All this should lead to the possibility of a new policy which will more readily express the unity of the workers in the industry.

CHAPTER V WAGE POLICIES 

It is the first and foremost aim of every union positively to maintain, and, wherever possible, increase the rates of wages of its members. Conversely, the wage rate is uppermost in the minds of employers in making arrangements with the union. Naturally, because of the human elements involved, this has always been and doubtless always will be difficult and hard to settle. It almost never is intelligently handled on an economic basis, but seems to find its strength in the expediency of the moment. 

The question as to whether or not the standard wage should be maintained without the privilege of individual bargaining seems never to enter. Every one agrees that the standard wage rate, as secured by collective bargaining, is always proper and should be maintained. Some of the building unions consider that there should be no individual bargaining at all, but this does not prevent some of the crafts from following a practice of setting up classes of workers within the union itself, at varying rates of pay for each. In general, however, the practice of the unions follows the thought in the resolutions adopted by the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners in 1888, which declared that they were firmly opposed to any system of grading wages in the local unions. They deemed this demoralizing to the trade, and having the tendency to allow first-class men to offer their labor for less than first-class wages.

Much controversy has arisen over the question whether or not standard wages shall be regarded as a

flat rate for the craft, or as a minimum above which any individual or group of individuals might rise. Here the correlative fact and criticism from outside takes birth in the statement that unionism levels down to the minimum the efficient worker. The unions hold to the view that the standard rate shall be regarded as a minimum wage, and are opposed to the insistence of the Building Trades Employers Association that the wage rate set by agreement shall be regarded as fixed, and neither raised nor lowered.

The General Agreement of January 20, 1921, stated that the "rate of wages" and the "standard rate of wages for all regular working hours" should be at a certain definite figure, but the first General Agreement of June 27, 1919, stated that the minimum rate of wages for all mechanics shall be not less than ninety cents an hour. The statement is alleged to be quotable from Mr. M. T. Guerin, a member of the General Executive Board of the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners, who was prominent in drafting the 1919 Agreement, stating two years later that the word "minimum" was included in the Agreement purposely. "During my twenty years' experience as National Representative of the Carpenters I have never put my name to an Agreement providing for a flat or maximum rate. It has always been a minimum and with the minimum that the poorest ordinary skilled mechanic was entitled to. Anything above that could be mutually agreed upon." (1)

The desire in the Building Trades Employers Association for a flat rate of wages seems to be based upon the desire to hold the organization together. There is nothing which so quickly wrecks an association of employers as competition for workmen. The unions themselves seem to recognize the danger of lack of unity which

The academic question naturally rises; for how long a period was the standard rate of wages to be fixed? The unions, with their traditions, answer: "only so long as it seems impossible to get more." The General Agreement of June 27, 1919, lasted until December 31, 1920, but it included the provision that a certain group of trades should receive ten cents an hour increase in March, 1920. In 1921 the Employers Association proposed a three-year Agreement; the unions rejected the idea. The Agreement of 1922 lasted for one year; the Agreement of 1923 lasted for two years, but this latter was a compromise affair. It is significant to know that no Agreement so far has lasted longer than two years, save those which provided for an increase in wages to some extent to some trades at a fraction term period. 

The unions do not believe in signing up for two or more years at a fixed rate of wages. It would seem as if the unions do not trust the Building Trades Employers Association in a wage rate matter. They believe the Association would not hesitate to cut a rate that seemed too high, if an unforseen period of depression arose. They believe that the Association would support a long-time agreement because agreements of this kind keep wages down. 

The aim of the unions is not one of co-operation with the employer in a program of stabilization, but one of advantage for its members. If a general agreement could

be standardized and observed, it would keep the unions from bearing the brunt of a depression, but most unionists in the building trades are willing to take a chance on security in order to avail themselves of opportunities to advance their standard of living. Probably no one would dispute the fact that were it not for unionism, the present happy plight of organized labor, compared to a period of fifty years ago, could not exist. It is further reasonable to state that cupidity on the part of the employer, without compulsion on the part of the employee through unions, would not have given the American working man his present state or standard of living. CHAPTER VI CLOSED SHOP 

Boston has long been regarded as a "closed shop" town. Unions in Boston have built up an extensive control over the terms and conditions of their labor. Employers, themselves, have been directly responsible, to a certain extent, for the strength of unionism in Boston because their early policy of showing a preference for union members furthered the growth of some of the most powerful unions in the industry.

Subsequent to 1890 the Master Builders Association encouraged the formation of subsidiary societies among employees in each trade, for the purpose of bargaining. 

One of the most effective was the Mason Builders Association. This body, from 1891 to 1903, carried on a joint board relationship with Bricklayers Unions Nos. 3 and 27 and the Stone Masons Union No. 9, a relationship which had for one of its chief features a policy of demanding that all journeymen stone masons and bricklayers should belong to their respective unions, and that preference of employment would be given to union stone masons and bricklayers in all hiring by members of the Master Builders Association. As a result of this policy, these unions grew and prospered, and there were no disputes of any importance for a dozen years; but, eventually, being well organized and powerful the bricklayers attempted to exercise the whip hand and broke away from the joint conference table. This break came in June, 1903, over "closed shop" issues. The objection was to two clauses introduced by the Master Builders Association for incor-

poration in the working rules of that year. One of these clauses was that no discrimination should be exercised against non-members, but that all members and nonmembers should be expected to conform to the rules therein defined. The second was that the business agents of the Bricklayers Unions Nos. 3 and 27 should be privileged to visit any job during working hours, and interview there the steward of the job for the purpose of "closed shop" only. There was nothing in the rules giving any such agents authority to issue orders controlling work or workmen, or interfering with the conduct of the work. Discord over these points led to a large and immediate strike which ended the close relations between the Master 

Builders Association and the unions.

Then the Master Builders Association became avowedly an "open shop" organization, and for a decade or more tried to limit and destroy the power of unions. The Association was very aggressive, numbering among its members most of the powerful contractors in Boston who were hostile to organized labor, but the crafts stayed with the definite "closed shop" policy. Since 1903 their policy has been equally aggresive and more successful. They increase in strength annually. By 1916 those members of the Master Builders Association who considered themselves realists decided there was nothing to do other than to deal with labor in its organizations, and endeavor to use against it its own weapons. Accordingly, they organized the Building Trades Employers Association and followed the path of definite recognition of the unions with the process of collective bargaining. Henceforward there were few to dispute the statement that Boston was a "closed shop" town on major construction policies. 

The means of enforcing the "closed shop" are first, the union rules which the members are obligated to ob-

CLOSED SHOP 

37 serve; second, the working card system augmented by the activities of the business agent to see that the rules are actually obeyed; third, restrictions and penalties on the use of non-union material. This latter has been observed sometimes in the breach. Unions have refused to go out on strike when served, for example, by trucks driven by non-union chauffeurs, or where fabricated materials arrive on the site from non-union mills. Strikes have been called for that purpose, but they have seemed to fail to acquire sufficient sympathy to make them successful in many instances. It has been said that the means which has brought this success to the unions is that the craftsmen without a card is a man without a trade.

In 1934, under the first of the alphabetical designations, the Federal Government went into the employment of labor. The direction of this labor was left to the municipal and state entities, but the employees were considered Federal employees. The first of these was the 

C.W.A. (Civil Works Administration, better known by these initials, as were its subsequent outgrowths.) The purpose of the whole was the Presidential idea of the priming of the pump. The men were to be taken, in the main, from relief rolls with a view to lightening the burden of relief administrtion from state and city, and the economic idea of spending money to make money. In the C.W.A. the unions were totally disregarded. The need of the individual was the paramount requirement. This scared the unions, and they stressed their legislative activities on adjustments to which they were partially successful.

The chief advantage of the C.W.A., other than the immediate relief to the employee, was the continuity of the labor, even though at a low wage. For instance, a

floor layer continuously employed at eighteen dollars a week for a three-day week, a total of twenty-four hours, could, in his other days, employ his time in earning another dollar or two, if he was industrious, and the combination would afford him a better yearly income that he was accustomed to make. This possible additional employment defeated one of the purposes of the Federal Government which wanted private industry, by assumption of the individual into its ranks, to remove him from the government agencies.

The C.W.A. was succeeded by the F.E.R.A. (Federal 

Emergency Relief Administration) which carried on in substantially the same way, with the chief exception that the sponsor of the project employing the men had to provide tools, materials and trucking, as well as supervisory costs. This varied in various states, ranging all the way from total contribution to not more than five per cent contribution, and was based, in the main, upon the ability of the State to pay. 

The F.E.R.A. was in turn succeeded by the W.P.A. (Works Progress Administration) which seems to be a difference chiefly of nomenclature. Then the P.W.A. (Public Works Administration) handled big projects and ran parallel to these other agencies and contributed in amounts varying from thirty per cent to forty-five per cent of the cost of the project in both materials and labor. 

In this the men were to be taken from relief rolls until the particular recognized journeymen were exhausted in the given locality and then from the ranks of organized labor exclusively. In all cases, veterans, and particularly the disabled veterans, had first preference.

The rates of pay of organized labor were the accepted rates, and the thirty-hour week was mandatory. If necessitated by the exigencies of the particular project,

39 a sixty-hour week could be used, but in no case could one man be employed more than thirty hours; in other words, a second crew could be put to work for a second thirty hours in any given week.

Labor exercised its rights of strike and collective bargaining under P.W.A. just as much as under any private auspice. So substantially labor had a "closed shop" with Federal approval, even in localities where the "open shop" heretofore had prevailed. This should be of ultimate benefit to labor, and while the W.P.A. is on the wane as a Government influence in industry it is with us at the time of this writing, 1936, but the P.W.A. is closed.

CHAPTER VII APPRENTICESHIP 

The control of apprenticeship has long been the source of conflict between employers and employees. Apprenticeship has a two-fold aspect: on one side it means a trade education; on the other side it is closely related to the monopolistic aspect of union policy. The aim is to regulate the direction of the supply of labor by restricting the numbers entering a specific occupation. The abuse is that employers find it profitable to use boys, or apprentices as helpers, to do the work for which they would otherwise have to pay skilled journeymen, for the very obvious reason that they can pay them wages much less than the standard for the craft. This is the viewpoint definitely opposed by the unions. On the other hand, they carry it too for and leave themselves with the possibility of not having sufficient new blood with which to stay alive. It can become unprofitable to the employer to hire apprentices and shift them about so that they may learn the necessary operations of the trade, since to do so must require the attention of a specialized journeyman or some other teacher. However, this is the very thing the union policy wishes the employer to do. 

Then there is a human element: the boys often object to long apprenticeship, and the time element is usually definite and not based on the rate of progress of the individual. Further, of course, the employer desires, when business is slack, to turn his apprentices into journeymen as soon as possible and get rid of them. The unions wish to maintain the craft standards and limit the num-

APPRENTICESHIP 

41 ber of those under instruction so as to render apprenticeship regulations effective.

The plumbers carry on apprenticeship in the right way and with rigid restrictions. With them, to a considerable degree mere watching is learning. The bricklayers and plasterers demand the handling of the tools with quite a different apprenticeship policy from the plumbers. 

The responsibility for the regulation of apprenticeship rests upon the local unions so far as most of the crafts are concerned. The general rule is that the local unions shall set what standards it can as to restriction and regulation of apprenticeship. These standards deal with the length of the term of the probationary period, age limits, limitations as to number, and the determination of competency.

Substantially, only two building trades are licensed, namely, the plumbers and electricians, and competency after examination is certified by a State Board having jurisdiction. While building trades licenses are necessary for the supervising force or resident superintendent on a job, it does not in any way state that the applicant must be either a carpenter or a bricklayer. Virtually any one is entitled, without any regard to any craft whatsoever, to take such an examination as building inspector, clerk of the works, or licensed contractor, and the only criterion is the successful passing of the examination. 

Of course, part of the competency is dependent upon past experience where the knowledge of a craft is almost as essential as in the first instance quoted above. 

In the latter part of 1923, the Apprenticeship Commission was established because of a widespread conviction that new mechanics in sufficient number were not being produced. This commission was composed of nine persons: three from the Building Trades Employers As-

sociation, three from the Building Trades Council, and three from the Building Congress. The purpose of the commission was to stimulate the formation in each trade of a joint committee of six to take active interest in the matter of apprenticeship and assume responsibility for them. This commission was intended at first to be merely a general advisory and consulting body, but such was the wall of indifference it met, that it had to enlarge its scope of operations to the point of initiating action. So far as possible it had the joint committees in the various trades do the real work, attend to the technical details involved (often special to the craft), and the general details. Upon the committees devolved the responsibility for keeping the apprentices continuously employed. 

An important part of the Apprenticeship Commission is the correlating of public and private school studies with job experience. The costs of the commission are borne by contributions from various sources, such as the Master 

Builders Association, the Mechanics Charitable Associa tion, the Building Trades Council, the Building Trades Employers Association and interested unions. The work of the commission, might be said to be still in its educational state with much to be accomplished yet, but the machinery of co-operation is being devised. There can be no doubt of the value of proper educational training for boys and young men desirous of entering as apprentices in the various building crafts.

CHAPTER VIII THE  

The Master Builders Association has never played a direct part in the connection with labor agreements. Currently it is playing no part at all. However, the Master 

Builders Association is the oldest and most influential labor organization of building employers in Boston. For forty years its influence has been weighty in the field of employment relations. It was incorporated February 17, 1885, having as its chief aim the establishment of the belief that membership in its Association denoted a reasonable assurance to the general public of the skill, honesty and responsibility of its members. 

Its first action was an effort to stabilize labor conditions. To this end it issued a code of "working principles" to which it invited the assent of contractors without its membership. The first article in this code was a change, a most important change, from the custom of paying by the day to paying by the hour. The second article specified nine hours in each working day. The third was a demand that there should be inserted in every building contract a clause, now currently common, relieving the contractor from any forfeiture or demurrage on account of delays caused by strikes. Article four was not very important, but Article five was a threat. The threat was against "improper interference with business" in the form of strikes for "trivial causes." The final article dealt with the subject of arbitration which specified that any group of workmen had at any and all times the right to have a grievance heard, but qualified and definitely stated that

"no person outside the employment of said contractor shall be allowed to represent them." This Code ended with a mutual agreement of the contractor members of the Master Builders Association to the effect they would assist each other in maintaining their stand against improper dictation, as was that taken and successfully held during the strikes of May and June, 1886. These were the principles upon which the organized contracting employers, the general contractors of Boston, acted at that period.

They are eloquent testimony to the weakness of the unions, for they denied every principle of trade unionism. 

However, this same code is notable, also, as being the expression of a new attitude on the part of contractors. This was a positive attitude which was to make a definite bid for the support of the working man by regarding his problems as something worthy of attention. Undesirable labor combinations were to have their thunder stolen by intelligent action on the part of employers, as labor has not often been noted for its farsightedness of its own interests. So ordinary employers have always been noted for being grossly indifferent to matters outside the sphere of their own narrow interests. It ended, therefore, by the positive action policy failing from lack of support; the unions seized the opportunity they had been waiting for, and all was as though the Code of Working Principles of 1886 had never been issued. The nine-hour arrangement of 1887 was successful, and it was not long before the eight-hour day became the rule for certain trades. 

The Master Builders Association was not composed only of contractors at the time of its founding, nor has it been since. A division of membership was considered important, inasmuch as those engaged in the construction trades believed that their interest in the establishment of

certain practices was greater than that of those engaged solely in the sale of materials. Therefore, in the new organization, the radical change was made by having two classes. Eleven of the principal construction trades were considered as being most concerned in this new order of things. Those engaged in carrying on those trades were to be considered eligible to full membership. The first one hundred persons admitted to this full membership composed the "corporation" and it became the governing body. 

Others in the trades admitted to membership were to be considered eligible to the "corporation" when vacancies occurred therein. The "Exchange Privilege" class comprised those engaged in certain minor building trades as well as dealers in building material or those engaged in a business or a nature collateral to the building trades. 

The eleven physical building trades eligible to corporate standing were masons, carpenters, iron workers, stone and marble workers, plasterers, roofers, copper workers, plumbers, painters, pile drivers and building movers.

In further addition to the corporate and non-corporate members there was, until 1922, a class of individuals, firms, or corporations "carrying on branches of business connected with or relating to the building trades but not comprehended under the list of constructive trades eligible to the corporation." This class might be admitted to the privileges of the "exchange" rules, but no application eligible to the "corporation" was admitted to this class. (1) 

In 1922 this division of membership was changed.

There were now only two classes that of general members, made up of individual firms and corporations whose usual business included execution of work on buildings as distinct from the manufacturer of or dealer in material used in building work (these first had to be in business

for at least one year); and associate members whose business was related to the construction industry but does not fall within the above description. 

From 1922 application for membership was by invitation, with only the general members having voting power. The Master Builders Association is the only building employers association in the United States that has kept its membership standards so highly selective. The association has never claimed the right to exercise mandatory powers over its members. Its relations have always been advisory in character. The membership has been surprisingly stable. In 1896, ten years after its founding, it had 300 members; in 1908, 242; in 1913, 282; in 1925, 341. At the present time, 1936, the Master Builders Association is practically inactive and has only 45 members.

There were five advantages to membership in the 

Master Builders Association: exchange, publicity, reputa tion, opportunity, and protection, and all are set forth by their own publications. In matters concerning labor the association supported the following general principles: 

1. That no person or combination of persons other than those directly responsible for the conduct of business should be permitted to control or dictate upon what terms or under what conditions or following what methods business should be conducted; 

2. That absolute freedom of choice in the employment of workmen is essential to the welfare of the employer, the employee, and the community. As a natural corollary, artificial restraints upon such freedom should not be permitted to exist; 

3. That combination of persons organized to operate

so as to create interference with the rights of others, directly or indirectly, should not be recognized as fit and proper bodies with which to have business relations. (2) 

Some of the members of the Master Builders Association have been the most ardent "open shop" exponents in New England. Other members have become convinced that the "open shop" was folly, that the unions, as they were, had to be dealt with. By the aid of these latter members the Building Trades Employers Association was formed in 1916.

The Master Builders Association engaged in special activities. The effect of their interesting activities on the building trades might be boiled down to two. These were 

(1) the National Association of Builders, 1887-1899, and 

(2) the Joint Board for Arbitration, 1891 to 1903. At

Conjunctive to the above is the National Association of Builders. This body was formed in 1887 following considerable research work by the Master Builders Association concerning builders' exchanges in various cities. their first convention delegates from nine separate cities met in Chicago. The purpose of this body was to assist in the organization of builders' exchanges where none existed, and to raise the standards of those in existence to as high a level as possible. A uniform apprenticeship system, the establishment of manual training schools, the hour instead of the day for the pay basis, the adoption of standardized forms of contracts, and agitation for securing effectual safeguards in building under construction to lessen the danger of injury to workmen, were among the policies advocated. However, this national association accomplished practically nothing in the way of education in its twelve years of existence. One positive contribution it did make was the discussion and promulgation of a plan of agreement with craftsmen to secure

the benefits of arbitration. Like most all similar ideas, this was first put into effect in Boston. After the natural demise of the National Association of Builders, the Master Builders Association took no further active part in national organizations of contractors. 

The Joint Board for arbitration is worthy of some small consideration. This plan of arbitration, of which the National Association of Builders was the sponsor was the outcome of discussions during the fifth convention of that body. This plan declared that while the absolute personal independence of the individual must never be questioned, yet much good might come to the industry from proper dealings by employers with associations of workmen, and especially from conferences and arbitration on matters to which that is properly suitable. A draft of a form of agreement for use of associations of employers and workmen in all branches of the building industry to secure the establishment of arbitration committees was offered. This plan of arbitration was embodied in the Agreement of the Master Builders Association with the Bricklayers Unions Nos. 3 and 27 and the Stone Masons Union

No. 9, and later with the unions of laborers and hoisting engineers. The plan consisted of an agreement between the journeymen and the employers that no question of mutual concern should be conclusively acted upon by either independently, but that all such matters should be referred to a joint committee for settlement. An important feature of the pact was that all questions of common interest should be settled by the trade involved, exclusively, without the intervention of any other trade whatsoever. 

This means the "sympathetic" strike was done away with It was further specified in the agreement that under no circumstances should there be any strike or any lockout, either before the matter pertinent had been submitted to

the joint committee or while the committee was still deliberating. With the finding of the joint committee both sides were bounden to abide without question. 

It was emphasized that suggestions of the exchange should be purely advisory. The need for some kind of joint agreement so that each trade could settle its own disputes for itself instead of referring them to some outside board unacquainted with the technical details of the craft concerned was all the greater in view of the general unpopularity of the State Board of Arbitration with both employers and employees in the building industry. Subcontractors, as differentiated from the general contractor, needed organizations in their several trades to meet committees from the particular unions working with them. 

Such was the sketchy plan which stabilized the building industry of Boston in the decade from 1893 to 1903 directly only so far as the basic mason trades were concerned. The union of enginers established much later their own joint committee with the Master Builders. Up to 1900, this umpire had been called in only three times. 

The "sympathetic" strike in that period was not exercised. 

The Joint Committee for Arbitration did achieve something in that it settled for the time being the vexatious question of apprenticeship in the mason trades. 

This had been a bone of contention for twenty years with many strikes on that point alone. The Committee was given authority to fix the age when apprenticeship should begin, the length of the period of training, the hours of labor and terms of service, the rights of the employer, and the rights of the apprentice, and, finally, the terms of admission to the status of journeymen. The bricklayers were even brought to the point of providing the establishment of a trade school over which union representatives were to unite with employer representatives in exercising

supervision. The very success of the joint arbitration scheme led to its collapse. 

In 1899, as a result of the preference of the masons for dealing with journeymen organized in unions, the unions of bricklayers and stone masons grew in numbers. Regardless of arbitration above and apprenticeship systems, with strength came the desire to try new paths. Until 

1903 the working rules were arranged amicably each year, but at that time there was dissatisfaction over certain objectionable clauses submitted by the master builders to be incorporated in the working rules. These paragraphs were: (1) The Joint Committee insists that no discrimination shall be made against non-members of Bricklayers

Unions Nos. 3 and 27, but that all members and nonmembers are expected to conform to the rules herein defined. 

(2) The Business Agent of the Bricklayers Unions 

Nos. 3 and 27, shall be privileged to visit any job during working hours only for the purpose of interviewing the steward of the job. Nothing in this rule shall be construed as giving any agents authority to interfere with the conduct of the work or to enter into discussion or to attempt to proselyte any workman during working hours or while said workman is on his employer's premises. 

The desire of employers to have these two rules adopted is evidence of their awareness of a changing spirit on the part of the union men. These were so well organized that they controlled the crafts in the Boston area, and their tone took on a certain arrogance. They refused to permit the difference of opinion over the two clauses to be placed before the joint board, and they withdrew their members from the committee. They even drafted a code of working rules of their own embodying their desires in

the matter of the points at issue, and included their extensive "work claimed" provisions. They wanted double time for overtime instead of time and a half as previously, and there were items objectionable to the master builders who promptly rejected their code. Thereupon, on July 

1st, the Bricklayers struck, and stayed out until August 24th when they were granted their demands. Henceforth these crafts, including the stone masons and hoisting engineers, had no more to do with the joint committee. 

The effect of this trend of events upon the Master Builders Association was unfortunate for them. They regarded the action of the unions as arrogant and faithless. They further thought that this was a repudiation of the possibility of contractual relations and equality between arganizations of employers and employees. The bricklayers had destroyed a mechanism which laid down a basis for stability in the industry, not because they had devised something better but because they had the power to do as they pleased. Henceforth the Master Builders 

Association became the home of the "open shop," the implacable enemy of unionism. Overtures were made by certain groups in favor of the resumption of joint board arrangements with the masons in 1904, but the Master Builders Association refused to have anything to do with it. The Master Builders Association was convinced that no good could be expected from unions so long as they were organized on a basis of opposition to the employer, and they went all the way in that they were satisfied that this was also true for organizations of employers formed to coerce workmen. The Master 

Builders Association now devoted itself to a policy of aggressive protection of employers' rights.

Since 1903, following the breakdown of the Joint 

Arbitration Agreements, the Master Builders Association

has followed a policy of aloofness as regards labor relations. While they said all along that they could foster no scheme, put forward no recommendation for stabilizing employment relation, as contrasted with their attitude before 1900, it remains to be noted that wherever unions laid themselves open to legal action, the Master Builders Association struck hard. This was not because they were hostile to the unions but because of a desire to defend certain individualistic principles to protcet its members against unlawful interference, and to clarify the law to what unions could and could not do. It has figured in many cases giving court precedents.

CHAPTER IX THE BUILDING TRADES EMPLOYERS ASSOCIATION The Building Trades Employers Association was founded in 1916 by a group of contractors, some of them members of the Master Builders Association, who were convinced it was necessary to organize in order to deal successfully with the unions. As employers, they realized the necessity for bargaining with union representation.

Early in 1917 there were eighty-three members of the Association. In 1919 it was enlarged by the addition of various organizations of employers commonly known as subcontractors. Thus, for the first time, was established a combined organization of employers in all trades concerned with building construction. At the same time, the labor unions organized to form the United Building Trades Council composed of all the unions employed on buildings.

Heretofore there had been two organizations: the 

Allied Building Trades Council and the Building Trades 

Council. Up to 1919, through conferences with the unions in the various trades, the Association had taken some action in relation to wages and conditions. Agreements were entered into with each trade union in the name of its employer without reference to conditions governing any other trades that might be affected. It now became possible to bring into existence an inclusive General Agreement for the purpose of stabilizing industry as had not been possible under the other and older methods. In accordance with these changes, membership in the Build-

ing Trades Employers Association was altered, and it now became an Association of Associations. Membership was open to any firm, person, trade organization, or corporation, employing labor in the building industry or having a capital investment in such undertakings. At the time of the revision if its constitution, in 1919, there were two hundred and fifty members, which increased to three hundred in 1932. In 1925 there were one hundred and eighty-seven members. Of course, there is some overlapping between the Master Builders Association and the

Building Trades Employers Association. 

The governing board is the board of councillors of the Association, and it has entire jurisdiction over the affairs and funds of the organization. The policy of the 

Building Trades Employers Association might be summed up in one phrase: the desire for uniformity. Its primary interest is in what it calls "stabilization" which may be expressed in three ways: 

1. The attempt to force the Building Trades Council to accept responsibility for all the unions of building workers, at least as far as wages, hours, and arbitration arrangements are concerned; 

2. The attempt to hold all employers of labor together as one unified body when dealing with the Council; 

3. The purpose of establishing a general agreement which should reduce to a common rule as many as possible of the matters related to wages and the conditions of labor.

Since 1921 nothing has caused more controversy between the Building Trades Employers Association and the unions than the demand that the Building Trades Council shall act for the whole number of the labor organizations. 

The Association regards this condition as indispensable, if

In 1921, after the expiration of the first general agreement, one of the chief reasons for the inability of the Council and Association to agree was the demand that the Council stand ready to answer for all the unions, together with the assertion that no conditions of agreement would be granted to any trade union not a member of the Council. In 1922 the Association modified its demands to an extent sufficient to permit of a new general agreement being consummated. The issue as to the membership of the Council still remained. 

This "all or none" policy the Building Trades Employers Association has been unable to put into practice, although it has exerted toward that end all the pressure of which it is capable. It has found it necessary to deal with the Council that could not control all the crafts; for instance, agreements with bricklayers and carpenters have been made since 1921 without reference to the Council, and agreements with the Council have been ratified when the endorsements of certain crafts have been lacking. The Association has had to take what the Council has been able to give it, in other words. It has demanded and received the assent of the Council to the condition that no craft should be supported by the Council in any attempt to secure from employers better terms than those that had been given to unions affiliated with the Council. 

The Association has been compelled by circumstances to

permit separate crafts to bargain with their own employers in regard to many matters over which it would have liked to have exercised supervision. The old “local autonomy" still prevails. 

Since 1919 the Building Trades Employers Associa tion has definitely favored a General Agreement. It has tried to secure one that will standardize wages on a flat rate. It has insisted that this shall provide a method of arbitration common to all trades, to avoid the stoppage of work. It has composed all arrangements for separate arbitration machinery within the trades between a craft and its own employers. Beyond wages and arbitration, the Employers Association has tried to extend the provisions of the General Agreement to cover hours of labor, overtime, the responsibility of foremen, and working conditions of labor on the job. In most of this it has been signally unsuccessful. The unions cling stubbornly to their "local autonomy" and their right to deal with their own employers in all these matters. And these questions seem to arise from one primary source: namely, its inability to secure the support of all the employers of the various crafts with which it wishes to deal. It has failed even to secure the adherence of all the organized employers. Although it is founded on an all-inclusive basis, it is defeated by the indifference and separatism of many of those whom it wishes to weld together. 

The difficulties might be listed as follows: 

1. Weakness of a number of the trades associations among its membership; 

2. Inability to maintain wage rates, as set by the General Agreement, free from all taint of bonuses, cutbacks, and so forth; 

3. Inability to force all the unions to enter a General

CHAPTER X THE NEGOTIATION OF TRADE AGREEMENTS 

The future of satisfactory mutual employment relations seems to be bound up with the possibilty of direct and conclusive negotiations between the necessary two parties to a resulting agreement to be held as the basis of action by both sides, This would seem to be necessary because of the complexity of employment conditions which renders outside regulation exceedingly difficult. The more general and inclusive that agreements can be made, the better for the industry as a whole. History does not seem to show that most labor disputes can be settled by arbitration as a regular procedure after animus has entered the picture. The disputants will not refer the points at issue, and cannot be made to do so. They prefer to fight. However if the points of disputes can be anticipated, constructive progress can be made. Direct negotiations should strike a balance of interest. The problem is to keep the balance stable. The collective agreement, or trade negotiation, may well be regarded as the means of industrial peace without in any way considering it a step toward full labor control. 

There was a general agreement in 1919. I have set forth the circumstances which led to the establishment of the Building Trades Employers Association and that policy whereby it became an Association of Associations.

There is a general significance to the General Agreement which it is pertinent to amplify. There is much testimony that the General Agreement of 1919 had results pleasing to both employers and employees. It tended toward peaceful relations and the feeling of security that when a job was started nothing was going to interfere with it. It eliminated wild-cat strikes and stoppages over petty grievances, both of which were boons to both journeymen and employer. The merits of the form of General Agreements lie in the fact that it combines advantages of uniformity with those of flexibility. Of course, from the employer's viewpoint there is still too much of the latter quality, while particular unions have held that less uniformity would be to their selfish interests. 

However, both qualities are necessary for stability. 

Uniformity cannot be enforced. It may be regarded as a frontier which employers are always desirous of pushing forward to cover matters not covered before. This trend the unions resist. Until the unions can be convinced of the wisdom of such extensions, uniformity cannot contribute to stability. Because most unions and unionists have come to think that they can gain more than they can lose by common standards of wages, hours, expiration dates and arbitration methods, it has been possible to reach the amount of uniformity that has even now been attained. The uniformity of the arrangement under the General Agreement is derived from the standard wage

71 rate which, in theory, is ratified by all the crafts, but there are exceptions. The danger of wage spiralling and invidious discriminations are removed despite the persistent desire of the unions that these wage rates should be expressed in terms of minimum. These all introduce a doubtful element from the employer's point of view. 

Flexibility under the General Agreement is secured by the guarantee of settlement in its own peculiar terms by each trade with subsequent ratification by the Council and the Board of Councillors. Trade autonomy creates the agreement as regards customs and conditions. It is then given official recognizance. Of course, certain trades must agree to terms that are repugnant to most of the others and to the general contractor, also, but even so, if pressure is to be applied, it can be done better within the agreement than without it. The fore-knowledge of scrutiny to come may act as a restraint. In any case, when employers and employees in any particular trade understand each other, then the understanding, short of conspiracy of chicanery, cannot be coerced. It is better to have it on paper and out in the open than verbally and secret.

In addition to these qualifications and qualities, there is a factor of prime significance, namely, that the General Agreement is a sort of form of government for the industry, automatically originated from within itself. It was hammered out by the persons most concerned who are those who know conditions best. As such it is probably a better instrument for peace than any other instrument capable of being devised by an outside agency.

CHAPTER XI CONCLUSION 

The most important contribution to the status of organized labor in the past ten years has been the shortlived but emphatic National Recovery Act or Blue Eagle Codes. The partial basic idea of the N. R. A. was that prices should be raised to provide more money from the consumer to meet a better wage rate and, more important still, to meet continuity of employment. As a corollary of these propositions competition was to be limited in that it was to be demanded that costs should not be cut nor profits sacrificed simply for the sake of getting an order. It might be construed as "price-fixing" for the benefit of the country as a whole. Ultimately the N.R.A. was declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court. While this put it out of existence officially, its impetus, though slowed, was not halted. Today reputable firms are still thinking along the lines of the Code, and in scattered industries organization has been built up, for example in the cement industry and gross lumber industry, whereby prices shall be maintained on quantity orders.

Another step of progress in the last ten years for local labor is the Building Trades Agreement entered into in 1935 whereby there should be an increment in wages by three steps on a time basis. A co-operative thought controlling was that a man who has a contract for a big building which will take a year to two years to complete, such as the proposed new Suffolk Court House, can figure and present an estimate, with all its attendant dangers, without the added danger of wondering about the consistency of his labor units on a money basis.

Worcester is mediocre in this regard, and Lawrence is largely "closed shop." In Boston this one hundred per cent "closed shop" on public works means "closed shop" on the site of the building project. If this did not have the sanction of the general public, as expressed through its representatives in the Legislature and in City Councils, it could hardly come to pass. At the same time there is this to consider that organized labor, in general, probably does not constitute thirteen per cent of the entire population, but it is the old story of the well-organized minority and the apathetic majority. Small house work, always a major fraction of the total amount of construction performed, is almost entirely out of the hands of organized labor.

One thing that labor in the building trades in Mas-

sachusetts can be justly proud of is the practical nonexistence of a "red" element. Probably this is because the men who make up the personal of organized labor are largely from racial stock that does not come from Asia Minor, Russia or the Orient. Their whole heritage is along democratic or monarchial representative backgrounds.

Where the building labor has left its own peculiar field it has not been conspicuous by success. The electrical workers have an insurance company that probably is adequate but does not go very far. The machinists once had a bank which failed, as did the bank of the Railway Engineers. I do not say that the men were incompetent, in view of all the other banks that went out of business, too, but these preceded the debacle of 1932. 

The unions have not done much, if anything, to improve the cultural status of their members. Cultural things have been talked about, but, as a matter of fact, when the men are regularly employed and are busy they hardly attend the regular meetings of their own organizations. When the men are not busy they not only attend the meetings regularly but their chief interest in attending is to find a job. It has now come to pass, for private work anyway, that the union headquarters is the employment agency. The contractor will call upon the union headquarters of the craft from which he seeks the artisan, and the business agent will to try to furnish him with men suited to his requirements. This puts the business agent in a difficult position. He is an elected officer of the union, usually for one or two years, seldom more and he holds his office as much by politics as does any public servant; in other words, if he does not please, or if some other person can give better prospects in advance of pleasing he loses his lucrative job. A term of one year,

CONCLUSION 

79 or even two, is too short a time for an elective officer to accomplish much other than the immediate detailed business of his particular union. Yet there have been men who for long periods of years have been the battle axe and running executive of their organizations. Probably it is the old story that if you do not command respect you cannot receive it.

Organized labor in the building trades is working in conjunction with institutions that teach craftsmanship; in particular might be mentioned the Boston Trade School for Boys, Wentworth Institute, and Franklin Institute.

Perhaps this is because some of the keener minds in organized labor in the building trades realize that the apprenticeship system, outlined elsewhere in this paper, leaves much to be desired. The influx of new blood properly equipped from a construction viewpoint and properly imbued with the ideas of organized labor is not yet well established. Today in Boston a marked spurt in general heavy construction would find a paucity of skilled labor. 

Unhappily at the time of this writing this spurt is not visible due to unsettled financial conditions and the inability to prognosticate costs on a rising market and further inability to have a general appreciation of economic equilibrium.

Inasmuch as the future can only be prognosticated by the experiences of the past, in any important field of endeavor, it would seem that I could say: (1) that organized labor in the building trades in 

Massachusetts is here to stay; (2) that if these unions continue as of 1936 they will probably grow stronger; the attitiude of organized labor, always with the exception of the "jurisdictional” dispute, is reasonable in its dealings with the general public and with employers, and there is, on the whole, a good

feeling between the important employer of building trades labor in all its crafts and the journeyman; (3) that organized labor never has and probably never will deliver a labor vote, as such, in a democratic government, It has had and will have various vigorous public servants in all branches of the Government, that are elective, but for a mayor, or a governor, or a senator, there is no labor vote to be delivered, nor has there ever been; (4) that as technical improvements come,—and there is plenty of room for many in masonry, in plastering in particular, and in synthetic advances in scarce material substitution,-more trouble will come over the "jurisdictional" dispute in the determination of which craft shall handle it; but ultimate advancement of the cause of organized labor will result, if these disputes are handled intelligently. I mean that a "baby" industry that may have marked importance, especially in synthesis, such as Celotex, and artificial stone, can be killed off by attenuated or unfair disputes; (5) that pre-fabrication, especially in houses, must advance, though all experiments to date, which have been elaborate, do not measure up enough for a public demand, and shop work increases proportionately to field work. 

These immediately latest premises must argue that the building trades must get into shop control much more than has been their accomplishment so far in their history. 

Pre-fabrication in preference to "knocked-down” material delivered to the site will always have a danger to organized labor in that the pre-fabrication may be done under the most adverse non-union conditions; (6) that the history of the building trades in Massachusetts from the very beginning clearly shows a marked advance for the good of the men almost exclusively

CONCLUSION 

81 through the efforts of organization of the men themselves, even before the time there was a union, as I have mentioned earlier. This is evidenced by shorter hours, better wages, better working conditions, greater economic happiness and security of the employee and his family which must be reflected in the general good and the gradual admission of the idea into the public mind that labor is not a chattel. Elaborating on this idea must come the conclusion that the six-hour day, five-day week is merely awaiting the passage of a sufficient number of days to be a fact; further, that through improved technique by great institutions in the training of their architects and engineers, supplemented by experience in the great laboratory governed by trial and error, the arrival of the conditions of the six-hour day and five-day week is sure to be hastened.

The compensation in dollars for the six-hour day and fiveday week must be yet greater. This is no harder to understand today than present-day conditions would have been in 1700 to the general public, the employer, and the employee, who considered that a fair day's work was sun-up to sun-down: (7) that statutes will lean toward labor provided labor, as does not seem likely with the building trades labor, does not get itself into some conditions where it irks seriously the lethargic great majority of the non-union eighty plus per cent of the people, as it did in England. 

Social security, workmen's compensation, unemployment insurance, old age assistance, all will be elaborated, as they can be, on a firm economic basis without getting into any 

Townsend or Alberta experiment; (8) that labor must watch in its future for the socalled fakir who has done great harm in the past by preying on the crudelity of the men by his chicanery while merely exploiting them to his pecuniary or political ad-

vantage. These who cry: "Lord, Lord," shall not necessarily enter the Kingdom of Heaven, and the remainder of the parable applies; (9) that the public must be educated to forget its jokes on the plumber and his lack of tools, on the bricklayer taken for the statue on the building, and deference paid, if further successes are to be attained in a minimum of time, deliberately to influencing public opinion through a legitimate propoganda; (10) that the entrance of an accredited, fitted journeyman to a union must not be made as difficult as entrance into an exclusive social club; (11) that there must be greater cohesion among all unions to improve the strength of unionism as a whole, with distinct understanding that union men are not entitled to claim exemption from general laws, an exemption foolishly assumed by some labor organizations, but not by the building trades, in the so-called "sit down" strikes of 1936.

Organized labor is here to stay. It is a force for good. 

It needs direction, but it has proved beneficial socially and economically. It has a good goal and a fair chance of attaining it.
