WASHINGTON, one of the most beautiful cities in the world, owes its picturesque and spacious arrangement to the vision of George Washington who engaged Major L'Enfant, the skilful French engineer, to design and plan it. The numbered streets run North and South and the lettered streets East and West. Avenues, named for the states, cross them diagonally, and are intersected at intervals by Squares or Circles bearing the name of the General whose statute it holds. 

This break in the continuity is very confusing to strangers, one of whom expressed it to a trolley conductor who replied: "Yes, Miss, they drop off and pick up all over the city." Pennsylvania Avenue, the most famous thoroughfare in the country, passes the White House and turning at the 

Treasury Building gives an unbroken vista to the Capitol a mile and a half away. All the 

Inaugural parades have passed there besides all parades of peace and war.

The CAPITOL is one of the largest build ings in the world and also one of the most imposing. It occupies a commanding position on 

Capitol Hill, with extensive terraces on the west and a broad Plaza on the east. Its stateliness is increased at night when the Dome is lighted, which looks like a great jeweled crown.

The corner stone was laid by Washington in 1793. 

Entering the Rotunda, through the great bronze doors in the center of the building on the east front, the grandeur of the interior is at once apparent. On the walls hang eight large historical paintings, and the beautiful fresco of the Dome is also historic. Just below is the marvelous frescoed frieze, in self tones, on historic subjects and so wonderfully painted by the great artist, Brumidi, that it is almost impossible to believe it is not a bas relief. He died when it was about half finished. It was completed by his assistant, Costaggini, and a third artist painted an isolated panel which shows, by contrast, the remarkable skill of Brumidi, who had restored some of Raphael's work in the Vatican. Statuary Hall was originally filled with statues of outstanding figures in the different states, each state having been permitted to contribute two. Many of the statues have been removed and distributed in other parts of the Capitol to relieve the weight on the floor. At 12 noon the sessions open in the 

Senate Chamber and Hall of Representatives in the two wings and may be watched by visitors in the galleries. Along the corridors are statues and statuettes and on the walls hang portraitsall of illustrious or prominent men. 

Half way

up the stairway of the House wing is the famous painting of Lincoln and his War Cabinet with the Emancipation Proclamation.* The building is open daily from 9 a. m. to 4:30 p. m. Closed

Holidays. 

Facing the Plaza on the east front, the Inaugural exercises and other events of national importance take place. 

The LIBRARY OF CONGRESS, the larg est library building in the world, stands opposite the Capitol, across this Plaza. Its marvelous interior has won for it the reputation of being one of the most beautiful interiors in the world. * Lincoln was painted from life. Behind Lincoln (standing) is Salmon P. Chase, Secretary of the Treasury; at the left of Lincoln (seated) Edwin M. Stanton, Secretary of War; in front (seated) William H. Seward, Secretary of State; behind the table (seated) Gideon 

Welles, Secretary of the Navy; the two men standing are Montgomery Blair (the taller), Postmaster General and Caleb Smith, Secretary of the Interior; at end (seated) Edward M. Bates, Attorney General.

It is finished throughout in Italian marble, with noble columns and perfect arches; the top ceilings are in exquisitely blended frescoes and the ceiling of the first floor, at the front, has a conventional design in 22 carat gold leaf, and at the rear, in richly-toned mosaics. The visitor, on entering, should at once take the elevator to the upper floor and slowly descend the massive marble staircase to best view the marvelous architectural and artistic effects. The Library contains more than 5,000,000 volumes, while its special collections of manuscripts, music, maps, prints, Chinese and Slavic literature are of surpassing importance. The magnificent octagonal reading room, with its beautiful ceiling and rich marbles, is of special interest. In front of the steps, leading to Elihu Vedder's famous

Mosaic of Minerva, stands a case containing the first book ever printed from movable type-the 

Gutenberg Bible-printed in Mainz about 1454

A. D. For the vellum of these three invaluable volumes the skins of nearly 3,000 sheep were required. The most important objects are the originals of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution of the United States, in the main hall of the second floor. They are enclosed in a specially designed shrine and covered with double sheets of plate glass with gelatin films between for preserving them. The building is open week days, 9 a. m. to 10 p. m. Saturdays closes at 

6 p. m. Sundays 2 to 10 p. m.

The FOLGER SHAKESPEARE LIBRARY 

is back of the Library of Congress. It is the gift of Henry Clay Folger, noted Shakespearean col-

lector, who died before its completion, and left the administration of the fund to Amherst College. It is of white marble of modern type, with leanings toward the classic. The interior conforms to the Elizabethan period. It contains his entire collection of Shakespeareana, considered the most complete one in the world, including, also, contemporaneous works. The planting of the grounds harmonizes with the building in a beautiful arrangement of green turf, shrubs and trees. Open from 9 a. m. to 4:30 p. m. 

Sundays and Holidays.

Closed 

The SUPREME COURT BUILDING, on the north of the Library of Congress, is a structure of impressive dignity and a fitting home for the U. S. Supreme court. The exterior is of Vermont marble; the lining of the four courts is of 

Georgian marble and the interior of Alabama marble. The appropriation for its cost was $9,740,000. The cornerstone was laid October 

13, 1932, by President Hoover. The Supreme 

Court held its first session there Oct. 7, 1935. 

When in session the Chief Justice and Associate Justices file into the Court room at exactly 12 M. 

The MARINE BARRACKS is situated about a mile beyond the Library of Congress. 

The Marine Band gives free concerts there at scheduled times.

The NAVY YARD, a little beyond the 

Barracks, has an interesting museum of war trophies. It is open to visitors week days from 

9 a. m. to 12 m. and 1 p. m. to 4 p. m.

The NAVAL GUN FACTORY is located at the Navy Yard and is the most complete gun

factory in America. All the great guns are made there.

The UNION STATION is situated about half a mile northeast of the Capitol, and the splendid structure of white granite, facing the great Plaza, which stretches to the Capitol and is part of the Capitol grounds, is one of the most imposing and, also, one of the largest buildings in Washington. Three large arches at the front of the main entrance lead into the open vestibule, where six gigantic statues of symbolic design by St. Gaudens are arranged along the cornice above the six columns. The vast waiting room is very handsome, with its high arched roof and the finely decorated sunken panels. The passenger concourse beyond is claimed to be the largest covered one in the world and is about twice the size of the Pennsylvania Station concourse in New York City.

The POST OFFICE BUILDING occupies the next block west of the Union Station. It is built of Italian marble with architecture of the Roman type. Between the 14 Ionic columns, along the front, are bronze panels. At the eastern and western corners of the cornice is a carved inscription, taken from an original text of the late 

President Eliot of Harvard with a few happy changes by President Wilson.

The GOVERNMENT PRINTING OFFICE, one block north of the Post Office, extending from G St. to K St., is the greatest printing establishment in the world. The new annex is adjacent on the east. In its work is included the daily printing of the Congressional Record, all 10

bills introduced in Congress, and all reports of the different departments and of the Government; besides money order forms and postcards. 

Open to visitors from 10 a. m. to 3 p. m. 

Saturdays, Sundays and Holidays.

Closed 

The New BOTANIC GARDENS, extend ing about a block south of the old ones near the west foot of the Capitol, are an enchanting place with blossoming plants, ferns and green shrubs in a rare state of cultivation. The new conservatory is high enough in one part to accommodate a full-grown tree. The girders are of aluminum instead of steel. Some of the trees in the old Botanic Gardens are historic. 

The GRANT MEMORIAL MONUMENT, near by, is of heroic size and a striking statue of 

General Grant on a fiery steed. It is a remarkable example of a horse in action. The sculptor,

Henry Mervin Shrady, studied horses in all motions for two years to observe the muscular effect.

PENNSYLVANIA AVENUE, just below the Capitol grounds, holds the large PEACE 

MONUMENT, dedicated to the men of the 

United States Navy who fell in the Civil War.

FORD'S THEATRE, where President Lin coln was shot, is on 10th St., a block and a half above the Avenue. It is now called the Lincoln Museum and contains the famous Oldroyd collection of Lincoln mementos and relics recently purchased by the United States Government. 

Opposite the Museum, at No. 516, is the Petersen House, where Lincoln died. The rooms have been restored as nearly as possible to their origi- 11

nal setting and the room where he died has been reproduced exactly as it was on that night. The entire task was accomplished through the work of several women's patriotic societies, under the direction of Lt. Col. U. S. Grant, 3rd. Together with the Museum it is a real shrine. 

Lincoln's pew, in the New York Ave. Presbyterian Church at the junction with H St., and the Chapel in the basement-once the room in which Lincoln was wont to rest in solitudeare objects of interest.

The bronze statue of BENJAMIN FRANK-

Pennsylvania Ave. and 11th St.

The NATIONAL THEATRE, on E St. be tween 13th and 14th Sts., is the sixth one on the site. Among the artists who appeared at the 

Old National were Junius Brutus Booth, Edwin 

Forrest, Jenny Lind, Charlotte Cushman and 

Edwin Booth, and the last named never appeared again in a Washington theatre after the assassination of Lincoln.

The WASHINGTON POST, founded in 

1877, is on E St. near the junction with Pennsylvania Ave.

The WILLARD HOTEL, on the corner of 

Pennsylvania Ave. and 14th St., is on the site of the old Willard, which was historic. Lincoln stopped there previous to his inauguration, in 

1861, and Grant received his appointment as 12

Lieut. General there. It was there that Julia 

Ward Howe wrote the famous "Battle Hymn of the Republic." In attractive "Peacock Alley" of the New Willard, distinguished men and women often congregate.

The NATIONAL ARCHIVES building, called the world's model, stands next. It is designed to hold all archives of American history, archives bearing signatures of emperors, kings, and world famous statesmen, and others of priceless historic value. All have been scientifically cleaned and placed in vaults having an installed plant to prevent deterioration. A fine collection of movie films is also placed there. 

Opposite the splendid bronze entrance doors is at semi-circular chamber with bronze gates. Inshrined there may be the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution of the United 

States. Open week days 9 a. m. to 4:30 p. m.

The NATIONAL GALLERY OF ART, holding the munificent gift to Washington of the $50,000,000 art collection of Andrew W. Mellon, comprising paintings and sculpture by the greatest masters, will be a beautiful structure on the Mall facing on Constitution Ave. between 4th and 

7th Sts. It is of classic design with a large' dome and great pillars at the entrance. The main part will have a wing on each side with shaded 14

garden courts within. Ninety rooms are in the design with several important canvases exhibited alone the famous "Alba Madonna" having a hall of its own. Mr. Mellon and the architect conferred with the leading authorities on gallery architecture and visited the best galleries in Europe to obtain ideas on perfect lighting. There are no staircases to tire visitors. The building, costing about $9,000,000, financed by Mr. Mellon, will be the finest structure of its kind in the world. It will be finished in April, 1939.

The SMITHSONIAN INSTITUTION, founded in 1846, is situated between 7th and 12th Sts. on the MALL, which runs from the 

Capitol to Potomac Park, on the south side of Constitution Ave. It was made possible by a legacy to the United States, given for the advancement of knowledge, by James Smithson, an Englishman. There are four buildings. The

Administration building contains, in an alcove near the entrance, the sarcophagus under which the donor's remains are placed. His personal relics are in this building. 

In the OLD NATIONAL MUSEUM is seen Colonel Lindbergh's famous plane, "The 

Spirit of St. Louis." Great cases hold uniforms of distinguished soldiers; Grant's uniform and the splendid souvenirs of his trip around the world; historic flags and those of all nations; famous heir-looms and relics. On one aisle are life-size models in the dresses worn by mistresses of the White House. The same face, modeled in plaster from Connelly's bust of Cordelia, daughter of King Lear, was used on all the fig- 15

ures, but seems different through varying pose of the head and arrangement of the hair. The original is in the New National. 

The NEW NATIONAL MUSEUM has, among its collections, a large art gallery containing masterpieces of the great artists; the Roosevelt collection of mammals from the African expedition; a complete collection of birds; and a vast collection of Indian exhibits, with Indian families in groups, showing their tribal customs and modes of life.

The FREER GALLERY (the fourth build ing) was the gift of the late Charles L. Freer with his entire collection. The main feature is the unique "Peacock Room," executed by 

Whistler for the London residence of Leyland, the great shipbuilder. The entire wall decorations and the furnishings are exactly as in the 

London house. 

All buildings of the Smithsonian Institution are open week days from 9 a. m. to 4:30 p. m. 

Sundays from 1:30 p. m. to 4:30 p. m. The 

Freer Gallery is closed Mondays.

The BUREAU OF ENGRAVING AND PRINTING is located on Potomac Park at 14th 

St. S. W. The extension oposite covers two squares between 13th and 14th, C and D Sts. A tunnel connects with the old building and another with the railroad yards. A guide will show and explain all the different stages of making our currency; the several processes of preparing the plates, printing the back and the face, examning, sizing, trimming, stacking, etc. The making of stamps is also seen there. The WASHINGTON MONUMENT, that towering granite shaft of marvelous symmetry, conforming to the lines of the Egyptian obelisk, stands in the center of a small park (a section of 

Potomac Park) near the end of the Mall. It is. 

555 feet high and can be seen from every point in Washington. The cornerstone, which was laid 

July 4, 1848, with Masonic ceremonies, contains, in a zinc-lined cavity, Masonic records and other valuable papers and coins. Inside are memorial, historic stones built into the walls about halfway up. An elevator runs to the top. Open 9 a. m. to 4 p. m. Sundays and Holidays 1:30 p. m. to 4:30 p. m.

It is built of white marble and approached by terraces. Thirty-six columns-the number of states at Lincoln's death-surround the outside and around the wall, at the top, are the names of the forty-eight states. In the center of the interior is the huge statue of Lincoln by Daniel Chester

French. On each side are bronze tablets holding his Gettysburg Speech and his Second Inaugural, respectively. Open 9 a. m. to 9:30 p. m. days and Holidays 9:30 a. m. to 4:30 p. m.

The beautiful SPEEDWAY runs through 

Potomac Park, thence around Hains Point and skirts the Tidal Basin. In April, the Cherry Blossoms along the way are a glorious sight. The trees were a gift from the Mayor of Tokio to the United States Government, and part of them were planted by Mrs. William H. Taft, while mistress of the White House.

The ARMY WAR COLLEGE is situated at the Washington Barracks at the southern end of 18

the city. Its aim is to train selected officers who are believed to have the ability to profit from lectures upon the campaigns of the great masters of the past, which will fit them for high military command. The ground where the College stands once held the old Arsenal, and the Penitentiary where those who planned the assassination of 

President Lincoln were tried and sentenced, and they were hanged in the yard.

The PAN AMERICAN UNION, at 17th 

St. and Constitution Ave. N. W., is one of the most artistic structures in Washington. The architecture is of South American type and the bronze grilles at the three gates are of Spanish design. The charming patio in the center of the building is lined with beautiful tropical trees and shrubs. Columns of African marble, below, and

French marble, above, contribute to the rich effect. Among the displays are cases of brilliant 

South American butterflies and humming birds.

A beautiful Aztec garden is at the rear of the building. Open 9 a. m. to 4:30 p. m. Saturdays 

9 a. m. to 12:30 p. m. Closed Sundays.

MEMORIAL CONTINENTAL HALL, at 

17th and D Sts. N. W., was built by the National Society, Daughters of the American Revolution. The side portico holds thirteen columns for the thirteen original states. It contains an auditorium where, on one wall, hangs the famous painting of "Washington on Dorchester Heights" by Darius Cobb; side rooms containing Revolutionary relics, and several rooms furnished by the different states. Open 9:30 a. m. to 4 p. m. 

Saturdays 9:30 a. m. to 12 noon. Sundays closed. 19

CONSTITUTION HALL is connected with Continental Hall by the Administration Build ing. It has a seating capacity of 4,000 and its acoustics are among the finest in the world. The building contains a most complete and well equipped genealogical library. 

The AMERICAN RED CROSS BUILD- 

ING, at 17th and D Sts. N. W., is dedicated to the memory of the women of the Civil War. 

In the assembly hall is a three-panel memorial window of favril glass made by Tiffany at a cost of $10,000, and considered by them to be one of their finest works of art. In the basement there are interesting miniature models of Red Cross work in the World War.

The CORCORAN ART GALLERY, at 

17th St. and New York Ave., N. W., founded by

William W. Corcoran, is filled with fine collections of pictures, bronzes, marble and plaster sculpture, and other art exhibits. The most notable collection is the "W. A. Clark collection" brought from his New York residence and placed in the wing especially built for the purpose. It contains magnificent Persian rugs, rare laces, exquisite china and fine paintings-several by Corot and Casin. One room holds four large 

Gobelin tapestries on mythological subjects.

Special interest centers in the room which shows a Salon of the eighteenth century, with furniture of the period of Louis XVI, and the furniture of Louis XV. Open Mondays from noon to 

4:30 p. m., other week days from 9 a. m 4:30 p. m. Sundays and Holidays 2 to 5 p. m. Mon- 20

days, Wednesdays and Fridays, admission 25c, other days and Holidays free.

The OCTAGON HOUSE, corner of 18th 

St. and New York Ave., is interesting. It was built by the rich Virginian, Colonel John Tayloe.

When the White House was burned in 1814, it was occupied for a year by President Madison, and there Dolly Madison's most brilliant receptions were held. In the circular room on the second floor President Madison signed the Treaty of Ghent and a copy of it is seen there. Just outside the basement door the beginning of a dark, secret tunnel is found, the origin and purpose of which has never been known.

Ave.. between 17th and 19th Sts.

The WAR DEPARTMENT is occupying temporary quarters, the largest single group of which is in the Munitions building, on Constitution Ave., between 19th and 21st Sts.

The NATIONAL ACADEMY OF SCI- 

ENCES on Constitution Ave. at 21st St., is a handsome structure with fine grounds. Its function is to aid the departments of the government on scientific subjects.

GEORGE WASHINGTON UNIVERSITY 

occupies the block between 20th and 21st Sts. on G St. and was established in 1821. Among its professors are outstanding scientists connected with the Smithsonian Institution.

Pennsylvania Ave., facing Lafayette Square.

President to occupy it. It was built of Virginia 22

The superb East Room-with its dignified furnishings-which has witnessed some of the most brilliant social events in the country; the great Hall; the State Dining Room with its splendid panelings of English oak and a seating capacity of 112, and the Red, Blue and Green 

Rooms are all on the first floor. East Room and lower corridor open 10:30 a. m. to 12 m.

The METROPOLITAN CLUB, at 17th and 

H Sts. N. W., is internationally known and a gathering place for prominent men in the United States and from all parts of the world. 

The DECATUR HOUSE, facing the north west corner of the Square, was built by Commodore Stephen Decatur of Tripoli fame, in 1819.

He was killed a year later in a duel fought with 

Captain James Barron on the Bladensburg 

Duelling Field.

The WOMEN'S CITY CLUB, just below, was occupied by President Theodore Roosevelt while the White House was being remodeled. 

The DOLLY MADISON HOUSE, on the 

S. E. corner of H St. and Vermont Ave., became the property of President Madison in 1835, and Mrs. Madison lived there for several years before her death. It was used by General George 

B. McClellan as headquarters for the Army of the Potomac during the Civil War.

The COSMOS CLUB, which occupies the Dolly Madison and Tayloe houses, has an international fame and is considered one of the most unique clubs in the world. Its large membership of many thousands of men, includes those distinguished in science, art and literature, combined also with social prominence.

FORT MYER was planned to be a defense fort at the close of the Civil War, but was not equipped with guns, so was leveled and made a signal station. It was named for General Albert

The MOUNT VERNON MEMORIAL 

HIGHWAY, running along the Potomac from the Memorial Bridge through Alexandria to Mount Vernon, is an unusual piece of engineering and affords the motorist a ride of special charm. Flower beds are laid along the sides. 

ALEXANDRIA has a wealth of history to interest the visitor. Old Christ Church is the most important landmark and Washington's square pew is still there. Across the aisle is the 

Robert E. Lee pew. The old communion table, chairs, reading desk and chandelier are also there and the vestry holds other old relics. Carlyle 

House, built in 1752, was headquarters for General Braddock, who met with the Colonial Governors there during the French and Indian Wars, out of which meeting grew the plan to tax the American colonies, and a conference held there in 1785 ultimately resulted in framing the 

Constitution. At Gadsby's Tavern Washington responded to a farewell address when on his way to his first inauguration. Lafayette first met John

Paul Jones in the Tavern, where he was several times entertained. Many historic events took place there and it was the scene of brilliant social affairs. The Masonic Lodge of Alexandria is one of the most famous Lodges in America. Washington was the Charter Master.

The GEORGE WASHINGTON MASONIC NATIONAL MEMORIAL at Alexandria, Va., was erected by the Masons of America as a 39

memorial to George Washington. It will be the 

National Headquarters for all members of the

Masonic fraternity. This colossal Temple is built of granite and the eight massive columns are of Doric design. A block of white Persian marble, weighing two and one-half tons, brought from the quarry that furnished the stone for Solomon's Temple, is placed in a certain spot. 

Special features are a great amphitheatre; a library to contain a vast collection of Masonic literature; the Memorial Room, in which is placed the valuable collection of Washington's relics owned by the Washington Lodge of Alexandria, and the observatory, giving a view of the surrounding country. The cornerstone, upon which is chiseled the Masonic emblem of the Square and 

Compass, was laid by President Coolidge, No vember 1, 1923. The Memorial was dedicated 

May 12, 1932. 

MOUNT VERNON, the stately home of 

George Washington, with its charming surroundings, reached at the end of the Memorial Highway, is a vision of serene beauty and a Mecca for all Americans. Through a series of ownerships, after the death of George Washington, the place deteriorated, but was finally rescued through the efforts of Miss Ann Pamela Cunningham, of 

South Carolina, who founded the Mount Vernon 

Ladies' Association which resulted in the obtaining of funds, the purchase of the estate and the restoration to its original appearance, with most of the furniture and a large collection of valuable relics. Among the celebrated relics are a large rug, with the Seal of the United States woven in 40

the center, made in France for General Washington by order of Louis XVI; the Key of the 

Bastile, presented to Washington by Lafayette; also brass Fire-Dogs from the same donor. The most precious relic is the original Houdon bust of George Washington, made in seventeen days at Mount Vernon by the sculptor, in 1785. An interesting feature of the garden is the original box hedge around old-fashioned flower beds. A replica of Mount Vernon, with its furnishings, sent to the Exposition of French Colonies, in Paris, in 1931, received more attention than any other exhibit. Open Mar. 1 to Nov. 1, week days and Sundays 9 a. m. to 4:30 p. m. Nov. 1 to
