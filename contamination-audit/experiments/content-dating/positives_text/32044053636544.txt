The many opportunities now afforded the student of law are in striking contrast to the lack of systematic instruction in this science which prevailed in Philadelphia at the close of the Revolution and for many years after. At that period the student received his training by serving an apprenticeship with a practitioner at the bar and by an occasional attendance at court. This, supplemented by his reading, comprised his entire legal education. The breaking out of hostilities with England had shortly before put an end to the education of the sons of a few of the wealthier families at the Inns of Courts of London. 

The benefits to be derived from the discussion of difficult points arising in the course of their reading, and the advantages of associated efforts in their common pursuit, had led the students at an early date to form themselves into a society for mutual improvement. The development of legal education in Philadelphia cannot well be traced without paying a tribute to the activity of these embryo lawyers, nor without a study of the society they formed, and which, under the name of the Law Academy of Philadelphia, still exists.

The beginnings of this unique organization are found in the year 1783, when it appears to have been called the Law Society of Philadelphia. Among its members at that time were Bushrod Washington, a nephew of General

In 1789, in order that they might have a regular place of meeting, the students, who were members of the society of that time, requested and received permission to meet in one of the rooms of the College of Philadelphia. The next year, in response to the growing demand for a course of lectures upon legal topics, Charles Smith, the compiler of the early laws of the State, expressed a desire to deliver such a course before the College, and assured the trustees that he had been encouraged to expect a considerable number of pupils.'

The impetus given to the project by Mr. Smith's application, although it was not granted, was productive of good results. It was resolved by the College to establish a Professorship of Law, and James Wilson, recently appointed to the bench of the Supreme Court of the United States, was called to the chair.

The initial lecture was delivered on December 15, 1790, and was an occasion of widespread interest. President Washington and many other persons of prominence attended. But the enterprise, which began under promising auspices, had a short life. During their second course the lectures were discontinued. 

About this time the College of Philadelphia united with a rival institution, and the present name of the " 'University of Pennsylvania" was adopted. Another attempt to establish a course of lectures was not made until 1817, History of Law Department of the University of Pennsylvania by

Although denied the advantages of systematic instruction, there is little reason to believe the students and younger lawyers abandoned their own organization. Evidences of their association are found at intervals and indicate the nature of their exercises. In 1798 there was such a society, of which the officers were a Chief Justice, a Judge and a Prothonotary. One of its members was Joseph Hopkinson, who later became a Judge of the United States Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, and who was for many years one of the Vice-Provosts of the Law Academy."

Under the original name of the Law Society of Philadelphia, the organization, in 1806, assumed a more definite shape and the members engaged in their intellectual combats with renewed vigor. An admirable constitution was prepared providing for the election of a President, a Vice- President, a Secretary and a Treasurer. The avowed objects of the Society were the attainment of “improvement in the knowledge of the law and forensic eloquence." The arguments took place before the president, who delivered a written opinion at the next meeting.³

The value to the profession of an organization of this nature was appreciated by the thoughtful men at the bar, and a plan was evolved by which it might be placed upon a permanent foundation. A number of judges and lawyers in 1820 organized "The Society for the Promotion of Legal Knowledge and Forensic Eloquence." They invited the 

Law Society then existing, of which Du Ponceau had again become president, to form themselves into a Law Academy and to become an annex to the new organization and under its control. A coalition was effected and Du Ponceau was elected the first Provost of the Academy and James Gibson, Vice-Provost.4

In the meantime, as has been seen, schools for instruction in law encountered little difficulty in obtaining a footing in neighboring States. A Professorship of Law had been established in the William and Mary College as early as 1782. A school at Litchfield, Connecticut, had been founded by Judge Reeve about the same time, and had existed for fifty years, when it was replaced by the Vale Law School. The Harvard Law School had been organized in 1817, and a much smaller one at Northampton, Massachusetts, in 1823. Ten years later the number had increased to nine. In 1843 Pittsburg could boast of a Professsorship of Law. Philadelphia, which had long been noted for the eminence of its lawyers, was alone without a place where its students might receive a systematic training.

Unable to attain their desires the members of the Law Academy seem, at different times, to have added law lectures to their regular exercises. Judge Joseph Barnes had delivered a course in 1821 upon the common law and upon statute law, and in 1843 and for several years thereafter lectures were delivered each session by the faculty of the Academy and other prominent members of the profession."

The death of Du Ponceau, in 1844, at the advanced age of eighty-four years, was deeply felt by the Academy. 

Many other of the institutions of the day had felt the impress of his scholarly mind and he was mourned by the members of the American Philosophical Society, the Law

Association, and the Historical Society of Pennsylvania, of all of which he had been their presiding officer. He remained the Provost of the Academy until his death and his unabated interest in its welfare can not be better shown than by the following extract from his will:5 "Having thus disposed of my worldly effects, I take the liberty to recommend to my brethren at the bar the Law Academy of Philadelphia, that they may take it under their special protection, so as to make it as useful as possible to the progress of our noble science. Du Ponceau had on more than one occasion suggested the desirability of a union with the University. Shortly after his death a committee was appointed to inquire into the expediency of such a measure and report thereon. The committee seem unable to recommend such a course.2

Viewed in the light of subsequent events it can not be regarded otherwise than fortunate that the plan was not carried out. In the final establishment of the law school a few years later, all the advantages the students had craved were fully obtained, while in its merger with the University the independent life of one of Philadelphia's historic institutions would have come to an end. 

During the session of 1844 the advisability of conferring the degree of Bachelor of Laws upon those members of the Academy who had performed the duties of active membership for three years, and who had been one year practising at the bar, was also under consideration.³ 

But the necessity for such measures was soon to be removed. The long delayed law school was about to have an actual existence. The Board of Trustees of the University decided, in 1850, to elect a professor of law for three years, and George Sharswood, then a Vice-Provost of the Law Academy, and whose eminent abilities afterwards graced the Supreme Court of the State, was selected to fill the position. The prominence of the lecturer and the pressing need for systematic instruction at once made the enterprise a success. Other professorships were added in the next few years and this department of the University was placed upon a most secure foundation.

In accepting the chair of law of the University, Sharswood did not sever his connection with the Law Academy. 

Two months before his death, in an address before this body, he speaks of his long association with the Academy and of the value of its training to its members, in the following words: "I can say, after a long novitiate as an active member, beginning in 1828, seven years as one of the Vice-Provosts from 1838 to 1845, and now twenty-eight years as Provost, my opinion of the great importance of the institution, more especially to the younger members of the bar, is constantly increasing and strengthening.""

After the establishment of the Law School its work was effectively supplemented by the Academy, in providing a forum where the advanced student and the young practitioner might engage in argument and perfect their powers of debate. The address delivered by Du Ponceau in 1821, at the opening of the Academy, inaugurated a custom which has broadened into an annual address upon some in-teresting legal topic. These addresses have frequently been the occasion of a notable utterance by a prominent jurist or statesman. Chief Justice Sharswood, Benjamin Harris

Brewster and David Dudley Field are but a few of long line of eminent speakers who have graced these occasions.
