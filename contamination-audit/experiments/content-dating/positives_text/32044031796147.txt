AN INTRODUCTORY NOTE 

THE HOGG FOUNDATION devotes its efforts to preventive work in mental hygiene. The improvement of any human relationship has mental health significance. All such constructive efforts begin in the family but the same principles apply to community relations, including work groups.

The return, now, of two million servicemen, later of six to eight million, highlights a continuous process of family and community adjustment. Almost single-handed, wives have maintained the unity of families during the war and, now that it is over, they will help their husbands return to family life, jobs, and civic interests. Realizing this, community leaders in Austin, Texas, gladly accepted a grant of fifty-five hundred dollars from True Story magazine for a special study of what wives can do in stabilizing war-time family living. 

In connection with this project Major Kraines lectured to community workers in Austin. 

On another occasion he addressed the entire faculty of The University of Texas at the request of the Veterans' Advisory Service. Following these lectures, the Hogg Foundation asked 

Major Kraines to prepare this special article which would stress "suggestions to wives of servicemen" but, at the same time, reveal general principles of mental health in many types of human adjustment.

CONCERNING THE AUTHOR 

The author speaks from personal experience. For the past three years he has talked to thousands of enlisted men and officers, has heard their problems, has treated them, and observed the effects off that treatment. He has listened to innumerable problems of family discord and has a wealth of experience in the reaction of soldiers to their wives, to divorces, to separations and family quarrels. He has seen and treated men back from combat zones, from the 

Aleutians, Africa, Italy, France, Guadalcanal, Leyte, etc., and knows what these men think and feel, and has watched their adjustment over many months. He has interviewed wives who have lived near the camp. His military experience fully qualifies him to talk about the returning soldier.

His civilian background is comparably effective. After completing medical school in 

1930, he married. He started his career by studying at Cook County Hospital, Chicago; at 

Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore; at Boston Psychopathic Hospital, Boston; and at the 

National Neurological Hospital, Queen's Square, London, England. For nine years before entering the military service he was assistant state alienist in Illinois, taught at the University of Illinois Medical School, and has been in private practice in Chicago. 

Major Kraines has written a medical textbook, The Therapy of the Neuroses and Psychoses, published by Lea & Febiger, and, in collaboration with E. S. Thetford, has written a popular volume, Managing Your Mind, published by Macmillan & Company. 

ROBERT L. SUTHERLAND.

CHANGING PERSONALITIES 

Women, many of them, await their husbands' return from the war with a sense of relief and with the feeling that their times at home will be once more happy and secure when they and their husbands are together again. 

However, many a woman has a certain amount of apprehension, wondering whether her husband has changed, wondering how he will take to the children, and fearful lest his war experiences may have made him a "stranger." 

Whereas most married men who return will settle down to normal lives fairly quickly, it is true that combat service and even service in the noncombatant zones changes men. We must face these facts realistically. We cannot blind ourselves to these changes.

For many men these changes have been beneficial. Many have become more mature, more stable, more determined. Many appreciate more keenly the value of their homes and have vowed never to jeopardize the happiness or security of their families.

The soldier has gone through a wide variety of experiences, experiences for which he is not responsible. He has been forced to accept difficulties which have tried his very soul. He has had to knuckle down to orders, to commands, to regimentation, in a way that has made him tense and irritable. He has lived in countries far from the United States, strange and unfamiliar and at times terrifying. He has suffered for long months in the frozen climate of 

Alaska. He has sweated, ached, and sickened from the diseases of the tropical jungle. He has had little social life. He has not been able to go as he wished or where he wished. He has had little or no female companionship. He has lost many of the habits of social living which are so necessary in everyday civilian life.

These pressures have operated on even the best and most stable of men. 

Many younger men who were tied to their mother's apron strings and who were unreliable and insecure have become more determined, self-reliant, and better able to take care of themselves; but even in many of these same men there have been additional and sometimes adverse personality changes. 

These personality changes vary from the extremely mild to the extremely severe. Many men in the service have become tense and restless. They cannot sit still. They find it hard to concentrate on one subject for any period of time. 

They cannot sustain interest in any one thing. They are irritable. They get upset by minor difficulties. They have a tendency towards temper outbursts. 

One can understand these temper outbursts when one remembers that in combat zones a man must be ready constantly when danger strikes. He must be ready to react immediately to danger if he is to save his life. He brings this same tendency back with him after the war, and the combat-necessary readiness for response manifests itself in temper outbursts. 623035 -3-

For the most part soldiers realize these personality changes. They do not wish to be irritable and distressed; and yet many of them initially, almost literally, cannot help themselves. They have been trained too specifically for too long a period of time. However, they can change again and they do change and return to normal if certain basic principles are followed. 

Many of the men coming back may have physical symptoms. Many will have visible injuries to their arms, legs, or faces-injuries which are not only physically incapacitating but which also cause embarrassment. The psychological complications of such wounds often alter a man's whole outlook on life and thus change his marital status. 

A man may have internal injuries in abdomen, chest, back, legs, and find it difficult to work steadily. He may have aches and pains. He may have recurrent attacks of malaria and other fevers, all of which may incapacitate him and all of which definitely influence his marital adjustment. 

He may have a group of symptoms which are technically called psychosomatic. These symptoms consist of headaches, pains in the chest, pains over the heart, pains in the stomach, loss of appetite, pains in the legs and arms, inability to sleep, loss of weight, all of which are not the result of physical injury but of nervous tension. 

These nervous symptoms are brought about in this way: The brain becomes upset and excited because of the chronic nervousness and sends many impulses down all the nerves connected to the brain. When these impulses go to the heart, the heart may beat fast, palpitate, and have pains. When these impulses go to the stomach the stomach may go into a spasm and create nausea or vomiting.

To the soldier, these symptoms, even though on a nervous basis, seem to be physical. It is hard for him to accept the fact that treatment must be of the nervousness and not of the heart or the stomach or the chest or the intestines. 

The soldier comes back with attitudes towards civilian life which make him upset and unhappy about things at home--about civilian 4-F's who didn't fight and got high wages, about civilian complacency concerning this war when he remembers so many of his buddies dying and being injured, about the fact that he cannot get as good wages as the men who stayed behind. 

These will increase his nervous irritability and he may "take it out on" his wife and children.

The soldier's attitude toward marriage itself usually changes. For most men, particularly those who have been married for a long period of time, there is greater affection for the wife and children, greater determination to make the most of the family, greater appreciation of what love and affection mean.

Some men go to an extreme. They idealize their wives. Such a man will remember only her virtues and will set her upon a pedestal. Noble as is this idea, it has in common with all other unrealistic evaluations the danger of boomeranging. When a soldier with such ideals comes home and finds his wife to be a human being with faults, with habits and emotional responses for which he may not care, his ideal may suddenly crash. He may find himself -43.

going to the other extreme, hating his wife, becoming unduly disgusted or disturbed by her ordinary limitations. 

Then, too, he may have heard many rumors and tales concerning the unfaithfulness of wives. Unfortunately, some men take this exaggerated gossip seriously and become suspicious even of their own wives. They become sullen and irritable. As the result of frequency of these stories, some few lose all faith in marriage and family loyalty. It is unfortunate that such feelings should be aroused, and although most married men are not affected by these rumors, there will be a few who are. 

The attitude of the returning soldier toward marriage will also be determined in part by the adjustment he had made to it before the war. If he knew his wife for any period of time, got to know her faults and to appreciate her virtues, if he learned to give as well as to take, if he learned self-control which is essential to a happy marriage, then the chances of his adjustment to his family after the war are good. 

If on the other hand he was unhappy, constantly fault-finding, disgusted with his wife, before entering the service, the increased irritability which often develops as a result of combat service may make it even more difficult for him to adjust. This difficulty is often true in the so-called "war marriages" where the soldier had not yet learned to appreciate the qualities of his wife and to overlook or live with her human failings. 

FAMILY PLANNING 

Marriage will best succeed when the wife takes it upon herself to make most of the initial adjustments. It is recognized that the husband has a responsibility. It is important that the husband make every effort to control and change himself.

Little success in adjustment may be expected if the woman demands that most of the personality adaptation to family and community living be done by the husband. If she is willing and able to understand why her husband is the way he is and is willing and determined to do all in her power to restore him to his normal, pre-army state, then she is prepared to make the necessary effort.

First, A Vacation 

Several weeks of vacation is an advisable "first thing to do" when the soldier returns. During this time he should not seek work. He should lie around the house as late as he pleases. He should do as nearly as possible what he wishes, within the bounds of reason. He should visit his friends, and visit old surroundings. He should talk freely to his wife. 

During this period the wife should be particularly receptive and should. make every effort to go out of her way to make him feel at home and welcome. 

If he has troubles, she should listen to them and sympathize with him. If he -53-

has stories to tell about his experiences, she should be intensely interested. 

She should not dwell upon her own problems. She should dismiss them lightly for the time being. These can be dealt with later. The husband must be made to feel happy that he is back home. 

After the first week or two, there should be discussion of the mutual problems which face the family. Then plans should be made for the family's future. In this vacation period the accent should be upon free, friendly discussion. Even when the husband is irritable and impatient, the wife must make every effort not to sulk, not to feel offended, not to be irritable herself, but to lead him back to a mutual understanding. 

Danger lies in a vacation that is too long. The ex-soldier does need to reëstablish habits of work, and it is important that he have restored to him the initiative which too many men have lost in the Army. After several weeks, varying in each individual case, the returned soldier should work out his future plans.

Plans for the Future 

Plans for the future will, of course, vary with each case. The wife should find out all she can and get all the literature possible about the opportunities open to the returned military man. Some may wish to go on with school work and education. Others may want to take training in various specialties and trades. Still others will want to go to work in certain industries which they think have a future.

As many of the facts about these possibilities as are available should be secured by the wife and understood by her before the soldier comes home so she can discuss them with him intelligently. Government departments, the different social agencies, the various local groups in each city will be able to supply the material. 

SPECIAL PROBLEMS 

Work may be a difficult adjustment to make. Because many men are unsure of themselves and uncertain about their own desires, they may try several jobs before finding the one that they like. 

Moreover, it may be difficult for men to take orders in industry or business so markedly different from military commands, to be treated as civilians, to have a definite standard for efficiency. Some men may become angry, upset, and irritated by their jobs. They may quit "in a huff"; or they may come home and "take it out on the wife."

Wives should be their husband's closest friend and most interested fellowplanner. They may not be the smartest persons in the world but they have their husbands' interests at heart more than anyone else.

When the husband is irritable, wives must not become irritable. They must remember that his irritability is only a symptom, that he is torn internally - 6-

by war experiences and upset by the difficulty in adjustment. He does not mean to be angry with his wife as a person. The little faults that he finds to pick on are not the real basis for his fault finding. 

Patience will help his irritability pass. Telling all about his experiences at work should be encouraged. Real interest in his problems should be evident. 

Discussion with him as to what he does and what his bosses do, what the other men in his plant do will help him to clarify his feelings. 

Talking in this way does two things. It lets off steam for the soldier and it also offers the opportunity for a sound sharing of points of view. It will often be true that the wife will be tired from her housework at the end of the day, that the children may have upset her, and that she may not be feeling too well physically. Under such circumstances it will be difficult to be calm, rational and forgiving. When the husband is upset too, the wife must be strong enough to control her own feelings and help guide him to a better outlook. If the husband, struggling toward adjustment, has such help he will find his place more quickly and begin to earn the family's bread and butter with a minimum of delay.

Social Life 

Many soldiers have become very sensitive and shy in the Army. They live in barracks or in camps in the presence of a large number of men. The situation is analogous to that of a person who lives in a big city among thousands of people but who knows no one, not even his next door neighbor. 

Even more important, men lose the finesse of social contacts. Being away from mixed groups and the company of wholesome and normal women, he may have forgotten the usual social amenities. He may have become shy and sensitive in the presence of large groups of men and women and wish to be by himself.

This tendency to be sensitive and shy must be overcome if the soldier is to live a normal life. However, he should not be forced into large groups since he may react violently and become more irritable and upset. It is wisest, therefore, in the beginning to have him meet only a few friends. The visits should, where possible, be in company with the wife. They should be fairly frequent and initially should be confined to small groups. 

It is unfortunate that too many friends of good intention but with little judgment may irritate the soldier by well meaning but unwise questions. 

As time goes by, the soldier should be encouraged to go to parties and larger gatherings. It may be difficult sometimes for the wife to go out with her husband because there is no one to stay with the children. Some arrangements for the children, however, should be made if it is at all possible. If there is no relative to stay, arrangements might be made with a neighbor who has a similar problem, in exchange for freeing her from her own children for a night every now and then. Every effort should be made to see to it that the returned husband mixes with other people. Participation in normal community life should be slow at first and extended to larger groups later. • 713-

Because so many returned men do not have much patience, card games which require a great deal of concentration should be avoided. He should choose for himself the kind of entertainment he wishes. When parties are attended, they should not be the wild and uncontrolled but decent, average human gatherings.

If, in these groups, he wants to talk about his experiences, he should talk and his audience should show intelligent interest. If he does not want to talk, he should neither be urged nor coaxed.

Physical Defects 

Many of the soldiers may have physical defects. Securing a sound and definite medical opinion, both as to the nature of the illness and as to what to do about it, is the first thing to do. If the soldier was not given clear information when he left the Army, he needs to go to the nearest Veteran's Hospital, or to his local physician if he prefers, and obtain definite advice as to what needs to be done.

When a man has a physical symptom which may take a long time in healing, he is likely to make the mistake of shopping around to see many different doctors in the hope that one may effect a miracle cure. Such shopping around is not only expensive and unwise but as the old saying goes, "Too many cooks spoil the broth." If he is in doubt, two or three doctors may be consulted and if they agree as to the diganosis and recommendations, then these should be followed as conscientiously as possible. It may take time to clear up the symptoms. 

In the meantime he has family responsibilities and a future to establish and will have to get to work on these. 

Where a man has defects which make him feel embarrassed, it is important that he be helped over his sensitivity. Above all, the man must be honest with himself. He must understand and accept his defect and not be ashamed to be with people. He must not think of himself constantly as an object of pity. 

He wants to be treated as a normal person by his wife, and by everyone else. 

Frequently he is overly sensitive about his problem. He may be helped by giving him reassurance, by treating him as a normal person, and by encouraging him to mingle with other people, even though he may be shy and backward about his defect.

Nervous Symptoms 

Some men, as we have mentioned above, have physical symptoms due to nervousness. Such cases are often the hardest to treat. This husband must learn to live with these symptoms. If, after thorough medical examination, no real physical basis is found for his complaint, he must learn and his wife must help him learn that the cure of the stomach or heart pains or the backaches can come about only by the cure of his emotional problem, his nervous tension. Physical nervous symptoms can be treated in psychiatric clinics if they are available. -83-

The wife herself under such circumstances must at all costs avoid complaining and nagging. She must give the soldier a great deal of sympathy and reassurance, although the sympathy must not be maudlin or coddling. 

It must be the kind which both understands the pain the man suffers and at the same time encourages him to fight it. The wife can do a great deal by such encouragement to improve her husband's symptoms which result from nervousness. In most soldiers who suffer from psychosomatic complaints a cure gradually comes about within a year or two, provided he has a normal type of life, a decent job, and a happy home. 

Common Personality Changes 

The most important personality changes which occur, in varying degrees of intensity, are those of irritability, restlessness, impatience, and difficulty in concentration. These changes are the result of experiences in military service, and time is necessary in their cure. Given enough time (the period may be from a few months to a few years), the man will more nearly achieve the type of personality he had before he went into service.

In addition to the time element, there are two other important conditions conducive to improvement of personality. The first is that life be as normal as possible. It is for that reason we have discussed above the question of social adjustment, the problem of work, the method of handling his physical defects, and his nervous symptoms. If the environment is normal, the soldier himself will tend to become normal. 

How his wife reacts to him is the third important factor in the soldier's readjustment and improvement of personality. Probably the wife will be the most important of all factors. The soldier, of course, must exert every possible effort to return to normal control; but the wife should go on the assumption that it is primarily her responsibility to help him get well. In so doing, she will be giving him the greatest her love for him can offer.

Thus, if the husband is irritable and impatient, if he flies off the handle, snapping back at him in an irritable fashion should never take place. It must be remembered that his irritability is not caused by his wife. It is the experiences he has been through that are cropping out. True, he should learn selfcontrol. Learning takes time. Irritability in others will increase his. If, on the other hand, the wife remains self-controlled, calm, and understanding, he will have an example before him which he can follow. Sometimes his temper will get the better of him and he may have a violent outburst of emotion. Whenever possible, especially when he first comes home, there should be very little criticism. Most men are immediately sorry for such emotional outbursts, and do what they can to make up for them, especially when the wife remains calm and truly understanding.

If the soldier is impatient and restless, he should not be forced into a mold which is regarded as normal. He must work off his extra energy. If he wishes to get up suddenly and go out for a walk, he should go and if possible his wife should go along with him. If he is at a card game and gets tired of if 90

it, no effort should be made to force him to continue. Sometimes in movies he may wish to get up before the picture is finished and leave. 

The returned husband knows that these things are not normal for him, but when he gives in to them it is because at first he is impelled to, more or less. 

He should have his way about it. Criticizing and moralizing will not help. 

Sermons and preaching may increase tension. He knows what is right. As long as he has sympathy, encouragement, and reassurance, this restlessness will wear off.

If sometimes he accuses his wife of not managing right, of flirtations while he was gone, he should be answered. He should be told the truth of the situation, but anger should not be allowed to enter the discussion. The woman must again realize that his anger and irritability are not too much his fault. She must realize that if she maintains self-control, he will recover. If, on the other hand, she becomes violently angry or holds grudges, she may actually ruin the marriage. 

If he has many hatreds towards civilian life, toward the Army, toward his friends, and different groups, his hate is an expression of his irritability. 

Things have been and are wrong in this country and do need correction. But he should be given counsel in which he will be interested. How to change those things that are wrong without blind hate and intense emotion which neither achieve his ends nor benefit his own emotional stability is what he must learn. The woman has always been known as the constructive member of the family. Whether this notion is fact or fiction as far as history is concerned, she can do a great deal to overcome the hate in this world through counsel, wisdom, gentleness, and understanding.

If his personality is such that he has lost his initiative, it is important he be encouraged. He must redevelop gradually a sense of civilian responsibility. 

He must make more and more effort to find a better job and build a better home. Such a change in his personality can come about not through preaching or nagging but through encouragement, through patient understanding, through sympathetic discussion. It will take a great deal of time in many instances; but the moral support, the faith in the husband is the key to success. 

Above all, it should be remembered that it will take time for him to return to normal. If little or no change in him is observable in the first few weeks or months, there is no reason to become impatient. In time he will be all right, provided he leads a normal life, and provided his wife gives him a tremendous amount of sympathy, reassurance, and encouragement. 

Marriage Problems 

Special marriage problems will arise which need careful handling. Although the men have been away from feminine companionship for a long period of time, experience has shown that their actual sexual desires have not increased appreciably. Upon return, for a few days and sometimes for a few weeks, there is a greater pressure of sex desire, but it very quickly returns to the usual and normal level. -10-

In a number of instances a wife may suspect or a husband may tell that he has had extra-marital relations. It is important for her to understand just what the significance of these relations are. For the most part they do not represent any change in affection towards the wife. Usually under the influence of alcohol, under the pressure from comrades, a sexual incident may occur and have no more permanent meaning for the man. In consequence, wives should do their utmost to curb their jealousy and should avoid recriminations and accusations.

Husbands are usually loyal and devoted even though there has been an occasional misstep during their period of service. The wife must understand, and what is more important, it is better not to discuss the matter. It should be dismissed entirely as something apart from their marriage. This need for tolerance and understanding is especially true in view of the many stories which a soldier hears about women. 

If he is suspicious of his wife, he should be answered honestly and frankly.

Gossip is a dangerous thing, and there are many persons of evil tongue, who have created false suspicions in the minds of men away from home. Understanding that he never was this way before the war and that his suspiciousness is not an index to his true feelings but merely a reflection of the stories he has heard and of his own irritability must be attained. "Return to mother" is an immature and childish reaction. Sulking and remaining angry or brooding is equally as bad. Firmness and objectivity without anger are indicated. 

Only a very few men will have such suspicions about their wives. It is in these cases that control of emotion, plus sympathy and understanding of his irritability will help dissipate his temporary feelings. 

Far more important in marriage than the sex drive is the husband's need for moral support. He has to feel that there is someone upon whom he can lean. Although a woman, the so-called weaker sex, in reality during these days after the war, the wife is the one who must be the tower of strength. 

When disappointments come and difficulties arise, she must be calm, objective, and rational. Even more, she must encourage and stimulate her husband to work and adjust so that he, already suffering from the strain of combat, will not be tempted to quit altogether. If the wife is stable, mature and affectionate, not only will his own strength soon return and his nervousness disappear, but she will again be able to lean on him. 

Let me again reassert that although husbands should make great efforts to adjust themselves, the wives must make the greater effort. Children

Children will be a source of difficulty in some families. Sometimes the soldier will be irritable with them. He will expect of them actions and emotions which they may not have. Sometimes the children may be frightened by him and upset over his irritations. Sometimes they will irritate him by their crying or by making demands. - 11 -

When such things occur, the situation should be smoothed over as quickly as possible. Do what you can to pacify the situation.

Long talks with the husband about the little things the children have done will offer relief. He should be told in detail their actions and reactions and his advice asked on methods of rearing them. Picnics and gay outings for the whole family, children and all, may well be arranged. He should be encouraged to play games with the children. Asking him to buy little things or help make toys for them to play with offer possibilities for understanding. 

On the other hand, the children should be told nice things about their father. They should be encouraged to expect much from him. Children will care for their father only when he manifests his affection, but in cases of the returned soldier it is important that the children show their affection as well. 

What Makes a Successful Marriage? 

Initially, a factor called love enters into the picture. There are many definitions of love; but after the honeymoon phase is over, the man and woman remain happily married only when certain other elements enter into their relationship. Basically there must be a mutual understanding between them, an understanding of each other's abilities and values as well as faults. There must be an appreciation of what the other person wishes, how the other person feels. This appreciation must be backed not only by a tolerance, a feeling of forgiveness and even a lack of irritation at those habits which we would dislike in others, but on the positive side there needs be an actual effort to try to satisfy the mate's wishes. 

In truly happy marriages each person does his utmost to give up his or her own wishes in an effort to please the partner. The idea of "fifty-fifty" in happy marriages does not exist. There is no measurement of how much one gives and how much one receives. The person who has a strong feeling of affection gives everything in his or her power and gains his happiness when he sees that his wife (or husband) has been pleased.

Mutual sharing of problems is a must in marriage. Events of the day which are of interest should be discussed so that both the man and woman know what the other experiences. Mutual interest must be built up in the life of the other. Not only are joys shared; but problems are shared and in the same way, solutions to difficulties are arrived at not by the decision of one person or the other but by mutual agreement after common discussion. 

When one is blue, the other, even though blue himself, does everything in his power to create cheer. When one is weak and tired and despondent, he nevertheless attempts to be strong for the other. Whenever one contemplates an act or a decision, he thinks first of what his mate would like.

When irritations arise and tempers get out of hand, the partner does not respond with irritation but with sympathy, understanding and encouragement. 

These are the essence of truly happy marriages. 

These essences of a happy marriage can be cultivated only by the person himself maturing and modifying his own character in countless thousands of -12 ...

daily reactions. These essences which make for a durable and contented marriage do not imply weak sentimentality. They imply a strong and active devotion.

If wives of the returning soldiers wish their marriages to remain on a solid plane, if they wish their children to grow up in an atmosphere of harmony and understanding, then they must be the first to give and not count on receiving. Love that is basic and mature gives all and is rewarded by the pleasure it creates.

Conclusion 

Ideal adjustment for the returning soldier implies that he must, himself, make a great effort to reorient his personality. He must set his own goals as to what he wishes and expects of himself and he must learn to practice both self-control and self-reliance. The Army is planning orientation courses for the returned soldier to help him get started on the road to civilian living. 

He may also avail himself of the various facilities set up by local agencies. 

A near perfect adjustment requires that the social situation be such that there be jobs open for him at a respectable "living wage" and that he be able to support his wife without having to resort to public support or bread lines. 

Such factors are beyond the control of the individual except as he expresses himself through his representatives in Congress. In a similar fashion his adjustment will be influenced by the provisions for medical aid, particularly where there have been physical or psychosomatic difficulties. 

However, from the point of view of the home, the wife will probably be the most important single factor in the adjustment of her returned husband. 

Granted that she too, being human, has many failings and cannot keep herself under complete emotional control, granted that she has difficulties in managing the household and the children and possibly has physical disturbances, it is nevertheless a fact that the more she can control her emotions and guide her husband the more successful will the marriage be and the quicker will be his return to normal. Some women may be able to approach the ideals set forth in this paper with 75 per cent perfection. In other instances, because of adverse circumstances, the wife may be only 10 per cent efficient in not becoming irritable when her husband is. Even when the woman isn't perfect in her self-control, she is helpful to the extent that she understands and guides herself. It is for this reason that it is important for her to assume the attitude that she must do everything to help her man make an ideal husband. 

Some Suggestions to Wives

The whole problem of the returning soldier is so complex and has so many individual variations, that it is difficult to encompass everything in one short discussion. In all cases, the individual, his preferences and dislikes must be considered. However, it may be said that the average veteran has benefited by being in the Army in (1) greater maturity, (2) in better comprehension of -13-`

what he wishes out of life, and (3) in greater appreciation of family and home.

Many men, nevertheless, return more irritable, restless, disconcerted. Many men have physical injuries which, in addition to their discomfort, produce feelings of inferiority and unhappiness. Many suffer from a psychoneurosisa technical term for "nervousness."

The main job of readjusting must be done by the men themselves. But if the wife wishes to help him adjust, she must learn to make herself a more perfect wife. She needs to exert a greater effort than ever before to make her marriage a happy one. The following rules are only general. Common sense must be applied in each case. (1) After leaving the Army, your husband will need a short vacation. (2) Discuss with him, in detail (do not argue), what he wishes to do, and how he needs to proceed. Get all the information you can, now, on education, jobs, and other things your husband writes about. (3) If he has trouble at work, listen to him, sympathize with him, be tolerant of his faults. A husband needs someone who believes in him. He needs encouragement, and moral support. (4) If he is irritable and scolds you, remember that nine times out of ten, he's just letting off steam. Be patient, understanding, and return a soft answer.

He may be shy and avoid company. Let him do what he wishes. 

Talk it over with him. He should mingle with people, since being a hermit is dangerous, but let it come about gradually. (6) If he has a physical defect, don't seek one doctor after another. Too many cooks spoil the broth. Pick out a good physician in whom you have confidence, and stick to him. Do what he says. Treat your husband as a normal man in his interests and activities. Avoid both extremes of pampering or of forcing. (7) If he has physical complaints which the doctors say come from "nerves," he may be very upset and seek vainly to find some physical cause. 

Physical pains due to "nerves" can hurt worse than pains due to disease or injury. The first problem he must conquer is that of his "nerves." Be sympathetic and reassuring. Encourage him, lead (don't drive) him to work and to recreational interests to divert his attention from his body. Make him laugh. (8) If he has personality changes, be tolerant of him. Avoid criticisms.

He knows when he does something wrong, and he is sorry for what he has done, even though he may say nothing. Do not let yourself get irritable. Be understanding. He will live up to your expectations much more quickly than if you "nag." (9) If he is angry with others, teach him tolerance. Hate begets hate.

Hate disturbs the smooth functioning of the body. And the only way to teach tolerance to someone else is to be tolerant yourself. Set the example. -14-

(10) Sex life for the most part will be normal. During the first few days, sex activity may be more frequent than usual but it quickly returns to its prearmy rhythm. Whatever you do, try to avoid being jealous. Treat his jealousy with tolerance, dignity, and understanding. He'll get over it. (11) Most fathers will love and enjoy their children. If he is somewhat irritable with them, talk to him about them. While he is in the Army, write him all the little things they do-good or bad. Discussion clears away most difficulties.
