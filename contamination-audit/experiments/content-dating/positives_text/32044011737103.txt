INTRODUCTION 

The growth of the petroleum industry as a whole within the United States during the past seventy-five years has been so enormous as to make it not only the nation's second largest but to give it world dominance in its own field. This has been due partly to the inexhaustible and easily accessible petroleum resources of this country, and partly to the permanency, efficiency and enterprise of those engaged in the industry itself. 

The success which has attended a combination of these factors has at all times engaged the attention of many thousands of outsiders who have rather enviously watched the achievement of such favorable results and sought to share in them themselves. In fact, most of these large and successful operations have been made possible only by the combined co-operation and investments of hundreds of thousands of just such outsiders who have had the vision and the courage to recognize and take advantage of such opportunities while still available and been able and willing to purchase the stocks, bonds and other securities of such enterprises while they were still young and disposed to offer attractive inducements in return for such capital. These great outlays of capital, however, have largely gone into the "working end" of the industry-into the drilling of wells, the marketing and transportation of oil. Most of such investors have, however, quite overlooked another equally great and in some respects even greater opportunity for safe and profitable investment in the industry. This is known as the "royalty interest," which is represented by the oil royalty owned and held by the oil land owner himself. It arises, not through the petroleum company's "working interest" or through the sale of its securities, but directly through the ownership of the oil land. The profits from this particular investment branch of the petroleum industry is estimated to have already equalled those derived from the other perhaps and better known branch. Since the oil royalty has practically the same liquidity as the oil security, with practically all and often more of the advantages of such security and with even less risk, the royalty investment business has within very recent years grown into one of the most important and serious/ avenues of profit for the outside investor anxious to share directly and permanently in the success and financial returns of the petroleum industry. A large and honorable class of royalty dealers has grown up in this branch of the industry which, with notable and obvious exceptions, has been of great service to investors, especially those inexperienced in the business themselves and not wholly familiar with it, its possibilities and dangers. These dealers as a whole have at the same time reflected great credit to the petroleum industry itself. It is the aim of this little work to present both sides of the royalty picture- "to hold the mirror up to nature," so to speak-so that royalty purchasers and dealers alike may be able to clearly see and understand not only the favorable and advantageous phases of the subject but also its drawbacks and limitations.

PETROLEUM ROYALTY HANDBOOK Petroleum Defined CHAPTER I HISTORY OF PETROLEUM 

Petroleum, or crude oil, as it is more often called, is a mineral substance found within or connected with sedimentary or water-formed structures in the earth, of somewhat mysterious origin, but generally believed to be the result of decomposition of organic matter, vegetable or animal or both, which has later developed into hydrocarbons and other elements producing petroleum. It is one of the most widely distributed substances in nature, ranking alongside water itself, and is found in varying quantities in almost every part of the earth. It is not usually found at its places of actual origin but by forces not fully understood has migrated to other regions and accumulated in porous formations and other natural subterranean pools and reservoirs.

Our World Supremacy 

At all times during its modern commercial history, the United States has been by far the largest and most important petroleum-producing country. Statistics indicate that out of 24,125,087,451 barrels already produced, inclusive up to January 1st, 1934, the United States has contributed 15,683,597,000 barrels, while of the world's present annual output of 

1,417,420,000 barrels, the United States has supplied 898,874,000 barrels for the year 1933. In other words, the United States produces two-thirds of the world's supply of petroleum.

Reasons for Our Leadership 

Our world-dominance of petroleum has been the result of many different reasons, among which may be mentioned our very much wider and extensive petroleum resources and their more scientific and intensive development; their greater accessibility to market; our greater mechanical genius and higher efficiency in developing methods of production, transportation, manufacture and marketing of the product in the industry as a whole, and our more favorable system of land laws and highly developed competitive methods which have enabled us to exploit our petroleum resources absolutely free and unhindered.

CHAPTER II OIL ROYALTY INTEREST What Oil Royalties Cover 

As a rule, the oil royalty shares not only in all oil and gas ever discovered, but also in all other mineral substances found under the land. These are important factors in nearly all the oil-producing states. 

In California many of the precious metals and other valuable mineral products have been found in and around oil fields. In Kansas large deposits of coal, building stone and other minerals are found. In Oklahoma many rare substances have been discovered in commercial quantities. In Texas alone there are said to be 84 such valuable mineral substances, some of which are found in and around the oil fields in enormous quantities, including gold, silver, quicksilver and precious stones. The oil royalty shares in everything under the surface of the ground all the way down, regardless of depth or discovery. It also shares in the potential and increased profits through the discovery and application of all new and scientific improvements in the methods of securing and recovering more of the oil from oil-producing formations. 

It also shares in all bonuses, rentals and renewals paid upon the oil lease up to the time of oil discovery, which often in themselves are quite large amounts, and then actually shares in the oil. It is not affected by any changes in ownership of either the land itself or the oil lease, nor by bankruptcy, assignment, receivership or other misfortunes of either the landowner or lease holder. Not even the sale or other transfer of the oil lease affects it, but, on the contrary it is. usually benefited very greatly thereby, as valuable oil leases almost always pass eventually into the hands of large oil companies where they usually remain for final oil development. 

Dual Ownership of Oil Lands 

It must constantly be borne in mind all through these pages and in fact, at all times in dealing with oil royalties that the entire petroleum industry is directly founded upon a dual or double interest or ownership in oil lands. These two interests are known as the "working interest, represented by the oil lease, and the "royalty interest" which is the interest retained by the oil land owner when he grants such "working interest" to such company or operator. It must also be carried in mind constantly that these two interests are at all times, not only separate from each other, but that they are undivided. Neither interest owns either any particular part of the oil land or any specific part of the oil. It is only when the oil has been produced and sold and the proceeds divided that either interest becomes entitled to any specific part or portion. 

The Working Interest 

The working interest is represented by the oil lease, which is the special contract made between the oil land owner and the oil company or oil lease broker, by which permission is given to come onto the oil

land and explore and develop its oil resources. This is considered the basic and underlying asset of the entire oil business. It is in many respects similar to a deed to a piece of land. Just as a house cannot possibly be built without a plot of ground, nor such a plot acquired without a deed, so an oil well cannot be drilled without a tract of land to drill it on, nor such a tract of land obtained without an oil lease. Such leases are almost always granted upon a "royalty basis," which means that when they are developed and oil begins to be produced, the land owner must be paid a percentage of the returns derived from the sale of such oil. 

The Royalty Interest 

Before an oil land owner gives an oil lease upon his land, naturally, he owns eight-eighths of the land, which includes not only its surface but everything under it, "to the center of the earth," as the law books express it. This underground ownership includes all oil and gas that may be beneath the land. Even though such oil has not been produced or even known to actually exist, it is still owned by the oil land owner just the same. When the oil land owner gives an oil lease or creates a "working interest" he disposes of whatever part of such oil is expressed in such lease and retains the balance. Usually, the working interest is seveneighths of the oil, and the royalty reserved one-eighth of such oil, although these shares may be in any other ratio, either more or less. In most oil fields they are seven-eighths and one-eighth, respectively. In 

California they are usually four-fifths and one-fifth, respectively. It is all a matter of custom or private agreement between the parties. The royalty interest retained by the oil land owner is of the same perpetual, never-ending character as all his land was originally, with the sole exception that he has given up a seven-eighths or four-fifths or other working interest to an oil company or oil lease broker.

Difference Between Oil Royalties and Oil Leases 

Oil royalties and oil leases have absolutely nothing in common, further than the fact that they cover the same tract or tracts of lands. 

The oil lease is made for a certain specified terms of years and covers solely the necessary provisions as to the drilling of oil wells, the marketing of oil and the division of its proceeds upon sale. Under it, all necessary expense and incident risk must be borne by the oil operator or "working interest." The oil royalty, upon the other hand, has neither limitation of term nor expiration date but remains in full force and effect perpetually and forever. It requires no oil development work to be done or the payment of any bonuses, rentals, renewals or other expenses or charges whatsoever, further than small oil production taxes in Oklahoma alone after oil has begun to flow. 

Classes of Royalties 

Oil royalties have been classified in various ways, such as "term" royalties, or those which run for a definite period of months or years and then terminate and return to the original land owner; "over-

riding" royalties, or those which are carved out of the oil lease instead of the oil land itself, and which are deducted from the oil lease and share and expire with such document; "perpetual" royalties, or those which continue for all time; "graduated" or "sliding scale" royalties, which are those where the amount of the royalty to be paid may increase or decrease under certain specified conditions. Of these various types of royalties, the "perpetual" royalty is considered the most desirable and the one most commonly used. Oil royalties are also classed as either "producing" or "non-producing," the former referring to those where oil is actually being produced, where the latter, also known as "undeveloped" royalties, cover lands as yet undeveloped and not producing oil.

Producing Oil Royalties 

Until oil is discovered, the oil royalty remains a "non-producing" or "undeveloped" royalty. When oil is discovered, such oil royalty by such oil discovery immediately becomes a "producing" royalty. In the early stages of oil production while the first flows of oil are at their height, they are known as "flush" royalties, while the flow of oil itself is termed the "flush production." As these first "gusher" flows begin to go down, these separate interests become "semi-flush" production royalties. After the oil flows have gone down to an average normal yield which may vary but slightly from time to time, they become known as "settled" production royalties. In time as the oil flows begin to visibly decrease in volume, they become known as "semi-settled" production royalties. 

Non-Producing Royalties 

Such royalties, also often called "undeveloped" royalties, are those which cover interests in lands not yet developed or producing any oil. The relationship between the two forms of royalty is similar to that of a deed to vacant real estate which is afterwards improved and made income productive. The non-producing royalty is the forerunner of the producing royalty. It is the "producing royalty of tomorrow." Such non-producing royalties may be immediately adjoining oil-producing lands, when they are termed "off-set" royalties. Or they may cover lands further away from such oil-producing lands but within the same general oil-producing district, when they are known as "close-in" royalties. Or they may be very far away from such oil-producing districts in which case they are said to be "wild-cat" royalties. 

Extreme cases of such kind are classed as "rank" wild-cats. The royalty directly attached to oil-producing land is called the "underthe-well" royalty.

Nature of Royalty Interests 

The essential nature and characteristics of royalty interests are that they originate with and grow directly out of the oil land ownership and not out of the working interest or oil lease; that being parts

of the oil land itself they are considered as in the nature of real estate and therefore can only be transferred or conveyd by some form of real estate deed, as distinguished from the working interest or oil lease which is transferred, as a personal property right, by assignment, bill of sale or other form for transferring such right; that it is always an undivided interest in all the land and in the oil under that land and never a specific, set apart interest; that it bears no part of the risk or expense incident to the development of the oil land, all of which is borne by the working interest; that it is usually perpetual and never-ending in duration, whereas the working interest or oil lease commonly runs for a stipulated number of years until oil is produced and then at most only as long as oil is being produced; that it cannot be affected by bankruptcy of the original owner for the reason that a farmer cannot be forced into bankruptcy under the law, as is the case with all oil companies or operators; that it may be divided indefinitely into smaller interests without impairing its inherent character, as is not the case with the working interest which would thereby be reduced so low in value as not to justify the profitable development of the oil land; that its return is always paid in oil, rather than in money; that upon the death of the holder of it, it descends to his heirs direct as a real estate interest free and clear of debt, as distinguished from the working interest, which, as personal property goes to the legal representative subject to the debts of the estate and that it is not subject to the interstate commerce law, which only covers personal property.

CHAPTER III THE OIL ROYALTY BUSINESS The Oil Royalty Business 

The oil land owner may keep all his oil royalty or he may sell, give away or otherwise dispose of all or any part of it. If he decides to sell any part of it, he may do so without hindrance or objection, and almost any operating oil company, oil royalty dealer, local banker or investor is glad to buy, often at very high prices, depending upon the relative proximity or nearness of the land itself to either producing oil pools or districts or those undergoing or promising immediate development. This is especially so where the oil lease or working interest is in the hands of a well-known operating oil company able and ready to develop the oil land. Such oil royalty purchasers then in turn decide to re-sell such royalty or some part of it, and such transactions are repeated over and over again from time to time, thus enabling many different persons in widely separated and remote localities and having totally different royalty interests to become interested and share in the same original royalty and participate in the profits realized from it through the development of the oil lease or working interest by the oil company. Advantages of Oil Royalties 

The most attractive features about an oil royalty are its exceptional speculative possibilities. This is especially true of non-producing royalties, with respect to which it is a very common experience to see such royalties originally bought at from perhaps $5.00 to $25.00 per acre soar in value to thousands of dollars per acre. Where such royalties have been selected and purchased in wide, cross-section spreads over wide and diversified areas, they have proved to eventually be worth even millions. The same is true, although in less spectacular degree, with producing royalties, carefully bought at, say, from $1,000 to $2,000 per acre upon the strength of the first producing oil well or a small, producing oil sand and then jumped to $5,000, $10,000, $20,000 and $30,000 per acre as larger, deeper oil-producing formations were discovered and opened up. Not until all undrilled well locations upon tracts of oil lands have been drilled, all possible oil-bearing formations tested and fully exploited and newer methods for complete oil extraction devised, can the speculative features of oil royalty investment be said to have become exhausted, and even then they will continue to remain attractive as the royalties continue to enjoy and receive their regular share of all income from all old or "settled" oil production until it has become eventually totally exhausted. 

Profits of Oil Operators and Royalty Owners Compared 

Many people are under the impression that the operating end of the oil business is the most profitable. This is not necessarily the case and is often not true. Notwithstanding the fact that the oil operator takes all the risk and bears all the expense, it is a matter of statistics that the profits of the royalty owner, with his complete freedom from all risk and expense, equal if they do not exceed those of the operator. To illustrate, let it be assumed that the total value of the oil recovered from one well during its first year amounts to $100,000. 

The royalty owner's share, free and clear of all operating risk and expense, would be % of the value of all such oil produced, or $12,500, the remaining %, or $87,500 belonging to the operating company, from which must then be deducted an average drilling cost of $65,000, plus an average annual operating cost of $10,000, or a total of $75,000, leaving the operator a net profit of $12,500, or exactly the same amount received by the royalty owner.

How Royalty Profits Are Paid 

After oil has been discovered in a given well, a contract is then made between the oil company and a pipe line company to purchase 12

the production, after which the latter company proceeds to lay its gathering lines onto the land in order to connect the well or wells to the storage tanks operated by it, preparatory to the oil being conveyed to the refinery. At these tanks the oil is from time to time measured and gauged by the pipe line company to ascertain the amount and quality of oil to be run and paid for, and also by state officials to complete their check upon the gross oil production tax. 

These tanks are then locked by the pipe line company so that none of the oil may be removed until it can be run into the pipe line company's line. A few days after the first day of the following month, but never later than the 12th, the pipe line company sends out to both the operating oil company and to each separate royalty owner direct, as required by law, a monthly statement showing the exact number of barrels of oil removed during the previous month, its grade, quality and price, accompanied by a check to cover the share of each interest in the purchase price of such oil. If the pipe line. company does not make payments in this manner, it may be compelled to pay the amount over again to any interested person who has not received his royalty check. None of the pipe line companies make these payments in any other manner, so that every royalty owner knows exactly how much oil is being produced and run at all times, and is sure to receive his money direct and regularly every month. 

CHAPTER IV ROYALTY INVESTMENTS Permanency of Oil Royalty Investments 

Another important feature about oil royalties is the longevity or "life" of producing oil wells and oil fields. Some oil pools naturally last longer than others, depending upon the character, number and other incidents of the oil-bearing formations, methods of production, etc. No oil pool yet discovered and producing from a reliable oil formation has ever been totally exhausted or abandoned. Some of the oil wells in Western Pennsylvania, New York, Kentucky and other fields have continued to produce for more than 50 years, some of them as long as 75 years. There are wells in the West Virginia and Ohio oil fields that have held up for over 35 years. Some of the California oil pools have been yielding large quantities of oil for over 28 years. Many Kansas, Oklahoma, Louisiana and Texas pools have produced considerable quantities of oil for 30 years and more. The celebrated Cushing pool of Oklahoma has produced thousands of barrels of oil daily for nearly 20 years. The Spindle Top, Electra and Corsicana pools of Texas have been producing for 40 years and more. The great Oklahoma Seminole pool which has already produced over 300,000 barrels of oil per day within the past six years, and, it is believed, will continue a remarkable yield for many years to come. The great East Texas fields which have produced as much as 11,000,000 barrels of oil monthly, are esti- 13

mated to be good for years to come, while the West Texas fields are said to be capable of producing hundreds of millions of barrels of oil for many years to come, with adequate field facilities. A great many pools within these states as they began to show noticable declines in production have also been deepened to even larger producing oil sands, and it has been a very common occurrence for districts originally abandoned as containing no oil at all to afterwards come in with enormous supplies of newly discovered oil through better and more modern methods. New methods and devices of increasing present yields have also been developed, which have increased known oil production very materially all over the oil-producing states. 

All these attractive features, of course, bear directly upon the oil royalty and assure its equal permanency as a profitable investment over a long period of years to come. 

Preferring Producing to Non-Producing Royalties 

Many royalty investors are inclined to consider only the purchase of producing oil royalties, regardless of their relatively much higher cost over non-producing or undeveloped royalties, because of what appears to be a steady daily, monthly and yearly income. While, of course, such considerations are important, it must be borne in mind that the non-producing royalty has none of its oil removed, whereas the producing royalty may have been largely drawn upon and at the time being nearly or has actually reached its production stage, and merely appears to have a dependable and permanent value and return. 

Advantages of Non-Producing Royalty 

Although such non-producing oil royalties do not enjoy any immediate income until they become producing royalties, yet they have many present profit features, such as the right to share in all bonuses, annual rentals and renewals paid on the oil leases themselves, which is often very considerable and frequently sufficient to completely offset their original cost. Non-producing oil royalties are naturally the speculative type of royalty investments and show the greatest increase in value, often jumping virtually overnight from a few dollars per acre original cost to many hundreds or even thousands of dollars per acre. Such profits have been common occurrences in every oil field ever discovered.

Risks In Non-Producing Royalty Investments 

However, one of the greatest dangers in the entire oil business is met right at this point, as it requires a thorough and scientific understanding of the entire oil business, access to all possible geological data, advance inside information of the movements and plans of the large oil operators before they become openly known, and the ability to get the proper scattering or wide "spreads" of royalty purchases made upon a scientific and well-informed basis. In this way, according to the law of averages alone, when wisely selected, properly "checker- 14

boarded" and carefully purchased, they become a most interesting and profitable form of oil royalty investment, and especially so for as those either able and willing to make such purchases for the "long pull,” or with sufficient capital to combine them with actually producing oil royalties in order to afford an immediate present income return while awaiting the "big day ahead." In fact, this is the method employed by practically all the big operating oil companies and royalty holders, and by following this plan it is almost impossible not to make money in oil royalties.

Great Future for Royalty Owners 

The two individuals most directly concerned with oil royalties are the landowner and the royalty investor. It is the former who to-day owns the great majority of undeveloped oil royalty, as well as a large proportion of producing royalty. The part which he has sold will ultimately reach the hands of the other most interested party, the royalty investor or speculator. The general public as it has become more and more familiar with oil royalties and their possibilities, has grown to be the second largest holder of royalty, both producing and non-producing, rivaling even the landowner himself. 

In between these two has grown up a great and rapidly expanding business, which they should both study to some extent at least, in order to be able to avoid possible losses and take advantage of opportunities and large profits. The landowner living in an oil country, or prospective oil country, is the potential head of an oil royalty business of his own; while the oil royalty investor who lives in a non-producing state remote from the centers of oil production and activity and looking at the oil royalty business from a distance should still be conservatively inclined to a degree equal to that of the landowner himself. For both there are unlimited opportunities in oil royalties where values are carefully weighed and the same degree of caution exercised as is called for in any other business or transaction. The future of the oil royalty business rests upon such stability and united action. 

Best Way to Buy Oil Royalties 

Owing to the highly specialized character and nature of the oil royalty business, the average investor is in no sense prepared to directly enter the field of buying royalties himself without great hazard and handicap. He can, however, safely rely upon the great number of reliable and well-known royalty dealers that are engaged in the business and who are intimately familar with oil royalties and their intrinsic value and income possibilities, and who usually operate along safe and conservative lines. Such dealers have their own organizations of experts, petroleum engineers, field scouts, etc., who are in direct and constant contact with all oil operations, widely experienced, and in a position to have available at all times much essential information beyond the reach of the royalty investor himself before royalty purchases may safely be made, able to handle all such transactions upon 15

a scientific basis and at a fair, reasonable profit. Reputable royalty dealers, as a rule, believe that too great a margin of profit should not be exacted for themselves, and that such practice is not only unfair and unjust to the royalty purchaser but that it very materially affects and impairs the ultimate payout of the investor's royalty itself with a handsome profit upon the investment. It is the commonly accepted view among the better class of such royalty dealers that, not only should all royalties be very carefully selected and thoroughly tested before they are offered to the investor, but that no royalty, whatever its status or wherever located, can possibly stand a greater inflation than 33-1/3 per cent. of its original cost price, intelligently and conservatively made.

How Oil Royalty Fortunes Are Made 

Most large oil royalty fortunes have been made by people who early recognized the advantages of non-producing oil royalties long before the oil lands began to be developed, and purchased such royalties at prices so low and on the strength of such good oil geology and oil production trends as to almost completely eliminate the element of risk, and then later watched them become immensely valuable and income-productive as producing royalties as such oil districts became developed. Perhaps the best possible way to "play the royalty game" is to combine the two forms of producing and non-producing oil royalties, in wide and diversified holdings, thus obtaining a better balance on such investments, and thus reap a full, safe, current investment return from the one, while at the same time enjoying the full speculative and infinitely greater future possibilities of gain from the other. This is precisely the way such investments are made by representative oil royalty buyers, who carry on their activities along lines similar to those followed by the big, producing oil companies. 

When an oil company enters a new territory to secure its first "checkerboard" or "spread" of oil leases preparatory to prospecting and drilling for oil, the same course is followed by the wise and foresighted oil royalty operator who is also constantly spreading out and "crosssectioning" his royalty holdings in the same manner, and is constantly enjoying the full benefits of the oil company's information, facilities, drilling operations and oil discoveries. 16

CHAPTER V BUYING AND SELLING ROYALTIES How Royalties are Bought and Sold 

Oil Royalties, being considered a part of the real estate itself, can only be transferred and conveyed in the same general way the oil land itself is transferred and conveyed, that is, by some form of deed duly executed by the oil land owner and delivered to the royalty purchaser, accompanied by the proper abstract or certificate of title to the land. A special form of warranty deed known as a "royalty deed" or "mineral deed" or "oil and mineral deed" is used for this purpose.

This deed, however, does not transfer or convey any part of the surface ownership of the oil land, but merely part of the minerals, including oil and gas, that are under the land. If at the time the royalty is being purchased, the land is actually producing oil, it is necessary to also execute the "transfer order." After this deed has been properly recorded and the transfer order filed with the pipe line company, the purchaser's title to the royalty is complete. In case the purchaser later decides to sell all or any part of his royalty, he goes through exactly the same procedure with his own purchaser.

Buying and Selling Royalties 

The owner of an oil royalty or interest therein has at all times the legal right to sell, transfer and convey it or any part of it at any time or to any person without limit, the same as any other interest in the land. In dealing with oil royalties, any interest, however small or large, may be disposed of and acquired. Even small fractions of a single acre are often dealt in. The prices paid for royalty interests may range from a few hundred dollars per acre up to as high as $50,000 or even more, depending upon the particular field, size of wells, oil production derived, number of wells undrilled, probable future value and many other factors. Royalty sales are conducted in the same general manner as ordinary real estate transactions. Where the parties are together, the royalty seller usually furnishes an abstract or certificate of title showing the title to be marketable, and tenders a legally executed royalty deed to the purchaser, and receives the purchase price in return, after which the parties sign the necessary transfer order and file it with the pipe line company. If oil is actually being produced, the purchaser then takes or sends his deed to the proper recording office to be recorded.

When to Buy Royalties 

The royalty investor should strive to make his royalty purchase at a time as close as possible to the period during which the oil well is at its peak of production in order to assure himself of the full return of his investment plus a handsome profit before the oil has had time. to subside to such a degree as to render this impossible. Just when 17

that time is must always be more or less uncertain. It must be borne in mind that when oil wells originally come in, their first free runs of production are often very great, frequently continuing so for weeks or even months, and at times being sufficient to repay the original cost of a royalty interest many times over. These large initial flows of oil are known as the "flush" production. The wells then begin to show a very perceptible decrease in output. The average oil well shows a decline in production of fully 60 per cent within the first year, after which it continues to further decline, often at an even more rapid rate, until it finally reaches an average, normal, steady yield of oil varying but slightly from period to period. This is known as its "settled" production. The royalty investor, in order to participate in these profits must be in a position to take advantage of this situation and be brought into as close contact with the "first fruits" of a well as soon as possible, either by being right on the ground himself or by being able to buy early enough at a fair price to the seller from whom he purchases to insure him a safe and substantial return upon his investment. 

In order to do this, it is believed that he should be able to so purchase his interest within the period of between six months and two years after the bringing in of such well. 

When to Sell Royalties 

One of the most difficult problems to be determined by a royalty owner is just when to enter a particular oil field, how long to stay in it with the assurance of satisfactory and dependable profits and just when to get out again without serious loss. It requires wide and practical experience, as well as scientific knowledge of the oil business to determine just when to dispose of a particular royalty interest, depending as it does upon the future or "life" of the well involved, special local geological and technical conditions, industrial and mechanical operations and many other vital considerations. Some of the greatest and most fatal mistakes in the business have been made right at this point. While on the one hand wells continue to decline in production until the settled production is reached, some wells "live" longer than others, special recovery conditions may tend to keep up if they do not actually increase production, and very often additional producing oil sands are opened up which may materially increase the "life" and production of the well; on the other hand, the well may in time go completely "dry" or become exhausted. Generally, it can only be stated that the closer the royalty is bought to the beginning of the well from which it receives its returns, the greater and longer-lived those returns will be; while such returns ordinarily decrease in proportion to the decrease in the output of such wells. As these decreases continue to grow, with no apparent prospect for either stemming such decrease or increasing the well's production, the royalty investor should seriously consider the advisability of selling his royalty interest and get out before any loss can occur. 18

Buying Overpriced Royalties 

Royalty investors should guard against paying unreasonable or exorbitant prices for royalty interests. A particular royalty that would show a good return upon an investment of, say $2,000 per acre, would be utterly prohibitive if purchased at, say $4,000. The test of the value and proper purchase of any royalty depends, of course, upon its ability to return the amount of its investment, plus a profit sufficiently large to justify its purchase. This can only be determined by taking into account the relation of such purchase price to the total amount of oil that will probably be produced by the well involved, plus that of any additional wells that may be drilled upon the same lease. Naturally the value of a royalty cannot depend entirely upon the showing made by a single well, but is also affected by the probable showings of new wells. But, as a rule, the safest criterion is to base the value of the royalty upon the single well involved. It is desirable to ascertain the purchase prices paid for other royalty interests out of the same land, as well as similar royalty interests in leases within the same particular locality. The average of such sales usually furnishes a safe and reliable test of the value and proper purchase price to be paid for a given royalty interest. 

Buying "Spavined" Royalties

As far as possible, royalty investors should avoid buying royalties that have gone through too many earlier hands before they reach his own, each passing them along to the next purchaser at increased prices until they finally reach the investor, by wihch time they are liable to be so greatly inflated as to have long before passed beyond even the remotest possibility of realizing even their purchase price, much less any adequate or permanent profit. Such royalties are often spoken of as "spavined" royalties. Very often such royalties have been "cleared" by one or more previous owners before they get to the final investor, by which is meant that such earlier owners have sold off enough of their original royalty or secured enough returns from it to fully cover its original cost and then enjoyed a "free gamble" or "ride" on the remaining royalty held and then sold to the investor, or held until it begins to show a very perceptible decline in returns or some other unfavorable feature, such as the drilling of "dry" or barren wells on the land, salt water flooding of wells, and the like, and then passed the royalty on to such investor. Such royalties, originally bought "right at the well," have often been held until all of their first free or "flush" production has been had and then "unloaded" upon remote non-oilstate investors at amazingly high prices far beyond their reasonable value or income-producing possibilities. Before purchasing royalties, the wise investor will investigate all such conditions carefully and satisfy himself that he is not buying into a "sinking ship." 20

Royalty Purchases Based Upon Monthly Income 

Many royalty purchases depend solely upon the monthly income derived from certain royalties at the time they make their purchases. 

In doing this, what such investors very often lose sight of and do not learn until it is perhaps too late for their use is the amount of oil which the land their royalties cover has already produced. Many thousand of such royalty owners all over the United States have not even the remotest idea of the amount of oil that has already been produced from the lands under which their royalties are owned and which information so directly affects and decreases the value of their holdings. 

Closing Royalty Sales at a Distance 

If the royalty seller and purchaser are not together, the royalty transaction must of course be closed at a distance. The greater part of royalty transactions are consummated in this manner. In active oil districts where interest and competition is keen, such transactions. must be closed almost immediately, with the money practically paid right on the spot. This means that it must be telegraphed, which is usually done to the seller's local bank, with instructions to release it to the seller upon deposit with such bank by the seller of the royalty deed, abstract of title and transfer order. Where such activity is not so great, usually bank drafts or cashier's checks are mailed to the seller's bank and so released upon the same conditions. Very often the royalty deed, abstract of title and division order are mailed to some bank designated by the buyer with a sight draft for the purchase price of the royalty attached, and instructions given to such bank to deliver such documents upon payment to it of the amount of the sight draft to cover the purchase price of the royalty. It is considered the better practice, however, for both buyer and seller to be represented by responsible agents who are well known to each other, and for the buyer to turn over the full purchase price to his agent, who then transmits it direct to the seller's agent who closes the transaction, records the deed, files the transfer order with the pipe line company and then forwards all papers to the purchaser's agent. 

CHAPTER VI FACTS THAT AFFECT ROYALTIES How Undeveloped Oil Sands Affect Royalties 

In most of the important oil fields numerous separate and distinct oil-producing sands are found. Frequently as many as eight or ten of such formations exist. As a rule, but one of these sands is developed at a time, sometimes the shallowest, sometimes the deepest, depending upon special oil field conditions or the choice of the operating oil company itself. So that as one oil sand being worked is exhausted or gradually so, the next one either above or below may then be opened up and worked. Cases are known where from five to six oil wells are 21

the same producing, each from a separate and different oil sand, upon land. The royalty owner naturally is benefited by this, especially where the more shallow sands are developed first, leaving the deeper and usually more productive ones until later, when the returns become very much larger upon the royalty. 

Amount of Oil Under a Royalty Acre 

Petroleum engineers and geologists are now able to estimate with almost exact accuracy just what the total yield of oil per acre in a particular oil field will be, covering either a single oil well or a group of wells. So that a royalty owner may determine pretty closely just what his royalty interest will "pay out" during the entire life of the well or wells in which he is interested. As an illustration, if a certain property is estimated as capable of producing, say, 48,000 barrels of oil per acre, then the royalty owner is entitled to receive one-eighth of that quantity, or 6,000 barrels. If the average price of oil is, say, $1.50 per barrel, the royalty owner will receive $9,000 per acre as his share from the sale of oil covering the entire period of the well's life. 

A great many oil wells have produced as much as 100,000 barrels per acre and are still producing profitably. 

Effect of Oil Prices Upon Royalties 

Oil royalties are naturally affected by the prevailing prices paid for crude oil. If such prices are high, royalty returns will be correspondingly high, whereas if prices of oil are low royalty yields will be affected in the same proportion. The average price paid for crude oil in the Mid-Continent District, where the greatest number of royalties are held, during the ten year period from 1921 to 1931 was $1.50 per barrel. While such price fell for a short time to as low as 22 cents per barrel in 1931 on account of the vast, unlimited production from the East Texas field, this condition was but temporary and it has since gone up very materially. The price of oil varies with its different. grades. For instance, high-grade Oklahoma oil is now $1.10 per barrel, with grades scaling down 2 cents per barrel for each degree of gravity under 40 degrees. The general tendency in oil prices at the present time is upwards.

How Royalties Are Affected by Proration, Potential and Allowable 

At the present time practically none of the producing oil wells within the important fields of the United States are permitted to produce their full capacity of oil, but are required to reduce it proportionately by orders issued, originally by the oil states themselves, but now by the Federal Oil Administration Board. This limitation upon production is known as "proration" and is designed to equalize oil production and demand and to stabilize the petroleum industry. The method of proration now in use is based upon the estimated maximum capacity or total quantity of oil probably contained under a certain oil well, oil field, oil state or the entire nation as a whole, and the proportionate share of such oil contained under each producing well. 22

This maximum capacity is known as the "potential," which is arrived at by first allowing all of the wells in the particular oil field to be turned on full force and run at their utmost capacities for a certain number of hours or days. The total result of such runs of oil is then treated as the potential or total estimated daily or hourly output for each of such wells or oil fields. After this has been done, either the state commission or the Federal Oil Administration Board issue their orders for periods of thirty or sixty days or longer, authorizing each well within such oil field, oil state or the nation to produce a certain percentage of its "potential." This percentage is known as the "allowable." Shortly before the expiration date of each of such orders, the potential of such wells is again taken in the same manner and new orders issued upon a similar basis. The tendency has been to increase such allowables from time to time and they now average around fifty per cent of the potentials or maximum capacities of all such wells. It is not the custom to prorate production over old oil wells or what are known as "stripper" wells, as the small oil production from such wells is not considered economically necessary to such methods. These methods of limitation of oil production, however, while of course. affecting the immediate returns upon royalty investments do not have any permanent effect either way, as the oil while produced more slowly, still remains under the wells and is shared in ultimately by the royalty owner just the same.

How Oil Land Mortgages Affect Royalties 

Oil royalties purchased covering lands already mortgaged become directly subject to such incumbrance and in case of foreclosure of such mortgages, are completely cut off and destroyed along with the other land ownership. Royalties properly and legally acquired upon unencumbered oil lands are not subject to or affected by mortgages afterwards placed upon such lands, and in case such mortgages are foreclosed, they must be done so subject to such royalties and without affecting them in any manner. Many serious losses have been sustained through such risks and prospective royalty purchases are advised to carefully look into such situations before completing their purchases, if necessary obtaining abstracts of title or the legal opinions of reliable attorneys as to the exact status of the land titles. 

Taxing Oil Royalties 

Oil royalty owners are not required to pay any general real estate taxes in any of the petroleum-producing states, as all such burdens are directly assessed against and paid by the land owners themselves. 

Royalty owners should, however, be careful to inquire into all such matters in order to see that all such taxes are promptly paid when due, and not allowed to become in arrears or subject the land to any penalties, liens or delinquent tax sales. In most petroleum-producing states such taxes are usually assessed during the month of January. 

All such information may be readily obtained either by communica- 23

22 ting direct with local county tax authorities or by securing regular tax searches from county treasurers at a trifling expense. No personal property taxes are assessed against oil royalties as such by any petroleum - producing states. A small "gross production" or severance tax usually around 2% or 3% is imposed by all the states except Kansas, although some of the counties of the latter state exact an ad valorem tax for delinquent school or other purely local purposes. 

All such taxes are, however, paid direct to the states or counties by the pipe line companies buying the oil and later deducted from royalty checks. A recent amendment to the Federal Revenue Act provides for a tax of 1/10 of 1% on each barrel of oil produced from all wells except "stripper" wells. This particular burden upon royalty owners is practically negligible and will probably be paid direct to the Internal Revenue collectors by the pipe line companies and adjusted in the same way as state production taxes. The new law is aimed primarily at illegal or "hot" oil producers, upon whom it imposes severe punishments and fines up to as high as $10,000 for extreme offenses. 

How Foreign Oil Imports Affect Royalties 

Several hundred million barrels of oil are annually imported into this country from various foreign oil fields of the world. Up until the summer of 1932 the value and income returns from oil royalties were somewhat affected by this enormous influx of foreign oil, by reason of the fact that all of it was permitted to enter duty free, which enabled such foreign oil to freely and successfully compete with our higher American grades for many uses. This tended to keep down oil production in the United States and to that extent affected the value and returns from oil royalties. However, Congress has since. enacted a law placing a duty of 21 cents per barrel on all such foreign. oil and a still higher tax upon foreign refined oil products. Since the enactment of this law, such foreign shipments of oil have declined very greatly and thereby stimulated our own production, which has thus increased the yield in royalty returns quite perceptibly. 

CHAPTER VII COMPUTING OIL ROYALTIES Methods of Computing Royalties 

Two methods for computing royalty interests and returns are used within the oil fields, which are known as the "royalty acre" and "fractional interest" methods. By the former, the royalty is split up into units known as "royalty acres," usually of the same number of the total surface acreage of the land, i.e., 40 surface acres, 40 royalty acres, etc., while by the other the royalty is reduced to fractions such as 2, 4, etc. While differing in principle, their respective results are always exactly the same. The "royalty acre" method is most commonly used within producing oil fields and in connection with produc- 24

ing royalties, and very largely employed in the Michigan, Oklahoma and Kansas fields, while the "fractional interest" method is largely employed within non-producing fields or districts where land ownership is in the form of ranches, leagues, surveys or other large tracts such as Texas and some other districts. This method is also very largely employed in dealing with producing royalties. Either method, however, may be and often is used in all fields and in dealing with both kinds of royalties.

Regardless of the method employed or how the royalty is divided, either into royalty acres or fractional interests, the total royalty is known as the "base" to distinguish it from the total output of oil from the land, or "production," as it is called. This "base" is always 25

less than "production" in the exact proportion that the royalty bears to the total output, i.e., if the royalty is %, it will be 8 times less than production, if 1/5, 5 times less, etc. In other words, each surface or "production" acre contains 8/8 or 5/5 of the oil or whatever other fraction it may be, while each royalty acre will contain %, 1/5, etc. of such total output of oil. If the "production" is, say, 320 barrels and the royalty, then the royalty will be % of 320 barrels or 40 barrels.

The other % of the oil goes to the oil operator under his oil lease or "working interest" and is not considered at all in royalty calculations. 

That interest is seldom sold; if sold, it is done by assignment, sublease, bill of sale or some other form of transfer. "Base" and "Production" Illustrated

The terms "base" and "production" may be illustrated by the simple example of a large tank containing 320 barrels of oil to represent the total oil output. Seven-eighths or 280 barrels of such oil is then removed from the tank, run into the pipe line as the share of the oil company under its lease or "working interest" and then forgotten. 

The remaining % or 40 barrels is then run into a smaller tank to represent the % royalty or "base." This 40 barrels is then run into either 40 separate barrels and placed to one side, or into 5 different sized pans of 20 barrels, 10 barrels, 5 barrels, 2½ barrels and 14 barrels, respectively, and placed to the other side. Either of these two groups still represents the royalty or "base." The purchaser of one or more of such separate barrels then becomes the owner of 1, 2, 3, etc., barrels out of 40 barrels, or the owner of 2, 4, % of the royalty or base of 40 acres. As the "base" will always be eight times less than "production," and as the oil is always undivided so that the acre, both surface or royalty, farthest away from the producing well shares equally with the acre immediately under the well, each surface or "production" acre carries 8/8 of the oil of each single acre, while each royalty acre carries only % of the oil of each single acre. This being so, the purchaser of, say, one of such separate barrels of oil would not only be entitled to 1 barrel out of 40, or 1/40 of %, but he would likewise be entitled to 1/40 of % of 8/8 or 1/40 of % of 320 barrels, also amounting to 1 barrel. CHAPTER VIII ROYALTY INFORMATION Necessary Royalty Information 

The average royalty investor usually can spend but a relatively small amount of time in studying the royalty business or in carefully weighing the merits of a special royalty purchase under consideration, as compared with the time and effort expended by the seller. He also 26

has, as a rule, little opportunity to judge the value of a particular royalty submitted to him and therefore is more or less at the mercy of the seller who is usually armed with the complete facts covering it. 

The investor should, therefore, be quite sure that all such facts are fully disclosed and not concealed, suppressed or "camouflaged" in any way. Often the absence of a single fact may affect the value of a royalty or the desirability of its purchase. The investor should not be too much impressed by statistics which may often be so cleverly manipulated as to appear attractive upon their face but may be really. meaningless. Any maps or production reports submitted, however plausible they may appear to reflect daily, monthly or yearly income upon such royalty but actually only give part of the information available, should be carefully scrutinized by the prospective purchaser. All these records are readily available to any royalty investor, not only reliably so but officially so. Most any reputable oil company is always glad to furnish the fullest information concerning any property being operated by it upon which a prospective royalty purchase is based, while every oil state has a special department of its government which specializes in compiling just such information. An inquiry addressed to either of such agencies will promptly bring all available information to the royalty investor and enable him to act wisely and profitably in the matter without danger of possible loss. 

The Royalty Deed 

The royalty deed, also known as the "oil royalty deed," "mineral deed" or "oil mineral deed' is the document by which a royalty or royalty instrument is legally transferred and conveyed from the holder to a purchaser. The seller is known as the "grantor" and the purchaser as the "grantee." There are thirty or more forms of such deeds in use for royalty transactions, and therefore the purchaser must be quite sure that the correct form is being used and that he is legally receiving the interest he has bargained for and should receive. The customary royalty deed is a form of warranty deed which conveys the amount of royalty specified in it to the grantee or purchaser forever, so that his title becomes one in fee simple and perpetual. After such deed has been signed by the land owner, his wife and other interested parties in the land, it is acknowledged before a notary public, and after its delivery is taken or sent by the purchaser to the county clerk, recorder of deeds, register of deeds or otherwise designated recording official of the county within which the land itself is situated, to be recorded. 

It is the custom for such official to stamp upon the back of the deed the date and time of such recording and then retain the deed until it can actually be copied into the county records, which usually takes from two to three weeks, depending upon how busy the recording official is. After it has been copied into the records, it is then returned or mailed to the purchaser. The usual recording fee is $1.25. 27

The Abstract of Title 

The abstract of title, or its equivalent, a certificate of title, has come to be generally used in all oil fields, and especially so in connection with the sale of oil well production to pipe line companies. This instrument is designed to show the exact condition of the particular oil land involved. It discloses the origin of the present owner's title to the land, what mortgages, unpaid taxes or other incumbrances are outstanding against the land, and particularly who owns the mineral rights, including oil and gas, attached to the land, and what disposition has been made of them. It will especially disclose the execution and recording of any oil leases granted by the land owner and by whom such leases are held, together with the working interest granted by such leases and the royalty interest reserved by the land owner, and will also show what if any part of such royalty interest has been granted and to whom. These abstracts of title are usually prepared by local abstractors, after full examination of the land records of the county pertaining to the particular tract of land involved, although they are often made in a similar way by county clerks, recorders of deeds, register of deeds or otherwise designated recording officials, or even by attorneys who are experiened and competent in such work. Certificates of title are merely affidavits of persons who certify that they have examined the records of the county covering the land in question and found them to be unincumbered, or if so, in certain specified respects, and are then sworn to before notaries public. Such certificates are, however, not as a rule considered either acceptable or complete, especially when dealing with producing oil properties. 

The Division Order 

When oil has been discovered and the output is about to be sold to a pipe line company, the latter circulates what is known as a "division order" which sets forth the exact ownership of the seveneighths or working interest and also the names, addresses and royalty interests owned by each recorded royalty owner. This division is in the nature of a supplemental contract to the original oil lease and sometimes contains clauses which have the legal effect of amending the original lease to the decided disadvantage of the royalty owner, and therefore every royalty purchaser should examine closely any such division order submitted to him for his signature affecting the oil runs from the property in which he is interested. After such division order has been properly executed and filed with the pipe line company taking the oil, any transfer of the royalty or any part of it is then made by the execution of the royalty deed accompanied by the transfer order which indicates the exact amount of royalty being sold and to whom the monthly runs are to be paid for.

Royalties Considered Real Estate 

While oil royalties, appear to have some of the features of both real estate and personal property, and while "term" and "overriding" royalties often are actually personal property, according to the wellsettled view of law, all mineral substances, including both oil and gas, while still under the land and not separated or removed therefrom, are a part of the land itself and therefore must be considered as real estate. Under all Federal and State laws and the decisions of practically all courts, oil and gas while still under the ground are classified as mineral substances which can only be sold and transferred by means of some form of real estate deed, just as the land itself or any other permanent or substantial interest in it is sold and transferred. This has given rise to the perpetual "deeded royalty," "mineral deed," "oil and mineral deed" or "oil royalty deed' now in general use throughout the oil fields.

What the Oil Lease Is CHAPTER IX OIL LEASES 

The oil lease is the basic right to explore and develop the oil resources of a particular tract of oil land. Many forms of this document are used in the oil fields, but "Form 88," so called, devised by the 

Prairie Oil & Gas Company, a Standard Oil Company subsidiary, is the one most commonly used on account of its all-inclusive and mutually fair provisions. This is a printed form which provides that for a stipulated consideration either of a single dollar to make it legal up to one or more dollars per acre paid in advance as a "bonus," the 30

oil land owner, as lessor, grants to an oil company or other lessee the exclusive right to enter and prospect, drill and operate for oil and gas, lay pipe lines and erect storage tanks, etc., for a specified term, usually five years or ten years, and as much longer as oil or gas is produced, the lessee to deliver free of cost in the pipe line to which it afterwards connects its wells a certain percentage of the oil producd as a “royalty," which is usually one-eighth of the oil produced and to pay a certain sum in advance each year for any gas produced where gas only is found, usually from $100.00 per well up, with free gas for all the lessor's domestic uses by making his own connections at his own expense, and further to pay the lessor a specified sum, usually every three months, for all "casing-head" gas. If no well is commenced within a specified period, usually one year, known as the "drilling term," the lease terminates unless the lessee pays or tenders either to the lessor or a local bank designated in the lease a certain sum, usually one dollar per acre, as a "rental" to defer the drilling of such well for a second year, and providing for similar future annual payments as long as such drilling is deferred until the expiration of the lease. If the first well drilled is "dry" or barren, a second well must be begun within the period between the last rental payment and the next similar payment date. If the lessor's interest in the land is less than a fee simple, his royalty is proportionately reduced. The lessee may use all necessary water produced on the land for drilling and producing purposes, except from the lessor's own wells, must bury all pipes below plowdepth, drill no well within a specified number of feet from the lessor's house or improvements without his written consent, pay for all damages done to growing crops and has the right to remove all machinery, casing, etc. The lease may be assigned by either party without the other's consent. Usually the lessor only signs the lease, its acceptance by the lessee being deemed the equivalent of a signature, although there is no particular reason why he should not and very often he does, especially where the lease is to be recorded. The lease is generally placed upon record with the recording official of the county within which the land is situated. The lease may be sold and resold by means of an "assignment." 

Oil Lease Investments

The oil lease also possesses very great investment possibilities, where wisely and carefully selected either in fields that have already become active or are rapidly approaching such point. Such leases range in cost from a very small consideration up to a few dollars per acre, depending upon their location with respect to such actual or projected drilling operations, and have a carrying charge of from perhaps fifty cents to two dollars per acre for each of the remaining 31

five years or ten years. In dealing with such properties, however, it is important to bear in mind at all times that the oil lease must be turned within the period of its life, either by re-sale or placing it in the hands of an operating oil company for development upon some contractual basis, in order to avoid a complete loss. Very often such leases are taken as the basic capital for the organization and promotion of a particular drilling oil company. In this way they have also often shown very great returns.

How Oil Lease Fortunes Are Made 

As new oil territory begins to become attractive and active, and especially so when the major oil companies enter it for the purpose of oildrilling operations, a wide scramble immediately begins for oil leases on other unleased lands, near and far, by other oil producing companies, and innumerable oil lease dealers and brokers, all anxious to participate in a possible "oil strike." As such drilling progress and favorable indications of oil discovery increase, such leases begin to rise in value, often jumping several dollars per acre from day to day. 

When finally oil is discovered, such leases command prices running into thousands of dollars per acre, and are eagerly purchased by other oil companies for drilling purposes or by lease speculators all over the country for resale at rapidly advancing prices. In time such traffic becomes enormous, and often runs into the millions within short periods of time, thus enabling their holders to withdraw from the discovery field with substantial fortunes from the sales of their leases or parts of them, enter the oil production business themselves, or turn over "operating interests" in them to oil companies willing to develop them "on the shares." The finest fellowship and co-operation usually exist between responsible ou producing companies and representative oil lease operators, even to the extent of holding back the opening up of new pools because of overproduction, or other reasons. Such groups have access to and freely exchange with each other first-hand, inside information, often long before it becomes generally known to the outsider, and which is usually indispensable in order to fully enjoy the advantages of the development of the district, and it has invariably been the case, as the result of this, that as new oil pools are discovered and developed, the representative and reliable oil companies and oil lease operators have always gone along together to their great mutual and certain success.

Canadian Oil and Gas Royalties 

No comprehensive consideration of oil royalties within the United States would seem complete without at least a brief mention of Canadian royalties. Scientifically regarded, the Canadian oil fields are but extensions and parts of our own fields. Geographically and geologi- 32

cally as well as economically and industrially they are one, while in their technical, mechanical and practical aspects and problems the fields of the two neighboring nations are practically identical. The International Boundary Line between the two countries is purely imaginary so far as oil is concerned. Its discovery, exploitation and marketing goes on along the boundary line often as one great movement. 

The same is true of its operators and royalty owners personally, among whom has always existed the very finest co-operation, mutual dependence and common benefit. The good fellowship and sportsmanship of both groups have often been commented upon. These groups have always recognized that what are the problems and results desired with their successful achievement by one group must necessarily be those of the other. As a consequence, Canadian and American methods have been constantly exchanged; the technique, practices and ethics of the one have invariably been enjoyed by and permanently affected the other. Large parts of Canada's oil resources have been developed through the efforts of American capital and operators, while the same has been true in equal measure of many of the American fields through the help of Canadian capital and enterprise. Millions of barrels of oil have constantly passed backward and forward between the two countries. In a similar manner has been the flow of oil royalties backwards and forwards. Canadian royalties have ever been in demand with us, while our royalties have invariably found ready markets in Canada. In fact Canadian royalties have an attraction to many American and foreign investors because of the less intensive and wasteful exploitation of the oil resources of that country than, unfortunately, has been the case within the United States. There are three kinds of Canadian royalties-Government or mineral owner's royalty, lease owner's royalty, and operator's royalty. The first kind are reserved by His Majesty the King of England, the Hudson Bay Company, the C. & E. Land Company, the C. P. R. or other corporations or individuals who own the patent rights to the oil and gas. This class of royalty is seldom dealt in. The second class represents those royalties reserved by lease owners who have acquired the rights from the patent owners and who have not elected to develop such rights themselves but transferred them to operators. The third class is subject to production costs and marketing charges. These are the royalties most generally traded in. The Canadian oil royalty business is dominated by an experienced, well-trained and high-minded class of royalty dealers, while the regulations of the Dominion Government, while liberal, are designed to keep the industry upon a lofty plane of ethical dealings, and the returns from royalty investments as a whole have been exceptionally gratifying. 33

CHAPTER X THE NATIONAL PETROLEUM SITUATION AS IT AFFECTS ROYALTIES How a Changed Industry Helps Royalties 

The oil industry of to-day is far different from that of past years. 

The modern oil cycle really began with the rise of the automotive industry, with its dependence upon gasoline and other refined oil products. Not only has the average recoverage of oil per individual well materially increased, but the hazards and difficulties of locating, drilling for and producing oil have been either completely eliminated or enormously reduced, while the number of refined products have been constantly increasing, as methods continually improve. In our foreign trade, petroleum and its products now rank among our foremost contributions to the commerce of the world. The ability to successfully reach larger and more productive oil sands thousands of feet below the ground has greatly increased and prolonged the outputs of all our oil fields. All these important factors of greater production, demand and distribution with the attendant increase of prices has been of great, direct and lasting benefit to royalty owners throughout the length and breadth of the entire industry. Such royalty owners are now no longer dependent upon the meagre and uncertain royalty returns of the past, where demands were largely local and confined to a few relatively unimportant and unprofitable petroleum by-products and uses, but are now able to enjoy all these multiplied and infinitely more valuable derivatives and uses for petroleum and the much larger returns that naturally follow such modern and efficient methods of operation.

How Waste and Extravagant Production Methods 

Injure Royalty Values

The success of the royalty business naturally depends upon the ultimate supply of oil under a given tract of land or oil field, in the same sense that the oil producer's interest is dependent. After such well or field has been depleted it cannot be replenished. The millions of cubic feet of valuable natural gas cannot be recaptured after it has been allowed to escape or has been needlessly destroyed. Once gone, these great natural resources are irreplaceable. For oil there is no present known practicable substitute. The very welfare of the nation itself directly depends upon the willingness and ability of oil operators and royalty owners to avoid any wanton or unnecessary waste and dissipation of these indispensable products. The past of this great industry has been characterized by reckless and unscientific exploitation, unnecessary supplies of oil above the surface, improvident methods of recapture, ruthless squandering of natural gas, the elements of human greed-all these have combined to bring about such conditions. 34

Happily, such conditions are now being gradually halted and corrected by far-seeing oil operators and royalty owners through everincreasing co-operation in trying to systematically and scientifically develop these resources in such a manner as not only to insure the greatest benefit to themselves consistent with the welfare, security and needs of the nation as a whole, but in such a way as to result in the permanency and proper development of the industry itself. 

How Present and Future Oil Supplies Affect Royalties 

The oil royalty commonly covers not only a share in all liquid petroleum produced from a particular tract of land but also a share in any oil shale or other mineral substances produced and used. Thus far, however, royalty returns have been derived solely from liquid petroleum, natural gas and natural gas gasoline or "casing-head" gasoline. Petroleum has been produced commercially in the United States since 1859; natural gas since 1882, and natural gas gasoline since 1911. 

The total amount of petroleum produced in the United States from

All of this tremendous output of petroleum has been fully and directly enjoyed by hundreds of thousands of royalty owners scattered not only throughout all the oil fields of this country but virtually by owners all over the world. 

Petroleum within the United States occurs more intensively than in any other country. When it is realized that these successful developments have been confined to a relatively small part of our potential national areas, it is easy to contemplate how much more greatly will the royalty owners of the future participate in the successful later developments of all these untouched favorable petroleum areas..

How Stripper Well Conservation Benefits Royalties 

"Stripper" wells are those which have long passed their early peaks of large production and declined to outputs of but a few barrels per day. There are approximately 320,000 such wells within the United

Rejuvenating Oil Wells 

Along with the subject of abandonment of old oil wells naturally goes the question of the rejuvenation of such wells. By the "rejuvenating" of an oil well is meant its reviving, stimulation or being made "young" again, with a view to increasing its production. The product of all wells sooner or later declined to a stage where their successful operation is impossible, while in time they tend to become completely exhausted. Very often such results are due merely to their clogging up with paraffin, sand or other accumulations which may be removed by cleaning, swabbing, etc., of such wells. Very often it is caused by caving conditions or exhaustion of drainage areas at the bottoms of the wells which may be corrected by re-shooting of such wells and creating new and more widely extended areas for seepage and accumulation of oil within such areas. Then again it is frequently the result of inefficient production methods or inadequate pumping facilities, operating wells too strenuously, thereby causing great inflows of water which shuts off the petroleum, trying to operate too many wells from a single pumping plant and the like. In some cases, however, it is generally the result of the gradual releasing or dissipation of gas pressure which has acted as a natural lifting process in elevating the oil to the surface. Various methods have been devised for rejuvenating such wells, all of them based upon more or less obvious scientific principles. The general object is to supply by artificial means this dwindling or exhausted natural pressure through the supplying of equivalent pressure. By one method, natural gas under high pressure is pumped into the wells; by another, water under similar conditions is thus used; by another, certain wells are "plugged" or cemented so that their combined pressure may be accumulated in a fewer number of wells remaining open under operation. By still another method, leases are "five-spotted," so called, by which the central or strategic well of a group of, say, five wells is selected, concentrated upon and subjected to great pressure. By these methods, production is frequently doubled, trebled and even increased in much greater proportion. Cases are common where five-barrel wells have been forced up to 100 and 200 barrels per day, thereby materially increasing production and royalty returns.

Effect of Private Oil Conservation Upon Royalties 

For a great many years the more representative oil operators have recognized the vital importance of more systematic and scientific development of oil properties, and have sought, both individually and through private co-operative efforts, to accomplish such results. Various methods have been employed, designed to accomplish this effect, by the more orderly spacing of wells, the better sealing off of oil sands from possible water instrusion, the saving of all possible oil and gas from waste or loss, the reduction of fire hazards, the posting of fair and equitable prices, the maintenance of ethical practices and in many other ways. The limitation of production within various pools by "unit" or co-operate drilling of several operators in order to avoid actual or possible over-production has been especially sought. Such operators have properly recognized that a large part of the oil within particular fields permitted to remain in nature's own store house was economically better than to throw it at any and all prices upon a demoralized market wholly unprepared to absorb it. These beneficent 38

results have, however, at all times been impeded and very often frustrated by the counter activities of greedy, impracticable and unethical operators only concerned in producing every possible drop of oil at the expense of their neighbors and without any sense of responsibility, either economic or moral. The invariable result has been that not only have the operators constanly succeeded in "cutting their own throats," but that they have in this manner very materially injured and even destroyed that other helpless and "innocent bystander," the royalty owner who has constantly been jeopardized both in his royalty ownership and his royalty returns by such improper and unfair methods. 

How State Oil Conservation Has Benefited Royalties 

Unfortunately, most of the oil-producing states have either been wholly indifferent to such evils or been unable or reluctant to undertake to correct them. Until comparatively recently, aside from very inadequate laws requiring abandoned wells to be "plugged," oil sands to be protected from water, pipes to be buried under the ground and the like, practically no regulations were promulgated by any of the oil states which were calculated to conserve, protect and foster the industry along any constructive lines. The result was that not only oil operators themselves but royalty owners and the public at large as well were seriously even if unconsciously injured. Through the constant efforts of the better class of oil operators and royalty owners, a more constructive group of state conservation laws began to later make their appearance upon the statute books of such states. Step by step these laws have limited waste, fixed ratios between the amounts of oil and gas that might be lifted from wells and finally through controlling or "prorating" of production have been able very largely to avoid or control overproduction with all its attendant evils, thus tending to stabilize the industry itself and bring consumption and demand requirements into harmony. As a whole, such laws have not only served a real and highly beneficent purpose in themselves but they have been the direct means of setting up better standards and blazing new trails toward still higher and more nationally extended methods and practices of regulation, control, conservation and stabilization of a great industry unable or unwilling to regulate and protect itself.

How Unfair Practices Affect Royalties 

During the past few years certain unfair and unethical practices have developed throughout the entire industry, but more particularly within some of the larger oil-producing states to such a degree that, notwithstanding the most drastic and persistent efforts of such states, it has been impossible to even control much less stamp them out. 

There has been widespread evasion of both state and federal taxes, illegal and excessive sale and distribution of oil not only beyond the authorized sanctions of such states but far beyond the legitimate consumptive needs of the market. The rights of oil land owners and roy- 39

alty holders have been wilfully ignored and flagrantly disregarded, with great consequent loss of legitimate profits and investment returns. The "bootlegging" of illicit oil at one time had developed into a veritable industry in itself. Hundreds of thousands of barrels of "hot" or illegally produced oil were being purchased and dumped upon the market at prices less than half the actual cost of its legitimate production. In the East Texas field alone oil was being sold as low as 4 cents per barrel; Oklahoma oil was being marketed at from 15 cents to 20 cents a barrel; while price wars were raging in almost every other field. The efforts of legitimate oil operators and royalty owners to end such orgies were completely thwarted. 

Even state conservation laws were contemptuously ignored and their administrators openly defied. The Governor of Oklahoma ordered the state militia to close down every well in the state and keep all of them closed until oil could be brought back to a fair price with a demand sufficient to absorb production. The state corporation commission was charged with the duty of ordering a system of fair apportionment of production throughout the entire state. The Governor of Texas, acting with equal determination and aggression, ordered the state rangers to close down the East Texas field and enforce the executive order at the point of the bayonet if necessary. In California some of the ugliest price wars in the entire history of the industry developed into a general federal court decree. Thus the entire industry was in a condition of utter chaos. 

Federal Oil Conservation and Royalties 

What has been presented in the preceding chapters is believed to be a brief but accurate view of the national oil situation as it existed in the spring of 1933 when the Federal Government felt called upon to enter the picture. Even during such practices the Government had begun to become concerned. As early as 1921 President Coolidge appointed the Secretaries of War, Navy, Interior and Commerce to undertake a survey of the petroleum industry in all its branches. This body, known as the Oil Administration Board, in turn appointed a Committee of Nine, made up of an equal number of members from the petroleum industry itself, the Government and the American Bar Association. This committee found and reported back to the Board the result of its investigations with recommendations for the corection of certain designated evils and incorrect practices. Special recommendations for the protection of royalty owners were also included. Later, the executive order of President Hoover withdrawing all Federally owned oil lands from further exploitation under the Leasing Act had a tremendous effect in checking possible overproduction of oil and unfair competition with private oil development and privately owned royalty returns. Aside from these movements, however, little was done by the Government to conserve, protect or regulate the industry. With the advent of the present Administration, but three oil-producing States, Kansas, Oklahoma and Texas, were making any constructive. 1924 40

efforts to conserve their oil resources or functioning at all in the way of regulating production methods and even such regulations allowed volumes of crude oil outputs far in excess of legitimate demands. The right or necessity of the Federal Government thus assuming control of the oil industry is a much-mooted question. Its objectors upon the one hand claim that it is not a matter of national concern at all but purely one of State authority, that if left alone under private control the situation will eventually correct itself, and that such Federal interference is not only unwarranted but calculated to create monopolies and exclusive cartels among big oil operators at the expense of the smaller, more helpless independent operators. Upon the other hand, its proponents contend that the industry has developed into such a deplorable condition of chaos and unbridled license as to constitute a national menace and therefore become of great national concern. Without attempting to either reconcile or take sides either way with these. fundamentally divergent views of the problem, the fact remains that the Federal Government has begun to regulate and control the industry; that such right or necessity is no longer a theory but a condition; that such right and necessity has been recognized and sanctioned by practically every court before which the question has been presented, and that ever since the Federal Government has assumed such control the tendency in every branch of the industry in every way has been toward a decided betterment and stabilization of conditions within the industry, not only with respect to the oil operators and royalty owners themselves but the general public as well. A comparison of former and present oil prices, company earnings, increased royalty returns, mounting payrolls and additional labor employed, not only of the larger companies but of an overwhelming majority of the smaller ones as well, as reflected in their periodical reports, is in itself sufficient to convince any reasonably minded person of such improvement. A comparison of payrolls alone for the years 1933 and 1934 is both gratifying and significant. These reports show 403,771 employees as of January, 1934, with a total average monthly payroll of $51,001,- 105, as against 334,784 employees with a total monthly payroll of $43,- 

197,699 for the month of June, 1933, showing an increase of 18 per cent in employment and a 16 per cent increase in earnings. Royalty returns, while not especially reported, are necessarily reflected in such returns at from one-fifth to one-eighth of such respective showings. 

Such a comparison is doubly significant in that it does not present really as favorable a picture of actual increases as they really are, inasmuch as such employment is always seasonably very greatly curtailed during cold winter months, while during the warmer months the situation is just the reverse, both in increased employment and in larger operative and royalty earnings. Therefore, it is reasonably safe to assume that the earnings for the second, third and fourth quarters of the year will show even larger increases. Meanwhile, during the same general period the price of oil has gone from as low as 10 cents per barrel to as high as $1.10 per barrel. 41

Benefits of the NRA 

Undoubtedly all these benefits have been derived directly from the operation of the NRA. Such benefits have, moreover, not only been temporary and striking but they undoubtedly may be permanent. 

Within the very first four months from August 19, 1933, to January 1, 1934, while the Code was in operation, the oil industry received for oil produced an amount equal to that received for the entire eight preceding months of the former year. The total production of the eleven main producing oil states for the first eight months immediately before the Code went into operation was 280,751,000 barrels which was sold for approximately $126,807,000 or an average of but 45½ cents per barrel; while during the last four months of the same year under the Code, regulations and proration allowables of the government, 125,224,000 barrels (approximately 50,000,000 barrels less) was produced but which sold for $117,545,000 or an average of 93 cents per barrel. This would indicate that losses due to waste, extravagant methods and price cutting in the industry were responsible for the earlier conditions. Both the large and the small oil producer as well as the royalty owner have shared directly in all these benefits. These figures and comparions have been officially compiled and released by the United States Bureau of Mines. These general evidences of improvement throughout the entire industry are unqustionably due to the part which the Code has played, with the aid of Federal and State investigation and regulation. The industry is now functioning as nearly normally as could be reasonably expected, and undoubtedly these salutary methods of regulation will eventually crystalize into definite and permanent forms of statutory direction and control. 

Federal Oil Regulation 

Such regulation of the oil industry as has been had up to the present time has been partly through and under the direction of the

CHAPTER XI REGULATION OF OIL ROYALTIES Private Regulation of Royalties 

The necessity for some form of regulation governing the sale of oil royalties began to be recognized early in the development of the royalty industry as a distinct business in itself. However, little could be done. The business was new, every issue of royalties was considered distinct and different from all others. Many such royalties were really worthless from the very beginning, or their original issues or some part of them originally or eventually fell into the hands of either inexperienced or unscrupulous dealers who sold them off at fabulous profits or mixed them with a few necessarily good ones, and then marketed them at prohibitive prices. Often such royalties were split up into such infinitesimally small interests, sometimes as low as 1/150,000ths of original royalty acres, thus making it impossible for any investor to ever hope for any realization of profit from such original royalties, or reasonably expect even the return of any substantial part of his investment, much less any profit upon it. Such royalties were being sold upon every possible plan and basis and upon every imaginable representation, true or false, by high-powered salesmanship. This was especially true in the non-oil-producing states and great industrial centers remote from the oil fields. Reputable royalty dealers, fully aware of all these unethical and dishonest practices, which were viewed with alarm, and also recognizing the great possibilities of the royalty business both to themselves and to hundreds of thousands of royalty investors, began to seek methods for effectually weeding out such unscrupulous activities. These efforts were inaugurated very largely through the organization of a number of royalty owners and dealers protective associations throughout the various oil-producing states, with strict and highly restrictive rules of membership, methods to be employed and the like. Many of these methods have since passed into both state and federal laws and regulations upon the same subject, enacted or promulgated to accomplish similar results.

State Regulation of Royalties 

As the direct result of the combined efforts of these privately organized and co-operating bodies of royalty owners and dealers, most of the states have eventually included oil royalties as one of the subjects of proper regulation under the various so-called "Blue Sky" laws. 

This has been accomplished usually in one of two ways-either by declaring such royalties to be "securities" and regulating them as such 43

along with all other forms of securities, such as stocks, bonds, syndicate interests, trust certificates and the like; or by declaring undivided shares in produced or unproduced oil under lands to be merely personal property rights rather than an integral and actual interest in the land itself, and thus regulating such rights. The principal difficulty with all these laws taken as a group has been their lack of uniformity, either in their wording, their construction, their operative effect or their respective objects sought. The result has been that very often it has been found that what might be considered unlawful and wrong in one state might turn out to be perfectly correct and proper in some other state, and thus such laws became completely evaded and frustrated. The need for a nation-wide law designed for such purpose has long been recognized by almost every one, including the great rank and file of royalty dealers themselves, who as a class rank with the highest of all other branches of business in technical and practical knowledge, character and a desire to serve and satisfy their customers. 

Federal Regulation of Oil Royalties as Securities 

The Federal Securities law of 1933, as amended by the Federal 

Securities Exchange law of 1934, declares a "security" to include “any factional undivided interest in oil, gas or other mineral rights," and further defines an "issuer" of such interest to be the owner thereof or right therein, whether in whole or part, who creates fractional interests therein for the purpose of public offering, regardless of whether such interests or rights are transferred by deed or contract, or whether treated as real estate or personal property by the individual state wherein the land covered by such interest or right is situated. Under the first-named law the interpretation, formulation of rules and regulations, prescribing of suitable forms to be used and the general enforcement and control of the law was vested in the Federal Trade Commission. By the last-named law, such powers have since been transferred to and are now vested in a special body of five members known as the Federal Securities and Exchange Commission. These interpretations, regulations and forms are announced and released from time to time, and are available to the general public without charge by addressing the Commission direct, at Washington. 

Commission Interpretations, Regulations and Forms 

Acting within its granted authority under the former law, the Federal Trade Commission has made certain interpretations of the law, adopted certain regulations and prescribed special forms to be observed and used in publicly offering royalty fractional interests for public sale. Thus far, the following interpretations have been made: 

The term "rights" as used in the law is deemed to be broad enough to include royalties both real estate and personal property rights. 

Fractional interests only are included within the law and not entire, original or undivided royalties not divided into fractions and publicly offered for sale. The transfer of the entire original undivided royalty 44

by the land owner merely as such does not come within the law, inasmuch as it neither contemplates the resale of fractional interests therein nor a public offering thereof. The re-sale of a single royalty interest by an individual owner is not deemed to be within the law, as such sale is not considered a public offering. To bring one within the law, three factors must co-exist-(a) the ownership of a royalty, in whole or part; (b) the creation of fractional interests therein, and (c) the offering of such fractional interests for sale to the public generally. Whether a given offering is within the law is to be determined, not by the number of sales actually made, but rather to the number of persons to whom the offer is open. All royalty interests are grouped into two classes-those involving more than $100,000 and those involving $100,000 or less. Royalty issues falling within the former class are required to be registered with the Commission within at least thirty days before the proposed public offering, and a special form designated as "Form G-1" is prescribed for that purpose. 

This class of royalty interests is confined exclusively to producing royalties. Royalty issues falling within the latter class are specifically and permanently exempted from the necessity of registration upon certain stipulated conditions to be hereinafter noted more fully. 

Royalty Dealers

The Commission divides all royalty dealers into two classes- "wholesale" dealers and "retail" dealers. These classes of dealers are declared to be such, regardless of whether they offer royalty issues directly to the public or are merely responsible for such offerings. "Wholesale" dealers are those who sell fractional royalty interests to other royalty dealers exclusively. "Retail" dealers are those that sell such fractional interests directly to the public only. Royalty fractional interests may be further reduced in fractions by dealers, either by re-registration or by amendment to original registrations in order to obviate the necessity of multiple registration. Sales of fractional royalty interests by one royalty dealer to another dealer for sale makes the former both an issuer and an insurer or underwriter of such royalty interests. Royalty interests offered for sale prior to July 27, 1933, are not subject to the law, provided no such material changes have been made in their terms and conditions as to constitute them new offerings. Royalties offered and sold by dealers which are confined solely to the state of their residence and places of business, either to other dealers or the public generally, similarly resident and doing business are specifically exempted from the law. 

Exemptions of Royalty Issues 

Under the interpretations and regulations of the Commission, certain classes of royalty issues are exempt from the terms and conditions of the law. These exemptions are divided into two, one of which is to be but temporary, while the other is permanent. Within the former exemption are included all royalty interests within the 45

$100,limitation if sold on or before August 14, 1934, in order to provide a period in which dealers may become acquainted with the requirements of the permanent exemption, as well as the working out of special regulations for oil and gas working interests as distinguished from royalties proper. The permanent exemption frees royalties within the $100,000 limitation from the obligation of registering such royalty interests at all so far as it applies to the activities of dealers and brokers, by the furnishing of an "offering sheet" to the purchaser at the time of sale specifying the items of information required by the regulations of the Commission. These items of information are practically identical in character and extent with those required to be filed on Form G-1 in registering royalty issues, except that they may also cover non-producing royalty interests. Such permanent exemptions apply to both producing and non-producing royalty interests.

Registration of Fractional Undivided Royalty Interests 

All issues of undivided fractional interests in royalties of over $100,000 are subject to registration with the Commission within thirty days prior to the proposed date of offering. This requirement applies to both producing and non-producing interests, and two special forms designated as "Form G-1" and "Form G-2" are prescribed for these respective purposes. These forms are in all essential respects the same, with appropriate differences to cover their specific nature and character. They require the disclosure by the proposed issuer or offerer of all posible information either within his personal knowledge or reasonably so, covering the exact details of the particular issue to be offered, as well as of the land covered by such issue, its past, present and estimated future production, if the interest is a producing one, or, if non-producing the specific facts as to its relationship to producing oil or gas lands, with distance, oil sands, thicknesses, transportation facilities, etc., together with all possible available information covering the land itself, all possible title information with appropriate references to public records and the like; the state, county and specific location of the land, with its owner's name, name of the oil field, if any, name of the operator, terms of lease, whether any deductions are to be made from royalty payments, whether land is under mortgage, deed of trust, tax delinquency or other lien or encumbrance with names and addresses of holders of such encumbrances, terms and conditions of royalty; whether any contract has been made to purchase oil output if a producing royalty and name of purchaser, with terms of contract; whether purchaser has recognized title of offerer or his predecessor by making any payments of royalties if producing, together with full information as to the offerer himself, his form of organization, whether acting as principal or agent, and all other detailed information. Accompanying such forms must be submitted four exhibits, the first of which is a plat upon a scale of not less than four inches to one mile of the land covered by the issue and surrounding 46

area to a distance of at least one-half mile from all sides if a producing royalty, showing the approximate locations and spacings and depths of all producing, previously producing and drilling oil or gas wells or either, and of all dry holes, or, if a non-producing royalty, showing a sufficient distance not less than two miles in every direction, with a statement of distance away from nearest production, number and character of producing formations, trasportation facilities and details. as to production. Accompanying such form must also be submitted copies of the opinion of a member of the bar of the state or county within which the land is located, showing condition of title and what examination has been made of it. A statement must also accompany the form as to whether the issuer has ever been denied the right to sell any securities by any governmental regulatory body, and, if so, the reasons in detail therefor. By rule of the commission, a copy of the instrument proposed to be used for the transfer or conveyance of registered interests, copies of opinions of members of the bar and certified copies of governmental regulatory bodies by which the right of the registrant to sell any securities has been denied, or of any subsequent orders of such bodies with respect to such denials, may be omitted from the prospectuses issued by offerers. Offerers are given the privilege of filing with the commission such other exhibits or information as they may desire, in addition to those required by the commission itself.

Offering Sheets 

Royalty issues of $100,000 or less are permanently exempted from the law, as before stated. This is subject, however, to necessary revisions as difficulties in present regulations develop. The reason for such exemption is stated by the Commission, because such an issue "is not deemed necessary in the public interest or for the protection of investors by reason of the small amounts involved or the limited character of the public offering." Such issues, however, are subject to certain terms and conditions so that the total offering does not exceed the exempted amount; that if the interests are producing they have actually been producing oil or gas in commercial quantities for at least seven days prior to the date of offering and have not since permanently ceased to do so to the knowledge of the offerer; that if producing royalty interests, the offering price will not be less than $250.00, or, if non-producing, not less than $100.00; that the dealer shall be subject to certain specified liabilities unless prior to sale contracts covering royalty interests sold to any other than a royalty dealer, bank, trust company, insurance company or person engaged in oil or gas production, he furnish the purchaser with a written and signed "offering sheet" and within seven days thereafter file with the Commission three copies of such sheet, together with a signed statement showing the date of delivery of such sheet, the date of sale and the name and address of the purchaser. Such sheets shall express upon their face that they are intended to be a compliance with the terms 47

of the regulations of the Commission and not intended to bind the Commission in any way to any representation made or its truth, with a clause thereupon prescribed as to the nature of the average royalty payments. Two schedules, known respectively as "A" and "B" are also prescribed, one of which covers producing royalty interests while the other is confined to non-producing royalty interests. These schedules must be filed with the other documents. The information contained in these schedules is in all general respects the same as that contained on Form G-1, except that Schedule "B" is confined to nonproducing royalty interests. These documents must also be accompanied by a plat of the land covered by the royalty interests in all respects the same as that required by Form G-1. 

Benefits of Federal Royalty Regulation 

The benefits and advantages to be derived from such wise and salutary laws, regulations and requirements are believed to be very great, both to royalty dealers and to royalty investors. They tend to eliminate entirely both worthless and either declining or improperly divided royalty interests throughout the entire nation. Their effect is precisely the same as that of separating the good from the bad. Not only will the royalty issues and offerings of the future, as a whole, be infinitely better, more carefully detailed in the way of information and of much higher character than many of those of the past, but these laws will also go a long way towards protecting both royalty dealers and royalty investors and enable them to become more familiar with the exact nature, background and possibilities of such forms of investment, by bringing the royalty itself and such dealers and investors closer together. They will also furnish a complete, dependable and permanent record of such royalty interests throughout the entire country, upon which all interested persons may safely rely with the same assurance of a perfected title to real estate. By thus placing the royalty business upon a much higher plane, it will not only have the effect of encouraging the present great and reputable class of royalty dealers, but will greatly strengthen and augment their number, while at the same time weeding out the inexperienced, uninformed, disreputable or unscrupulous royalty dealer or broker. Royalty dealers and investors alike should feel grateful for this splendid piece of regulatory and corrective legislation, the very first of its kind ever to be enacted, and destined to rank with the finest contributions to the "New Deal." 48

Producing Areas APPENDIX UNITED STATES 

Beginning in Western Pennsylvania in 1859, from which it rapidly spread into parts of New York, West Virginia, Ohio, Kentucky, Tennessee, Indiana and Illinois, and later into parts of Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Texas, Louisiana, Wyoming, Montana, Colorado,

Utah and New Mexico, and finally California, petroleum has within the past seventy-five years grown into its present world-wide importance. 

Our principal producing states in the order of their present rank are 

Texas with 44.1 per cent. of the total production, Oklahoma with 20.3 per cent. and California with 19.3 per cent. The combined production of these three states represents 83.7 per cent. of our total national output, while Kansas, Arkansas, Louisiana and Wyoming account for approximately 11 per cent. The single state of Texas produces almost half as much petroleum as all the foreign countries combined; while the combined production of the other states amounts to about 70 per cent. of the total world output.

The Oil Producing Districts 

The principal oil-producing districts of the United States are the Eastern or Appalachian, comprising the States of Pennsylvania, New 

York, West Virginia, Ohio, Kentucky, and Tennessee; the Lima-Indiana embracing northeast Indiana and Lima, northwest Ohio; the Michigan, embracing that State; the Illinois-Southwest Indiana, covering the State of Illinois and part of the State of Indiana; the Mid-Continent, including the States of Kansas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, northern Texas and northern

Louisiana; the Gulf Coastal, comprising the southern parts of the States of Texas and Louisiana; the Rocky Mountain, including Wyoming, Montana, Colorado, Utah and New Mexico; and the California, embracing that State. Of these districts, the Michigan, Mid-Continent, Gulf Coastal, the New Mexico portion of the Rocky Mountain and the California regions have been the most widely advertised and dealt in with respect to oil royalties, although equally good and often even better opportunities are available to both royalty dealers and investors within many fields of the other districts. In view of their larger activities in royalty dealings, it has been thought best to confine this work to those particular districts only and to omit entirely lengthy descriptions of the other less active ones. This information, which will be found in the Appendix, has been taken from the larger and more comprehensive work of the authors, "The 

World Petroleum Industry," which royalty dealers, owners and prospective purchasers are advised to read carefully.

Michigan District

MICHIGAN. The petroleum producing districts of the State of Michigan are divided into the northeast, or Ogemaw, the central, or Saginaw, the southeast, or Port Huron, and the west, or Muskegon, districts, covering 14.7 square miles of pro- 50

The northeast field embraces Ogemaw county, with the Ogemaw

Township and Muskegon Township fields; and Osceola county with the Austin, Evart and Laketon fields. The first commercial petroleum production began in Muskegon county in 1898. From that date to about 1920 the output varied from less than 1,000 barrels to nearly 11,000 barrels per year. From 1921 to 1925 no petroleum was produced, but production was resumed in 1925 with the discovery of petroleum at Saginaw. From 1898 to 1928 inclusive some 1,231,000 barrels were produced. Since that time, large production has been discovered in the central district, notably in Midland county, and the output has since increased very much. The production for 

1933 was 7,851,000 barrels, giving the state the rank of 10th position. The principal producing sands are the Berea, Dundee, Guelph, Medina and Trenton sands. The grades of petroleum ranges from 36 degrees to 52 degrees gravity, the bulk averaging about 44 degrees, and are of paraffin base, mostly green or brown in color, with fair percentage of gasoline and small amounts of sulphur impurities. It is conservatively estimated that the petroleum resources of the state are probably less than five per cent. developed, and that the prospects for future discoveries are good. Present production methods are not generally as efficient and conservative as might be, except among the larger operators. With the state very likely to develop into a major producer of both petroleum and natural gas, the economic position of Michigan will be important in the future. 

Natural Gas. Natural gas has been utilized in Michigan since very early days, but has only become significant since about 1909, when the volume sold amounted to 510,000 cubic feet. The maximum output was reached in 1915 with 2,060,000 cubic feet. From that date to the present time approximately 30,000,000 cubic feet have been sold. In 1928 the state of Michigan ranged twentieth among the 28 natural gas producing states.

Crude petroleum was first produced in Oklahoma in 1891, during which year 30 barrels were sold. During the next six years the output was less than 1,000 barrels per annum. All of this production came from the northeastern part of the state, where the first development occurred. By 1900 the yield had increased to nearly 

6,000 barrels per year, through the discovery of transportation facilities and the difficulty of perfecting titles to Indian leases obtained from the Government. By the discovery of the first flowing wells with improved grades of petroleum, the production increased to more than 1,000,000 barrels by 1904, and to about 43,500,000 barrels by 1908. The State has ever since remained high in the ranks of petroleumproducing States, often ranking first, but now ranking second with a production of 56

181,506,000 barrels for 1933. The fields cover an area of 985 square miles of petroleum-producing territory and 259 square miles of natural gas producing territory, within which about 140,000 wells have been successfully completed and 359 petroleum and 28 Natural gas fields discovered, most of which are located in the eastern half of the state, many rather insignificant and little known, varying in size from a few acres to many thousand acres in area, with an average of about 2,000 acres. 

Wells have averaged about $20,000 within proven areas and about $35,000 in wildcat territory. The time consumed varies from a few days to two years. The early wells were never over 2,000 feet deep, usually under 1,000 feet; subsequent wells ranged from 2,00 to 3,800 feet, and still later wells from 3,800 to 4,700 feet, averaging about 4,400 feet, the deepest field being the Lovell. Most drilling was done by cable method, but later as drilling extended westward and to greater depths, the rotary system came into common use and has since solved many practical difficulties. 

Initial productions range from a few barrels to several thousand barrels, the largest well on record in the state being one in the Garber deep field with a production of approximately 38,000 barrels daily. Wells in the shallow sands rarely run higher than from 50 to 500 barrels daily; later production averages around 518 barrels daily, the general average over the entire period being approximately 158 barrels per day. The principal productive of the older fields in the northeastern part of the state have been the Red Fork, Taneha, Bartlesville (or Glenn), Burgess, Dutcher, Wilcox and Turkey Mountain; of the Seminole area, the Calvin, Gilcrease, Cromwell, Misner, Hunton Lime, Viola Lime, Simpson and Wilcox; of the Oklahoma 

City Pool, the Wilcox and Turkey Mountain. Petroleums range from 17 degrees to 

65 degrees gravity, with the bulk ranging between 30 and 40 degrees. Most of both the lowest and highest or "freak" oils are found in small quantities in the southwestern fields, but some grades of over 50 degrees are found in substantial quantities at Stroud, Creek county, and Davenport, Lincoln county, in the north. The oils averaging 38 degrees are of intermediate base, dark green or brownish in color, of low viscosity and with gasoline contents often as high as 40 per cent, averaging around 28 per cent. The grades are remakably free from impurities, water or sediment. Crude petroleum prices are controlled largely by the Praire 

Oil & Gas Company, one of the Standard Oil Company subsidiaries, whose posted prices are generally followed sooner or later by the other large purchasing companies, the most important of which are the Magnolia Petroleum and Sinclair 

Crude Oil Purchasing Company, Sun Oil Company, Humble Oil & Refining Company (also Standard Oil Company affiliates) and the transporting and refining subsidiaries of the Gulf Oil Corporation, The Texas Corporation and the Sun 

Oil Company.

The petroleum resources of Oklahoma are conservatively estimated to be little more than 30 per cent. exhausted. Ultimate production has been estimated at more than 8,600,000,000 barrels, of which 2,600,000,000 barrels or 30 per cent. has been produced during the 38-year period of past development; at least 2,100,000,000 barrels or 24 per cent. may be expected during the subsequent 21-year period to full development of present fields; and an additional 3,900,000,000 barrels or 45 per cent. may be expected during the 150-year period of post development to practical exhaustion. 

Natural Gas. Previous to 1902 and for many years following, enormous quantities of natural gas, including casing-head gasoline, went to waste in the state, largely because markets were lacking and also because it was prohibited to be piped out of the Indian Territory. The product was first commercially produced in 1902, with a total quantity of about 500,000 cubic feet. From 1902 to date approximately 4,500,000,000 cubic feet or about 16 per cent. of the total national output came from this state, with the maximum yield of 326,864,000,000 cubic feet in 1927. 

Natural gas gasoline has been produced in Oklahoma since 1911, during which year 388,000 gallons were produced. The highest recorded production was reached in 

1928, with 619,691,000 gallons, or 34.2 per cent. of the total national output. It is estimated that about 4,500,000,000 gallons have been produced to date or about 40 per cent. of the total national yield. Gasoline percentages average quite high. 57

Crude petroleum was first produced commercially in Mid-Continent Texas in 1889, when 48 barrels were reported sold. Up to 1896, the annual output never exceeded 100 barrels. From that date, when about 1,000 barrels were produced, the annual output increased rapidly for a time but rarely exceeded 1,000,000 barrels until 1911, when slightly more than 2,500,000 barrels were produced, and from then on the annual output increased steadily to over 100,000,000 barrels in 1923, with continued increases from year to year until 1933, when the annual production was 396,453,000 barrels, placing the state first in rank.

The petroleum resources of Texas are estimated to be less than 17 per cent. exhausted at the present time, and the prospects for very large future production are very great. It is conservatively estimated that of the original estimates of 

7,500,000,000 barrels potentially contained within the State, 1,200,000,000 has already been produced or 17 per cent. during the 40 years of past development; that 2,900,- 

000,000 barrels or 34 per cent. may be expected during the subsequent 27 years, and an additional 3,400,000,000 barrels may be expected during the 125-year period of entire development to total exhaustion. 

Natural Gas. From 1889 when gas was first produced to 1928 inclusive, approximately 1,312,024,000,000 cubic feet of gas was producd, or about 5.5 per cent. of the national output. This is the largest gas-producing state in the union. Initial outputs range from several thousand to nearly 100,000,000 cubic feet a day, with an average of probably 1,000,000 cubic feet. Casing-head gasoline has been produced since 1915, when 130,000 gallons were sold. From 1915 to 1928 inclusive, 1,697,267,000 gallons or 16.4 per cent. of the total national output were produced.

About 3,000 wells have been successfully completed at depths ranging from 110 feet in the shallowest well in the Jennings field, up to 6450 feet in the Sweet Lake field, the average depth being about 2369 feet, with initial productions ranging from a few barrels up to 20,000 barrels per day occasionally, the largest wells occurring in the Jennings field, but with an average flush production of about 550 barrels per day per well, which declines very rapidly to an average of about 106 barrels per day settled production. 

Average production per proven acre is about 40,000 barrels; average life of wells is less than three years. The rotary method of drilling has been used exclusively in these fields. Usual grades are black and very dark in color, of asphalt base and average about 22 degrees gravity, some running as low as 11 degrees, while some of the lighter green-colored grades of the mid-continent district containing considerable percentages of gasoline are also found in limited quantities. Commercial production was first obtained at Jennings, but important production followed the Spindle Top discovery of 1902, when 550,000 barrels were produced, which by 1909 had risen to more than 9,000,000 barrels annually, all of which, with the exception of about 50,000 barrels, came from the Jennings area. The petroleum resources of the district are estimated to be from five to twenty-five per cent. developed, probably being about ten per cent. Future discoveries will be many and exceed those of the past.

CALIFORNIA. While petroleum has been discovered from north to south over a distance of more than 600 miles, the overwhelming bulk of it, as well as of natural gas, is found in the southern part of the state. Various efforts have been made to group these producing areas. The state officially groups them into five districts designated by numbers, 1 to 5, containing respectively 6, 1, 10, 3 and 38 counties. They are popularly known as the "Valley" (San Joaquin Valley), "Los Angeles," and "Coast." The petroleum fields number 91 and occur in 12 counties, the most important being six in the southern part of the state. Perhaps the best way to classify them is as follows: 

SAN JOAQUIN VALLEY, embracing Kern, Fresno and Kings Counties, the principle producing areas being the Belridge, Elk Hill, Fruitvale, Kern Front, Kern River, Lost Hills, McKittrick, Midway, Mount Poso, Round Mountain, Sunset and Wheeler Ridge fields of Kern County; the Devil's Den field of northern Kern and southern Kings County; and the Coalinga field of Fresno County and the Kettleman 

Hills field, partly in southern Fresno and northern Kings County. 

LOS ANGELES BASIN, embracing the southwestern part of the state and including principally Los Angeles and Orange counties, the principal_producing areas being the Alamitos, Aliso Canon, Beverly Hills, De Witt Canon, Dominguez, 62

Inglewood, Lawndale, Long Beach, Los Angeles City, Los Cerritos, Montebello, Mud Springs, Pico, Placeritas Canon, Potrero, Puente Grande, Redono (Torrence),

Salt Lake, Towsley, Whittier and Wiley Canon fields of Los Angeles County; the 

Newhall field located in the western part of Los Angeles County and extending into the eastern part of Ventura County; the Brea Canon, Coyote Hills, East Coyote, Huntington Beach, Olinda, Richfield and West Coyote fields of Orange County; and the Salt Beach field located partly in southeastern Los Angeles and northwestern 

Orange Counties. 

VENTURA BASIN, embracing most of southern Ventura, northwestern Los

Angeles and Western Santa Barbara Counties, the principal producing areas being the Adams Canon, Bardsdale, Borros, Conejo, Eureka, Four Forks, Hopper

Canon, Modelo, O'Hara, Pirie, Piru, Rincon, Saltmarsh, Scarab, Chiell's Canon, Simi, Sisar Creek, Slocum, South Mountain, Squaw Flat, Sulphur Mountain, Tapo

Canon, Tar Creek, Tar Flat, Temescal, Timber Canon, Tip Top, Torrey Canon, Ventura Avenue and Wheeler Canon fields of Ventura County, some of which overlap parts of Los Angeles and Orange Counties. 

SANTA MARIA BASIN, embracing western Santa Barbara and southern San 

Luis Obispo Counties, the principal producing areas being Arroyo Grande, Cat 

Canon, Casmalia and Santa Maria fields.

OUTSIDE AREAS, consisting of several scattered districts outside the four main productive areas, near Petalume, Solusa County; near Williams, Colusa 

County; near Sargent, Santa Clara County; at Moody's Gulch in the same county near the San Mateo County line; at Half Moon Bay in Mateo on the east coast about forty miles south of San Francisco; at Buttonwillow, Tulare County, at Stockton, San Joaquin County, and in Humboldt and Sonoma Counties.

Of these districts, the Los Angeles Basin and San Joaquin Valley are the most important, having produced about 87 per cent. of California's total output. 

Up to about 1900 small petroleum discoveries were made in Ventura County and within the city of Los Angeles, when a tremendous impetus was given to the industry with the large and rapid development of the Kern River and Coalinga fields in the San Joaquin Valley. Beginning with 1920 with the discovery of the Huntington Beach, Santa Fe Springs, and Alamitos extension of the Seal Beach fields, the celebrated "town-lot" development began. As many as 60 wells were drilled on one 

40-acre lot at Santa Fe; wells often only 50 and 60 feet were drilled; at Huntington Beach single wells were frequently drilled on 25 and 50 foot lots; at Alamitos five wells were drilled on less than a single acre. It was not uncommon for wells to be drilled into each other. There are about 9,000 wells in the San Joaquin Valley district, about 10,000 in the Los Angeles basin, about 9,000 in the Ventura basin and about 1500 in the Santa Maria basin, with perhaps an equal number throughout other districts, with initial productions ranging from a few to as many as 58,000 barrels daily from the famous "Lake View" gusher discovered in 1910 in the Sunset field of Kern County, with a great many gushers of from 1,000 to 10,000 barrels, but averaging about 1400 barrels, at depths ranging from 40 to more than 8200 feet, with wells as deep as 7,000 feet quite common, especially in the Long Beach field, and as deep as 8,000 feet in the Santa Fe Springs field, although the average is around 2265 feet. Both the rotary and cable drilling systems are employed. Grades of petroleum range from 8 to 64 degrees gravity, with the bulk of them averaging about 26 degrees, essentially of asphalt base, black or dark colored with many impurities such as nitrogen, sediment and water emulsion. 

The grades are generally divided into the "lights," or over 18 degrees which are as a rule refinable, and the "heavies," or under 18 degrees which are purely fuel oil grades. The proportion is about 85 per cent. "light" and 15 per cent. "heavy." The lower gravity oils come usually in the shallow and the higher from the deeper producing sands, although the reverse is often the case. Many grades are of paraffin base. 

Outside of the "town-lot" areas, the principal production has been under the control of a few large producing companies. The state also has a large natural gas and natural gas gasoline production; initial gas productions range from a few thousand to 90,0000,000 cubic feet, with an average of less than 1,000,000, producing about 250,000,000 cubic feet per year or about 15 per cent. of the national output; casing-head gasoline first produced in 1911, now has an output of about 600,000,000 63

gallons or about 35 per cent. of the national output. The petroleum resources of the state have been variously estimated at from 25 to 75 per cent. developed, with probably 60 per cent being about right. Future discoveries, while not so large as present ones, are probable. At the present rate of drilling it is expected that all fields will have been discovered within the next fifteen years. It is estimated that about 

4,037,738,000 barrels have already been produced; that about 2,200,000,000 barrels will be produced from present producing areas, and that about 2,600,000,000 barrels will be the result of future discovery. The production for 1933 was 173,085,000 barrels, placing the state in the rank of 3rd position.
