BRIEF GLANCE BACKWARD 

The history of modern Greece is the story of a passionately independent people who have struggled for 128 years against foreign rule, war, civil war and the exigencies of a pauper economy to build a workable responsive democracy. Like the 

United States, the roots of modern Greece are sunk in revolutionary soil. From 1453 to 1821,

Greece was part of the Ottoman Empire. But even in the darkest days of Ottoman rule, the ' spark of Greek independence and democratic institutions was not completely extinguished; all over Greece there were patriots who defied torture and summary execution to form secret societies and labor for Greek liberation. The French revolution gave impetus to the Greek revolutionary idea and in 1821 the Greek people rose against Ottoman rule. Victories were quickly won, a provisional government was established which drew up a constitution modeled after the French. But the Ottoman Empire reorganized, drew upon its might and attacked the young Republic. 

The Greek struggle inflamed the conscience of the West. Europe was in the grip of the post- 

Napoleonic reaction, so no nation would send official aid to Greece, but volunteers came from all parts of the world. The poet Byron came to fight and die. Admiral Cochrane came to organize the Greek fleet. The United States was the first to extend de facto recognition to Greece. Finally, England, Russia and France decided to intervene and by defeating the Turkish-Egyptian fleet at 

Navarino, put an end to the war.

The treaty of London established a Greek kingdom which was comprised of the mainland south of Thessaly, the Peloponnese, some 

Aegean islands and Euboea. This left nine-tenths of the Greek population under Ottoman and British control, and set up a political dynamic which was to dominate Greek political life for the next ninety years the desire to reestablish the boundaries of the state until it should include all Greeks living under foreign rule. 

England, Russia and France, the "Protecting 

Powers" chose Otto, a prince of Bavaria as King of Greece "by the Grace of God". A coup d'etat in 1843 forced Otto to grant a constitution. He was deposed by a military revolt in 1863. 

The three Powers then chose a son of the 

Danish Royal House to succeed Otto. William, renamed George I, assumed the throne in 1863 and ruled for fifty constructive years. During his reign, the people of Greece made giant strides toward building a modern democracy. A new constitution was adopted providing for a freely-elected Parliament, sharp limitation of the Crown function, and a program of civil liberties comparing favorably with the most advanced democracies in the world.

This period was dominated by three great statesmen. The first was Alexander Koumoundouros, who succeeded in keeping Greece out of the Russian-Turkish war and whose policy was rewarded by the acquisition of Thessaly. The second was 

Charilaos Trikoupis, who achieved significant reforms in civil administration, who strengthened the Parliamentary system, and accomplished a great deal of internal reconstruction. The third was Eleftherios Venizelos, the most remarkable figure in modern Greek political life.

Named as Prime Minister in 1910, he carried out drastic reforms in the Army and Civil Service. The Administration of government was strengthened and unified, the Navy which previously had been built up with the American aid was also strengthened. Commerce and Industry were inspired to unprecedented development and foreign relations with other Balkan countries were immensely improved.

In 1912, Greece joined Bulgaria, Serbia and Montenegro in a military alliance directed against the Ottoman Empire. The first Balkan war resulted in great territorial gains for Greece. Epirus, a part of Macedonia, and the islands of Crete, Mytilene, Chios and Samos were annexed. Bulgaria became dissatisfied with her share of the territory won from the Turks and attacked her former allies. This short campaign which was known as the Second Balkan War ended in Bulgaria's complete defeat.

George I, was assassinated in 1913, by a madman, and his son Constantine, assumed the throne.

GREECE AND THE FIRST WORLD WAR 

Thunderheads of the first World War were forming over Europe. Greece was torn by dissension. King Constantine wished either to keep 

Greece neutral or to enter the war on the side of the Allies under specific formalized agreements. 

Venizelos wished to enter the war and fight on the side of the Allies unconditionally. This disagreement was not settled peacefully. Venizelos, on the ground that the King had illegally dissolved Parliament, led a revolution. The Western 

Allies gave him help, Constantine was dethroned, and Greece entered the war against Germany. 

By the treaty of Sevres in 1920, Greece came near to realizing her national dream. She was given the whole of Thrace including Callipoli, the 

Smyrna district in Asia Minor and the Dodecanese islands. But the strife between Venizelos and the 

King had created a deep division-the people were disunited. They could not act together to secure the territories which the treaty of Sevres had given them. Disagreement between England, France and 

Italy on the Middle East served to complicate the picture. The Allies withdrew their support from 

Greek aspirations in Asia Minor. These factors combined with the resurgence of Turkish nationalism under the great leader Kemal Ataturk frustrated Greek plans in the Middle East. The Greek army was defeated, and the campaign ended in the 

Asia Minor debacle of 1922.

GREECE A NATIONAL UNIT 

A major consequence of this defeat was the expulsion of a million and a half Greeks from 

Asia Minor and Eastern Thrace, one of the biggest mass exiles in history. The economy of Greece, already strained to its last resources by ten years of war, had to make room for a million and a half exiles, most of whom needed complete rehabilitation. But the country won a kind of human victory out of the depths of military disaster. The refugees were cared for, and by their industry and fortitude became an asset to the Greek state. New industries were introduced by them. Aided by the American Near East Relief Organziation and foreign loans, Greece launched an intensive program of national reconstruction. 

There was another less tangible but even more significant result of the Asia Minor campaign and the exchange of minorities which followed defeat. 

Although Greece yielded its territorial aspirations and economic resources in Asia Minor, on the other hand, a strong, cohesive, national unit was established on the peninsula and surounding islands. For the first time in 3,000 years, the

Greeks were united within their own boundaries, under their own flag except for three parts of the Balkan and Middle East area, Northern

Epirus, the Dodecanese Islands and the Island of Cyprus. 

Although the period between the two world wars was marked by a great deal of political instability, Greece was able to make great social and economic advances between 1922 and the outbreak of the second World War. 

Following the military disaster in Asia Minor, a revolutionary movement gathered strength. 

Six ministers of state were tried by military court, sentenced to death, and executed. King Constantine was forced again to abdicate and George II became King.

The country was distracted by the question— 

Constitutional Monarchy or Republic. The tide of public opinion was running against the King. In 

1924, a Republic was proclaimed, and the king was deposed. The new Republic was immediately devilled by intense political rivalries, the final expression of which became the coup d'etat. Three cabinets rose and fell in the first year after the plebiscite, the prize of government was snatched by force several times. Finally in 1928, Venizelos again became prime minister, and ushered in a period of tranquillity and prosperity which was to last four years:

During this period of the Republic, Constitutional monarchy forces were increasingly active and succeeded in winning back a large measure of popular support. They were aided by the

gathering effects of a world-wide depression combined with the impact of European tensions. Uneasy public opinion began to swing back toward the crown as a symbol of stability and security. 

This was expressed in the elections of 1932 in which the Populists picked up considerable Parliamentary strength.

The tenth anniversary of the republic was celebrated without incident under an administration of Constitutional-Monarchy sympathy, but Parliament was deadlocked, and the country profoundly divided. The situation began to deteriorate. A black year followed, marked by coups d'etat. 

In the General Election of 1935, the parties for 

Constitutional Monarchy won a huge majority.

The new Constitutional Assembly declared itself for the constitutional monarchy, and a plebiscite confirmed the resolution. George II was recalled. 

Metaxas took advantage of a series of strikes and an atmosphere of fear and civil unrest to persuade the King to dissolve Parliament. He thereupon superseded several provisions of the Constitution and ruled by decree. The people were tired of coup d'etats and revolutions and were passive under the Metaxas rule, which was very similar to the Olivera De Salazar, the Portuguese 

Premier's regime. He ruled quietly, without any of the shenanigans of other dictators. His most important political success was that he aligned Greek foreign policy with the democracies against the Axis powers, and finally sent the Greek armies into battle against the invading Italians. Metaxas died during the war against Mussolini. 

The war and post-war period will be more fully examined when we study the current Greek crisis and in roots.

GREECE FROM 1936 - 1941 

The election of 1936 produced another deadlocked Parliament in which the 15 Communist members held the balance of power between the Republicans and the Constitutional Monarchists. 

An attempt at coalition between the Liberals and the Populists failed, and a non-party Prime Minister was named, with John Metaxas as War Minister.

The Prime Minister, Constantine Demetzis, died soon after he took office. Panagis Tsaldaris, Populist leader, died. Venizelos died. This series of deaths-all natural-and the fact that Greece was threatened to become a second Spain, made the road easy for Metaxas. The Parliament temporarily gave him its confidence and he became Prime Minister.

The King once again asked the political parties to establish a coalition Government and prepare the country for the coming war, of which he was very much aware. He also asked for a coalition to fight against the growing Communist pressure. 

The King failed, no coalition was formed. 

GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF GREECE AND GREEK PEOPLE 

In its national life, Greece is a mirror of western culture reflecting toward the east. 

Greece, of course, is the cradle of democracy.

And the Greeks, heir to a giant cultural and political tradition, are passionately proud of their legacy intensely aware of themselves as a people and as a nation.

Indeed the Greeks are a passionate people. They are certainly a passionately political people. It is said that politics is the national game of Greece, that wherever you find a Greek you find a political party. It is quite true. They are deeply concerned with their government and the way it works. They are personal about it. This attitude is reflected in the character of their argument and their journalism.

The typical Greek is usually short by American standards, with an alert intelligent face. Although there are a great many fair Greeks in the highlands the majority are dark complexioned. 

---

The Greeks have a very strong national feeling. 

Fiercely individualistic, the Greek does not care for large collective enterprises — he is happier in a small independent personal enterprise. But the 

Greek is commercially gifted. He has always been the entrepreneur par excellence of his part of the world.

Each Greek village is essentially a small democracy, as vibrant with local patriotism as an ancient city state.

There is nothing resembling feudalism or its vestiges in Greece. There is a King, but there is no nobility outside the Royal Family. There are no titles, no grants of honors, no counts or barons. 

Local squires and landlords are non-existent.

The villages are vigorously independent. 

The Greeks are people of great enthusiasms. 

Lord Byron is loved by the Greeks not only because he came to fight with them against the Turks but because he symbolized the national character. 

On the other hand, the long period of Ottoman rule has left its imprint on Greek customs. In the rural districts, women play a somewhat oriental role. They wait at the table, and sit down only after the menfolk have been served. They also work in the fields. 

The Greeks love Turkish coffee, which is thick, black and syrupy. The tiny brass coffee pot is seen everywhere, and is used at all times of the day. 

The peasants are lavishly hospitable. When a visitor comes, one of the four or five chickens of the house is killed and cooked, eggs are gathered and hard-boiled and food is procured from the whole village. Coffee or preserved fruit is offered on a tiny plate with an immaculate lace napkin. 

The house may be poor but the welcome is princely and no payment is allowed.

Greek villages are built around a central square with a few old trees shading a church and a spring. 

The surrounding hills are terraced, the houses are shaped like cubes, circled by olive trees. While sanitation in the modern sense of plumbing, is neglected in the more remote villages, the level of household cleanliness is very high. The village church is whitewashed and gleaming; the houses are whitewashed from roof to foundation. The kettles and pots gleam with scrubbing, spotlessly clean sheets are on the beds. 

The houses in the villages are small. Sometimes only two rooms for five or six people, men, women and children, with a room underneath for animals and for stores. There is an open fire-place. Strings of onions hang from the rafters. The windows have painted wooden shutters to keep out the sun. 

Every village house has its ikon. It is usually a picture of the Virgin Mother or of a saint mostly of St. George or St. Nicholas. Before it an oil lamp burns day and night. 

Women can be seen sitting in the doorways of their little houses, spinning on primitive looms. 

Fine silks are woven on these looms and a strong silk-cotton material known as koukoulariko is woven in the districts where the mulberry grows. 

Brightly-colored carpets and blankets are woven. 

A peasant usually owns a small vineyard somewhere in the hills near his village. He makes a wine called retsina, which has a strong flavor of pure sap. The peasants use each others' wine presses and olive presses.

The Greek peasant family lives on a remarkably small amount of food and clothing. Bread, olives, vegetables and herbs cooked in oil form the basis of their diet. Cucumbers, grapes, melons, figs, and other fruits are eaten in great amounts. 

Yogurt or thick curds and whey is a staple of diet. 

It is thick and cheesy and made from sheep's or goats' milk.

Modern clothes are worn both in villages and in the large towns and cities. The French influence is marked. Sometimes the peasants still wear their beautiful colorful costumes-especially on holidays. 

But costumes in Greece are rapidly disappearing, the village girls are beginning to wear the same cotton and silk dresses as their city sisters. 

In some villages the dead are carried to the tombs in open coffins; in the country wild sad dirges are chanted by professional mourners. The body is washed with wine and vinegar and crowned with flowers.

-- The dominant religion is the Greek Orthodox. 

The Church is important in Greek life — in private life, not political. Many of the churches and chapels are built on the foundations of the early pagan temples. Local religious festivals are ardently observed. On Easter holidays lambs are roasted on the spit, and sweet pastries are made of nuts and honey. Peasants travel on donkeys or mules to attend the festivals of the Saints. There is a general fast during Holy Week. On Good

Friday, the bells toll all day long, and the sacred pall representing the bier of Christ is carried outside the church.

It is not a political church, its rhythm is slow and easy; but it is very important in the life of the Greek people. The mystery and ritual and beauty stem from something deep and basic in the 

Greek soul.

It is impossible to understand the political and social problems of Greece without understanding that the country is poor. There is an ancient saying, "poverty and Greece have always been sisters".

Although the economy of the country is basically agricultural, mountains, uncleared forests, and arid rocky soil make two thirds of Greece unsuitable for farming. Soil erosion ruins much of the arable land. And the crops which can be grown are luxury produce-tobacco, currants, grapes, fruits, and olive oil. In order for the population to live off the land they must export these products and import foodstuffs. So the economy of Greece is inextricably bound to the conditions of free-trade and to the import situation of other countries. 

It is interesting to repeat here that there is no such thing in Greece as the large land-holder.

Three quarters of all the farms are under seven and a half acres, one third are under two and a half acres.

The Occupation worked unspeakable havoc upon Greek agriculture. Draft animals were seized from the farms, 15% of all cultivated acreage was transferred to Bulgaria, tobacco and grain stores were seized, compulsory crop delivery was enforced. Greece was left with one harvest famine. Over 400,000 Greeks died of starvation during the war. 

Tobacco, the chief crop of Greece, is grown in most sections, but the finest varieties are found in 

Macedonia and Thrace. Grapes and olives are grown mostly south of Thessaly. Beans are grown. Cereal cultivation, especially wheat, has increased in recent years. This, to some extent, acted to decrease dependence upon imports. But 

Greece can never grow enough grain for her population's needs. In 1939, the best year for yield per acre, more than one third of the total wheat consumed was imported.

The hot rainless summers of Greece do not provide rich pasturage for cattle. Most of the cattle is raised in the north and are used as draught animals rather than as a source of food supply. But 

Greece is rich in sheep and goats which provide wool, hides, milk, meat and cheese.

The mountainous nature of the terrain makes land communications very poor. There are few railroads. In many mountainous districts, pack animals are the sole means of transport. 

Because of the heavy winter rainfall which creates great swamps in this country of forests and mountains, Greece was the most malarious country in Europe. This had further impeded the progress of the country. In some malaria-stricken districts, the people were unable to work more than a hundred days a year. The high rate of tuberculosis is also due largely to malaria which weakens the organism and allows tuberculosis to take hold. 

Recently, this situation has been alleviated by government and American-inspired action-Greek sanitation men have studied in American colleges and returned to institute swamp drainage, mosquito control and modern sanitation methods. 

Malaria in Greece does not exist anymore!

Greece has a very long coastline and many fine harbors. But she suffers from lack of roads over which the produce may be carried from the villages to the harbors. Road construction is a vital need.

INDUSTRIALIZATION AND MERCHANT MARINE 

What Americans understand as a large-scale manufacturing industry was unknown in Greece until about 1920. There were home craftsmen making clothes and tools, milling corn, pressing olives and grapes for oil and wine, drying currants and curing tobacco, but there was no manufacture on a big scale using machinery and power. Since 

1920, there has been considerable development of manufacturing. Textiles, chemicals, leathers, building materials are the most important products. 

Most of the raw material is imported, and much of the work is done on imported semi-finished goods. Main concentration of industry is in the 

Athens-Piraeus district and Salonica-Kavalla is the home of the tobacco processing industry. 

The occupation practically ruined Greek industry. The stifling of imports, the lack of fuel, the seizure of machinery, and the disruption of transportation almost completely destroyed what had been so slowly and painfully built up. There has been recovery since the war, but most remains to be done to restore the manufacturing industries to their pre-war level. And Greece, now, is on the road to a big scale industrialization, especially under its four-year Marshall Plan.

The Greeks have been wonderful sailors since the days of antiquity, and overseas trade is the lifeblood of Greece. She is pre-eminently the merchant nation of her part of the world. As has been seen, the agricultural luxury products must be exported and food imported. Manufacture depends upon the import of raw and semi-finished materials. The Greek merchant marine ranks high among the world's fleets. Although shipping was suppressed during the war, and many ships were destroyed, Greece has rebuilt her merchant marine to an amazing extent. It is estimated that during the current year Greek shipping will have reached a volume of 1,328,046 tons, which compares with 

1,892,745 tons in 1939. And new ships are being purchased steadily.

Greece is not the center of the most important part of Greek shipping. Most of the Greek Merchant Marine consists of ships engaged in international transport. But several Greek-owned ships are under foreign flags. Chief reason of this phenomenon is the very progressive Greek labor code affecting seamen which makes the Greek sailor second only to the American in regard to wages and working conditions. It is thus more profitable for the shipowner to sail under another registrythis naturally cuts off much of the normal revenue accruing from the important shipping industry. 

As we have seen, Greece depends on importsshe must import food, fuel, machinery, raw materials and semi-finished products. To pay for this she grows and exports tobaccos, currants, grapes, olives. Her agriculture was devastated by the war, her manufacturing industry almost destroyed. It is easy to see how greatly Greek economy depends upon the condition of the world market and the prosperity of other countries with which she trades. This exaggerated sensitivity to world economic conditions has been a key factor in the instability of the Greek economic structure. 

GREECE DURING THE WAR: 

(DEMOCRACY'S FRONTIER) 

Eight years ago the small Greek army amazed the world and inspired the hearts of free men by defeating a giant Fascist invasion force and completely upsetting the Axis time-table of conquest. 

Greek people claim, and they are right, that if they did not contribute to the final Allied victory they helped to shorten the war and save hundreds of thousands of Allied soldiers, by preventing the Germans and the Japanese from meeting in the Persian Gulf. Since the hour of the invasion, Greece has not known a day of real peace. The 

Germans came and in their wake came the Fascist Italians and Bulgarians to plunder the country, enslave and murder its people. And when the 

Germans were finally forced to withdraw under Allied pressure, the Communists attempted to seize power through an act of treachery which has remained one of the secret chapters of the war. 

Many fantastic lies have been written about ELAS communist-controlled guerillas. They have been portrayed as heroic resistence fighters 

---

who helped drive the Germans out of the country. 

Actually, they killed more Greeks than they did Germans. In January, 1941, after Greece had been attacked by the Italians, just three months before it was to be overrun by the Germans, Niko Zachariades, Secretary General of the Greek Communist Party, was denouncing the war as "imperialistic" and calling for a fraternization with Axis soldiers and peace with the Axis. The Germans invaded in April, 1941. The Germans, in appreciation, released many Greek Communists from the prison at Nauplia.

After Russia was invaded the Greek Communists, like communists all over the world-changed their tune. They organized guerrilla bands-consolidated them under the name ELAS. They fought the Germans on occasion, more often pretended to fight them. In return they received from Great 

Britain the arms, ammunition, and money they needed in their subsequent attempt to seize the state power. 

They killed many more Greek patriots and 

British soldiers than they killed Germans.

They actively collaborated with the Bulgarians, because a secret agreement had been reached between the various Balkan Communist Parties, providing for the dismemberment of Greece, by the separation of Macedonia as a "soviet" republic. 

From 1942 onwards, ELAS began to fight Greeks much more intensively than they fought Germans, to whom they made formal overtures in July, 1944. There was a meeting in a village near 

Salonika, named Khortiati. A banquet was heldsymbolizing new friendship according to Greek custom. The German commander, Major Fenske, and Captain Kitsos, of ELAS, embraced fraternally. More meetings followed. At Agios Kharalambos an ELAS guard of honor paraded to welcome the German commander. Finally a secret agreement was signed at Leivadi on September 1, 1944, whereby ELAS promised to allow the Germans to retreat from Greece without any fear of guerrilla attack. In return, the Germans promised to supply ELAS with war material. 

This treacherous pact was faithfully adhered to by both signatories. It helped the Germans to withdraw from the peninsula with little loss, and to redeploy their troops to other fronts. It provided 

ELAS with more of the materials it needed, enabling the Communists at that time to establish their terror over a major part of Greece, and later to attack the British troops defending Athens as well as the Greek patriots actually fighting in the mountains against the Axis.

High inter-Allied policy, hinged on the fear of offending Russia, kept this traitorous agreement a secret, the same policy which painted the traitors, collaborationists, and murderers of the ELAS as brave patriotic resistence fighters.

The name of ELAS became so foul among the Greeks, that for political purposes it was dropped. 

After the war, they began to call themselves the Democratic Army- they are often referred to in the press as the Andartes, a picturesque and innocent name for murderers. For they are neither democrats nor bandits, but armed Communists who massacre whole villages, kidnap children and transport them to other countries and engage in the most sickening tortures in order to extend their terror and break the spirit of resistance. 

In December, 1944, the forces of ELAS massacred tens of thousands of Greeks-they did not spare women or children. During 1946 there were wholesale massacres in the village of Skra and Mandalo. During 1948 they entered and burned the town of Naoussa. They butchered whole families, defenceless civilians, pregnant women, children.

THE 1946 ELECTIONS 

On March 31, 1946, a general election was held. 

This election took place under a Republican-Liberal government headed by the Republican leader M. 

Themistocles Sophoulis; stationed at the polls throughout the country were observers from the United States, Great Britain, and France, members of the "Allied Mission to Observe the Greek Elections". The Communists boycotted this election to conceal the smallness of their following. The results showed that 63% of those voting had declared for the Populist party, led by M. Constantinos Tsaldaris. The three Democratic center parties polled 19 percent of the vote, and the Liberal party of M. Sophoulis polled 14 percent. The 

Communist following was revealed after all by those abstaining they totalled 9.3%. The Allied 

Commission pronounced the elections "free and fair.. a true and valid verdict of the Greek people." 

A coalition government was formed which decided to submit the question of the Regime to plebiscite. An Anglo-American Mission was named to observe the voting. The plebiscite resulted in an overwhelming victory for the Constitutional

Monarchy. The report of the Anglo-American mission stated that the plebiscite "faithfully reflected the people's will."

Leaders of the Center and Liberal parties an nounced their willingness to abide by the will of the majority. Meanwhile the Communists took advantage of a general amnesty and summoned their followers from the towns and villages and from across the frontiers to the mountains. They started their second rebellion.

They crossed the frontiers in small groups and moved toward previously picked concentration areas in the Pindus range, and in the mountains of Thrace, Macedonia, and Thessaly. They engaged the Greek army in scattered hit-and-run frontier engagements. 

The Greek army was not big enough to protect all towns and villages. It had to protect the larger towns and keep communications open. The rebels were prevented from establishing a solid front, or from occupying a single Greek town they did succeed in establishing themselves in mountain positions throughout Greece. From there they attacked roads, communications, power plants, and isolated villages. They have disrupted, ruined, killed and kidnapped. They are reinforced from 

Albania, Bulgaria, and Yugoslavia, when hardpressed retreat across the frontiers for rest, reequipment and redeployment. Their positions have been steadily reduced, but they have accomplished much of their purpose, which is not military but economic. 

Because of the intense individualism of the Greeks, and their hatred for any kind of regimentation thanks also to a program of labor and agrarian reform carried out between the two world wars, there was never any chance in Greece for a Russian-type revolution of workers, peasants and soldiers. If the rebellion were not supported from outside of Greece, it would have failed very quickly, it has no real roots in the will of the Greek people. 

But it does achieve the overall goal of international Communism at this stage in world history it exhausts the struggling Greek economy. It weakens the people and their state. It halts rehabilitation 

---

and industrial recovery. It uses up men, money, and goods which could revive the nation. The rebels have destroyed homesteads, farms, granaries. They have impeded and in some cases paralyzed agriculture by firing on the farmers in the fields. Bridges and railroads are blown up, roads are mined, telegraph wires cut, trains and trucks looted. There has been a dislocation of internal trade. Local markets have been cut off from sources of supply. 

THE AMERICAN AID 

If it were not for American aid, it would have become impossible for the Greek state to have kept on paying its civil servants and its military forces, Greek credit would have collapsed, foreign trade would have disappeared, internal trade would have been reduced to local bartering, and the parts of Greece that did not grow their own food would have been brought to famine. 

It can well be asked how conditions were allowed to deteriorate so. The answer is in part that Greece is a democracy, guaranteeing freedom of speech, press, assembly - and as democracies do, it has hesitated to suspend constitutional guarantees in order to protect itself from enemies within. Up until a short time ago it was possible for the Nikos 

Zachariadis head of the Greek Communist Party, to move, speak, write, raise funds, and organize with complete freedom in a state which he denounced as Fascist, while his followers were waging active warfare in the mountains. Communists always invoke the civil liberties they plan to destroy - democracies are always loth to suspend civil liberties even in order to preserve them. The 

Communist Party is now illegal in Greece, not because of its ideas but because by force and violence by murder and fire it was trying to destroy the freely-elected government of Greece, impose a vicious police state on the people, and make the peninsula safe for Soviet imperialism. The Greek 

Army is moving with increasing success against the rebels the people of Greece despite their appalling destitution and misery after eight years of constant warfare, are succeeding in beating off the latest enemy who has sought to enslave them. 

In this battle, American help has been and continues to be decisive. The Greek people are profoundly grateful to the Americans, but they are conscious too, that the battle they are waging is not only for Greece but for democracy as a whole.
