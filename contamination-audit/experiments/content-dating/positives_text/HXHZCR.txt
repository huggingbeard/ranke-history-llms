Our Rabbi 

"And they shall make Me a Sanctuary and I shall dwell therein." (Exodus 25, 8)

The Jewish Community of Gloversville is dedicating its new Sanctuary in fulfillment of these sacred words from our Bible. This is a rare experience of which all of us are justly proud. Here we will glorify God's Name. Here we will sing praises to Him who is the source of our being, in the idiom and language of our people and our Faith. 

It speaks well for our Jewish community of Gloversville that we have paused in the course of our daily activity to think in terms of the needs of the spirit. It is indeed praiseworthy that we have made the necessary sacrifices which made the new Synagogue possible. It is the greatest proof of our devotion to our Faith and of our consecration to the ideals of our people. It is this kind of devotion that made possible the survival of our people throughout the ages.

Centuries ago, when our people were still struggling in the talons of the Roman eagle, its brave sons set about building Sanctuaries in the lands of their dispersion. Their longing for the better and finer things of life expressed itself through the construction and maintenance of places of worship. Here it was that they could invoke the blessings of God, teach the Torah of Truth and assemble together to enjoy fellowship with their brethren. In their continuous wanderings through the ages, the Synagogue became the pulsating center of their lives. It gave them hope in their despair. It brought meaning and significance to their difficult existence. Within the walls of the Synagogue they gathered for worship and they preserved the essence of their ancient Festivals. It became their very life-blood and they built it and rebuilt it, together with the Ark of the Covenant, in every land and in every community in which they lived. It was the first thing they constructed upon their arrival and the last thing they abandoned upon their departure. 

This is the same spirit that moved the early arrivals in Gloversville to build the first Synagogue. The surviving members can testify to the sacrifices that were made in those early years and to the spirit of dedication that went into its construction and completion. It served the community well for over half a century. 

This is the spirit that aroused the present generation to build again and to construct a Synagogue which is beautiful to behold and an inspiration to all who enter within its portals. Deep within out hearts is the spirit of our ancestors which has come down to us through the ages. Deep within our hearts is the devotion to great ideals and the thought that these emanate from the Sanctuary of God. We remember that we are children of God, created in His image with lofty aspirations which reach beyond the capacity of man to achieve and which, if realized on earth, would make of mankind a God-like community. It is in the Synagogue, in our worship together, and in the repetition of our age-old prayers with their lofty sentiments that our spirit grows, our ideals take on greater reality, and we give them a place of primacy in life.

As we complete the Synagogue and begin our regular worship, let us resolve to give emphasis to the real purposes for which it was created. The Synagogue walls are not an end in themselves. They are a means to a higher end. The 

Synagogue is built for worship and for the intensification of the religious spirit of our people, that we may build a greater world under God, where men will be brothers all. Perhaps we can reinterpret the Biblical phrase, "From 

Zion shall go forth the Torah," to read, "From the Synagogue shall go forth the Torah." We will then realize in the not-too-distant future the vision of the Prophet Isaiah, "And they shall beat their swords into ploughshares and their spears into pruning knives. Nation shall not lift up sword against nation, and they shall not learn war anymore." 

Rabbi Manuel Greenstein

Our Cantor 

Not every event occurs accidentally; most of them are irrevocably foregone. Our great Rabbis used to say, "Hakol tolluy bemasal, a fillu Sefer Torah-all depends on fate, even a Torah Scroll." The destiny of my life was, that after years of wandering and travelling through many countries of the world, I came to Gloversville to take over the devoted and responsible position as your Cantor and Hebrew School Teacher. 

The responsibilities of these positions require many qualities, among which are devotion, effort, equity, and of course, much knowledge. A Cantor is called in Hebrew "Shaliach Tsibur-the deputy of the congregation," actually he who stands between God and the congregation. It is he who prays and asks God, for the sake of his fellow men, for their health and for their peace. 

To perform this function, a man ought to be first of all pure of heart and clear of conscience. In musical affairs he should be concerned with making the services more inspiring and more delightful for his people. Each congregation has its specific versions and requirements as to what a service should contain. To this end we are developing a mighty choir, which we hope will bring greater enjoyment and attendance. I am also trying to introduce additional melodies for our traditional prayers.

With relation to the other part of my position, that of Hebrew Teacher, there is no less responsibility. Here, one is required to keep a steady and careful watch over our growing younger generation. To teach them diligently, to show them the beauty that is in Judaism and to instill in them the spirit of the past and a love and feeling of our everlasting faith. I am confident that with my background of living many years in Israel and serving in Jewish Education, the task will be done. 

Now, with God's help, we shall enter into this new Synagogue. These devotions will become even more significant and more responsible. It will be my privilege to continue in the service of a creditable people, who created for me and my family a warm atmosphere and a field for dedicated work. 

May every deed and project undertaken by our community be crowned with success. May God bless the entire congregation with good health and peace. Amen.

Cantor Zachary Kuperstein

Our President 

Having been born in Gloversville and raised by Orthodox parents, I have vivid memories of Judaism at home, in the community and at the Synagogue. My fondest memories go back to my mother's getting ready for the Sabbath: papers on the kitchen floor, chalah baking in the oven, noodles drying on the dining-room table and three candles in the old-country candelabra ready for lighting at sundown. 

I remember the parade of Hebrew School teachers, Chazonim, and shnorers who ate and slept at our house-and the long hours at the kosher butcher shop waiting for the Shochet to slaughter our chickens. At home, at the butcher's, on the street-one topic was paramount-the Schule!! 

My early recollections of Synagogue were not pleasant ones. Sitting in the front row with my father, I was seldom overlooked when a boy was needed to hold the Torah. I accepted as normal, but with misgivings, the utter confusion of the service, the lack of respect by members of the congregation for each other and for the leaders of the services. 

Orthodox services, as practiced by our elders, had no place for the "American born" youth. Rarely did a youngster hear an encouraging word. Our elders prayed with great devotion but most of us couldn't follow or understand the service. And I thought that this was the way it should be. 

During the eary 1950's, I offered my support and services to those young men who had already started the Conservative movement. Many of them, coming from other cities, had seen Judaism practiced with reverence, understanding, dedication and dignity—and still retain the wonderful tradition of our faith. Slowly-ever so slowly the changes took place. No longer did we have the continuous walking in the aisles. . . no longer did we have a hodge-podge of chanting. The Sanctuary was cleaned regularly, there was decorum at the services, and the general tumult disappeared. The services, in Hebrew and English, took on a new meaning. The congregation prayed with reverence and feeling. There was a change in the atmosphere; there began to be a feeling of being in a House of Worship. We started to serve our congregation in every area, with understanding, respect, and dignity. Our religious services throughout the year had devoted attendance. 

From these changes came our need and desire for a new Synagogue. It started with a whisper and ended with a roar of enthusiasm and approval. This approval and support of our new building is a monument to those farsighted young men of the early 1950's.

Now we have our new beautiful building. It is our House of Worship. But Stone-Brick-Mortar-will only give us shelter. We must set the example for our children by giving our Synagogue a Life, a Religion and a History. 

Now that we dedicate this Sanctuary, it is our responsibility to attend its services, support its concepts and strive to make our services beautiful and meaningful. Our religion is not a challenge-but a highly ethical way of life. 

We've built a Synagogue for the future. Let us make sure that we use our future by dedicated attendance, moral reflection, inner meditation and peaceful communication with God. Then, and only then, will we be able to say that our Synagogue justifies our work. Our Synagogue will be a living symbol only if we give it Life. Our children will learn to understand and appreciate our religion only if we expose them to it. Let us do our part. Let us plant the seed of Judaism in the fertile young minds, and the future of Jewry in Fulton County will always remain in strong hands and open hearts. 

Milton Berger

Our Building Committee Chairman 

In February of 1962, the Board of Directors of the Synagogue organized the New Building Committee. It was my honor to be selected to act as its chairman. A campaign for funds was organized. The response from every family was so encouraging that steps were immediately taken for the acquisition of a site, the selection of an architect, and the preparation of plans for a new Synagogue building. 

The committee concluded that the most desirable location would be a site contiguous to the existing Jewish Community Center. Through a gift of the Hippodrome Theatre properties to the east, extending to the corner of Fremont and East Fulton Streets, the area was obtained and cleared. 

After much deliberation, Edgar Tafel, A.I.A.-noted for his work in Synagogue design and construction-was engaged. Conferences were held by the committee and the architect concerning the site, its relation to the surrounding area, its size, and all of the other prerequisites for the building. The architect then submitted a series of preliminary studies, working drawings, and specifications, the data for bidding proposals and contracts, the issuance of certificates of payment, and all of the drawings and specifications for the interior furnishings, pews and Ark. 

Construction contracts were awarded to the lowest bidders: Geo. W. Randall Co., Inc., Johnstown, general contractor; Treher & Jung, Inc., Gloversville, plumbing, heating and air conditioning; Hardies Electric Co., Inc., Amsterdam, electrical services. Construction commenced December 1, 1962, with completion of the building in the fall of 1963.

The committee met with representatives of the three local banks, the Fulton County National Bank and Trust Company, City National Bank & Trust Company, and the Gloversville branch of the National Commercial Bank & Trust Company, to arrange for interim and permanent financing. The banks provided their full support to the project. 

The committee's decisions did not result from any preconception of what a Synagogue should look like. They were determined by careful analysis of the site, the community, its function as a "house of prayer," and basic elements of construction. The final criterion was the qualitative selection of the building as a work of art, worthy of its purpose, a service to its people, compatible to its location, and a credit to the community. 

There is a covered colonnade entrance to the Synagogue on East Fulton Street, leading into a vestibule and lobby. 

The lobby serves all of the areas of the building. To the east of the lobby is an entrance to the library. To the south are entrances to the rabbi's study, secretary's office and daily chapel. Convenient facilities are provided for kitchens, cloak room and storage.

The building is constructed of special-size Roman brick which is seen on both the exterior and interior. This, together with the exposed laminated beams supporting the roof of the sanctuary and reception area, expresses the simplicity of the structure. 

The sanctuary has individual pew seats to accommodate 360 persons. A "flexible" plan arrangement was conceived in the construction so that the sanctuary area can be opened by a sliding door and the adjoining reception area can be used to provide seating for an additional 200 persons. Visibility is excellent from any point in the sanctuary or reception area to the Bima. A complete sound system has been installed to accommodate the entire area. LO

In the east end of the sanctuary, the Bima extends the full width of the area. Here, the architect's conception provides a distinguished and inspiring background for the Ark containing the Torahs. The east wall is constructed of ten precast double-faced concrete tablets, commemorating the Ten Commandments, which are connected by a beautiful pattern of stained glass. The stained glass was designed and made by the distinguished artist, Jean Barillet, of France. The Ark housing the Torahs was conceived in a unique design of a Torah scroll and has been built of oak and brass. The Ark stands free of the wall and is suspended aloft as, after the Reading of the Law and "according to the commandment of the Lord by the hand of Moses, it is a tree of life to them that grasp it, and of them that uphold it every one is rendered happy...' 

The committee, with the assistance of many members of our community, worked hard to achieve the highest level in the construction of this building, one worthy of being God's House. It was inspired by the beginning of the 

96th Psalm: "OH SING UNTO THE LORD A NEW SONG." 

I am deeply grateful for the opportunity of being involved in the building of the Synagogue. May I extend my heartfelt appreciation to the members of the Building Committee and to all of the members of our congregation who worked so hard in realizing our new building. I wish also to express my appreciation to our architect, builders and contractors for their inspired participation in this endeavor. It is this devoted effort and cooperation that make it possible to now submit: "HOW LOVELY ARE THY TABERNACLES, O LORD OF HOSTS" (Psalm 84). 

May it always serve to be a place full of spirituality in which each of us, by our spirit and continued devotion, invests the place with holiness. 

Jacob Schulman, Chairman.

Years Ago 

Of the several articles of interest to be found in the cornerstone of the old Synagogue, the "Laws of the Gloversville Hebrew Mutual Assistance Association" were the most interesting. This book was printed by 

P. Brown, Essex Street, N. Y. C., dated March 12, 1902. With the exception of the preface, which was in English, the entire book was printed in German. Without going into the fullest details, we mention some of the by-laws: 

Purpose of organization: to aid members in critical days as in death, to provide a grave, in sickness and death to provide people to spend the night, to assure a Minyon during Shiva and the Yahrzeits. 

Cost of joining is $1.00 initiation fee.

New members must be voted upon and five blackballs can keep him out. 

Dues, including cemetery benefits, amounts to 40c per month. 

Books of organization must be kept in Yiddish but English is not ruled out completely. 

Benefits: in case of death, wife of deceased receives $40.00; in case of death of child at birth or twins before thirty days, member receives $9.50; age one month to five years—death benefit is $15.00; five years to eighteen years-$25.00. Organization has right to withhold $4.00 for stone. 

Members are also entitled to Shiva money amounting to $6.00.

The secretary will notify members by card of a Minyon on Yahrzeits. A fine of 25c will be the penalty for not appearing.

Members back in dues for three months lose benefit rights. 

Members must give correct address. A fine of 25c will be levied against any member who does not notify secretary of change of address within ten days.

Members who find themselves in difficult financial straits and treasury is not able to help them, the organization has right to assess membership, in order to raise money for such necessity. 

Organization, when running a fund-raising affair, reserves right to determine price of tickets and members cannot refuse to purchase tickets.

Members who use improper language at meetings or services against a fellow-member, or who raises his hand against him to strike him, is fined $1.00 to $5.00 and may even be suspended. 

President has the right to aid members with $2.00 to $10.00 at his discretion. 

Treasurer may receive money in name of organization, but never have more than $10.00 of their money in his pocket.

Among the duties of the trustees: to see that a Christian minister does not participate in Jewish burial. 

To see that the Synagogue is not rented to a Christian organization. To see that burials are not made without a certificate of Board of Health. 

Officers who lose time from their work for Synagogue duties, must be reimbursed at the rate of 30c per hour. 

Trustees and committees who fail to fulfill duties are to be fined 25c. 

Members leaving a meeting without permission will be fined 25c.

The constitution committee consisted of: A. Shapiro, I. Lowenstein, H. Zerni, S. Wachtel, E. Weingradt, I. Marzon, and W. Denkert. 12

Taken from Mr. Milton Berger's address at the Cornerstone Service: "Our service today is unique. First, we are here to set not one stone, but two-and second, we are not even setting a new stone-but two old ones. 

Let me take you through the events of time to show you how this happened. 

Groundbreaking services were held on November 25, 1905, and the cornerstone was set the following month .. December 25. With several quarries in the area, the decision was made to use local granite-and a stone weighing nearly five hundred pounds was brought in for the appropriate lettering. Shortly before the actual service was to be held, news of a massacre of fifteen thousand Russian Jews reached Gloversville. It was decided to dispense with the services, but the actual setting of the stone was still carried out by members of that congregation. Attending that service and attending our service today-fifty-seven years later-are Lazarus 

Rubin and Joseph Rockovitz.

Today, November 11, 1962, with two hundred forty-five members in our congregation, we meet here for another Cornerstone Service . . . the one I call unique. A double ceremony uniting the old and the new and this is how it happened: In the early planning stages, the decision was reached to place the old stone into the new Synagogue. We spoke casually about getting a matching stone. It didn't occur to us that we didn't know where the granite came from, or if it could be duplicated. Anyway, the stone was removed, and not even a crack or chip was lost, but granite of the same color and grain couldn't be found anywhere in the East. We learned that the original stone came from nearby Bleecker. The quarry was located, but no vein even closely resembling our stone could be found. The original stone was cut in half and the second half was finished, inscribed, polished and set into place. We have matching stones and a new inscription for our new building. 

Realizing what took place, we decided to use this service to dedicate both stones, old and new..." 13

Women's Auxiliary 

Seated: Rose Sussman, 1st Vice Pres.; Ruth Galinsky, Pres. 

Standing: Barbars Solomon, Rec. Sec'ty.; Jan Dorfman, Finan. Sec'ty.; Ruth Berger, 2nd Vice President. 1 The Women's Auxiliary of the Jewish Community Center was organized in April 1928 with 28 members; its present membership is 150 women.

The organization has two main functions: one is to aid the Jewish Community Center in furthering its objectives; the other is to represent the Jewish women in general civic projects and activities. 

The Auxiliary was instrumental in raising funds for the Center building; it completely furnished the original kitchen and has kept both kitchens up-to-date with the most modern and efficient equipment. It furnished the original Teenage Lounge and re-furnished the Arts and Crafts room. The Auxiliary has made regular contributions to the working budget of the Center and towards the salary of the Program Director. 

It contributes to the Center Day Camp and to the Hebrew School and Sunday School and helps with the children's Seder, the Purim celebration, and the sale of Chanukah candles and the requirements for other religious observances. 

The Auxiliary published a very successful cookbook in 1938, and the 'Gloversville Hostess Cookbook' has so far been printed in 5 editions. The 'Student Loan Fund' created by the Auxiliary has given loans. to worthy students to help them complete their college education. The Auxiliary has also contributed to the 'Troy Home for the Aged' and the 'United Jewish Appeal.' 

The Auxiliary has also contributed to the 'Community Chest' and other charitable organizations, and has furnished workers for all civic activities. It took an active part in creating the Interfaith group, through which 

Jewish, Protestant, and Catholic women meet at the 'Interfaith Tea' to become better acquainted. 

In addition to all these projects, the Auxiliary has arranged many social activities including the joint Open House program, Cafeteria Suppers, Fashion Shows, Card Parties, Dances, the Mothers and Daughters Banquet and Plays. The Auxiliary has also provided interesting and informative programs at its meetings and has played an important role in all Center activities. 20

Hadassah 

Jean Berg, Program Coordinator; Minette Klein, Finan. Sec'ty.; Betty Mironer, Pres.; Esther Tasner, Treas.; Lillian Silverman, Vice

Pres.-Fund Raising; Helen Rose, Vice Pres.-Membership. 

The Gloversville Chapter of Hadassah was organized on May 29, 1936, with 50 members; its present membership is 192 women.

Hadassah's aims and objectives cover the whole range of human physical and spiritual needs to provide medical care, medical education and research; to assist in the rescue and rehabilitation of Jewish children all over the world; to provide education and vocational training for boys and girls; to support reforestation, irrigation, and land reclamation in Israel; to foster the ethics and ideals of Judaism among American Jewish women and American Youth; to strengthen American democracy through accurate information on the issues of the day and through emphasis on civic responsibilities. 

Gloversville Chapter of Hadassah has tried to acquaint its members with those aims and gain their support through active participation in its many projects. To mention just a few: 

Hadassah has made a table cloth which is decorated with the embroidered names of its members and their families; this cloth is used by other Jewish groups on special occasions. 

A radio program "Vistas of Israel" is sponsored by Hadassah and may be heard every Saturday at 6:40 p.m. over WENT.

Hadassah has sponsored study groups in conversational Hebrew, the study of the Bible, and the history of the Jews.

A choral group, the Saron Singers, has performed many times for various groups. Hadassah has often invited other Jewish and non-Jewish groups to special programs and has tried through discussions, talks and presentation of slides, to acquaint other members of the community with Israel, the country and the people.

Hadassah sponsors and contributes to camp scholarships to Camp Tel Yehudah, a camp which trains 

Jewish youth in leadership.

Hadassah participates in Friday night services at the Synagogue, in the annual Interfaith Tea, in the Israel Bond Drive, and in any project consistent with its policy. 

Throughout our programs we have provided the means to a better understanding of our Jewish heritage and of the state of Israel. 21

B'nai Brith 

Seated: Max Leiser, 2nd Vice Pres.; Eugene Spritzer, Pres.; Charles Edelstein, 1st Vice Pres. 

Standing: Mitchell Fox, Treas.; Shalom Seroussi, Warden. 

Gloversville Lodge B'nai Brith #1620 was granted its charter on April 14, 1946, with Robert Goldman as its first President. The B'nai Brith Lodge immediately after its inception replaced the Center Men's Club, which went out of existence. 

B'nai Brith is a Hebrew term meaning "Sons of the Covenant." Its founders desired to embrace the noble objectives of the Covenant made by God with Abraham. 

B'nai Brith is the oldest and largest Jewish service organization in the United States and in the world, having been founded in New York City on October 13, 1843.

Today, after 116 years of service, the program of B'nai Brith includes fighting anti-Semitism and building better inter-faith and community relations; an intensive program for training future Jewish leaders, and instilling Jewish loyalties in our youth; adult Jewish education; community and social services, and kindred innumerable other services. 

On the local level, B'nai Brith assumes the role of the men's clubs for the Center, for the Synagogue, and for the entire community. 

From its ranks come the volunteers for the Community Chest drive, the Red Cross campaign, the Heart 

Fund, and other worthwhile charitable functions.

Jewish History of Fulton County 

This is the story of our beginning: 

The first Synagogue in America was 200 years old James Buchanan was President of the United States Indians were still roaming our forest and plains The Pony Express had not yet been organized The Gloversville Union Seminary (High School) was just completed Gloversville had about 385 dwellings and about 1500 people when in 1856 Nathan and Harriet (Sporborg) Littauer moved to Gloversville.

Aleph-The Synagogue 

Records show that Gloversville, first called Stump City and Kingsboro, had its first settlers in 1816. 

It was not until 1828 that the actual name of Gloversville was adopted. At that time, the area had fourteen houses. On November 16, 1852, it was incorporated as a village and had its first legal form of government. A city charter was granted on March 18, 1890, to a thriving population of approximately 13,000 people, most involved in the glove and leather industries.

Nathan Littauer, born in Breslau, Germany, on December 17, 1829, was the first Jewish settler in Gloversville. Mr. Littauer came to this country at the age of seventeen, first working in a store in Albany and later peddling drygoods and notions through the Adirondack Mountain foothills. On November 8, 1856, Nathan married Harriet Sporborg of Albany and soon after the wedding, Littauer and his bride settled in Gloversville.

Shortly thereafter, he bought a storehouse and barn located on the present site of the Littauer Building at 92 So. Main Street, and started his first drygoods store. 

After several years experimenting with the importing of finished leather gloves and lambskins for glove linings, Mr. Littauer established a glove factory in his building. The glove business was an immediate success and word went to the "old country" that there was a need for glove workers. 

In 1883, Mr. Littauer transferred his business to his sons Lucius and Eugene and retired from active business three years later. Nathan Littauer was a man who enjoyed the respect and confidence of his fellow citizens. He supported many civic institutions with moral and material help. In 1885, he arranged for free use of rooms in his building for the Gloversville Library Association. At the time of his retirement, he was a director of the National Fulton County Bank and the Fonda, Johnstown and Gloversville Railroad. 

Lucius Littauer, born January 20, 1859, was the first Jewish baby born in Gloversville. He attended the 

Gloversville school system and received his diploma from the Gloversville Union Seminary. After his gradu- 25

ation he entered Harvard and graduated in 1880. While at Harvard, he became friendly with Theodore 

Roosevelt. It was primarily through Mr. Roosevelt's influence that Mr. Littauer returned to Gloversville and devoted a great deal of his time, energy and money to the improvement of civic and religious life in the community. His efforts were soon recognized and in 1896 he was elected as Congressman of this district, serving for ten years. After the death of his father in 1891, Lucius Littauer made a substantial contribution to the building of Gloversville's only hospital, which still bears the name of his father-Nathan Littauer Hospital. In succeeding years, he made many contributions to Gloversville: a playground on Union Street and an outdoor swimming pool and recreation building on West Street and added buildings and wings to the hospital. He played a very important role in the building of the Knesseth Israel Synagogue and the Jewish 

Community Center. 

On January 20, 1929, his 70th birthday, Mr. Littauer founded the Lucius Littauer Foundation . . . established, in brief terms: to aid the needy, general education, medical research and the promotion of Judaism 

Lucius Littauer passed away in 1944. 

Between 1880 and 1890, a considerable number of Jewish glove cutters came to Gloversville from Central Europe. The Littauer factory, which employed many of these immigrants, became the rendezvous for the local Jewish leather workers. According to our records, the first Jewish glove cutter to be employed in 

Gloversville was a Mr. Pfefer. Following closely were a Mr. Sesonske, Daniel Wittenberg, a Mr. Alphin, John Vassenberg, Adam Klopot and a Mr. Oliver. Sam Goldfish, who later changed his name to Samuel 

Goldwyn, was also one of those early glove cutters. 

Louis Myers, Albert Klein, Seymour and Henry Lebenheim, Charles Adler and Gustav Levor, all early settlers here, became prominent in the glove and leather tanning business. Barney Galinsky and a Mr. 

Applebaum, coming here about the same time, opened general stores in both Johnstown and Gloversville. 

Gustav Levor, closely associated in the early days with the Jewish religious life, settled in Gloversville in 1873. His first venture was a store on Bleecker Street, then a general store at 3 South Main Street. By 1876, he was tanning leather in the cellar of that store. A year later, he took over an abandoned church on James 

Street and continued the tanning business. Married in 1882 to Louise Lehman of Utica, he continued the store and tannery until 1890. That year, he built a tannery on the present site. After the destruction of the buildings in a fire in 1913, the present buildings were erected during the next several years. 

Samuel Rothschild, who came to this community in 1902 to work for Mr. Levor, married the youngest of the two Levor girls. Mr. Rothschild, who passed away in 1962, left a heritage of charity, civic and cultural accomplishments. He was a generous supporter of Jewish projects. 

The year 1884 saw the beginning of Jewish cooperation for religious and communal affairs. That same year, nineteen Jewish families formed the Fulton County Hebrew Cemetery Association and bought land on what is now known as Clyde Street. The officers of that first organization were: 

Pres. M. Steidel; V. Pres. M. Meyers; Treas. I. Myers; Sec'y. S. Johnson.

The oldest grave marker in the cemetery is for Annie Steidel, 1884-1886. Charles Glantz, 1818-1889, appears to be the first adult to be buried there.

First mention of a religious service (probably a minyon) was held at the home of Isaac Meyers on N. 

Judson Street in 1889. Mr. Meyers was one of the first settlers here, making a living peddling jewelry throughout the area. It is recorded that he was "a truly religious man" and the legacy he left would bear this statement true. His name was closely associated with the original "assistance" and "burial" organizations and he was an active member of the Synagogue until he died. 

A peddler from Albany who made many friends here passed away shortly after the turn of the century. 

In his memory, his son Max donated 18 acres of land to Gloversville . . . now known as Herman Myers Me morial Park... 1909. 

By 1891, the Jewish immigrant workers outnumbered the original settlers and thirty-six new families joined with the nineteen member cemetery association to form a Hebrew Mutual Assistance Association. 

The officers of this group were: Pres. Abraham Spiro; Vice Pres. Samuel Wachtel and Secy. David Postman. 26

The incorporation papers were filed in the County Clerk's Office on March 16, 1892. At the same time, a hall was rented at 55 So. Main Street, with regular and holiday services conducted by Rev. Abram Zuckerwar.

Abram Zuckerwar came to Gloversville in 1891 and immediately assumed leadership in religious affairs. 

He was born in Warsaw, Poland, and arrived in this country at the age of thirty-three. On May 26, 1894, only three years after being in Gloversville, Mr. Zuckerwar was appointed Reverend and served in that capacity until he died on April 27, 1937.

Records show that many Jewish families settled in Johnstown during the late 1890's. A Hebrew Temple was established with a Rabbi L. Yasgour officiating. Abram Harrison, owner of a drygoods store, went on to become the first Jewish Mayor in 1912. Wolfe Horwitz, who came to Johnstown in 1890, opened a tannery before the turn of the century. His devotion and dedication to the religious life lies in his record as the first president of the Gloversville Hebrew School, first president of the Fulton County Relief Society and his close association with Synagogue and Jewish Community Center work. 

The first Jewish affair on record in this vicinity was a Sium Hatorah and Ball in 1895, in a rented hall on Main Street, Johnstown. About the same time, Gloversville organized the Bikur Cholim and the Gloversville Israelite Cemetery Association.

By 1896 the Fulton County Hebrew Cemetery Association and the Hebrew Mutual Assistance Associa tion had merged. Two years later, the coordinated efforts of all the Jewish Organizations raised $2,000 to buy land on East Fulton Street for the eventual building of a Synagogue. Seven years later, November 25, 1905, ground breaking services were held and the cornerstone for the new Synagogue was set one month later. 

The Cornerstone Service listed the following for participation: Rabbi B. Grad of Amsterdam, Rabbi M. 

Meyers of Albany, Ralph Moses, Solomon Schrecker, Gustav Levor, Jacob DeBeer, Hon. Lucius Littauer, 

Mayor Eugene Beach and the Sunday School Choir. Mr. Littauer was given the honor of spreading the mortar. 

Following the completion of the Synagogue in 1906, a number of societies were organized and they became very important in giving aid to the Jewish immigrants who were to come to this area in large numbers during the next ten years. We list those of record:

The Brith Abraham, The Brith Sholom, Free Sons of Judea, The Fulton County Relief Society, Hebrew 

Ladies Aid Society, The Workman's Circle, Sons of Benjamin, Council of Jewish Women, Fulton County Hebrew Aid Association, Jewish Chautauqua Society, Jewish Socialist-Territorialist Labor Party. In 1908, 

The Jewish Synagogue Agudath Achem was organized by a group of men who were displeased with the way things were being done at the new Synagogue. This group had its own Torah and held services in the Arietta 

Hall on Bleecker Square for about one year. No records were kept, but for a time it appeared that this group would gather enough strength and support to build another Synagogue. 

Although the cornerstone of the 1905 Synagogue contained the letters H.M.A.A., the congregation was generally known as Knesseth Israel Synagogue. However, it wasn't until March 12, 1918, that the formal change was made at a special meeting called for the sole purpose of authorizing that change. The meeting notices were signed by Jacob Meltzer, President and the Secretary Morris Kletcher. 

The dedication of the Synagogue took place on Monday evening, September 17, 1906. The Sanctuary was filled to overflowing and it was obvious that everyone was deeply impressed by the beauty of the Synagogue. The master of ceremonies for the occasion was Joseph Bachner. The program began with the children of the Sabbath School singing a ritualistic hymn and Rev. Abram Zuckerwar chanting the "Ma 

Tovu." Rabbi George Bergman of New York offered the first prayer. It is reported that his voice "rang clear in supplication . . . and the occasion was a memorable one." 

The honor of lighting the chandelier was given to Wolfe Horwitz; the lighting of the Menorah to 

S. Lehr; and the lighting of the Perpetual Light to Abe Nelson. Leonard Meyers, vice-president of the 

Synagogue, gave a short talk about the beginning of religious services in Fulton County and finished by calling on Joseph Bachner who had the honor of naming the New Synagogue: Knesseth Israel. Ralph Moses, entrusted with the presentation of the key, was the next speaker. In presenting the key to Gustav Levor, Mr. 27

Moses said, "May the door ever be open to him who wishes to worship God."

It is interesting to note that a Rev. F. W. Betts, a D.D. from a Universalist Church, had been invited to give an address. It is recorded that his speech was interrupted by "volleys of applause" and we quote from the record: "that a Jew should be proud of his ancestry, of his race, its history and traditions, and its priceless gift to the world. It constitutes one of the most magnificent chapters by the gift of the mother religion of us all. Wrought into the morals of the world is the Decalogue and its ten clauses. Be not ashamed of the faith of your fathers."

The Sabbath School sang the dedication hymn and scrolls were taken to the Ark by Isaac Meyers, Morris Feldman, O. Osterlink and S. Wachtel. Barney Pollak read from one of the Torahs. Honored with the opening of the Ark were X. Rothheim and J. DeBeer. The placing of the scrolls in the Ark was done by 

S. Schrecker, J. Weingrod, D. Postman and Mr. Whitehead.

Rabbi Bergman gave the main address, and we quote from the Morning Herald issue of Tuesday, September 18, 1906. “Rabbi Bergman, as though inspired, thrilled his hearers with his splendid oratory, his plea for the Jew, his recital of the trials through which his people have passed for centuries and his tribute to the land of the Stars and Stripes where the Hebrew people have at last found the dreamland of Palestine -the land flowing with milk and honey." In summation, we again quote "The name which this beautiful 

Temple bears does truly not make any discrimination between Reformed or Orthodox Jews. Knesseth Israel means the gathering of Israel without any regard for his private views concerning Judaism. Knesseth Israel represents more the spiritual ideal than anything else. It means that this place shall be a center where the ideals and beauties of the Jewish religion shall be taught. It stands as a silent witness to a grand and lofty conception of a Supreme Being who, evidenced by the philosophy of history, delights in protecting His peculiar people, carrying His message of righteousness to the people of the earth. To promulgate this ideal, this handful of men and women believed that today their efforts find reward in that they lived to dedicate such a beautiful Synagogue, accentuating a sublime spiritual ideal and a noble ethical principle which is to become the common heritage to humanity. Amen.”

The years 1910-20-30 saw the Synagogue functioning as an Orthodox group, serving the Jewish community from birth to death. The responsibility for a Mohel, Shochet, Chazon, Rabbi, Teacher, Chevra Kadisha and Cemetery came under the watchful eyes of the administrations. Our history of Chazonim included Krubitsky, Haber, Katz, Marmonstein and in our own time, Abram Shapiro. 

These were difficult years for the Synagogue. Internal politics, finances, social standings, policy, and the lack of interest of the young people plagued the administrations. In spite of the problems, the Synagogue continued its services to the community. 

The early years of the Synagogue were filled with social and cultural events of excellent caliber. Speakers, travelers, dances, plays, shows, parties, anniversary celebrations, Bar-Mitzvahs—all these and many other events took place in the Vestry and Sanctuary. Throughout all these years, the Synagogue enjoyed good relations with our non-Jewish neighbors. There existed only the highest respect for our Jewish community.

However the needs of the community were changing. It became more difficult to get the young to attend services and the membership began to dwindle. With a drop in income, only those areas necessary to the religious life of the Synagogue were attended to. 

The late 1930's and 40's saw a new interest in Synagogue affairs. Gloversville was fortunate in having an influx of new businesses, professionals and associated families. The war years and its affects saw a resurgence of religious thinking and soon a new group of younger men became interested in the administration and religious affairs of the Synagogue. 

These years were not easy ones. . . many of the elders resented the encroachment of the "Young Turks" and used every means to discourage and delay any change. The motives of the new group were always questioned and it took many years of dedicated attendance, participation and financial responsibility to earn some semblance of acceptance. During these years, time took its toll . . . Many of the men who resented the 29

"Young Turks" as well as some who supported them were called to rest. Looking back on those years, there were times that progress seemed hopeless... but Time is always on the side of the young, the vigorous, the capable and the dedicated. By 1948 it was inevitable... the Orthodox service would soon be coming to an end... and the new Conservative service would take over. Among the Orthodox-observing elders who supported the Conservative Movement and its changes was Sam Madora. Mr. Madora also served us as our kosher butcher.

To those men before us, we owe a great deal. Since 1891, under the most adverse and trying times, these men, by their dedication and devotion to their religion, found time and money to build and maintain a Synagogue. This background cannot be ignored nor depreciated. Those of us who are now involved in the continuation of those early ideals can only hope that we will make as deep and as lasting an impression. 

In view of the religious and cultural thinking that existed from the very beginning of Jewish life in this area, it is not surprising that three of our native-sons have chosen Judaism and its environs as their professions.

Rabbi Eugene Sack, a Chaplain during World War II, is now the spiritual leader of Congregation Beth- 

Elohim in Brooklyn.

Graenum Berger, long identified with Jewish life, is Camp and Y.M.H.A. Consultant with the Federation of Jewish Philanthropies of New York.

The third "worker" served in the Second World War following his college graduation. After a thirteen year business career, Howard Berger returned to college, obtained his degree in Social Sciences and is now Organization Consultant with the Jewish Community Federation of Cleveland. 

The "change" was a gradual one. Examination of 'Minutes of Regular Meetings' tells the story best: 

The Original Minutes were recorded in Yiddish. 

The first minutes written in English were in February 1937. At that time Mr. Resnick was the teacher of the Hebrew School. During that year, Rev. Abram Zuckerwar passed away, and the Bleecker family of five and Harold Pozefsky were killed in an auto accident. Meetings were held with the Jewish Community Center to discuss the possibility of having a Community Hebrew School. It was also the year the mortgage was paid off.

The following year, a mass meeting of the Congregation was held in the Sanctuary and a resolution was passed protesting the German treatment of Jews in Europe. A telegram to this effect was sent to the 

President of the United States. It was during this year that a Memorial Service was held, honoring Rev. 

Abram Zuckerwar, and a bronze tablet of recognition was placed in the vestibule. H. Andrew Schlusberg and Jack Kolins, members of the Synagogue, brought up the possibility of starting Conservative Services. The suggestion was tabled. 

A year later, 1939, a trial Friday evening Conservative Service took place. Max Alderman, chairman for this special service, invited Rabbi Michalson of Peekskill, N. Y., to officiate. Interest in this type of service was substantiated by the ninety persons attending, but it was not continued. 

During the next several years, there were many discussions about starting the Conservative Service, but with our young men at war, the pressure of war news, the lessening of participation made necessary due to all the aspects of "war years," little was done to further the movement. These years served well for the reorganization of the 'Orthodox Group' and in 1944, the Synagogue affiliated with the Union of Orthodox Congregations of America. Orthodox services continued during the next several years, but the war was coming to an end and new faces began appearing at the services. Conservative Services were discussed at almost every meeting. Several attempts were made to involve the youth in the Sabbath Services and there was discussion about hiring a Rabbi/teacher to serve the community. The war over, a fitting Memorial Plaque honoring our boys killed in the service of our country, was placed in the sanctuary. 

In 1947, H. I. Berger passed away. To honor his services to the Synagogue, Hebrew School and the Jewish Community at large, a special funeral service was held in the Sanctuary of the Synagogue. 

The tempo of criticism of the services picked up about this time. Books, decorum, length of service, the 30

Chevra Kadisha, condition of the Sanctuary, lighting, heating, and general administration of affairs all felt the pressure of the fresh 'Young Turks.' At one meeting, discussions of building a new Synagogue in another neighborhood was ruled 'out of order.' By the end of 1948, the repeated recommendations of Joseph 

Lazarus and Dr. Harold Bauer to try 'Conservative Friday Night Services,' for a trial period of one year, were accepted and Rabbi J. Leonard Azneer was engaged for two 'Friday Night Services' each month. 

Our first Rabbi stayed only a few months, and Rabbi Gordon was next to fill the vacancy for the bi-weekly and holiday services.

There was criticism of these services from the very beginning. The introduction of English readings labeled the proponents as 'goyim' and the predictions of the 'end of Jewry in Gloversville' could be heard at every meeting. Motions to change all service from "Orthodox" to "Conservative" were defeated meeting after meeting. It wasn't until the October 1949 meeting that a resolution was accepted for a "trial period" of one year for fully "Conservative Services."

The following month, Rabbi Wasser was engaged as the first Rabbi/teacher. 

The first Friday Night Oneg-Shabat was held in his honor. His request for an office in the Synagogue was tabled, and it was several years before an office was made available for a secretary. A regular system of billing was instituted and the auctioning of 'Aliyahs' from the pulpit was discontinued. A new front entrance to the cemetery was donated by Jacob Zuckerwar. The discussions for modernizing the services kept many meetings going well past midnight. 

1951 was the turning point-Rabbi Herman Grossman now led the Services. Joseph Lazarus, one of the earliest supporters of the "Conservative" movement was the President of the Synagogue and Isaac Nayman was hired as part-time cantor to participate in the Friday Night Services. The Daily Minyan Services of 5:45 were started. A house at 21 Prospect Avenue was purchased by the Synagogue for our Rabbi and the first Brotherhood Service was held in the Sanctuary. The vestry was renovated, a kitchen was built and fund-raising by special events was instituted. Ushers were used during the High Holiday Services for the first time and a part-time secretary was hired. A study for a new constitution incorporating the 

Conservative Movement was authorized.

Our next Rabbi/teacher was Samuel Schaffler. Rabbi Schaffler participated in the Gloversville High School 

Baccalaureate Services for the first time. Dues were raised from $12 to $18 per year (this increase had been "under study" for 6 years every previous attempt to raise the dues was defeated.). Our next president was Leon Harris. During his tenure, the Synagogue became affiliated with the United Synagogues of America, and the first written rules and regulations governing cemetery procedure were prepared. The effect of Rabbi Schaffler on the community at large led to an invitation to participate in 

Inter-faith Services.

The third president was Dr. Joseph Berg. Our Rabbi asked to be released from his contract to accept a position with the Education Department of the Jewish Theological Seminary, and most of the year was spent in interviewing Rabbis. Few changes were made in the services, as there was still a resistance from some of the elders. It became necessary to adjust dues again . . . this time to $30 per year. The interviews produced Rabbi Isaac VanderWalde as our next religious leader. 

October 1955 saw the installation of Milton Berger as president. With the able guidance of the Rabbi, the experience of four years of Conservative Services, and the support of the general Jewish community to move forward, many of the controversial subjects were tackled and resolved. Arrangements were made to replace the Chevra Kadisha with a Jewish undertaker . . . Levine Memorial Chapel of Albany. The 

Four Year Rule (minimum acceptable formal educational for Bar-Mitzvah) was instituted. The first Memorial Service was held at the cemetery between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kipur. The new constitution, changing the concept of services from Orthodox to Conservative was approved on October 29, 1956. Now, for the first time in our history, a family membership existed. (Formerly, only the male head of family was a member.) Instead of congregation meetings, an elected Board of Directors and Officers consisting of 33 men and women would be responsible for the religious, financial and physical affairs of the Synagogue. 31

Rev. Abraham Shapiro passed away in 1957 after thirty years of serving our community as Cantor, Shochet and Mohel. His passing ended the era of the traditional Orthodox chanting of the psalms. 

During the next two years, other new services were introduced. The Synagogue sponsored a lecture series of well known Rabbis, formed a Post Bar-Bas Mitzvah Club after the (first) Bas-Mitzvah service of Debora Berger was held in the sanctuary on a Friday night. An Israelie member of the United Nations was invited to address the Local Ministerial Association at a luncheon in the vestry and the first Youth Seder was held in the vestry. 

In September 1959, Bob Ekstein and Jack Schulman purchased buildings surrounding the Synagogue, started demolition of the several houses and stores, and donated the land to the congregation. This was the beginning of a new phase in our Synagogue life. 

The annual Meeting of October 1959 voted in Merwin Greene as president. During the next two years, the Board of Directors discussed renovation, rebuilding, additions, and remodeling, and a new Synagogue. 

An architect hired to submit plans for a renovation and addition submitted an estimate of $100,000. Placing this amount of money into an old building led to considerable discussion and dissension among the membership. In the midst of these problems, Rabbi VanderWalde submitted his resignation . . . and again a committee was appointed to interview candidates. 

In August 1960, Rabbi Manuel Greenstein came to Gloversville. A gentle, dedicated and learned man, he was a consolidating influence in the Jewish Community and helped immeasurably with the building plans.

In a report made by a professional fund-raiser, it was noted that our community preferred to contribute to a new Synagogue rather than remodel the old. In view of this report, Jack Schulman presented a resolution to the Board of Directors in January 1961 calling for a New Building Fund Campaign in the amount of $250,000. This was passed unanimously and Mr. Schulman was made Chairman of the project. Mrs. Martha 

Schine started the campaign with a pledge of $25,000 in memory of her husband, Louis W. Schine. The campaign was a "soft-sell" type, but the results were remarkable. The February report showed pledges of $145,000, and March reported $175,000. By the end of the year, $190,000 had been pledged. 

Property was purchased at the corner of East Fulton and Fremont Streets in March 1962, and the Hippodrome Theater property was donated to the project by J. Myer and Hildegarde Schine. Edgar Tafel of New York was engaged as architect, and the first plans for a new building were submitted in May. 

The death of Hebrew School teacher Dr. Louis Greenberg in February created a void in the Jewish organizational system. Our Rabbi and Mrs. Greenstein took over the duties and finished the school year. During this period, the Synagogue and the Hebrew School decided to look for a teacher/cantor, and after several interviews, Leopold Kagan was hired for this dual purpose. Although Mr. Kagan served only one year, the concept of teacher/cantor proved very beneficial to the needs of the community. 

Mr. Greene's term of office ended October 1961. Once again Milton Berger was elected president of the congregation. By this time, ritual, services, finances, and general administration were well established and the next two years saw concentrated efforts in the erection of the new Synagogue. The final plans for the new building were approved at the June 1962 meeting and demolition of the buildings was started. 

Pledges now totaled $205,000. The ground-breaking service was held at 8 A.M. on a cloudy Monday morning, September 10. About 75 members of the congregation attended the brief service and most of those present took turns at turning the earth. Construction started the same week. 

At the time of the cornerstone service in 1905, Lucius Littauer was serving our country as Congressman. 

In 1962, at the time of our cornerstone service, Samuel D. Berger, another native son, was representing our country as United States Ambassador to South Korea. During the intervening years, no other member of our Jewish community had attained such high honors in our governmental service. 

In August, 1962, the committee proposed Zachary Kuperstein as teacher/cantor. Mr Kuperstein was promptly engaged and participated in the High Holiday services. With Rabbi Greenstein and Cantor

Kuperstein on the pulpit, a trained choir in the balcony, the Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur services reached a new high. It would be the last High Holiday services in the old Schule, and there seemed to be 32

a quiet reverence and awe about those last moments. Every seat was filled and sermons and messages from the pulpit brought back old memories and new hopes for the future. This last service was different ... and everyone recognized it.

The following month saw the removal of the cornerstone from the old Schule and on November 11, 1962, the Cornerstone Service for the new Synagogue took place. The outdoor service attracted about 300 members and friends. The thermometer dropped to 20 degrees but the hour-long service was so inspiring that not a person left the gathering. Although Gloversville experienced one of its worst winters in thirty years, construction continued throughout the winter and the Dedication Date of September 9 was announced at the regular meeting in March.

Looking back on the past 12 years, the Synagogue may be proud of its congregation. The 5:45 Daily 

Minyun Services are still being held, and only rarely is the quorum lacking. The Friday Night Service and Oneg-Shabat have never missed a quorum, and the choir is as much a part of the service as are the Rabbi and Cantor. The Conservative Service, with mixed congregation, uniform books, English readings, modern administration, beautiful Bar-Mitzvahs by boys excellently prepared, High Holiday and other Holiday Services prepared with the fullest knowledge that they serve the best interests of Judaism . . . these and countless other achievements are now being enjoyed by the Jews of our community. 

The seeds sown 12 years ago have borne fruit. We are proud of our accomplishments, our local history and our eternal heritage.

Bet... The Hebrew School 

The Jewish people of Gloversville, like their forebears, appreciating the worth and necessity of Torah study, began teaching their children how to read the Hebrew prayers as early as 1893. The first recorded 

Hebrew teacher in Gloversville was a Mr. Lempert, who went from home to home and taught the word of 

God to the children. It is recorded that "small amounts of money were paid to the teacher.” In 1895, a Mr. 

Yaffe assumed this responsibility and he was succeeded by a Mr. Benjamin in 1898. 

In 1900, Mr. Ashe, Mr. Simon Stein and Rev. Zuckerwar offered their services for teaching without tuition. A school was organized and these men took over the lessons for the fifteen to twenty youngsters. 

Parents contributed only for light and heat. In 1902, a Sunday School was organized to acquaint the children with Jewish history and culture. Miss Belle Lebenheim and a Miss Ashe assumed the teaching responsibilities. Mr. Shapiro, Mr. Tanzman and Rev. Zuckerwar also helped in the Sunday School activities. Religious services, the Hebrew School and the Sunday School classes were held in a hall at 55 So. Main Street until the new Synagogue was dedicated in 1906. 

The Jewish Community was growing and there was a need for additional classes. Rabbi Grad of Amsterdam and a Miss Lazarus were added to the teaching staff, and Rabbi Grad acted as principal until 1909. 

Between 1910 and 1913 there is no record of formal instruction, but we know that a few learned men and women took it upon themselves to continue Hebrew education on a family or friendship basis. 

Mr. Winick, with a reputation of being a great Talmudist, taught here between 1913 and 1916. It is reported that his methods were very formal and strict. Two years later, the first Hebrew School was organized and a Mr. Imbiow, a qualified teacher, was engaged. The thirty-five children were divided into three classes, meeting in the vestry of the Synagogue every afternoon between four and seven o'clock. Their studies included Hebrew, Jewish History, and the Bible. Mr. Imbiow taught for two years and was succeeded by Mr. Godman, who instructed through 1920. The original organizers of the school were: H. I. 

Berger, Jacob Meltzer, Max Pozefsky, Morris Pozefsky, Albert Sack, Simon Stein and Rev. Zuckerwar.

During this period the Y.M.H.A. had been organized, and the directors offered the facilities of the Elm 

Street rooms to the Hebrew School. Mr. Katzin was now the teacher, and he was followed by Dr. Richter, who taught through 1922. 333

During the early days of the Hebrew School in the J.C.C., Wolfe Horwitz, Abe Ferber, Ralph Moses, and Rabbi Jason of the Jewish Community Center ardently supported the movement. Center directors Abram 

Magida and A. J. Glusker acted as president and superintendent during their tenure, and contributed greatly to its improvement. Directors of the Hebrew School in 1922 were: 

H. 1. Berger, Mrs. Isaac Finkle, Isaac Pozefsky, Max Pozefsky, Nathan Rosenbaum, Lazarus Rubin and 

Gus Wertman. We quote from an early communication written on December 14, 1922 ... "The existence of the Hebrew School in Gloversville is in a large measure due to the untiring efforts of H. I. Berger and 

Albert Sack . . .'

In 1922, the school listed forty-five children and was under the direction of Hyman J. Grant, who stayed with the community until 1928. Mr. J. Ruttenberg was the next instructor, and the School continued in formal session through the early 1930's. 

From the time Mr. Ruttenberg left Gloversville, the School continued with a succession of full-time and part-time teachers, with classes being held in the new Jewish Community Center. The budget was met by personal solicitation and donations. Several executive directors of the Center acted as teachers . . . always with the single purpose. . . that our children must be taught; no effort was too great; any amount of learning, however small, was better than nothing at all. 

From the turn of the century to the late 1940's, Mrs. Isaac Neivert, a resident with deep religious feeling and talent, prepared a large number of our boys for their Bar-Mitzvah. Although this was done on a private basis, Mrs. Neivert cooperated fully with the Synagogue and Hebrew School officials. During the many changes of Hebrew School teachers, Mrs. Neivert's dedication to her Synagogue was one of the outstanding services made to our Jewish community. Our historian Sam Alderman was among her first students.

It was the good fortune of the Gloversville Jewish Community that George Dorfman, a newcomer, took a great interest in Hebrew School work. It was by his urging that the Hebrew School be reorganized, that on 

October 30, 1938, a meeting was called and interested members of the community gave their views. It was decided that a board of directors be formed, to be made up of three members appointed by the Jewish Community Center, three from the Synagogue, and six members from the general Jewish community. Officers were elected, with George Dorfman President; Isadore Heiman, Vice-president; Samuel Kristt, Treasurer; Mrs. Winslow Klopot, Recording Secretary. Other members of this Board were: Abraham Fink, Mrs. Bob Goldman, Mrs. Isaac Neivert, Max Pozefsky, Lazarus Rubin, Louis Rubin, Sol Sackheim and J. 

Myer Schine.

The Board assumed the responsibility of meeting the budget, hiring a competent teacher, developing curriculum and general administration of Hebrew School activities. Hilel Jacobson was the first teacher under this new system, and the first registration was thirty-four students. 

The Epsilon Alpha Tau Sorority of the Jewish Community Center formed a Sisterhood to the School, and in February 1939, Lillian Shwartz and Yetta Neigelberg were appointed as members of the Hebrew 

School Board. 

Mr. Jacobson served the community for eight years, and it was during that time that the continuity of a capable instructor proved so effective. The students who came through the school during those years were well educated in most areas of Judaism. 

In July 1947, Mr. A. S. Weiller was hired. The registration had grown to fifty-three children. The system was good. Competent administration and modern teaching methods built a foundation that would endure for many years. Abraham Alpert, Bob Goldman, Jacob Lazarus, Jack Schulman, and Sam Solomon were now members of the Board. With the increase in student registration, Mrs. Barney Mironer was hired as an extra teacher. Although Mr. Weiller left after two and a half years, he made a great contribution with his introduction of religious services. It was at this time that Dr. Harold Bauer, Bob Ekstein, Lou Jacobson, Joseph Lazarus and Harry Rubin became associated with Hebrew School work. For a short time, Rabbi 

Wasser acted as the Hebrew instructor; then the Hebrew School collaborated with the Synagogue in August 34

by engaging Rabbi Herman Grossman to assume the religious and educational duties for the community.

At a regular meeting of the Hebrew School Board of Directors held in November 1948, a vote of thanks was given to Mr. Dorfman "for his services as President during the past ten years . . . and the hope that it will be for fifty years more." Mr. Dorfman's reply in full was as follows: "It has been a real pleasure to continue to do my little part in connection with the school. Often I was tempted, because of the pressure of many other things, to give up this work. Then I would attend a Bar- 

Mitzvah or some other occasion, and I was so impressed with the work we were doing that I would become inspired and say it's the least I can do. I feel that all of us here are doing something of real value for the future boys who will take over the work of the Synagogue. I sat in the back recently, during the High 

Holidays, and had occasion to view our young boys that were recently Bar-Mitzvah. I noticed that, despite the lack of interest on the part of the parents, these boys would come to observe and follow the services and adhere to them, and that gave me a great deal of pleasure. I thank you for the rising vote of thanks.” 

The death of Mr. George Dorfman on March 2, 1952, came as a shock to our entire community. The loss of such a leader was bound to affect all of us. But Mr. Dorfman's personality, interest and religious standards were bound to leave their mark on those associated with him. The community was fortunate in having Mr. Bob Ekstein to assume his vacant chair and continue the concepts established by Mr. Dorfman. 

Mr. Ekstein is still serving as President and the school has progressed under his competent and dedicated leadership.

Rabbi/teacher Samuel Schaffler came to Gloversville in 1952. A young man with great talent, he left us in 1955 to work with the Educational Department of the United Synagogue of America. Milton Berger, Leon Harris and Leon Siegel were appointed to the Board about this time. 

Hoping to avoid "short-term teachers," the Board decided to hire a man just for the Hebrew School. 

Dr. Louis Greenberg, quiet, unassuming and very dedicated, came to our community and continued the high standards left to him by his predecessors. Dr. Greenberg's sudden death in February 1961 created a void, and once again the Synagogue and Hebrew School collaborated, and Rabbi Greenstein, assisted by his wife, took over the duties of teaching. The long established tradition of "learning" was carried on. 

Shortly after, the two Boards obtained the services of Cantor/teacher Leopold Kagan. This association lasted for one year.

Following this experience, the two Boards met again to evaluate the requirements of our Jewish community, and once more set into motion a committee to obtain the services of a cantor/teacher. After interviewing several applicants, we were fortunate in obtaining the services of Zachary Kuperstein. 

Things are running smoothly now. Graduates from our school, with a minimum of four years, conduct the Friday Night Services, and the entire Musaf Service following the reading of the Haftorah on their Bar- Mitzvah. As long as there are children to be taught, there will be a Hebrew School in Gloversville. The problems are many. . . but the results speak for themselves. 

Gimel... Jewish Community Center

From 1906 through 1917, the Synagogue vestry was the meeting place for practically every organization. 

Cantors gave concerts, Rabbis and travellers gave talks, parties followed almost every holiday service. 

Jewish families for the most part lived in the Judson Street-Steele Avenue section. The kosher butcher and 

Alderman's Grocery were on Market Street; Goldin's Grocery and Frisch's Delicatessen were on Washington Street; and Mrs. Berliner and her daughters ran a wonderful kosher restaurant on South Main 

Street. Elias Robb, a Mr. Kaufman, and Mr. and Mrs. Salm were successors in the delicatessen ventures; the Synagogue was the hub of Jewish life. 

The old slowly gave way to a group of young men and women who were looking for something other 35

than the Synagogue for their Jewish identification. These young men and women, sensing a need for changes, after several starts formed a new Jewish organization in 1912. . . the YM and YWHA, which lasted only two years. Reactivated in 1915, this movement was led by Joseph Moses, Joseph Bachner, and 

Ralph Moses. This new concept of Jewish identification took its form in dances, basketball and baseball teams, intercity affairs, debating teams, Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts, young men's clubs, sororities, and later, a very talented minstrel group that performed in the local halls and adjoining cities. 

That same year, Rabbi Joseph Jasin was engaged by this group as the spiritual leader for the entire community with the Synagogue as his pulpit. 

Within a few years, actually on November 2, 1919, a conference of the major Jewish organizations was held in the Arietta hall on Bleecker Square to establish a Jewish Community Center. Lucius Littauer addressed the gathering and offered to contribute twenty-five percent of the money required to maintain the new J.C.C. Joseph Bachner, Wolfe Horwitz, Dr. Morris Kennedy, Jacob Lazarus, and Nathan Sanger were elected temporary officers to promote this new concept. Before the end of the year, the second and third floors were rented over the Abdella Ice Cream Co. on Elm Street, and Rabbi Joseph Jasin was engaged as the first executive director.

A Council of Jewish Women, the Sunday School, and several junior clubs were immediately organized. 

The Mefizai Daath, a cultural study club organized as a High School group in 1912, transferred its meetings to the new J.C.C. rooms. Prof. Ernest Meritt, principal of the Gloversville High School, helped organize this group and acted as its advisor through 1918.

The formal opening of the new room on Elm Street took place on Thursday, April 20, 1920. This new venture in Jewish life for Gloversville was immediately accepted by all those present. 

The second president of the J.C.C. movement was Ralph Moses. He was followed by Nathan Sanger and then Wolfe Horwitz. Rabbi Jasin resigned as director in 1920, and on October 17 of that year Rabbi Maxwell Dubin was engaged as director. It was under his administration that the first Reformed service was held in the J.C.C. for the High Holidays. Rabbi Dubin stayed with our community for only one year, and Sol Sackheim was appointed as temporary director, serving well in this capacity for several months. Later that year, a Rabbi 

Goeffrey was engaged as director, but stayed only a few months.

In 1922 the fifth floor of the Knox Building was rented. These new quarters gave the community a lounge, library, gymnasium, executive offices and assorted meeting rooms. 

On November 1 Abram S. Magidah, a trained social worker who studied courses offered by the Jewish 

Welfare Board, was engaged as director. It was during his administration that the J.C.C. introduced the psychology of group work and management of a social center. Albert Glusker, David Dressler and Isadore 

Solomon followed Mr. Magidah as directors in the Knox Building.

In 1927, during the first administration of Albert Rosenthal, the movement for a new J.C.C. building went beyond the stage of talk, and into the area of action. At this time, Lucius Littauer made a contribution of $100,00 toward a goal of $200,000, for a new Jewish Center. 

The passing of Wolfe Horwitz on November 17, 1927, was a great loss to this community, as his close 36

association with the Synagogue and Jewish communal affairs was an inspiration for all the men around him. 

It was with great sadness that the Board of Directors entered into their minutes: "a great and good man has passed and we mourn for him immeasurably . . we are to have the building that he hoped for and worked for."

The spring of 1938 saw a new activity overshadowing everything else in the Jewish community. Meetings and plans for a building fund campaign got underway, and committees were chosen and charged with the responsibility of raising $75,000. The directors attending that meeting immediately pledged $27,000 and the campaign soon returned a figure of $80,000 in pledges. 

It was during this period that the Women's Auxiliary and Camp Littauer on West Stoner Lake were formed. Prior to Camp Littauer, the J.C.C. had a camp at Hope Falls. 

On April 1929, negotiations with Dr. Edwards' Estates developed and land was bought adjoining the 

Hippodrome Theater on East Fulton Street. Victor Rigaumont was engaged as architect to draw the plans for the new Center. Morrell Vrooman Inc., a Gloversville contractor, began construction in September 1929. 

The building was completed in April 1930 and dedicated on May 18 of the same year.

This building is a monument to the Jewish people of the area, and a definite contribution to the civic community. Every conceivable thought was incorporated, from a fireplace in the main lobby to a swimming pool twenty feet wide and sixty feet long. Kitchens, classrooms, gymnasium, social and meeting rooms, locker rooms, library and executive offices. . . nothing was overlooked.

Joseph Ruttenberg was Hebrew School Teacher and Mrs. Joseph Gerb was principal of the J.C.C. Sunday School.

The dedication lasted all week, with civic and dramatic nights, Junior Night, and athletic and dance nights following in succession. There were exhibits in boxing, swimming, billiards, and basketball. The 

Junior Dramatic Club, a talented group of Jewish young men and women from Amsterdam, presented a 37

three act play called "A Bachelor's Honeymoon." A Community Center Orchestra was organized and led by 

Mrs. J. Myer Schine; and children of the Sunday School, the Hebrew School and the Boy Scouts all had a part in the Dedication Services.

The Jewish Reformed movement held its services in the auditorium of the new Center, and from time to time executive directors or Rabbis held the administrative post. Service to the Jews and the communityat-large increased, with the auditorium being used for communal lectures and inter-faith programs, and the swimming pool serving the Red Cross and Y.W.H.A. programs. Cultural programs were introduced and the needs of the Jewish community were evaluated and served. 

From 1941 to 1960, the Center prospered in membership as well as service. There was rarely a time that some activity was not in full swing, with emphasis placed on youth programs. 

In 1955, David Van Santen donated land on Sacandaga Reservoir to establish a children's day camp. 

The most modern concept of rustic buildings and waterfront area were built to make our camp one of the finest in upper New York State.

The latest capital addition of service took the form of a modern Health Center of steam rooms, massage rooms, etc.

Through all of the Center's years, both husband and wife worked on Center projects and activities. This concept of dedication has been passed on to the present generation, and we can proudly say that our present officers and members of the Board still give unstintingly of their time, their talent and their material aid. 

The Center, like the Synagogue and Hebrew School, played a very important part in our Jewish history. 

Those young people who rejected the Synagogue and its "old fashioned" ways were not lost to Judaism. 

The association and direction of Center policy was "Jewish" without the religious connotations. The Center started and maintained a Sunday School, teaching Jewish history, holidays, and culture. . . thereby filling a void that existed in both the Synagogue and Hebrew School at various times. 

The meeting rooms were available to any Jewish organization holidays were observed, inter-city athletics and social youth programs arranged with other Jewish Centers and Y.M.H.A.'s. The director or 

Rabbi acted as "Jewish spokesman" for our entire community. The results of the combined Jewish and religious programming during the past fifty years speak for themselves. The number of "delinquent juveniles" during that period was so small that it was considered non-existent. On the other hand, our contribution to Jewish and civic life in Fulton County is one that every Jew can be proud of.

It is our hope that our Jewish life in Fulton County will continue to grow and prosper.

In Memoriam 

DR. HAROLD BAUER "EVERY VALLEY SHALL BE LIFTED UP, AND EVERY MOUNTAIN AND HILL SHALL BE MADE LOW, AND THE RUGGED SHALL BE MADE LEVEL, AND THE ROUGH PLACES A PLAIN, AND THE GLORY OF THE LORD SHALL BE REVEALED." 

(ISAIAH 40, 4)

These words from the Book of the Prophet Isaiah characterize our beloved friend and leader whose untimely death occurred on Saturday May 11, 1963 (Iyar 17, 5723). As an orthodontist, he served the children of our community with dedication. Through his search for perfection he brought comfort to those who needed it and better health to those who sought his service. 

Through his sincerity and devotion, "he revealed the glory of God." His profound love of Judaism reached to the innermost recesses of his soul and he was able to transmit to all who knew him and followed him a sense of the Divine. His plaintive melodies chanted in the Synagogue touched all the worshippers and filled them with religious fervor and spiritual warmth. 

His consecrated effort as a member of the Building Committee was in fulfillment of the words of the prophet, "Every valley shall be lifted up." The old Synagogue must give way to the new. Working closely with the other members of the Building Committee, there was created a Synagogue of which all of us are so proud. It is indeed a memorial to him who served so selflessly. 

His help to those in need, without asking for praise or thanks and without revealing his identity to the receiver, was in keeping with the highest ideals of Judaism. He was humble. He was gracious in his manner. He was a friend to all. He was gentle; he was courteous; he was sympathetic. His total commitment to all worthwhile endeavors will indeed assure him a place in heaven with the righteous of all ages and all generations.

May his memory be for blessing and may our Heavenly Father care for him who will be so greatly missed in our community. 40
