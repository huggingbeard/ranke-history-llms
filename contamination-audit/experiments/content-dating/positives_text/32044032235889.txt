Figs, Dried 

(August 1955)

Report by the United States Tariff Commission, Under 

Paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401, of Developments with Regard to Dried Figs Since Modification of the Trade-Agreement Concession on August 30, 1952 

This is a report made pursuant to paragraph 1 of Executive Order 

10401 of October 14, 1952 (17 F. R. 9125), 1/ of developments with regard to dried figs since the tariff concession granted thereon in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was modified and the import duty increased from 2 cents per pound to 4 cents per pound, effective August 30, 1952 (Proc. No. 2986; 17 F.R. 7567). This is the third report with respect to this matter under Executive Order 10401. The first report was made pursuant to paragraph 2 of that order and was transmitted to the President on June 3, 1953; and the second report, pursuant to paragraph 1, was transmitted to the President on August 24, 1954.

The 1953 report contained the results of the Commission's formal investigation instituted, pursuant to the President's request dated March 5, 1953, for the purpose of determining whether, and, if so, to what extent, the modification of the tariff concession on dried figs remained necessary in order to prevent or remedy serious injury or the threat thereof to the domestic industry concerned. As a result of that 

1 Prescribing procedures for periodic review of "escape clause" modifications of trade-agreement concessions. (TC28338) 836553

investigation, the Commission found that the modification, by Presidential proclamation dated August 16, 1952, of the concession granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, as supplemented by the Torquay Protocol to such agreement, remained necessary in order to prevent serious injury to the domestic industry concerned.

The 1954 report under Executive Order 10401 was concerned with developments in the trade in dried figs during the marketing year 1953/54. 1/ The 

Commission informed the President that, in its opinion, the conditions of competition with respect to the trade in imported and domestically produced dried figs had not so changed during the marketing year 1953 as to warrant the institution of a formal investigation under the provisions of paragraph 

2 of the above order.

The Commission's report on dried figs, dated July 24, 1952, based on its investigation under section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 

1951, contains information concerning the structure and condition of the dried-fig industry up to July 1952; the 1953 and 1954 reports contain information with regard to developments up to July 1953 and July 1954, respectively. As indicated in the earlier reports, commercial drying of figs is confined to California where 85 to 90 percent of the fig crop is marketed as dried figs with the remainder marketed as fresh or canned figs. Until 

March 1955 the marketing of California dried figs was controlled by a State marketing program, which prescribed regulations for the handling of dried figs by growers and packers. In March 1955 the United States Secretary of 

Agriculture, in response to a request by the California dried-fig industry, 1/ Dried figs begin to be harvested in California about July 1. In this report the marketing year for the crop covers the period from July 1 of each year to June 30 of the following year.

established a Federal marketing agreement and order for California dried figs pursuant to the provisions of the Federal Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act of 1937, as amended. 1/ The Federal marketing program is to be operated in conjunction with the California State program. 

The fig-bearing acreage in California has declined almost steadily since 1930 (table 4). Among the factors contributing to the decline have been more profitable returns from alternate crops, increasing costs of production for dried figs relative to average prices received, and urbanization. Notwithstanding the decline in acreage devoted to fig trees, the trend in output of figs was almost consistently upward until 1948 because of the increase in average yield per acre which resulted primarily from the removal of the least productive acreage and improved control of pests and diseases. In each year from 1948 through 1954 the domestic output of dried figs was less than the average annual output of the preceding 12 years. Production of merchantable dried figs in 1953 amounted to about 37 million pounds, the smallest crop since 1936 (table 3); merchantable output in 1954 was about 42 million pounds. 

According to the trade, the 1955 merchantable output may also be about 42 million pounds.

In the United States about two-thirds of the total consumption of dried figs is by biscuit manufacturers for the production of fig bars. Reports to the Commission from biscuit manufacturers who produce 70 to 80 percent of the total United States output of fig bars indicate that the consumption of this important fig product has increased in recent years.

Before the middle 1920's imports supplied the major portion of total consumption of dried figs in the United States; imports declined sharply thereafter, but they accounted for about 10 to 20 percent of this country's consumption of dried figs in the second half of the 1930's. Imports, which come mainly from Greece, Turkey, Italy, and 

Portugal, virtually ceased during World War II. In the 3 crop years 

1946-48 imports supplied about 6 percent of domestic consumption in each year; in the years 1949-54 they again accounted for about 10 to 

20 percent of annual domestic consumption, as they had in the late 1930's.

In many years before 1952 about two-thirds of the total United States imports of dried figs went to fig-bar manufacturers. During the marketing years 1952-54, however, less than one-fourth of the total quantity of dried figs imported into the United States was consumed in fig bars; the rest was consumed directly as dried figs. In the 1954 marketing year the quantity of dried figs imported for consumption in fig bars consisted of virtually all the 1.6 million pounds from Portugal, which accounted for nearly 25 percent of total imports of dried figs. 1/ 

1/ It may be noted that during the 1954 marketing year imports for consumption of fig paste for use in the manufacture of fig bars amounted to 6.2 million pounds. Moreover, on June 30, 1955, there were about 3 million pounds of foreign fig paste in bonded warehouses. 

Imports for consumption of fig paste amounted to 5.0 million pounds in 

1953, 1.1 million pounds in 1952, 2.1 million pounds in 1951, and insignificant amounts in previous years (table 12). Fig paste, which is not the subject of a trade-agreement tariff concession, was not covered by the escape-clause investigation with respect to dried figs. 1

Average annual imports of dried figs during the marketing years 1950-54 were at a higher level than imports in any preceding year since 1931, and in relation to domestic production were larger than in any preceding year since 1936. During the 1954 crop year United States imports for consumption of dried figs amounted to 6.5 million pounds. 

The 1954 imports were nearly 20 percent smaller than imports during 1953, 50 percent larger than those in 1952, and 20 percent larger than average imports in the years 1935-39. 

The high level of imports during the marketing years 1953 and 1954 resulted primarily from the large volume of Greek string figs which were entered for consumption--5.7 and 4.1 million pounds respectively as compared with only 3.1 million pounds in 1952 and 2.6 million pounds in 1951 (table 7). 1/ Before 1953 a large share of the annual shipments of Greek figs to this country was generally refused entry by the United States Food and Drug Administration; in 1951 and 1952 about 

50 percent of the shipments were so rejected. Because of the small volume of rejections during 1953 (less than 4 percent), importers received a somewhat larger quantity of string figs than was required to meet the demands of the limited number of consumers, who, for reasons other than price, prefer such figs to all other types of figs. 

This excessive supply of Greek string figs proved unprofitable for the importers. Because of their experience with the 1953 crop, many importers of Greek figs, anticipating a small volume of rejections 1/ In recent years, virtually all dried figs imported from Greece have consisted of string figs.

Reports to the Commission from biscuit manufacturers indicate that Portuguese dried figs, the only imported dried figs used by them during the 1954 marketing year, were purchased at prices averaging 14-15 cents per pound delivered to the plant; this price was somewhat less than the average delivered price of domestic Adriatics and Kadotas. The small spread between the prices of Calimyrnas and Adriatics this past season induced some biscuit manufacturers to buy Calimyrnas in place of Adriatics.

During the 1954 marketing year, as during the preceding year, there were some signs of improvement in the situation of the domestic dried-fig industry. The average farm price received in 1954 for the total output (including nommerchantable figs) was about 6 percent higher than that received in 1953 and about 16 percent higher than 1 The price of fig paste imported from Turkey for biscuit manufacturers averaged about 16 cents per pound, and the price of fig paste imported from Portugal averaged about 13 cents per pound.

1954 crop was 75 percent of the parity price for 1954 (as calculated by the United States Department of Agriculture); the growers' average price for the 1953 crop was 65 percent 1 of the parity price; and that for the 1952 crop was 58 percent (table 10). The rise in the average farm price from 7.8 cents per pound for the 1953 crop of dried figs to 8.3 cents for the 1954 crop, accompanied by a proportional increase in total output, increased total returns to fig growers by 12 percent. Returns for the 1953 crop had been smaller than the returns for any crop since that of 1941. A change in the minimum standards for growers' deliveries to packers, effective beginning with the 1954 marketing year, was partly responsible for the improvement in the returns to growers. Changing the tolerance allowance for total defects to 33 percent (from 25 percent) increased the proportion of merchantable figs in the total output. 

For all varieties except Black Mission, the estimated average prices received by growers for the 1954 crop of merchantable figs were lower than those for the 1953 crop (table 11). One factor affecting the prices of the merchantable output for 1954 was the lowering of minimum standards for growers' deliveries without comparable change in the minimun standards for packers' shipments. For Adriatics and Kadotas, the average prices of merchantable figs were lower in 1954 than in each of the years 1950, 1951, and 1953; the average prices for these two varieties were, however, nearly 30 percent higher in 1954 than in 1952. The average price received for Black 

Missions was higher in 1954 than in each of the 2 preceding years. improvement in price probably resulted, in large measure, from the domestic industry's program to curtail the merchantable output of Black Missions which for many years were in excess supply. On the basis of quantity and share of merchantable output of all varieties, the merchantable output of Black Missions was smaller in 1954 than in any recent year (table 6). Another factor contributing to the increase in the price of Black Missions is the development of a market for 

Black Mission fig juice. 

The movement of the 1954 crop, like that of the 1953 crop, was concluded rapidly; by October 15, 1954, about 95 percent of the 1954 crop had been purchased by the processors. The dried figs remaining in the hands of growers on that date consisted primarily of Kadotas that were originally intended for canning. When the Armed Forces curtailed their requirements for canned figs, growers had to traydry a larger share of their Kadota output for the dried-fig market than in recent years.

There are some indications that the packers' market for dried figs was less favorable during the 1954 marketing year than during the preceding year. The inventory of dried figs in the hands of packers (including amounts under contract and held for future delivery) was somewhat larger on July 1, 1955, than on the corresponding date of the preceding year. Packers' physical inventory on July 1, 1955, was somewhat smaller, however, than that on the corresponding dates of 1951 and 1952. On July 1 of every year since 1949, packers' physical inventory has been larger than the amount usually considered sufficient to supply the market for dried figs until September, when the dried figs from the new crop become available in large quantity. 

On the basis of information received from packers who handled nearly 90 percent of the domestic output of merchantable dried figs in both 1953 and 1954, it appears that the small decline in average prices paid to growers for Calimyrnas and Adriatics was accompanied by a slightly larger decline in average prices received; for Black 

Missions, the increase of about one-half cent per pound in the average price paid to growers was accompanied by an increase of only one-fourth cent per pound in the average price received. Average packers' prices of all varieties of domestic dried figs except Black Missions were slightly lower in 1954 than in the preceding year. Average prices received for Adriatics and kadotas packed for use in fig bars were about 2 percent less in 1954 than in the preceding year; the decline in average price for Calimyrnas of similar pack was 6 percent.

It is not possible at this date to estimate precisely the volume of imports of dried figs that may enter the United States during the oncoming marketing season (1955). According to preliminary information received from the principal exporting countries it appears that there will be available for export to the United States a quantity of drie figs at least equal to that shipped during the 1954 marketing year. 

In some respects the situation of the domestic dried-fig industry at the close of the 1954 marketing year resembles that at the close of the 4 preceding years: (1) Abnormally large carryover in the hands of packers at the start of the new crop year, and (2) prospects of a supply of new-crop figs available from domestic production and imports equal at least to the supply of the year just ended. These two facts tend to offset the signs of improvement noted above for 1954. 

On the basis of the facts set forth above, the Commission finds that the conditions of competition with respect to the trade in imported and domestically produced dried figs have not so changed during the 1954 marketing year as to warrant the institution of a formal investigation under the provisions of paragraph 2 of Executive Order 10401.
