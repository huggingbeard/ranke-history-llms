JOB CONTENT AND WORKING CONDITIONS OF ENGINEERS Introduction My name is Eric B. Herdman. I am Director of Personnel for the Denver & Rio Grande Western Railroad Company.. 

I began my railroad career in 1916 as a brakeman on the "Big Four" Railroad. I worked in the same capacity on the 

New York Central, Pennsylvania, Rock Island and Santa Fe

Railroads and was promoted to a conductor on the Santa Fe in 1919. From 1920 to 1923 I worked as a switchman, yardmaster, and assistant trainmaster with the Southern Pacific Company. 

From 1923 to 1937 I was with the Union Pacific Railroad as a switchman, assistant yardmaster, assistant general yardmaster, general yardmaster, traveling conductor, and trainmaster.

Between 1937 and 1943 I was with the Rock Island Railroad as a trainmaster, assistant superintendent, and division superintendent. In 1943 I went to the Denver & Rio Grande Western 

Railroad as an assistant trainmaster. After working with this railroad as an assistant trainmaster, trainmaster, and superintendent, I was promoted to my present position in 1949.

- 2 - I am a member of the Carriers' Conference Committee representing the western railroads in these proceedings. I have been a member of all Western Carriers' Conference Committees handling concerted national wage and rules movements since 1949, with the exception of the 1954 movement. 

The frontispiece in my exhibit is a map of the Denver & 

Rio Grande Western Railroad. We operate 2,130 miles of road, in the states of Colorado, Utah, and New Mexico and this represents about 1% of all Class I railroad mileage. Our principal lines run west from Denver and Pueblo, Colorado, to Salt Lake City and Ogden, Utah. A portion of one of our divisions comprises about 300 miles of narrow gauge track; some of this is laid with a third track so that it can be used by standard gauge trains. Most of our operations are in mountainous or desert terrain. We have 640 enginemen according to the I. C. C. midmonth count for 1959; 263 of these were classed as engineers, and represented 0.7% of the engineers in the industry.

My testimony considers those three of the seven Railroad Labor Board criteria for passing on wage disputes which have to do with the training, skill, responsibility, and character

I shall show that the training for promotion to engineer, which is done under generous pay as a fireman, has become far easier than in the past, that it does not require the skill it formerly did, that the responsibility of the engineer has decreased; and that in character, the employment is much less difficult and more comfortable. 

Major Classes of Service 

My first subject will be a brief description of the three main classes of service, passenger, freight, and yard. 

Yard work

About 41.5 per cent of the service hours of the Engineers before this Board represent work in yard service. generally is confined to yard or switching limits. 

The men work only in a relatively small area as compared to road engineers and do not generally operate over the road. 

They are engaged in handling the cars brought in by inbound trains or destined to leave on outbound trains. There are three principal types of yard work. The most common is the breaking up and making up of trains, usually referred to as classification switching. The personnel in this work are engaged in shifting cuts of cars from one track to another in the process of assembling trains for road crews to take out or assembling cuts to be 969087

handled to industrial areas or other yards. The second type is transfer and interchange. The employees in this work move cuts of cars from one yard to another on the same railroad or from the yard of one railroad to the yard of another railroad. The third type of yard work is industrial switching, which consists of placing cars to and removing them from industrial locations.

A survey of 106 yard tours of duty in October and November of last year showed that the average crew handled about 192 cars per day. This survey included transfer and humping crews, where the total number of cars handled per crew may be much higher, as well as some industrial and flat classification crews, which normally handled a lesser volume of cars. 

Local and through freight service, along with passenger service, differ fundamentally from yard service, in that freight and passenger trains move over the road. Approximately 43.4 per cent of all engineers' service hours were in freight service in the first half of 1959. Freight service, therefore, as a whole is the most important class from the point of view of time spent by engineers on duty. Freight service is divided into two main groups, local freight and through freight. About 

19.7 per cent of all engineer service hours are in local freight

5 - service and 23.7 per cent are in through freight service. 

Through freight service, therefore, consumes more engineer service hours on the whole than local freight service, even though the average time on duty per trip of local freight runs exceeds the average for through freights. There are, obviously, more through freight runs than local freight runs. 

Through freight trains have few or no intermediate stops and travel at faster speeds than local freights. Through freight service, of course, has a lower basic rate of pay compared to local freight because the employees generally are able to take substantial advantage of the greater train speeds under the dual basis of pay rules. 

In local freight, the runs usually are shorter than in through freight or passenger service. The work consists of setting out and picking i up cars at stations and industrial sidings along the way and doing such switching as is necessary at points too small to require a regular switching crew. 

There are some other types of freight service, such as mine run, work train, and wreck train service, which are sometimes considered to be separate types of freight service for one purpose or another. 

Mine run service consists of placing empty

- 6cars for loading and pulling loaded cars out of the mine yard, together with any necessary switching. Work train, wreck train, and miscellaneous service is small in volume; and the nature of the work varies with the type of project involved. 

Passenger service, of course, is basically just what its name implies, that is, the carrying of people from one passenger station to another. There are three basic types of passenger service, main line through service, local service, and commuter service. All three types of service are probably thoroughly familiar to everyone. The main line through service operates with faster trains and fewer stops over much longer distances, some of these trains operating over 2,000 miles from Chicago to the Pacific Coast with no change in equipment, although the crews change rather frequently on such a run. Local service operates over much shorter distance at slower speeds and usually makes stops at each station. Commuter service is usually operated over a shorter distance with frequent stops, but usually on faster schedules than local trains operating over a longer mileage. Approximately 15.1 per cent of all engineer service hours were in passenger service during the first half of 1959. 

On the Denver & Rio Grande Western we have no commuter trains and only five passenger trains each way daily.

The Engineer Operates the Locomotive 

Today, the vast majority of locomotives are diesel. There are electric locomotives in the New York area, New York-Washington territory and on at least one Chicago commuter service. 

Steam engines are being replaced more and more each year and the following description covers diesel locomotives. 

In all classes of service the engineer has the same fundamental job; he is in essence the operator of the locomotive which pulls or pushes the cars of the train. In performing this duty, he sits throughout his trip or tour of duty while the locomotive is moving in an adjustable cushioned or upholstered chair in the cab of the locomotive, He has within easy reach the few levers which he moves to control the speed and direction of his movement and the application of his brakes. These levers are: (1) the reverser, when pushed forward, it places the locomotive in position to go forward and when pulled back places the locomotive in position to back up. (2) The throttle which governs the speed of the locomotive. (3) The transition lever which is similar in principle to the gear shift of an automobile. 

About half of the road engines in use today have automatic transition, which is similar, in so far as the skill or effort

- 8 of the engineer is concerned, to the automatic gear shift on a car. (4) The independent air brake lever, which controls the brakes on the locomotive; and (5) the automatic brake lever, which controls the brakes on both the locomotive and the cars coupled to the locomotive. These levers are normally moved one at a time and can very easily be moved by one hand with very little effort. Between stops, the engineer will not move the reversing lever. If normal speed is maintained, it will usually not be necessary to change the transition lever, even when it is not automatic. Where it is necessary to reduce speed, this will be accomplished by either the use of the throttle or by means of the air brake system. Other than this, the engineer together with other members of the crew on the head end will keep a look out for signal indications and highway crossings, sounding the necessary whistle signals when required. When it becomes necessary to stop the train; this is accomplished by placing one of the brake levers in position which automatically controls the braking system on the train. Regardless of the length of the train or the number of cars, the physical effort required of the engineer is the same.

When the locomotive is being used for switching operations, the engineer will have to open and close the throttle more frequently; and he will operate the locomotive back and forth over relatively short distances. He will have no selection, normally, as to the braking system which he will operate; but he will simply move the one lever which applies the independent brakes on the locomotive itself. In general during the switching operations, the engineer will move the locomotive forward a short distance, stop, pause a moment or so, and then reverse it for a short way. Yard operations normally are performed at slow speeds, and the frequent stops of the locomotive will give the engineer numerous short rest periods, while he waits for cars to be coupled or uncoupled, switches to be thrown, or hand brakes to be applied by the ground employees. The distances between stops may be only a few yards, especially in classification switching. He will watch for hand or lantern signals from the ground crew to signal him when to move, what direction to move, how fast to move, and when to stop; it is his job to observe the hand signals and move the locomotive controls in response thereto.

In any class of service, in order to move or stop the locomotive, all the engineer needs to do is to move the controls. Automatic transition, automatic braking systems, safety controls and alarm systems on the engine all simplify the work of the engineer. In the handling of a train, regardless of which class of service he is in, the engineer can do no more than turn the throttle up or down one notch at a time or adjust a brake system, depending upon whether he wants to increase or decrease his speed. Unless mishandled, the automatic transition systems and automatic braking systems of the locomotive. will perform their functions after the levers have been moved by the engineer. Such minor adjustments as may be necessary for the proper handling of the train over the road can normally be made without hurry and with the slightest of physical effort. Locomotive engineers, whether in road or yard service, generally operate over the same territory for many years, so that these territories become extremely familiar to them. The occasionally necessary minor adjustments of the throttle or of a braking lever will normally have been done by each engineer many times before. 

The men get to know exactly what adjustment will be needed

for what type of train at each location on their district or division.

They know the location of fixed signals, grade crossings, sidings, switches, and stations along their district. 

In yard service, the men have no away-from-home-terminal time, and the day is a regular 8-your day, with regular times on and off duty. In local freight service too, many crews return to their home terminal each trip and the crew will usually start at the same time and place every day, and go off duty at the same place, and often at approximately the same time. In through freight and passenger service, the run generally consumes a very short period, so that the working day is short. In yard service, there are almost no schedules to be observed and local freights rarely operate on a timetable schedule. But on the other hand, through freight trains and passenger trains will generally have the rightof-way over the road, as compared with local freights. yard service the engineer and his crew are under the constant supervision of the yardmaster, to whom they can look for the solution of any minor problem which may present itself. 

Engineers on through freight and passenger trains, in addition to being able to complete their working day in a Ji In

.: few hours, generally use heavier locomotives, which give the engineer a higher basic rate of pay without any additional work at all; it takes exactly the same movements to operate four or more units coupled together as it does to operate a single unit. In all classes of service, no more is required of the engineer than the reliable movement of his few levers in response to the signals displayed, whether hand signals are passed by the road or yard crew or fixed signals lighted by a dispatcher.

In yard work, the responsibility of seeing that the cars are properly switched from one track to another rests upon the yardmaster and ground crew. The engineer only operates the locomotive in response to hand signals or other instructions from the ground crew. In road operations, the responsibility for moving trains between terminals rests upon the train dispatcher; it is up to the dispatcher to see that the proper signal aspects are displayed and to see that the proper train orders, if any, are conveyed to the train crews on the road. 

The engineer again need only operate his locomotive in the proper manner in accordance with the train orders and signal aspects which he receives. It is more appropriate to say that

..."reliability" is what is required of an engineer, rather than "responsibility. It cannot be called a "responsibility" to move forward when someone signals to move forward, as in yard or other switching operations; nor can it be called a responsibility to proceed when a fixed signal says to go ahead. 

Basically, the ultimate that is required of the locomotive engineer is that he reliably move the control levers in order to move the locomotive in the proper manner in response, tor the instructions which have been given him. When the locomotive originates at the initial terminal of the engineer's run, he may also operate it from the roundhouse down to the train; this is a light movement without any cars and movement is at slow speed through the yard tracks. In many yards these preliminary movements are made by a hostler; and the engineer simply waits in the office until the train is made up and engine placed on the train. 

In any event, the great bulk of initial terminal time is likely to be idle time and requires little if any effort at all. The figures on line 3 also include time spent at the final terminal. At the final terminal the reverse of the actions taken at the initial terminal are usually carried out. 

If the locomotive is going on through, the engineer usually does nothing but get off the engine. In passenger service

-and frequently in through freight service, the locomotive is apt to go on through in this manner to distant terminals. 

If the final terminal for the engineer's trip is also the final terminal for the locomotive, the engineer will wait for his locomotive to be uncoupled from the train and then operate it slowly through the yard to the roundhouse; or a hostler may take over as soon as he arrives at his final terminal and operate the engine to the roundhouse. At the roundhouse, he may simply turn it over to shop forces. Sometimes the engineer will operate the locomotive into the roundhouse himself and will shut off the motors, turn out the lights, close the cab windows, and do such other duties of that nature as may be required in order to leave the locomotive unattended. 

None of these duties actually require the employee to be qualified as an engineer, as hostlers are not promoted men. Much of the time at the initial terminal will be spent simply: sitting and waiting for other employees to get the train and engine ready, or at the passenger station waiting for the train to come in. He did not, however, mention that a skilled occupational prefix number either 4 or 5 is also used for many other occupations similar to positions for which men are hired by the railroad industry. A skilled occupation prefix number is a part of the code number of such jobs as the locomotive and car repairman, the carpenter, including maintenance carpenters, bridge carpenters, and locomotive carpenters, and the electrician and electrical repairman, including the track and switch electrical maintainer in the railroad transportation industry.

And the same skilled occupation number "5" is prefixed to the bus driver as to the engineer. The Federal Agency which published this volume headed it up with a "Special Notice" which warned, however, that, "It should be clearly understood, therefore, that because of their nature, job definitions published by the United States Employment Service cannot be considered standards for the settling of hours, wages, or jurisdictional matters.

By collective bargaining procedures, the United Steel Workers of America, C.I.O., and the United States Steel Corporation, worked out a system of job classifications whereby all jobs of hourly rated production, maintenance, and clerical employees were placed in a series of classifications, numbered from 1 to 32, inclusive. The classification system, the classifications themselves, and the position of each job in the classification ladder, were each agreed upon by joint study and collective bargaining agreement between the United Steel Workers and the United States Steel Corporation for each nonsupervisory job in the company. The result was a series of classifications covering over 4,000 jobs based on the principles enunciated in¨ a volume of approximately 3,000 pages published on January 1, 1953, jointly by the Steel Workers and the U. S. Steel Corporation and entitled "Job Description and Classification Manual for 

Hourly Rated Production, Maintenance, and Non-Confidential Cleri cal Jobs.

' In the course of the study, each particular job was

• E dissected, examined, and classified into one of the thirty-two job classifications. The purpose of the entire program of job classification was to ascertain the relative pay rates to be applied to each employee of United States Steel Corporation. 

Each job was rated as to its content, including pre-employment training, employment training and experience, mental skill, manual skill, responsibility for materials, responsibility for tools and equipment, responsibility for operations, responsibility for the safety of others, mental effort, physical effort, working conditions, and hazards. A numerical rating was applied to each of these considerations for each job; and the job classification into which the job fell was determined therefrom by mathematical means. These job classifications have been the standard used for the application of all pay rate increases for United States Steel employees ever since. used !

In yard service, as I have noted before, the engineer is directed and guided in his every movement by the conductor and brakemen. It is the conductor and brakemen who give him the hand signals which tell him which direction to move and when. 

It is the brakemen who 'line switches for the movement of his locomotive. It is the conductor or brakeman who couples his: locomotive to cars, and uncouples it. It is usually the conductor who obtains the instructions from the yardmaster. Training and Promotional Examination Requirements 

Most railroads under normal conditions will not hire a fireman unless he has completed a high school education and can read and write the English language and can pass a physical examination. These, on the other hand, are all the requirements that are necessary to enable any fireman of ordinary intelligence to pass the promotional examination in order to become an engineer. 

During shortages of labor, however, we have hired firemen with only a grade school education; these men too have passed the examinations. While it is certainly not necessary that a man must have been a fireman to become a fully competent and qualified engineer, the normal procedure is to recruit our engineers from the ranks of the firemen. The exact sequence of the promotional examination varies from railroad to railroad; but in general a fireman with about three years experience is allowed to take the final promotional examinations to become an engineer. 

On some railroads, the fireman will take his examination in three sections, the first after one year of experience, the second after two years of experience, and the third sometime after three years of experience.

On the Denver & Rio Grande Western Railroad, we follow the

latter procedure.

The entire examination system is carried out according to schedule agreement with the Firemen's organization on our property; the chief concern of the organization before this Board in the promotional procedures is to assure themselves that it is done in strict seniority sequence, and its schedule agreement, insofar as it discusses the promotional examination, is so oriented. According to our agreement with the BLF&E, we give the first year's promotional examination after one year's service, the second year examination after two years of service, and the final promotional exam after three years of service. 

Under this procedure for the first examination the fireman will have a full year of actual service, not including any furlough time, to learn the subjects upon which he will be examined by actually doing them. When a class is to be called up for examination, he will be notified thirty days in advance in order that he may prepare himself. The examination will be arranged so that it can be taken during a layover at the home terminal of the employee involved. The examination itself is written; but no particular grade is required in order to pass. If the fireman desires, he may have another fireman or engineer present at the examination; this, of course, would most likely be the local chairman of his organization.

After two complete years of service, the identical procedure is followed to give the fireman his second year examination. 

And after three complete years of service, he will again be notified 30 days in advance of the date set for his examination. Although during his first three years or more with the railroad, he will have had every conceivable opportunity to learn the subject matter both by daily practical experience and by reading the manuals furnished at company expense when he first goes on duty, the fireman coming up for his final promotional examination is entitled to be instructed one day in advance of the date of the examination by a representative of management. 

During this instruction management goes over with the fireman each of the questions in the examination, and gives and explains the proper answers to each, and clarifies anything the employee may wish to question him about or which may not be thoroughly understood by the employee. After this day of intense preparation, the employee begins his final promotional examination, which is in writing and on which, by agreement with the organization, a grade of 80% correct answers is sufficient for passing. Again the fireman may, if he desires, have another fireman or engineer present during the examination: During the day of instruction

1 and the day of the examination the fireman is compensated for this attendance by the payment of two days at pro rata at the rate applicable to the last service performed, In the event a fireman fails to complete his instruction and examination within two days, he may complete the examination on his layover time; but by the agreement he is in no event allowed longer than 30 days from the date of his instruction.

It is apparent that any fireman of average intelligence should be able easily to pass these examinations. 

In case, however, a fireman fails to pass a promotional examination, he is returned as a fireman without prejudice. Anytime within six months he can apply for re-examination; and he will be given the examination again when the company is in need of engineers. If he fails his second chance, he is returned to the bottom of the seniority roster and is allowed to try again when he works up for promotion.

Since the inauguration of the three-year examination on our railroad a number of years ago, no fireman has refused to take the promotional examinations and none has failed them, except that we have had a few failures on our steam engine narrow gauge operations: The job of an engineer on a steam locomotive requires a degree of skill which is not necessary to a diesel

Railroad Operating Rules Are Basically Simple Common Sense Rules 

The organization has presented testimony to the effect that the operating rules of the railroads make the job of the engineer in some respects difficult. Any such implication is a gross exaggeration of the actual situation. The operating rules have one simple basic purpose, and that is to apply common, ordinary safety procedures to railroad operations.

They do not present any problem at all to the engineer. 

In the first place, the engineer is not alone in observation of operating rules, but, in nearly all cases, has the assistance of the conductor, one or more brakemen, and a fireman in.complying with operating rules. He is not required to memorize any huge mass of data, but merely to follow the rules pertinent to his particular operation with the assistance of the other members of the crew. In the second place, the engineer is thoroughly familiar with the operating rules because he has worked with them every day since he was first hired as a fireman. Thirdly, the operating rules themselves are simple and easily applied. In composing them every effort is expended to make them as clear and understandable as possible. And fourthly, the subject matter of the operating rules is not difficult. 

They are basically descriptions of the types of operating situations which may confront an engineer, and of what to do in the face of them a sort of handbook on operations. 

Looked at from another viewpoint, they are the safety rules required by courtesy and common sense in the running of the locomotive. For example, the very first rule in the Denver & Rio Grande Western's rule book is, "Safety is of the first importance in the discharge of duty."

The organization has shown the rules books from several different railroads but has not attempted to explain the meaning of any of them. This silence, in effect, may tend to exaggerate the complexity and prolixity of the operating rules. 

But you may recall that Mr. Hirst read a great many rules. These were all typical operating rules; and I am sure that the Board had no trouble understanding them.

No engineer must know everything in any one of these books, for they all contain rules applicable only to conductors, dispatchers, agents, trainmen, and classes of employees other than enginemen. There may be rules in a particular railroad's book that do not apply to every division or seniority. district, or that apply only to types of operation of no concern to engineers on certain districts. As a normal matter, an engineer will operate only over one division or district and he will study only the rules or special instructions applicable to that division or district and the operations thereon. 

Another unsaid inference of the organization's method of presentation is that these rules must be memorized. This simply is not so. The standard provision is that the "Employees must be conversant with and obey the rules..." And this provision

· itself further implies that assured knowledge of the rules is not required by the rule which states "If in doubt as to their meaning, they (the employees) must apply to proper authority for an explanation." Every operating employee has a copy of the rule book from the day he is first hired and is expected to carry it with him at all times and to refer to it whenever necessary. 

In considering the rule book generally, one factor must be borne in mind. Insofar as operating employees are concerned, it is addressed to railroad men who ride on trains daily and observe at first hand everything that they ever need to know from this book. We do not expect a fireman to know all of the material in it when he first goes on duty under pay. A newly hired fireman on the Denver & Rio Grande has no student trips to make, other than those hired for steam engines on the narrow gauge. 

He is instructed on the fundamental rules and the first trip he makes, he is under pay. A road foreman of engines may ride with him for a trip or two to instruct him generally in his duties, or he will learn from his fellow employees, by asking questions, by personal observation and experience, and from reading. 

The railroad operating rules are perhaps best explained in the familiar terms of the traffic laws with which every automobile driver is familiar. The two are very much the same, and

in Colorado, and many other states, a person is required to pass a written examination on traffic laws in order to get a driver's license. As an example, an auto driver may start out from any town or city and he knows by signs that the speed limit is 25 miles per hour. On the railroad the engineer finds his speed limit in the time-table, or sometimes by signs along the right of way.

62Recent Technological Changes Account for Much of the Ease of the Engineers' Job 

The Class I railroads have spent over 18 billions of dollars from 1935 through 1958 for additions and betterments to roadway and equipment; this has averaged over 1 billion dollars per year since World War II. All of these improvements have contributed to make the job of the engineer easier, safer, and more comfortable. one.

The most noteworthy improvement of all has been the dieselization of locomotives. This has transformed the job of engineer from a difficult and uncomfortable one to an easy and comfortable It has virtually eliminated the mechanical duties of the engineer; although the steam engineer had many parts to lubricate, maintain, and on occasion repair, the diesel engineer has little or few of these duties. There is almost no mechanical work which the engineer can do on the modern diesel. If the engine breaks down, he must look to the shop forces to repair it. In the operation of the engine, all difficult physical manipulation of heavy rods and throttles has been eliminated. On the diesel, there is no lever that cannot be operated by the least physical effort. The skill necessary to adjust the reverse lever and the throttle on the steam locomotive has been replaced on the diesel by a throttle which is simply moved in a regular pattern, one

· · 

notch at a time from idle to a notch which gives the train a proper speed, where it may remain until the train is to be stopped or its speed reduced. To start his locomotive the engineer need only set the throttle lever to notch #1, hold it there for a couple seconds, then set it over to notch #2, hold it there for a moment, and then move it to notch #3, and so on up to the 8th notch for the highest speeds. The old cab, with the fire box blazing hot in the summer, and open to blizzards in the winter, has been replaced by an enclosed cab with an electric heater for winter warmth. The old seat box has been replaced by an upholstered chair. The small window to the front of the steam locomotive, limiting the vision of the engineer, has been replaced by larger windows with better visibility. The steam and cinders and smoke which made steam locomotives uncomfortable and often obscured visilibity ahead are gone. The diesel locomotive rides more smoothly. The ever-present danger of bursting steam lines and of being thrown by a sudden lurch against the hot firebox. has been completely eliminated. The job of the modern locomotive engineer cannot really be compared with his job a few years aga; the two jobs must be contrasted with each other.

Other improvements have assisted the engineer just as much New air brake systems have given him a much as dieselization. easier control over his train and have eliminated from train handling much of the skill which was required with the older types of brakes. The new dynamic brake has added an altogether new dimension to accuracy and facility of train control on grades and when slowing down. Improved types of signal systems and additions of better quality signals have substantially reduced the discretion to be exercised by the engineer in operating his train. Whereas formerly engineers operating in unprotected territory had to rely on train orders or upon train schedules to tell them what tracks were available for their use, this is now on most main line's done by signal systems of one sort or another, so that the engineer can tell from a glance as he approaches a signal whether he should stop the train, or whether he can continue on.

In some areas where traffic is most dense, automatic train control has been installed, so as to stop locomotives automatically if they should pass certain signals without acknowledgment by the engineer. 

One other aspect of signals which has greatly improved the lot of engineers is the method used to convey to the engineer the signal message. In the old days of balls and chains or semaphore arms, it was often extremely difficult to see the signal, especially through clouds of steam and smoke or in bad weather. Today, the searchlight type of signal is observable from great distances and in the worst types of weather. It will be recalled that there were no semaphore signals remaining along Mr. Hirst's normal runs.

Overpasses and underpasses, partly or wholly paid for by the railroads, are constantly replacing the busier grade crossings. Automatic gates and flashing light signals are more dependable and easier for highway traffic to observe than the fast disappearing crossing watchman. It should be remembered that in most states the Public Utilities Commissions or municipalities, upon independent investigation, formally assert that the grade crossing protection systems installed at every public crossing are safe and appropriate under the circumstances present at each particular crossing. At private crossings the individual in whom the crossing easement rests is generally legally required to protect against trains. 

There have been other technological improvements, paid for by the railroads, which have improved the job of the engineers. 

One is the increasing use of radio. Walkie-talkie sets can be used between the conductor and brakemen, on the one hand, and the engineer on the other. Radio communications are common between the train itself and dispatchers or telegraph stations along the way. In switching operations this has allowed the conductor to talk to the engineer about the necessary movements, instead of relying on hand or lantern signals, which might have been confusing or difficult to see over a long distance or through bad weather.

The technological improvements which we have just mentioned have allowed engineers, because of their graduated rates of pay and because of the dual basis of pay under which they work, to earn more money for less work than ever before. The locomotives of today are heavier than the steam locomotives which were in use at the time the graduated rates of pay were introduced.

The technological improvements, particularly dieselization, improved signal systems, and radio communications have allowed trains to run from terminal to terminal faster than before, giving engineers and other operating employees the benefit of high speeds and shorter hours; and their earnings per hour worked are higher than they would otherwise be. In other words, the engineer of today can earn more money for less work, in less time, than ever before. And he has a comfortable job, works under pleasant conditions, and exercises little skill or physical effort, especially when compared with that required of the engineer on steam locomotives. 

In conclusion, I cannot emphasize too strongly that in the past decade, the railroads have spent billions of dollars to improve their properties and this has resulted, in, among other things, making the jobs of locomotive engineers more comfortable and much easier than previously and I do not know of any additional duties, skill or responsibility which has been placed upon them which would merit their requested increase in pay based on these reasons; but to the contrary, the simplifications in their jobs make the carriers' requested decreases entirely just and appropriate.
