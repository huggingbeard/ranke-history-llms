PREFACE 

WE MUST penetrate to the very foundation of our economic system. Skimming along on top can but result in chaos and destruction. 

The IDEA advocated herein goes to the very bottom of our economic structure and its operation will insure the most intense and healthful activity, and bring to our democracy the live red blood it requires to maintain and continue its existence. The long delay (1932-40) in bringing forth this message has been due to a sincere desire to see something equal to or better develop for the general good of our great democracy.

It is earnestly hoped that a careful perusal will be given to this representation by those who think and know as well as those in authority to propose and act.

The application of no abstruse formula is required to reach a clear understanding. 

Only plain "brief illustration" (see page 32) is necessary to bring to view what can result from the simple course pointed out. Upon examination it will be found to be American, needful, workable, desirable, dependable entirely within the reach of suitability.

"Progress is the Law of Life." 

-R. B. "The moving finger writes, And having writ, moves on." -E. F.

The Situation Viewed TARIFFS as a source of revenue, dependence upon the precious metals only as a foundation for a medium of exchange, inflation and deflation, contraction and expansion of the currency, supply and demand, income tax the doctrine of inter-dependence-all must go to the scrap heap with their contemporaries, the oxcart, the horse and carriage, the walking plow, the dinky steam locomotive, the wooden steam boat and other by-gones too numerous to mention. 

Let those who have tears shed them; for the American people are determined to become, in effect as well as in fact, free economically as well as politically, from their trans- 

Atlantic friends, foes and defaulters.

The constructive course dictates that one horizontal stroke should yield sufficient revenue to meet the requirements of the national budget, and liquidate all other major expenses; that an equilibrium should be estab-

lished between the actual distributable products and the money in actual circulation; that the difference between the value of active products and the amount of money necessary to move them should terminate; that communism, socialism, bolshevism, 'hogism' should find no place in the economic life of the American people. Of course, provisions for persons who are weak on management in their personal affairs who have made investments resulting in conditions hurtful to society, who are old, unfortunate or improvident-would be wise, generous and noble. But the great body of working, thinking, advancing American humanity acts on terms of individual effort and achievements, and will respond willingly only to means which determine such a course.

THE LOW-DOWN ON MONEY A SUPPOSED CASE 

Suppose several persons go to distant parts, taking with them sufficient supplies,

etc. to last them until they have planted and harvested, one of their number being a miner-a gold miner. If the miner succeeds in finding a given quantity of gold and takes it to the mint, he can get money (currency) for his gold, say $1,000. Now if the others of the company (say 20) should succeed to the amount of $1,000 each, then the entire company will have produced $21,000. But there are only $1,000 among them and they owe one thousand dollars FOR TAXES WHICH MUST BE PAID. It is clear that the man with the $1,000 can buy the entire $20,000 worth of produce and become economic master of the community.

THE ACTUAL CASE 

Over and above real estate and permanent fixture value the American people can produce yearly something like 84 billion dollars worth of wealth in the United States, but there are in circulation only about 7 billion dollars

of the entire stock of 10 billion dollars in and out of the Treasury of the United States*. The money in circulation and that only is what the producers receive for the huge quantity of wealth products which they handle. The wealth, then is collected on a basis of about 12½¢ on the dollar and distributed on a basis of 100 cents on the dollar-hence hard times.

The equilibrium must be established, or goodbye to capitalism in the United States.

The immortal Declaration of Independ ence upholds the equality of all men; their endowment with the inalienable right of life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness; the institution of Government to secure this end; and when not attained, the people's right to alter or abolish the Government. 

In the case at bar, it is the economic foundation that needs laying on such principles and economic powers which demand organiz-

ing in such form as shall seem most likely to secure the economic safety and happiness of the American people. "Permanent National Trustees for Permanent National Prosperity" is not a fancied Utopian scheme. It is an IDEA evolved from the actual present, past and perhaps future doings of the American people placing farmer, stock raiser, manufacturer, all producers in all the industries, on equal footing with the gold digger.

The temporary or emergency measures instituted and put into execution by the President have yielded great benefit, and will doubtless continue to do so until their stream is exhausted and their usefulness ceases to apply. But all temporary measures must eventually serve their purposes, and when continued beyond their legitimate ends, a condition may arise that will furnish a foundation for a series of reversals, perhaps more violent than any predecessor. 

One of America's most outstanding com-

mentators in the field of journalism has repeatedly called attention to the need of an idea; and has even suggested that a certain form of money be issued instead of certain securities to render them inactive, relieving some of the burden to prevent the sinking of the ship. But it does not require deep penetration to observe that a house without a foundation can not stand; it must surely fall.

The idea introduced in this booklet is merely an idea: but at once practical and workable; it is American and business-like, eliminating unsuitable encumbrances; preserving our democracy, intensifying individual effort and achievement, opening a plurality of sources from which money may flow as opposed to but one small constricted golden stream, opening the way for continued progress and expansion, carrying its own safety valve; in its development we should all be fully ready and willing to participate.

Of the vast amount of gold held in the 10

United States Treasury*, more than 3 billion dollars, (figures for 1934) only 156 million dollars (round numbers) are held as a guarantee for the payment in gold of the outstanding 7 billion dollars (round numbers) in circulation (see World Almanac 1934, p. 311).

Since there are a thousand mills in one dollar, there are 156 thousand million mills in 156 million dollars and in 156 million dollars there are 156 billion mills; we then have 156 divided by 7, which gives 22.8 mills, a little more than 2 cents each of the 7 billion it would bring, if exchanged for gold, more than 3 billion dollars. This represents money held in trust for the payment of certain certificates and notes which of course are worth their face value. With this arrangement we seem to be well pleased.

The purpose of this analysis is not to attack the gold standard or any policy thereof, but to show beyond all doubt whatever that *The upholding of the “Gold Clauses" by the Supreme Court has proved this contention completely. 11

Congress has full power to create a trust of any kind where the regulation of the value of money is concerned. For the power to cause 

2 cents in gold to pass for 100 cents, represents unlimited power to create a trust. A further inspection discloses that the 2-cent dollar was, prior to an act of the 73rd Congress and the order of the President under that act, held by a different class from that of the 100-cent dollar, and his order wiping out the distinction caused a mighty rumbling.

The currency of the United States is issued in forms of bills: On the one-dollar bills is 'found the portrait of Washington, on the 2s, Jefferson, on the 5s Lincoln's, Hamilton's on the 10s, Jackson's on the 20s, Grant's on the 

50s, Franklin's on the 100s, McKinley's on the 500s, Cleveland's on the 1000s, Madison's on the 5000s and Chase's on the 10,000s.

A great number of people see Washington's face on money every day. Two dollar bills are not so numerous, so Jefferson's face 12

on money is not so often seen. When Lincoln's face is seen on money, though grave and thoughtful, its possessor feels himself well blessed. Hamilton's face seen on money is a rare occurrence. Sometimes the ordinary being will see Jackson's face; but from Grant on up to Chase, the multitude of American humanity live, breathe and die and never hear of the existence of such faces on money, all the denominations from $50 to $10,000 inclusive have solid foundations beneath them, but for those that fall in the hands of the dear people, their house is built upon sand. It may be argued that the amount of outstanding certificates and notes should be deducted from the amount of money in circulation. If so, then the 156 should be divided by 3, which would give 55 mills for each dollar, or 5½ cents as the gold value of our circulating money.

If the present Administration had continued the policy of its immediate predecessor, by this time all the gold in the United States Treasury would have have been safely locked 13

in the Treasury vaults of our foreign defaulters and debtors, and the value of those fat denominations would have descended to about 22 cents to the dollar-a one thousand dollar note would have been worth just $25 in gold. It was an easy matter for the big foreign governments to get money out of our Treasury, but hard indeed for Mr. American to rest his optics on something like a gold coin of the United States. Under such conditions could anything but gloom, chilliness and unbroken depression be expected? 

Elaborate preparation has been made by the United States Government for the safety and protection of the vast amount of gold held in trust and as a support for the paper and other forms of currency in circulation. Much of it has been safely and quietly resting there for more than a generation. All of it may under present conditions be held forever in a place where it will be more secure than it is now. Yet, not one ounce of it can be used for 14

food, clothing or building material, in case of absolute necessity. It costs the government heavily to guard and protect this metal; and if the cost of its care should be charged against its intrinsic value, title to it would soon pass to its keeper. 

But coal, iron, copper, lead, oil, all the minerals could be kept as long and as safe from all harm, and at a small fraction of the cost of keeping this gold and with certain definite knowledge that they could be drawn upon and used for the same purposes that they are being used today.

Cotton and all textile products can be kept and passed to the next generation. The same may be said of wheat and all the cereals. The flesh of animals has been preserved for thousands of years and the meat then eaten and found wholesome and nutritious. It goes without saying that vegetables and fruits of all kinds are being kept sound and good for indefinite periods of time. In fact there is not 15

a single article mentionable that cannot be kept for great periods of time-so says science. 

Unlike the squirrel, neither the birds nor the fishes are burdened with the economic instinct. But no animal endowed with the faculty of saving will busy itself in preparing places for hoarding material that can be of no actual service to him in case of real need. 

The economic sense of Government seems to run counter to nature. It seems to dictate the hoarding of fictitious values, waste and destroy serviceable things, and in time of real need, to turn to charm for obtaining what must be had from others.

New discoveries, science and useful art have paved the way for the American people, above all others, to use sufficient protective measures to avert destructive conditions. Let the engineer busy himself and devise scientific structures for storing away products of necessity and use of every kind and characterwarehouses of immense capacities, locating 16

centers and systems of centers and subcenters to connect with the various systems and forms of transportation and communication. There are, as we all well know, regions of perpetual sunshine as well as continuous cold within the domain of the United States, all of which may be called to the service of the engineer in devising means for taking care of products of 

American skill and labor.

The man with the bag of gold will say, "I have not the money for all that and some of that stuff will surely spoil. I cannot finance such a stupendous undertaking." But the people of the United States will say, "We have our President and our Congress with unlimited power to regulate money and its value; we have the same printing press that prints your money-your 2-cent dollar and your 100-cent dollar, and under this new arrangement, all shall be 100-cent dollars."

For the care and protection of all our products (intellectual excepted) Congress 17

should create and establish a permanent trust (trusteeship) to be managed and controlled by a permanent Board of National Trustees (with the Secretary of the Treasury as exofficio member thereof); a commission of experts to make preparations for the Trustees, and adjust prices, provide for expenses, etc., and all such acts and arrangements should be subject to the review of the President who should make appointments as may be provided by law. It would be easy for the Commission to arrange for the payment of all national expenses through the fixing of prices when distribution is made.

The National Trustees would have power to receive all products of the people; to take care and custody of them, to hold them until called for, and to deliver to the people their National Trustee notes (money) in payment therefore, which would be acceptable when presented by the holder for such portion as he may be entitled to, regardless of whether the 18

holder may or may not be a depositor with the Trustees. All lawful money of the United 

States would be equally receivable and acceptable as Trustee Notes, all such notes being lawful money of the United States.

The Secretary of the Treasury would provide the Trustees with National Trustee Notes for the use of the people in such quantities and at such times as may be required, as Congress would provide for their printing in suitable form and denomination-just like any other money. And for every dollar of outstanding Trustee Notes there would be a true 100-cent dollar in products of various kinds ready for delivery.

The progress of the people must not be retarded. To introduce something worn out or discarded, or attempt some imitation that is being introduced among a people of a past and different motives and activities may invite a set back that would be hard to overcome. 

We must strike out for ourselves and in a man- 19

ner dictated by our own structure and movements. We must institute among ourselves economic methods suitable to our needs and along lines of our own advancement and achievements. No other people have struggled harder to correct their past mistakes and lay a foundation for peace and happiness. Witness the vast and continuously increasing outlay for stamping out ignorance; and the collossal sums being left by private individuals for the good of humanity in general. One cannot resist the conclusion that there are at work somewhere powerful motives for good. 

Let such motives be now put to work in the direction herein pointed out.

To insure a sound and permanent economic condition, the people must not be interfered with, but must be assisted by the Government. 

To retard or decrease production increases unemployment and restrains individual efforts. The people should be productive in every line of industry. They should be encour- 20

aged to produce to their highest capacity. Real progress comes from well directed periods of intense activity followed by intervals of rest, recreation, and not by prolonged depression, gloom and more depression.

The farmer, for example, could, if he wished, produce 20 million bales of cotton and instead of seeking foreign markets among defaulted debtors, deliver his entire produce to the National Trustees for safe keeping at 25¢ a pound, alfalfa at $20 per ton, and other products along the same strain, thereby being able to pay a just wage to labor and have something for himself as well as for his creditors. 

Wheat, all the cereals, coal, oil, sugar, tobacco everything produced for consumption could be scientifically stored away and kept for an indefinite period of time. 

All manufactured products, steel, iron implements of every description could be taken care of by the trustees when desired. There 21

would be no bankrupt stock and, no unnecessary waste. All goods, wares, household furnishings, all second hand articles, all cast-off merchandise, used machines, everything, even junk could be taken and their values determined in lawful money and paid to the persons entitled to receive it.

This arrangement would in no way interfer with the operation of wholesale businesses, packing houses, retail establishments, banking or any other legitimate enterprise.

On the other hand in case of failure or retirement from any kind of business, the goods, wares, merchandise could be returned to the Trustees at their just value for redistribution. 

The Conclusions Are: 

1. That a National Trust should be created with the Secretary of the Treasury as ex-officio member; that it should receive, store away and when called upon, deliver products of every kind and character, and through the Secretary 22

of the Treasury, issue therefore its notes which should be a form of currency and constitute a true medium of circulation. 

2. That through the Secretary of the Treasury the Government could issue the said notes to the National Trustees; that the Government should provide the necessary facilities for taking care of the people's products and establish a National Adjustment Commission to cooperate with, but to have a separate existence from the National Board of Trustees. 

3. That the said Board of Trustees should have power to extend credit in certain cases. 

4. That every line of industry could and should be taken care of in and throughout the entire United States and possessions regardless of foreign markets; that all the people could work at their full capacity, and the entire nation would abound with prosperity. 

5. That an equilibrium would be established between active products and circulation. 

6. That disturbances caused by strikes, etc., could be easily provided against. 23

7. That periods of national holidays could be used to control overproduction when occurring in certain lines of industry. 

8. That full payment could be made to former holders of gold certificates, coin and bullion. 

Trustees (a national corporate agency), through the Secretary of the Treasury could as easily furnish our economic system notes (money) as the miller does checks for his clientele, as the transportation company gives checks for parcels received, or as the Government provides currency for all the gold in the world at the best prices ever known. 

A thousand billion dollars issued to a properly constituted agency for the purpose of acquiring the products of the entire Western Hemisphere should be the high point cited for permanent prosperity in the United 

States and the Western Hemisphere. Should that be the case, the little national debt we have before us would soon minimize and be- 24

come a mere bagatelle in the face of the enormous "toll" that would accrue to our national and international "miller," created under our laws, by our Congress and President, and protected by our courts, militia, army, navy and air forces. Minds that have not sufficient strength to think in such enormous terms as are herein proposed will eventually be relegated to the past and regarded as would-bebuilders of a "Maginot Line" in our national economic defense. Our present dwarfed financial set-up, once thought to be adequate, must give way to something comparable to our vast ought-to be stream-lined economic activity.

Nothing serious happened because of the introduction and extensive use of the electric curent; nothing violent can take place when the operations of our economic activities are elevated to the level of present day scientific and mechanical progress. The fault lies not in incompetent political organization or administration, but in failure to persistently ap- 25

ply and follow up the principle "to the miller belongs a just toll;" to produce a national and international "miller" whose enormous "toll" would soon supply all our national and international economic needs and dispense with income taxes, tariffs and much other tomfoolery of great interest to politicians but opposed to the best interests of the people. Soon no taxes would be needed-all "toll" for national expenditures and public debts. 

Unlimited power to collect (purchase) must not be confined to gold alone by the Government (no objection to the purchase, however), but such power should be lodged in a national agency, created by law and protected by the courts for the people. Such agency should collect (purchase), conserve and equitably distribute every species of property (except real estate and intellectual products) produced and consumed by the people, with a view of justice to all and discrimination against none, so that all would have an 26

equal and common right to produce, sell, distribute or consume according to their respective individual abilities and merits-incompetents provided for and protected. 

A letter stamp costs no more in San Francisco than it does in Washington, D. C., at the engraving office. Our distribution system should be so unified and simplified. There would be no more waste as we have recently witnessed (in California), and extravagant retail prices would soon disappear. 

A national agency or agencies clothed with a sufficiency of power and adaptability would be able to correct and adjust the major and more dangerous of our national ills, without government ownership or control. A new adjustment would gradually take place without disturbance or business loss. Once upon a time there was no electric light, but its coming and extensive use have not destroyed the use of oil and gas.

Under the new set-up the farmer would 27

demand and receive, upon delivery 25c a pound for his cotton-all he produced-he could expand his acreage, absorb more labor at not less than 50c per hour. Grain producers, vegetable and fruit growers, raisers of livestock every industry would expand, labor would be absorbed at once upon permanent terms and employment. Manufactured articles, coal, oil, minerals-all would enjoy the same liberal consideration as cotton, gold and other produce. Everything of value without regard to size or quantity would fall within reach of the great national "miller."

Gold as we have already noted is now being properly treated, and with the coming of the set-up herein advocated, all persons, corporations or banks who formerly held gold and gold certificates could be paid the balance they claim to be due them according to the present prices of gold. The intrinsic value of gold and silver, when the metals are used as a medium of exchange, should receive no higher consideration than is given to the in- 28

trinsic value of paper when used for our money-currency.

It would be no disadvantage to the oil industry to be able to dispose of all the oil they produce at the price they demand, with certain and definite knowledge that they would receive in payment therefore true and lawful money of the United States, gold, silver or currency, upon delivery. Science and useful art, idle skill and labor would soon enable the "miller" to conserve for distribution all of the nation's wealth entrusted to him. 1 With the entire nation at work with both mind and body, young people leaving school and college would take pleasure in finding or carving out for themselves suitable places. 

Banks would be willing, ready and anxious to advance money for expanding present enterprises and for the launching of new ventures, as the foundation underlying their investments would be as solid as the national structure upon which our national economic system would then be resting. In no case would 29

there be any necessity for resorting to compulsion. Prices and adjustment would be the factors determining the proper course to be pursued. If necessary, national holidays could be established when overproduction takes place in any line or industry.

Exchanges would perform their true function, as prices would always remain unchanged regardless of the market value of stocks and bonds.

The huge "toll" would soon be sufficient for the payment not only of all national debts and current expenditures but also furnish means for the payment of old age national pensions and securities of every nature.

The present Administration and Congress have opened the way for real prosperity in the United States including all its possessions and friends. Perhaps they have built wiser than the people or even, they themselves have stopped to consider.

One must look far beneath the surface to 30

understand the needs of this nation. Tens of thousands of dollars were spent before the production of a single electric light. Was the time or money wasted? The life of an individual or of a generation constitutes but a short span in the life of a nation. The little money which has or is now being spent in the adjustment of some of our national irregularities should soon be forgotten in so far as concerns its being a large sum. 

The IDEA involved herein and the process required for its development are strictly American from start to finish, through and through. It is self-demonstrating, and its operation would verify its claims.

Brief Illustration 

A miller whose clients would deliver to him only 10 bushels of corn per week should and would retain for his pay for grinding 25 per cent or 2½ bushels. If and when his clientele increased until they brought him 100 31

bushels per week, his toll would be 25 bushels; at the same rate a thousand bushels would yield him 250 bushels, a thousand billion bushels, 250 billion bushels. Substitute for corn, every conceivable article we produce and use, manufactured and otherwise (tolled, of course) after reserving the "national miller's toll," using dollars for bushels, we would have 250 billion dollars "toll" for the use of the Treasury of the United States when required by Congress.

The miller, at first poor, would become rich. His clients already rich would become richer by reason of the services rendered them by their miller.
