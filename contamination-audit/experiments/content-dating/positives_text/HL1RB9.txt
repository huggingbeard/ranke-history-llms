INTRODUCTION 

Under the laws of most states, when a person is charged with violating a traffic law, he is generally given a citation directing him to appear in court at a designated time and place to answer to the charge. If the person signs a promise to appear as required by the citation, he is released. When he later appears, the court can adjudicate the charge. But what happens when he ignores his promise to appear and fails to come to court? Since the person is not before it, the court cannot adjudicate the charge. Obviously, this is a serious problem. 

When faced with this problem, the court can issue a warrant commanding that the person be arrested and brought before the court. Another possibility is to be more restrictive in releasing the person in the first instance. Instead of accepting his promise to appear, the officer could keep him in custody until his trial, or release him only if he deposits something of value as bail, which will be forfeited if he fails to appear. These are the traditional solutions to the failure-to-appear problem. They are somewhat effective, but not entirely satisfactory. 

Another possible solution is to take administrative action against the driving privilege of the person who fails to appear. 

The purpose of this Traffic Laws Commentary is to review the status of state laws which in some way provide for administrative action against the drivers license of a person who fails to appear in court to answer a traffic charge.

State laws of this type have generally taken one of three different approaches:

The unilateral approach. The Uniform Vehicle Code and the laws of some states provide for suspension of the driving privilege of the person who fails to appear. Some states, instead of suspending, simply refuse to renew the license of a person who has failed to appear. This action, whether suspension or refusal, is taken by the states unilaterally. It is not based upon any agreement with other states; it does not necessarily depend upon the cooperation of other states. This approach is most effective and useful with residents who fail to appear, but it can also be applied to nonresidents and can be equally effective with nonresidents whose home states have similar laws. - 1 -

The driver license bail approach. The second approach is similar, but it applies only where the arrested person has deposited his drivers license in lieu of bail in order to be released.

If he fails to appear, he does not get his license back. 

Generally, the license will be forwarded to the department for suspension. This approach is frequently limited to residents, but some states allow nonresidents to deposit a drivers license in lieu of bail.

The reciprocal approach. The third approach also results in suspension of the driving privilege, but it involves a bilateral or multilateral agreement with other states. Under this approach, a state enters into a formal agreement with another state or with several states. Such agreements generally provide that if a resident of one state which has entered the agreement is arrested in another state which has entered the agreement, he will be released upon signing his promise to appear. If he fails to appear, the home state agrees that it will suspend his driving privilege until he appears.

The Commentary will analyze existing state laws which follow one or more of the approaches described above. It will evaluate some of the strengths and weaknesses of each approach. The 

Commentary does not discuss all of the existing or potential solutions to the failure-to-appear problem. It is limited, rather, to those laws which in some way address the problem through administrative action against the driving privilege.1/

THE UNILATERAL APPROACH Uniform Vehicle Code 

The Uniform Vehicle Code authorizes the department to suspend the driving privilege of a person who fails to appear in court when required. The Code provides as follows: 

The department is hereby authorized to suspend the license of a driver upon a showing by its records or other sufficient evidence that the licensee: 

Has violated his written promise to appear given to an officer upon the issuance of a traffic citation in this or any other state or has failed to appear in court in this or any other state at the time specified by the court; 2/

This section applies to a resident or a nonresident who fails to appear. In the case of a nonresident, the department would be authorized to suspend the nonresident operating privilege of the person, giving notice of the suspension to his home state.3/ The section would also apply to a resident who fails to appear in the court of another state. If the department receives notice of such failure, it is authorized to suspend the resident's license.4/ 

It should also be noted that the Code's definition of "state" includes any state, any territory or possession of the United States, the District of Columbia, the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico, or any province of Canada. 5/ Thus, a failure to appear in the courts of any of these jurisdictions could result in suspension if the home state has adopted the Code provisions. - 3 - 

---

These provisions will work as described above without regard to whether other jurisdictions have adopted similar laws. That's why we have referred to these Code provisions as the "unilateral approach." 

Under the Uniform Vehicle Code provision the court must report a failure to appear to the department.6/ Issuance of a warrant is not required, although the court could do so in lieu of or in addition to notifying the department. 

As with any discretionary suspension, the Code requires that the person be given written notice and an opportunity for a departmental hearing prior to the effective date of the suspension.77 the The duration of the suspension is in the discretion of th department, but it may not exceed one year..

THE DRIVER LICENSE BAIL APPROACH Uniform Vehicle Code 

The Uniform Vehicle Code does not provide for the deposit of a drivers license in lieu of bail to secure release when arrested for a traffic offense.

State Laws Substance of the Laws 

Twelve states have laws which provide for deposit of a valid drivers license in lieu of bail. Alabama Arkansas Georgia Illinois Indiana Kansas Louisiana Mississippi Ohio Oregon Tennessee Wisconsin

Two of these states, Indiana and Oregon, have already been discussed as having laws which incorporate the unilateral approach. 

Both approaches are apparently utilized by these states. The 

Indiana driver license bail law is unique, however, because it applies only to nonresidents. No other state limits the driver license bail approach to nonresidents; many in fact, as will be discussed below, limit the approach to residents. 

Deposit of license in lieu of bail. Most of these 12 states allow deposit of the license when a person is arrested for any traffic law offense, although three (Illinois, Mississippi, and Ohio) exclude certain enumerated serious offenses, and two others (Georgia and Tennessee) exclude any offense which would result in suspension or revocation of the license upon conviction. One state (Kansas) allows deposit of the license only when charged with an offense included in a statutory bond schedule; the schedule includes the most common traffic law offenses. 

One state (Tennessee) allows deposit on a local option basis only. Another (Mississippi) appears to allow deposit of the license only when arrested by a state police officer. One state (Indiana) applies the law only to nonresidents. - 12 -

Five of the states (Georgia, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana and

Wisconsin) specify that the license is to be deposited with the arresting officer. One other (Oregon) allows deposit of the license only with the court or magistrate. In one state (Illinois) it is not clear who accepts the deposit. The other five states allow the license to be deposited with either the court or the officer.

Five of the states (Arkansas, Illinois, Ohio, Oregon and Wisconsin) allow only their own state drivers licenses to be deposited in lieu of bail. Louisiana will allow an out-of-state license to be deposited only if the person is a student or armed forces member or dependent actually residing in Louisiana. Two states (Georgia and Kansas) allow deposit of out-of-state licenses only if they have a reciprocal agreement with the issuing state. 

The other four states (Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi and Tennessee) allow deposit of a license issued by any state.

Procedure upon failure to appear. Four of the 12 states (Arkansas, Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio) require the court to delay for a brief period before taking action against a person who fails to appear. This gives the person a further opportunity to make an appearance before action is taken against his license. Of these four, however, only Illinois requires notice to the licensee, giving him a specific new appearance date. The other eight states all require immediate action by the court upon a failure to appear. 

None of the 12 requires the court to issue a warrant. Since the court has the person's drivers license in its possession, it takes action against the driving privilege, rather than attempting to bring the person before the court by arresting him. 

Ten of the 12 states (all except Arkansas and Wisconsin) require the court to forward the license to the department. Most of these laws then require the department to suspend the license. 

One (Ohio) specifies cancellation rather than suspension. Another (Indiana) applies the law only to nonresidents and so requires only suspension of the nonresident operating privilege. Two states (Louisiana and Tennessee) do not require suspension but require the department to hold the license, not returning it to the licensee or issuing any duplicate or renewal. 

The other two of the 12 states (Arkansas and Wisconsin) provide for mandatory suspension or revocation of the license by the court, rather than by the department. The court must then give the department notice of the action taken.

The duration of the license action in 10 of the 12 states is indefinite, continuing until the court notifies the department that the case has been adjudicated. One of these 10 (Wisconsin) specifies a minimum duration of 30 days, although the suspension can be removed by the court in less time if it finds the person 

18

was not seriously at fault in failing to appear. Only Ohio limits the maximum duration of license action to one year. Arkansas provides for revocation with no specified period of duration.

Three compacts addressing the nonresident violator problem have been developed and utilized to some extent.

Only information about the three attempted compacts is presented below. 

The compacts generally provide that a resident of any party state, when stopped for a traffic violation in another party state, should be given a citation and released on personal recognizance on the same basis as a resident of the state where the violation occurred. OBSERVATIONS The Nonresident Violator Compact. 

The focus of the current effort to deal with the problem of nonresidents who fail to appear is the Nonresident Violator Compact of 1977. The National 

Committee on Uniform Traffic Laws and Ordinances supports the 

Compact. The Uniform Vehicle Code was recently amended to contain specific authorization for participation in that Compact, and to clearly provide authority to carry out the agreement. 39/ 

Nevertheless, the National Committee staff believes there are some problems which need to be addressed. Specifically, we have the following concerns about the Compact: 

1. The 1977 Compact allows entry by administrative agencies acting under proper legislative authority. 40/ 

This procedure may make it easier to secure widespread acceptance of the Compact, but it also creates potential for states to enter the Compact without having the proper authority. The administrative agency needs legislative authority to enter the agreement, and clear authority to suspend drivers licenses for failure to appear when necessary to carry out the agreement. Our research indicates that more than half of the states now participating in the Compact may not have the necessary authority. If a state driver licensing agency does not have authority to suspend licenses for failure to appear, it does not get that authority by entering a compact. 

Such authority must come from the legislature. but 2. The 1977 Compact also allows entry through legislative adoption of the Compact language. Such adoption would clearly provide adequate authority for the state's participation, only if the legislature adopts the appropriate Compact language, without variation. Where the legislature varies the language or adopts the language of some earlier compact version, their adoption may not provide adequate authority for the state's participation. 

3. After any compact has been adopted by several states, it becomes very difficult to make amendments. The 1977 Compact includes a procedure allowing amendments to be made by the Compact's governing body, with ratification by the member states. 

Silence of a member jurisdiction for 120 days is considered a ratification. 41/

This procedure may make amendments more feasible. 

On the other hand, since the Compact allows entry by legislative adoption of the agreement, it seems likely that the amendment problem has not been completely eliminated.42/ For any state which enters the Compact in that manner, legislative approval of 39 

---

amendments would certainly be required. If the legislature fails to approve an amendment, what is the effect on that state's participation? 

4. Another problem with the Nonresident Violator Compact is its potential for conflict with the general laws of the party states. The Compact is not a comprehensive treatment of the problem; it deals only with nonresidents from other party states. The states' general laws probably will contain provisions to deal with other nonresidents and residents. Also, all states' vehicle codes currently have provisions relating to driver license suspensions, and release of persons arrested for traffic offenses. 

These provisions may conflict with the Compact, and the effect of such conflicts is not clear. The states will need to be very careful to modify their laws as necessary to avoid conflicts with the Compact itself and also with the procedural provisions contained in the Compact Manual. 

5. A comprehensive treatment of the nonresident violator problem should recognize the involvement of Canadian residents traveling in the United States, and residents of the States traveling in Canada. As originally written, the 1977 Compact would not allow participation by Canadian Provinces.43/ We have been advised that the Compact has now been amended to permit Canadian Provinces to join. 44, But the validity of such an amendment is certainly open to serious question. The United States 

Constitution prohibits the states from entering into any treaty. 45/ 

Another section of the Constitution specifically prohibits any state from entering, without the consent of Congress, any agreement or compact with another state or with a foreign power 46/ Congress has given its prior consent to compacts among the states dealing with highway safety matters.47/ This consent appears to be sufficiently broad to cover a compact treating the nonresident violator problem, but the consent does not refer to participation in such a compact by Canadian provinces. We believe that Congress has not consented to Canadian participation in the Nonresident 

Violator Compact. 

The Unilateral Approach. This approach to the failure-to-appear problem is comprehensive in that it deals with residents and nonresidents. A state adopts a law which authorizes its driver licensing agency to suspend the driver license of a person who fails to appear in court after giving his promise to do so. The law should authorize suspension for such a failure to appear in the home state or in any other state. An opportunity for a hearing 

40

should be provided before the suspension takes effect. This will give the person additional time to make the required appearance, thus avoiding the suspension, or it will allow him to explain why his license should not be suspended.

Once a state is taking this action to compel its licensees to comply with a promise to appear, police officers will be able to take that into consideration when deciding whether to release a person on the basis of such a promise. In order to maximize the benefits of the law for its licensees who are traveling in other states, the state might want to let other states know that it will compel its licensees to appear. It might even consider adding a statement to that effect on the back of the license card, to give notice to police officers in other states. The state driver licensing agency might also want to reach understanding with similar agencies in other states as to the use of common forms and procedures for reporting failures to appear. The state might also want to enter the Nonresident Violator Compact, since the two approaches can be quite consistent. 

The Driver License Bail Approach. We believe this approach is unnecessary and undesirable. It is not necessary to take possession of the drivers license card at the time the person is released from custody in order to suspend his driving privilege if he later fails to appear. Most suspension actions are initiated while the license card is still in the possession of the licensee. If the privilege is suspended, the licensee is required to surrender the card, which is only evidence of the privilege. 

If he fails to surrender the card, his privilege is suspended, nevertheless. There is no reason why suspensions for failure to appear cannot follow the same procedures which are effective for suspensions on other grounds. The licensee should retain his license card until the driving privilege has been suspended. 

The undesirable aspect of the approach is that it takes away the licensee's identification. The drivers license bail laws provide that the licensee is given a "receipt" for his license, and that he is entitled to continue driving, using this receipt in place of his license. But how effectively will this receipt perform the identification function of the drivers license card? Will the receipt bear the licensee's photograph? His description? His signature? Will it note any restrictions on the driving privilege? Will it contain all the same information which is on the drivers license card which helps to connect a particular driver with his driving records kept by the department - 41 - 

---

of motor vehicles? Even if it contains all of this information, will it be in a format which is readily recognizable and usable? 

Will a police officer from another state recognize that this receipt is a drivers license? If he does accept it as a drivers license, will he know where to look on the receipt to see, for example, if the licensee's privilege is restricted in any way? 

What steps will be taken to prevent counterfeiting and other fraudulent use of such receipts which purport to be drivers licenses?

The approach is based on the fallacy that the drivers license card is something of value. In fact, the card has no intrinsic value. It is only evidence of the underlying driving privilege, and it is a means of identifying the person who holds that privilege. The driver license bail approach can be effective, but only in the way that the unilateral or reciprocal approaches are also effective. They are all effective to the extent that they threaten the person with loss of the driving privilege if he fails to appear. This can be done effectively without depriving the licensee of his best drivers identification at a time when he is still entitled to drive.
