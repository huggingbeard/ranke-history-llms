PREFACE Any attempt to revise the Why and How of Woman's 

Missionary Union by simply adding the new plans and methods that have been developed within the past few years would be like patching an old dress with the unfaded material of which it was made. It was impossible to recast phrases of 1928 and 1934 and make them fit the trend and tempo of present day thinking without re-writing the whole book. Although the title has been changed, chapter headings and general outlines remain the same. 

Some material was omitted in this edition because it is carried annually in the W.M.U. Year Book. Other topics have been enlarged upon and as far as possible the whole content has been brought up to date.

CHAPTER I HIGH LIGHTS IN THE HISTORY OF WOMAN'S MISSIONARY UNION 

Whereunto shall I liken the kingdom of God?

It is like leaven which a woman took and hid in three measures of meal, till the whole was leav ened.-LUKE 13:20-21. 

The history of Woman's Missionary Union reads like romance, or a modern miracle. Its amazing record of achievement in missionary endeavor becomes a rich heritage of courage, faith, and self sacrifice which should be treasured by every member of the organization. 

In this period of the greatest activity of women the world has ever seen, we need to look back and see how we have come, as well as how far we have come. Today the doors of business, professional, industrial, and even political life are being thrown open to women, who by thousands are daily entering and proving their ability and efficiency by their achievement. The privileges and opportunities which we enjoy today are ours because of the special ministry of Jesus to the womanhood of the world, and to the emboldened spirit of those missionary-hearted women of long ago who launched out in an undreamed of undertaking which later became "the open sesame" to other women's organizations and activities down through the years. It is a significant thing that one of the earliest organized efforts on the part of women was for the extension of Christ's kingdom throughout the world. "In what other field than missions could a woman or young person hope to influence the whole world?"1

Children and the Kingdom

The mothers who eagerly brought their children to Jesus for his blessing were rebuked by the disciples, but the Master used this incident to teach the importance and place of children in the kingdom.¹ 

Sacrificial Giving

The most notable gift in Christian history-though the least one was that of the poor widow who gave two mites. Loving Service

The most sublime service rendered the Lord in his earthly ministry was that of a woman whose spiritual discernment leaped ahead of that of the disciples. She quietly slipped out and brought her treasured alabaster box to anoint the body of her Lord for burial aforetime. The disciples rebuked her but Jesus commended her saying, "wheresoever this gospel shall be preached in the whole world, that also which this woman hath done shall be spoken of as a memorial to her." This story of a woman's love and service is to be forever linked with the story of the life, death and resurrection of our Lord!

Prayer 

Paul's party upon arriving in Philippi found no synagogue. The only place of worship they found on that sabbath day was a woman's prayer meeting out by the riverside. Business Women

II. THE LEAVEN AT WORK 

William Carey's going as a missionary to India in 1793 inaugurated the modern missionary era. The first response in America was a "concert of prayer" which was taken up by Christians one group after another. People were eager to learn of the progress of this new venture. A missionary magazine was published. Missionary societies began to spring up here and there. 

The First Missionary Society, 1800 

The first Woman's Missionary Society on record in America was organized by Miss Mary Webb of Boston, Mass., in 1800. She at one time, though bound to a wheel chair, was fostering 121 societies. So strongly was the missionary zeal stirring in the hearts of Christian women that it had to find expression. They began to meet everywhere in groups to pray and sew and work for both home and foreign missions. Naturally the next step was "Sewing Circles," "Mite Societies," "Mission Bands," and so on.

First American Missionaries, 1812 

The going of Luther Rice, Adoniram Judson and his lovely young bride, Ann Hasseltine Judson, to India, as missionaries was the Macedonian call to American Baptists. On the long, tedious journey over, these young mis-

sionaries made a diligent study of New Testament teaching regarding baptism in order to defend their doctrine before the Baptist missionaries they knew they must meet. 

The result was they were converted to the Baptist faith. 

No longer could they expect support from the Congregationalist Board which sent them out. Mr. Rice came back to America to enlist the Baptists to support the Judsons. 

He gave himself with utter abandon to the stupendous task. The response to this appeal served to unite scattered Baptist churches and so gave cohesion to Baptist life, in other words, gave birth to the denomination. 

Triennial Convention, 1814

The first year after returning from India, Mr. Rice rode 8,000 miles on horseback, electrifying the Baptists from Maine to Georgia with his messages. It seems incredible, but within eight months after he arrived in America the General Convention of Baptist Denomination in U.S.A. was organized. The organization was later called The Triennial Convention because it met every three years. This marks the beginning of foreign mission work by American Baptists. Mr. Rice was appointed the general mission agent of the new movement. The Triennial Convention's second report showed 187 missionary societies contributing to missions, 110 of which were women's organizations.¹

At the first meeting of the Triennial Convention honorable mention was made of the generous gift for missions sent by the famous Wadmalaw Edisto Missionary Society of South Carolina.2

The Movement Grows 

The missionary movement among women and young people was gaining momentum daily. They were finding needy fields of service all around them. As early as 1816

there were six functioning societies in Kentucky. Three years later they organized a school for the Indians, enrolling seventeen students. Nine years later two of these students went as missionaries to their own people. Thus we see Baptist women concerned for the "heathen at home" as well as for those abroad. 

First American Baptist Missionaries to China, 1835

A new impetus was given to missions by the going of Mr. and Mrs. J. Lewis Shuck to China. It was especially significant to Southern women for a lovely young Virginia woman, Henrietta Hall Shuck, to become the first 

American woman missionary to China. Her husband when a young man heard Luther Rice deliver one of his soul-stirring messages. An offering for missions was taken at the close of the meeting. Having no money, he wrote on a slip of paper, "I give myself," and dropped it into the plate. He and his wife became the first Southern 

Baptist foreign missionaries. 

It is interesting to note also that the missionary society of Beulah Church, Virginia, helped send him through college and "gave him an outfit made of Virginia cloth"¹ when he sailed for China.

Home Missionaries 

The year the Shucks went to China as thrilling a chapter in missionary history as was ever written was made by young Mrs. Annette Bledsoe out in the Republic of Texas. Though not a regularly appointed missionary, this cultured, well-educated young woman, sister-in-law of General Sam Houston, and a zealous soul-winner, was in a large measure responsible for the establishment of Baptist work in Texas while it was still a part of Mexico. 

She did a remarkable work among the Mexican women and children, organizing Sunday schools and societies, In Royal Service, pg. 58-Heck.

even helping to organize churches. She and her mother, brother and sister and General Houston were among the charter members of the Education Society which later founded Baylor College.

Little is known of the pioneer women who have labored so heroically in the homeland. Some day they will be listed with those who "shine as the brightness of the firmament" for, having turned many to righteousness, they too, "shall shine. . . as the stars for ever and for ever." 

Southern Baptist Convention Organized, 1845

Mission work was growing rapidly by 1845. There was a need for more frequent meetings, but transportation difficulties over such wide expanse of territory made many meetings impossible. The growing controversy over slavery however was the deciding factor in the dissolution of the Triennial Convention. Leaders north and south agreed that the only solution was to organize into separate bodies. 

In May, 1845, Southern Baptists met in Augusta, Georgia, and organized the Southern Baptist Convention. Two boards of missions were established, the Foreign Mission Board with headquarters at Richmond, Va., and the Board of Domestic Missions, later called The Home Mission Board with headquarters at Marion, Ala., later (1882) moved to Atlanta, Ga.

It was soon discovered that both conventions were doing more mission work separately than they had done together. 

During thirty-one years under the Triennial Convention, Southern Baptists contributed $212,000 to foreign missions.

Bible Women Supported 

The appeals of her missionary son from China prompted 

Mrs. Ann Graves of Baltimore to call the women attending the Southern Baptist Convention meeting in her city in 1868, to come together to pray for missions. She told

them of the need for native women to go into the Chinese homes to read and distribute portions of the Scriptures which had been translated into Chinese. She begged the women to go home and organize societies to raise funds for the support of these Bible women. Mrs. J. B. Jeter, inspired by this appeal, began to write articles for the Religious Herald and other papers in the South calling on the women to organize. Far-reaching results came from this first general meeting of Southern Baptist women. 

Central Committees, 1874

The Home and Foreign Mission Boards soon realized what an asset this "woman's movement" could be to their work, In 1874 Dr. Tupper, secretary of the Foreign 

Mission Board, recommended that central committees be appointed in each state to promote women's work. South 

Carolina led out with the first one. In January, 1875, the society of Welch Neck, which furnished the first president of Woman's Missionary Union, was asked to serve as central committee of the state. Opposition

Not all was smooth sailing for women's work. Criticism and opposition waxed stronger and stronger. A climax was reached when two women delegates from Arkansas came to the Southern Baptist Convention in 1885. 

Hot discussion ensued, the constitution was changed in order to unseat them. The word member was stricken and brethren written in. Not until 1921 was it changed back to member.

Southern Woman's Missionary Union Organized, 1888 Several preliminary meetings were held by women of the South during Southern Baptist Conventions, each one bringing them nearer to organization. At such a meeting in Montgomery, Ala., 1886, eleven states reported a total

of $20,000 contributed to missions. The next year in 

Louisville the women were asked to express their opinion and that of their states, regarding a general organization. 

A resolution offered by Miss Annie Armstrong that each state appoint three delegates to come to the next session instructed to vote on the organization measure was enthusiastically adopted. 

May 11, 1888, thirty-two delegates from twelve states met in the basement of Broad Street Methodist Church in 

Richmond, Va., to decide the momentous question. Women from Virginia, North Carolina and Mississippi were there and just as eager for the organization as those from Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Missouri, South Carolina, Tennessee and Texas but, desiring to await sanction by their respective states, were prevented from casting their vote on this historymaking day. Mississippi did come into the Union two months later. 

Miss M. E. McIntosh, of South Carolina, was chosen president; Miss Annie W. Armstrong of Maryland, corresponding secretary; Mrs. J. F. Pullen, Maryland, treasurer. 

A report of the sixteen years' work under the direction. of state central committees showed 1,206 organizations and $45,768.32 contributed to eight different denominational causes; $15,000 for foreign missions; $7,000 for home missions.

III. LEAVENING THE WHOLE LUMP 

Behold how a little leaven leaventh! The hearts of

Southern Baptist women are warm and responsive to the purposes of God. The missionary leaven was working. 

Its permeating influence was being felt among the children and young people of the churches and to the ends of the earth.

HIGH LIGHTS IN THE HISTORY OF W.M.U. 21 NEW LEAVENING AGENCIES INAUGURATED The Literature Department 

In 1886 the churches of the Maryland Union Associa tion established "The Mission Library and Reading Room" 

to be managed by representative women from the Home and Foreign Mission Societies of Baltimore. It was to this "Publishing House" the newly organized W.M.U. turned for material. The first year thousands of leaflets, programs, prayer cards, mite boxes were sent out. Scores of Baltimore women gave untiring service in the mission rooms without salary or typewriters, while the demands for supplies increased by leaps and bounds. 

In 1906 the mission rooms were transferred by the women and the Maryland Union Association to the Southern W.M.U. for larger service. The $1,402 in cash and assets accrued from endowment and profits on sales for twenty years, with the stock on hand, became the Litera ture Department of Southern Woman's Missionary Union. 

Although Southern Union policy requires that it function as a missionary agency and not for profit, it has been so successfully operated that many causes and agencies have received assistance from its surplus from time to time.

This department annually handles thousands of Standard of Excellence charts, pins, pennants, armbands and other emblematic devices, manuals, year books, priced leaflets, and other supplies for missionary organizations. 

Mrs. H. M. Wharton in her interesting booklet, Ready

Pens Proclaiming Missions, gives us the history of this department. 

W.M.U. Magazines 

Royal Service

The Heathen Helper served a splendid purpose in preorganization days. The state papers and mission journals

graciously opened their pages to the Union. However, rapid growth in organization and general expansion of the work made it necessary to have a distinctive W.M.U. publication to promote unity of plans and purposes in organization, study, prayer, and missionary giving. 

In 1906 Our Mission Field was published. Two years later it was succeeded by the monthly magazine Royal Service which has grown steadily in usefulness and favor until the subscription list now goes well beyond the 120,000 mark. 

World Comrades

Miss Heck in her annual message in 1911 made a plea for a young people's magazine. Eleven years elapsed before World Comrades came into being under the splendid leadership of Miss Juliette Mather, Southern W.M.U. young people's secretary. The magazine contains attractive program material for Sunbeam Bands, Girls' Auxiliaries, and Royal Ambassador chapters and many interesting stories, which delight every boy and girl who have access to it. It should be found in every Baptist home where there are children.

The Window of Y. W. A. 

The young women in Y.W.A. began to clamor for a magazine of their own. The work was growing, college 

Y.W.A.'s, the Grace McBride and church organizations really needed specially prepared material and helps. In 

1929 The Window of Y. W. A. came out containing a wealth of material, instructive, inspiring, and stimulating. 

Miss Mather, as editor in chief, is assisted by many writers and program editors. 

Margaret Fund

The initial gift of $10,000 was made in 1905 by Mrs. 

Frank Chambers of New York, formerly of Alabama.

The amount was used to purchase a home in Greenville,

S. C. This property provided a home for missionaries' children while at school in the homeland and also a resting place for our missionaries when on furlough. Forty boys and girls and 15 missionaries enjoyed its comforts. In 

1915, progress and changing conditions on mission fields pointing to paths of greater usefulness, the home was sold and the enlarged amount invested in an educational fund which more fully meets the needs of our missionaries. 

The first Margaret Fund scholarships were granted in 1916 to eight students. In 1942 there were 109. The total number of beneficiaries of the Fund since 1916 is 

455; the total expenditure $410,422.64.

The beautiful name of Margaret was given to the home by Mrs. Chambers in honor of her mother and daughter.

The memorial name was retained when the home was sold and the scholarship fund established.

How Does the Fund Operate? The Margaret Fund now has four departments. Scholarship Fund. The amount needed each year for scholarships is obtained through the 

Lottie Moon Christmas Offering for foreign missions and the Annie Armstrong Offering for home missions. "Burney Gifts" is the term designating occasional uniform gifts of money sent to Margaret Fund students for their personal use. The name is a constant reminder of the devoted and wise service of Mrs. Frank Burney, Southwide chairman from 1922 through 1938. Contributions to the Burney Gifts are gathered by societies and associations, and forwarded according to state plan to the state W.M.U. treasurer or to the state Margaret Fund chairman. 

The Elizabeth Lowndes Scholarship Award of $200 under the Margaret Fund is a memorial that honors a former beloved treasurer, Mrs. W. C. Lowndes. It is awarded for the senior year to a Margaret Fund student

distinguished during three preceding college years for high scholastic standing, leadership, personality, and character. 

It is in addition to the regular Margaret Fund scholarship to said student.

The Endowment Fund. This fund was established with the receipts from the sale of the Margaret Home. 

The original amount has been increased by several memorial and endowment gifts. It is invested in U. S. Treasury bonds, and the interest is retained for emergency scholarships. 

These scholarships are granted for four years college work and then if students enter the ministry or plan for missionary service additional scholarships are granted for this professional training.

A large majority of these sons and daughters of our missionaries have dedicated themselves to definite Christian service and many have gone or are preparing to turn to the fields where their parents labor. No richer returns could be had on funds invested in mission work than that invested in the training of these young people. 

Until 1938 Margaret Fund scholarships were granted to children of regularly appointed foreign missionaries and those home missionaries who resided outside the United States. It was voted in annual meeting of 1937 to discontinue state apportionments to the Margaret Fund; that certain amounts be designated in the Lottie Moon Christmas Offering and Annie Armstrong Offering for the

Margaret Fund scholarships for the foreign missionaries' children and all regularly appointed missionaries of the 

Home Mission Board. 

Each society, association and state is urged to emphasize the importance of this work and have it presented on the program.

HIGH LIGHTS IN THE HISTORY OF W.M.U. 25 The W.M.U. Training School 

The establishment of the Training School is another link in the chain of evidence that God from the beginning had a great mission for Woman's Missionary Union. 

Just a year after Woman's Missionary Union was organized an appeal for trained helpers came from Dr. E. Z. 

Simmons, a veteran missionary to China. "For years," said he, "I have dreamed of trained young women coming to help me. . . . Those who have come, readily learn the language, but they do not know how to teach the Bible for they do not know it themselves." He pled for a school to train women who were called to missionary service as the Seminary trained the young men. 

In 1904 four young women, Misses Alice Huey, Ala., Clemmie Ford, Tenn., Rena Groover, Ga., and Ella Jeter, Okla., came to Louisville to enter the Seminary. None of the four knew the others were coming nor were they sure of being permitted to enrol in the classes when they arrived.

They had become acquainted after seeing one another in classes and had finally arranged to room together in a low-ceilinged upstairs part of a house occupied by a sympathetic Seminary student and his wife. Here by co-operative housekeeping and cooking they reduced their living expenses to a minimum. When their presence and living condition became known by the Baptist women of Louisville, Miss Eliza Broadus was sent to tell the young women that they, the women of Louisville, wanted to provide a suitable home for them. The girls were not very eager to accept the proffered help as they thought it would increase their living expenses. Not wishing to seem ungrateful, however, they decided to ask Dr. W. O. Carver's advice. When he told them that the enrolment of other young women in the future might depend upon their decision they gladly accepted the generous offer.

As more women students sought admission into seminary classes they were cordially received by the faculty. 

The Louisville women continued to provide the home with the help of other friends of the movement until Southern W.M.U. decided in 1907 to adopt the school as her own. 

Here we see the call of need from foreign fields; the four young women, simultaneously hearing the call, seeking training for the task and the preparation of W.M.U. to accept the responsibility of this new venture which was destined to become a mighty factor in missionary endeavor. Surely Divine Providence was directing these events, timing them to fit each one into the glorious design of an exquisite mosaic of service.

The W.M.U. was only nineteen years old and had not a penny to start such an undertaking but Providence was leading. What else was there to do?

The First Home 

A home must be found. A committee was appointed. 

Dr. J. M. Frost, secretary of the Sunday School Board, was on the committee. After diligent search the property at 334 E. Broadway was selected. A purchase contract for $20,500 was made, most of which would have to be borrowed. 

One night Dr. Frost was deeply impressed in a dream that the Sunday School Board should give the amount needed to buy this property. Three times this dream, clear as a vision, came to him. Convinced that it was of the 

Lord he presented the matter to the Sunday School Board and a check for the full amount was sent to Miss Heck, the president of W.M.U. So, encouraged by this magnificent gift, the W.M.U. gave $20,000 to set up an endowment fund after they had furnished and equipped the building, and raised $3,000 for current expenses. Soon thereafter they started an enlargement fund.

HIGH LIGHTS IN THE HISTORY OF W.M.U. 27 The First Principal 

Here again was divine guidance manifested in the choice of Mrs. Maud R. McLure as first principal. What a tower of strength she was! So outstanding was her leadership that her name among Southern Baptists will ever be associated with missionary education. The indelible imprint of her personality was left not only upon the lives of all those who attended the school during her eighteen years of service as principal but upon all those whose lives they touch.

The Building Programs 

House Beautiful, 334 E. Broadway

By 1916 the school had outgrown its quarters. A new building was erected at a cost of $150,000. This was such a huge sum to the women of that day they literally gasped when it was presented to the annual meeting for approval. 

Nevertheless the motion was made and adopted and every penny was paid by the time the building was completed. 

On May 22, 1918, House Beautiful was opened for its first commencement.

The New House Beautiful, 2801 Lexington Road 

In 1925 the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary moved to a beautiful suburban location on Lexington Road where it erected spacious buildings calculated to care for its expanding life. Since it did not then seem advisable to move the Training School a system was worked out for the Seminary professors to meet classes in the Training School while a number of students journeyed to the Seminary for classes not available at the downtown location. 

Although there was the best possible co-operation, the arrangement became an increasing burden to teachers and students. From the widely scattered alumnae and from 

W.M.U. leaders there began agitation to move to a place

Management and Support 

The management of the school is in the hands of a representative board of trustees, composed of the president, executive secretary and treasurer of Southern W.M.U.; one member from each state, three trustees at large and six resident members, all of whom are elected annually; and an advisory board is composed of the executive secretaries of the Foreign Mission Board, the Home Mission Board and the Sunday School Board, the president of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary and one member of its faculty and two laymen residents of Louisville. This advisory board renders invaluable help to the administration in business and professional matters.

The school is supported almost entirely by gifts of W.M.U. members. The states accept an annual apportionment for current expenses. This is supplemented by interest on invested fund and a small allowance from the 

Co-operative Program.

In the thirty-six years of the school's existence more than two hundred young women have gone from it to the far corners of earth holding high the banner of the Cross, while two thousand and more are engaged in all phases of denominational work here in the homeland.

8. The Hundred Thousand Club 

In 1933, the "zero hour" for Southern Baptists financially, the hindering, humiliating debt of approximately $6,000,000 pressed down upon the Convention meeting in 

Washington, D. C., like a pall. Co-operative Program receipts had so decreased that not even the interest could be paid and the work carried on. Certain creditors were present to see what Southern Baptists would do about their debts.

The Executive Committee and leaders of the Southern 

Baptist Convention and Woman's Missionary Union met together for prayer and consultation, continuing until the

wee small hours. Finally the Hundred Thousand Club plan was adopted. The plan was to secure as speedily as possible 100,000 members who would each give $1.00 per month over-and-above their regular church contributions. 

Had the 100,000 members been secured, the debts would have been paid in five years. Even so, the Hundred Thousand Club was a life-saver, for Baptist credit was saved and morale restored. The boards and institutions of the 

Southern Baptist Convention began to feel the surge of new life and hope. "From the very beginning," Dr. J. E. Dillard, promotional secretary, S.B.C., says, "the Woman's Missionary 

Union has rendered great and continuous help." 

By 1940 the debt had been reduced to approximately $3,000,000. The whole Convention was thrilled when 

W.M.U. announced it would undertake to raise one-third of that amount by 1945, the centennial year. An intensive campaign was launched immediately for memberships, ranging from centennial memberships of $100, down to tiny group memberships where even ten little Sunbeams giving ten cents each per month, could have a membership in the Hundred Thousand Club. While most of the states adopted this plan a few had other debt paying plans. All entered heartily into the debt paying effort and the money began to roll in. By April, 1943, the Foreign Mission 

Board made the final payment on its debt and in May the Home Mission Board redeemed its last note, leaving a total of slightly more than a half million dollars which was paid in full by the end of 1943.

Baptist Women and the Baptist World Alliance 

The ever-widening circles of influence and participation in denominational and world affairs may be seen in the following account given by Mrs. F. W. Armstrong president of W.M.U., who has the distinction of attending two 

Baptist World Alliance meetings, one in Europe and one in America, during her presidency of Southern W.M.U. 

She, better than anyone else, can help us see the vast opportunities for service that awaits us in the future to strengthen the bonds of Christian fellowship the world. around.

The Baptist World Alliance is a voluntary fellowship of those "of like faith and order" in sixty-nine countries of the world. Meetings are held every fifth year though this cycle has been broken now and then by world condi-

tions which made travel hazardous or wholly impossible. 

This is the largest free church fellowship in the world representing more than twelve million members with an even larger constituency since Baptists number only those who are "baptized believers."

In each session of the Alliance the work of its women membership has been recognized, a woman's session serving to bring into closer fellowship and sympathetic understanding the Baptist women of the world. From such meetings there has grown a desire for wider fellowship with groups not represented in these meetings. The result of this desire has found expression in the woman's committee, composed of the five women members of the Executive Committee of the Alliance. Following the great 

1939 meeting of the Alliance in Atlanta this committee began to seek corresponding members in each of the fellowshiping countries. Twenty-one such members had been secured when the present World War made further activity impossible. It was expected that a newssheet would be issued periodically, that each group might have information as to the activities of other groups, that problems and plans would be thus shared, quickening the spirit of prayer one for another and provoking one another to good works. The very consciousness of fellowship broadens the interests of each group, preparing minds and hearts for the day of peace and the part Baptist women will have in the making of a new world. 

Woman's Missionary Societies should use in programs such news and appeals as appear from time to time in the denominational press concerning the Alliance, thus keeping its local constituency informed as to activities and plans, praying ever for those others with whom we have "fellowship in furtherance of the gospel."

The Woman's Missionary Union has long been recognized for its system and efficiency in organization and in methods for promoting its work. 

Beginning in 1888 with 1,206 Women's Missionary So cieties and children's organizations of various names and ages, Woman's Missionary Union gradually developed a unique system of missionary education. January 1, 1943 a total of 40,472 organizations was reported, 12,845 of these were Women's Missionary Societies and 27,627 were young people's organizations. II. WHY EVERY BAPTIST CHURCH SHOULD HAVE A FULL-GRADED UNION 

There are many reasons why every Baptist church should have a Full-Graded 'Union. First of all because—

Missions Is Our Mission 

The church's mission, every individual Christian's mission. Not being a regularly appointed missionary releases no one from the obligation to "go into all the world and preach the gospel to every creature." They will not go if they do not know, neither will they pray, nor give, nor serve. Therefore, our people should be indoctrinated with the explicit teachings of the Scripture concerning missions. 

Their eyes must be opened to see and their hearts to feel the need for the gospel all around them and to the uttermost part. They must be led to face realistically their individual responsibility to go in person at home and if God does not call them to a foreign field, they must go through their prayers and their gifts.

Woman's Missionary Union Tries to Meet This

Bible and on the mission fields.

Through its program of prayer. Believing that intercession is the greatest work any Christian can do, W.M.U. places prayer first in her chief aims.

The inspiration and knowledge obtained from the missionary programs and literature kindle the desire to pray, teaches what to pray for and reveal the fact that great pray-ers are as necessary to kingdom work as great missionaries.

III. ORGANIZING NEW SOCIETIES 

Considering what a W.M.U. can do for her church and the fact that nearly half the southern Baptist churches have no W.M.U. organization in them, we readily see what responsibility rests upon every society to become a missionary to her unorganized sister church. 

Much as it would mean to the missionary enterprise to double our activities and gifts, that should not be the primary motive in our going forth to organize. The spiritual enrichment and development of individual lives, so far-reaching in their influence, outweigh any value that can be computed in numbers. 

It should be the aim of each society to see that every church around her has, at least, a W.M.S. and a Sunbeam 

Band and a Full-Graded Union as soon as possible. The following suggestions may be helpful:

Preparing for Organization 

Contact should be made through the pastor or key women in the church, friendly relations established, a meeting arranged where W.M.U. and mission work can be presented. In country churches invite everybody to come, they will thoroughly enjoy the meeting.

The associational superintendent or W.M.U. field worker might be called on to assist; it is not necessary, however.

Perfecting the Organization

Prepare a brief program which presents missions so attractively that every man, woman, and child present will want to have a part in promoting it. 

Show how easily it can be done through W.M.U. organizations. Many country churches have a Full- 

Graded Union. Some meet at the church on the regular preaching day afternoon when the pastor and his wife can help with the leadership. Others meet some night during the week when most convenient for all to come. 

A motion to organize should be made and discussed by those present. All who are willing to become members should signify it. Then vote to perfect the organization. 

A committee may be appointed to nominate officers. 

The duties of each officer should be clearly understood and care exercised in choice of the person best qualified to fill each office. Mistakes made here will not only affect the success of the present effort but will hinder any future effort to organize. Decide on time and place for meeting. 

Have the secretary notify the state W.M.U. headquarters immediately, giving names and addresses of the officers of the new society and the counselor of each junior organization. Helps will be sent to them at once and additional materials will be mailed periodically. 

The Follow-Up Work 

Keep in close contact with leaders until the organization is perfected, the leaders trained, the program of activities thoroughly understood and necessary working materials provided. Take subscriptions for Royal Service, also for The Window of Y.W.A. and World Comrades if the young people's groups are organized. 

Those who go out to organize new societies should realize it cannot be done by stirring up a little interest in missions, by calling a few women together, electing some

IV. CIRCLE PLAN 

The circle plan has proved to be the best method for enlisting and training all women members of the church in missionary work through membership in the Missionary Society. It can be used just as effectively in the rural church, where the membership is scattered, as in the town or city churches. Some rural churches use the "Group 

Plan" which may seem a little less complex than the circle. Organization

If there are fifteen or twenty members in the society and a number of unenlisted women in the church, at least two circles should be formed. The president and first vice-president should take the initiative. Plan for as many circles as needed if all women of the church were enlisted; allow for growth, by assigning only eight or ten members to each circle. 

Choosing a chairman

Care should be exercised in the choice of a chairman for she is to the circle what the president is to the whole society. In the circle is where the real work of the society and the training of members are done. The chairman

should be named by the nominating committee and elected by the society or chosen by the Executive Committee. 

Officers 

The circle officers correspond to those of the W.M.S., a chairman and three co-chairmen whose duties are the same as the society president and three vice-presidents, a secretary, treasurer, mission study, stewardship, and community missions chairmen, and others as needed to promote all phases of W.M.U. work in the circle. They also serve on the various standing committees of the society. 

Written reports should be made monthly to their corresponding society chairman.

Grouping Membership 

Local conditions largely determine the method of grouping. The plan should be varied from time to time. "Our best becomes second best with constant use." 

Neighborhood circles form a natural division.

The circle leaders may draw alternately after dividing the members according to talent, ability, leadership, members with cars, and so on, so that equal distribution is made to all circles. 

Avoid any plan that might cause cliques. Rarely is it wise to have a brides or young matrons circle. A time limit of membership should be set if used.

Activities of the Circle Meetings 

At least two meetings a month should be held alternating with the business and missionary meetings of the society, if these are held at different times. Every circle should try to have 100% attendance at monthly missionary program meeting of the society as well as at circle meetings.

V. BUSINESS WOMEN'S CIRCLES The Importance of Enlisting Business Women 

Enlisting the tremendous unused power of business and professional women in Southern Baptist churches today, linking them with the greatest business enterprise in the world, presents an imperative challenge to the Woman's Missionary Union. Many of the finest Christian women we have are business women. 

It is estimated that one-third of our women today are gainfully employed outside the home. There are the school teachers, professional women, office secretaries, clerks, stenographers, telephone, and beauty operators, welfare workers, nurses, industrial workers, and hosts of others! Their experience and contact with life in all its phases; their efficient, systematic and businesslike habits make them a wonderful asset to our missionary work. 

They are accustomed to undertaking big assignments and seeing them through. If the fine abilities of these capable women were captured and consecrated to the cause of 

Christ what a powerful impact could be made upon the world, the business world, the social world, and the cause of missions around the world.

VI. THE STANDARD OF EXCELLENCE The Standard Defined 

The Standard of Excellence is simply a measuring rod for the society by which it should lay out its plan of work and gauge its efficiency. It should never be regarded as an end in itself but simply a means to an end. 

No society, large or small, should think it is too large or too small to reach the Standard. The size and strength of the society is not the determining factor; it depends upon the leaders' attitude toward the Standard. If the leaders look upon it as something to be attained for recognition, just to be able to say they are A-1, the society will take that attitude. But if the officers and chairmen regard it as a practical plan that will enable the society to do Standard Work, and so present it to the members, they will feel its importance and strive to attain it. 

The Standard really represents the minimum of attainment for a missionary society, rather than the maximum. 

No society should be content just to meet these requirements, nor will it be if Christ's standard, "Be ye perfect," is the real goal for which it strives. 

Why Every Society Should Strive to Attain the 

Standard

It presents challenging goals in each phase of the work.

It outlines a practical program of activities for every society.

It keeps the aim of the W.M.U. constantly before the members.

It prevents careless and slipshod planning. 

It prevents overemphasizing some things and underdoing other things.

It stimulates the officers to their best endeavor.

It gives satisfaction of heart to know that your society has done "Standard work" for the Master. 

It inspires courage to undertake greater things in the future.

Steps Necessary 

The Standard should be thoroughly explained to all officers in Executive Committee meeting early in January. Each one should know what points she and her helpers will be responsible for reaching.

It should be adopted as the society's goal for the year and all should pledge themselves to help attain it. 

A Standard of Excellence chart should be purchased from W.M.U. headquarters, and the most attractive way of marking it should be decided upon. 

Marking it should be "featured" at each monthly business meeting, and recognition given to the officer and committee having the most outstanding report. 

Who Is Responsible for the Standard?

The ten point Standard of Excellence is not reached by sudden roundups. It is a year-around task. It is not the responsibility of the president alone, but every member of the Executive Committee shares this responsibility.

CHAPTER III  I. IMPORTANCE OF CHOOSING RIGHT LEAD- 

ERS The success of any organization depends largely upon its leaders. Every society, old or new, would do well to give time, earnest thought, and prayerful consideration to the important matter of choosing and training its officers and committees.

The rank and file seldom, if ever, advance beyond their leaders in spirituality, vision or courageous achievement.

Surely the need was never so urgent, nor the reward so great for those who really love the Lord to devote time and talent to his service.

Any woman should feel it a great honor to be chosen president of a missionary society. She and all the officers and committee chairmen should accept the responsibility as a sacred trust-one that not only deserves but demands their sincerest loyalty, their most careful preparation and their best effort in the performance of these duties. 

A Danger Signal

Any society that finds among its members a growing unwillingness to accept the responsibility of leadership should recognize this as a danger signal and begin immediately to correct such a condition. Usually it can be attributed to one or more of the following causes: 

Lack of spirituality

Manifested by an unwillingness to put first things first, so concerned about the "many things" of life that "the better part" is neglected. [69]

Lack of vision

Ignorance of world conditions and its need for the gospel; indifference to their individual responsibility as Christians to help meet these needs produces a spiritual blindness and unconcern that makes missionary leadership impossible. 

Lack of training

A woman may be interested in the cause of missions and willing to give of her means and service to the Lord, but if she is untrained she naturally hesitates to accept the responsibility of leadership. 

These conditions may be overcome by a wise leader without great difficulty 

First, she will plan prayerfully that a spiritual atmosphere may pervade each meeting; then patiently and persistently impress the fact that "we are not our own”we are commanded to "seek first the kingdom of God." 

Second, she will realize that missionary information must not only be presented attractively but must also arouse a feeling of personal responsibility and stimulate a desire on the part of all to do more, pray more, and give more that the "kingdoms of this world may become the kingdom of our Lord and his Christ." 

Third, she will give leadership training a prominent and permanent place in her program of activities. Any woman, whose heart has been warmed in the spiritual atmosphere of a missionary meeting, who has a vision of world needs and has seen how she as an individual can help meet those needs, will be anxious to fit herself for service. To have a group regularly studying the Year Book, the manuals, the missionary periodicals, state and Southern W.M.U. minutes and material which may be had for the asking, will make it possible for the society to carry on

a class in leadership training the year around. The auxiliary leaders and prospective leaders would find the Correspondence Course wonderfully helpful and interesting.

IV. THE COMMITTEES AND THEIR DUTIES The Importance of Committee Work 

It can truly be said, no society is stronger than its committees and the committee no stronger than its chairman. 

Therefore, the importance of wisely choosing committee members is self-evident. Each committee must specialize in and promote its phase of work. To do this, regular monthly meetings are necessary for study and for planning the work of the society. All available material for information and ideas should be used and the most effective methods adopted to accomplish the work. 

The chairman of each committee should have a looseleaf notebook, to keep suggestions from Royal Service and W.M.U. Year Book, leaflets; articles, and so on, from every source on her particular phase of work. The notebook should contain list of her committee members, plans and goals for the year and copies of monthly reports. An envelope may be pasted on the inside cover of notebook to hold clippings, pamphlets, and so on. This notebook should be passed on to the chairman's successor. 

The Committees a Society Should Have

The "average" society should have the following standing committees: executive, enlistment, program, young people, literature, publicity, stewardship, mission study,

and community missions. The nominating committee should also be a standing committee.

Large societies will need other committees, such as, social, library, Training School, white cross, benevolence, and so on. Smaller societies will, of course, have to combine the work and have even fewer committees. 

Where the circle plan is used the standing committees shall be composed of a member from each circle, with a chairman, appointed either by the president, or by one who assumes responsibility by virtue of her office. 

The Nominating Committee 

This is perhaps the most important committee the society has to choose, because the responsibility of selecting the officers who direct every phase of the work for a whole year rests upon it. It should be retained throughout the year so that vacancies occuring during the year may be filled more efficiently and without delay.

As a rule the nominating committee is selected by nominations from the floor and election by voice;¹ if there are circles, each circle should be asked to nominate one member for this committee.

The committee should be elected early in the year, preferably in July, certainly not later than September. 

They should meet and prayerfully consider the duties of each office selecting the best talent in the society for each. 

They should consult with those selected and secure their consent to the presentation of their names. The committee reports at the October meeting, when election takes place. 

This gives the officers for the coming year three months to plan their work before taking office on January first. 

The Southern W.M.U. year runs from January to

January. Local societies will avoid much confusion if they too measure their work by the calendar year.

CHAPTER IV  

Take this child away, and nurse it for me, and I will give thee thy wages.-EXODUS 2:9 I. THE IMPORTANCE OF YOUNG PEOPLE TO KINGDOM WORK 

What more wonderful or significant words could have been spoken to an anxious mother's heart than those of the Egyptian princess to the mother of the baby Moses?

Jochebed must have believed that in saving his life, God had a purpose and a great mission for her son to fulfil. 

And she was given the privilege of preparing him for it! 

That was reward enough, had the princess known it she need not have offered other wages. The time was short. 

There was much to do. How quickly, how carefully, how faithfully she must teach and train him. The destiny of 

God's Chosen People was wrapped up in that little babe. 

Is it different now? The destiny of the world is wrapped up in the youth of today, and they are in our care. The time is very short, the work is hard, but the reward is great to those who are willing to dedicate themselves to the task of leading young people to understand and to fulfil their God-given mission of giving the gospel of peace and salvation to a sin-cursed, weary world that needs it so.

The prophetic words of Miss Heck in her annual message of 1893 have come true in our generation: “Teach that child to think God's thoughts for the nations, ere the clash of nations dull his ear to the calling Father's voice."

What grave responsibility rests upon Woman's Mis sionary Union to give missionary education to the young [99]

people of our churches. It is they who must build that "better world of tomorrow" if it is to be, and they must build it with the tools and material which we have placed in their hands.

Until our women realize that in the modern strategy of missions the key position is held by youth, and rise to their responsibility of giving the missionary education needed to fit them for their task, Woman's Missionary Union cannot fulfil her greatest mission. 

Miss Mary Webb understood the value of children to missions and organized a "Children's Cent Society" which, in 1811, contributed $27.00 to missions. Dr. George B. 

Taylor recognized the importance of children to the missionary program and started the Sunbeam Band in his country church in Virginia. During the six years of his leadership many other Bands were organized and all together gave $25,000 to missions. This indicates their monetary value but who could measure the influence upon their young lives and their worth to the missionary enterprise down through the years and even to the ends of the earth?

As time went on children outgrew the Sunbeam Band. 

The quaint minutes of a Georgia Sunbeam Band dated January 26, 1890, record that "six larger members withdrew to join the Young Ladies' Mission Band." There were even Sunbeam Bands in colleges! It soon became `evident that a uniform name and plan of organization were necessary for each age group in order to meet the needs of growing youth and conserve the best interests of missions.

II. THE FULL-GRADED UNION 

Many of our Women's Missionary Societies have seen the importance of young people's work. We rejoice to know that 2,955 Southern Baptist churches maintained a

Full-Graded Union in 1942. Was your church among that number? Could it have been? Of the 12,845 Women's Missionary Societies reported in 1942 there were at least 3,148 with no Girls' Auxiliaries for their young daughters; 5,953 had no Sunbeam Band for their little children; 6,966 of them gave no missionary education to their sons; and 7,676 failed to foster a Young Woman's 

Auxiliary. Children neglected, with no missionary education, will find it difficult to overcome racial prejudices and provincial attitudes and outlook. Instead of happy meetings where habits of world thinking and interests were developed they will always have wasted years to remember. 

The Organizations Comprising the Full-Graded 

Union Beginning with a few Sunbeam Bands in 1896 the Woman's Missionary Union has developed a fully graded system of missionary education for the women and young people of our Baptist churches.

In 1942 little children from four to nine years of age were letting their light shine in 6,892 Sunbeam Bands; girls from nine to thirteen and thirteen to sixteen were studying missions together in 9,687 Junior and Intermediate Girls' Auxiliaries; boys of those same ages were learning to be true ambassadors for their King in 5,879 Junior and Intermediate Royal Ambassador Chapters; while young women from sixteen to twenty-five were finding great inspiration for missionary living in 5,169 Young

Women's Auxiliaries. 

The Y.W.A. includes three branches, namely, the regular church auxiliary, the College or Ann Hasseltine Y.W.A., of which there were 115 in 1942; and the nurses' or Grace McBride Y.W.A., numbering 34.

A Distinctive Program of Missionary Education W.M.U. Specializes in Missions. Missions is the objective of all W.M.U. work. Missionary programs of world-wide interest coupled with fascinating and purposeful handwork, intensive mission study periods, using approved texts; practical missionary activities in the community which enable the young people to give expression to the impulse to serve in Christ's name and for his sake.

As members of these organizations the young people may attend camps, house parties, city-wide and associational rallies, Royal Ambassador conclaves, and other meetings where they hear missionary speakers, state and Southwide denominational workers whose messages extend the horizons of these young people and link them up in a very personal way with Christian work around the world. 

W.M.U. Provides a Weekday Program of Religious 

Activity. To commit all religious and denominational training to Sunday services encourages the idea that religion is a "Sunday business." By having the missionary education program and activities during the week young people are impressed with the fact that every day is God's day for kingdom building. 

Weekday programs enable children and young people to combine study with play, a very important factor in a child's life, by means of dramatizations, missionary games and projects, contests, songs, and so on. It also teaches them to couple work with worship, through community missions activities, thus making missions a real and personal experience to them and religion an every day business.

W.M.U. Teaches and Trains in Stewardship. Woman's 

Missionary Union has been constantly mindful of the necessity for teaching children the principles of stewardship. The plans for educating young people in stewardship of possessions have developed through the years,

leading them to memorize Scriptures, to study the teachings of God's word regarding the tithe, to work out original debates, plays, posters and projects on stewardship. These plans are climaxed with a public 

Church Night of Stewardship which has met with an enthusiastic response from pastors and young people alike. 

We rejoice over the 39,581 tithers reported in 1942, among young people. This is one in eight members in W.M.U. young people's organizations. 

W.M.U. Emphasizes Prayer. Great emphasis is laid upon prayer in each of the auxiliaries. Sunbeam children are taught to love and pray for all the children of the world. The Sunbeams, G.A's., and R.A's. eagerly follow the "Pray Day by Day" page in World Comrades magazine, remembering the missionaries even if it is simply adding another name to the list at the close of "Now I lay me down to sleep. (That is when mothers and counselors are wise enough to provide the magazine for them and lead them to include the whole world in their prayers.) ... 99 The habit of daily prayer is established in the life of the Y.W.A. member who comes up through Sunbeam

The fields of work and the names of Southern Baptist missionaries around the world have become so familiar to her they seem like personal friends when she hears them speak or sees their names in print. III. FOSTERING THE FULL-GRADED UNION The Meaning of Fostering 

How appropriate is the term chosen by the W.M.U. to express her relationship to the auxiliary organizations. "To foster" means to nourish, sustain, or support; to cherish; to keep with care. Much faithful, loving, needful service is implied in the word "fostering."

Three things are necessary for adequate fostering. First, the Woman's Missionary Society should magnify the work of missionary education for the young people until it is recognized by all members as a major activity of the society, and by the church as vitally necessary to its missionary life. Second, dignify the work by selecting the most capable, consecrated, and dependable leaders the society affords. The strategy of youth demands sustained leadership. Older organizations can survive frequent changes of counselors but the younger ones languish and die without adequate faithful leadership. Third, support the work and the workers with interest, co-operation, money, and prayers. A sufficient amount to take care of the materials and equipment needed by counselors in each organization should be included in the annual budget of every missionary society or church. 

Fostering in the Society

The W.M.S. cannot afford to be content with appointing a young people's director, the several counselors necessary, and then forget them. Even though they are willing to serve to the uttermost, the entire society should share in the responsibility of fostering. IV. LEADERSHIP 

The missionary education of young people of the church is so important none but the best leaders should be entrusted with the responsibility. No officer of W.M.U. should be more prayerfully and more carefully chosen than the young people's director, and the counselors of the various auxiliaries. The society should not wait until all of the officers, circle leaders, and committee members are chosen and then get the young people's leaders from those who are left, but rather select those who are specially fitted to do young people's work first. 

The Young People's Director 

The W.M.U. young people's director-the connecting link between the young people's organization and the W.M.S.-should be a woman of uncompromising Christian character, one who loves and understands young

people, is thoroughly missionary in spirit. She will need executive ability and tact in order to guide and direct the work of the counselors wisely and well.

No director can do efficient work until she has made a thorough study of organizational manuals and the W.M.U.

VI. SPECIAL ACTIVITIES 

Camps, House Parties, Conclaves, and so on.

For years Woman's Missionary Union has been promoting camps, house parties, and conclaves which have brought recreation and fellowship along with inspiration to Christian living and increased missionary zeal to tens of thousands of her young people. Last year (1942) in spite of all the limitations of war times, there were at least 26,455 boys and girls in such gatherings; by seashore, in mountains, on college campuses, or wherever state or district W.M.U. young people's leaders found choice facilities for them. Boys gathered in Royal Ambassador camps or conclaves; girls and young women in house parties and camps. Missionaries were present, missionary talks were made, mission study classes taught, handcraft hours were provided with expertly directed recreation and carefully disciplined rest hours and sleep at night. Much lasting good has already resulted from these camps and much more will follow as the young people converted in these services grow in Christian graces; as those who volunteered for definite Christian service continue to follow the Lord's leadership here and there throughout the world and as others develop into missionary-hearted adults feeling a sense of world responsibility. 

The climax of camp life is reached in the Southwide 

Y.W.A. camp held annually at Ridgecrest, N. C. During these twenty years fully 150,000 of the choice young women of the South have come apart to that scenic spot so charged with spiritual atmosphere that their lives have

been greatly influenced by contact with notable Christians and denominational leaders. The missionaries and guests from other lands give reality to the call and urgent needs of missions, lifting their vision and fixing the life purposes of these young women. From these mountaintop experiences they have gone back to revitalize the churches from which they came.

The results of camps, house parties, and so on, are seen in more vigorous organizational activity, increase in gifts, large numbers of conversions and dedications to definite Christian service. The books studied at camp are taught in the local auxiliary. So rich are the returns from this summer activity that several states have purchased lovely camp sites and others are planning to do so as soon as conditions make it possible to erect the buildings necessary.

The Ranking System and Forward Steps The Ranking System for Royal Ambassadors and For ward Steps for Girls' Auxiliary members present a challenging form of activity which is interesting an increasing number of boys and girls from year to year. It provides a plan for recognition of individual effort in memorizing missionary Scripture passages, learning much about missionary and general denominational life, reading many books of missionary biographies, stories, and so on. 

The Ranking System will develop the member in the ideals of the order, the study of Bible, history of his own church as well as Southern Baptists' missionary program. 

The first three ranks, Page, Squire, and Knight savor of

King Arthur and his Round Table. In them the boy is crusading for his King. The other three, Ambassador, Ambassador Extraordinary and Ambassador Plenipotentiary, employ terms used in diplomatic service and the boy is a representative of his King. Wherever you find boys advancing in these ranks you find a wide-awake chapter. 

The Forward Steps are never-forgotten mile stones in the life of a girl as she progresses from Maiden to Ladyin-Waiting to Princess, then on to Queen, Queen with

Scepter, and finally Queen Regent. The many coronation ceremonies which take place at G.A. house parties, camps, and so on, all over the Southland indicate the popularity of this phase of work among the girls. It is wonderful to see the earnestness and enthusiasm with which a Queen strives to become Queen Regent, developing real queenliness in heart and life as each task is completed. 

Articles in World Comrades and the testimonies of boys and girls are evidences of the great value in character building this work is.

The reason, no doubt, why so many adult Christians are inactive, indifferent, and unconcerned today is because they did not have such training in their early Christian life.

Sunbeam Babies

For years Woman's Missionary Union has neglected to include babies in their plans for the missionary education of young people. In the Golden Jubilee year, 1938, the old Sunbeam baby plans used from 1889 to 1892 were revived and enhanced a bit.

The plan calls for a carefully chosen Sunbeam baby visitor, who will visit the mothers of new babies and little children. Too often the young mother is out of touch with her missionary society except for the contacts with the Sunbeam baby visitor and the missionary atmosphere of the home is so vital to the child's development. There is a lovely prayer card to be presented to the mother soon after the baby's birth. Then on the first birthday a beautiful printed letter is to be sent; on the second birthday the Girls' Auxiliary will have a Sunday scrapbook for the child; on the third birthday provide a world globe bank (from Ten Cent Store) and begin teaching the child the joy of giving. I. THE LEADERSHIP PROBLEM A Church Problem 

Perhaps the greatest, certainly the most prevalent, problem confronting churches today is the lack of leaders. It is the hue and cry everywhere, in the big church as well as the small one, in other departments as well as W.M.U. 

So vital is leadership to the whole church program that something more should be done than talking or complaining about it when election time comes around each year. 

It is too late then; the faithful few will just have to go on doing three or four people's work. Why should a church that has spent time, effort and money on training its young people for a decade or more have such a dearth of leaders? Something is wrong somewhere when leaders cannot be found to do the Lord's work. First discover the cause and the remedy will not be hard to find. 

Is it timidity, ignorance, or laziness on the part of the individual, or, the lack of systematic, purposeful training by the departments of the church? The training of a [125]

constant supply of workers should be a major objective in every church program. 

Of course the W.M.U. cannot solve the problem alone, but it could encourage the church officers to study the situation and adopt some method by which it could be solved so each department would have sufficient leaders, well trained leaders.

How Some Have Solved It in W.M.U. 

One society president says "we try to develop the spiritual life of all our members so the Holy Spirit can lead them to accept responsibility of leadership." "The circle," says another, "is where we discover and train our leaders. All those who have leadership abilities are encouraged to attend W.M.U. conventions, district, and associational meetings." "We use the 'Commitment of Self' cards at the beginning of the year and arrange special study groups for those expressing interest in the various phases of work." 

A copy of the "Commitment of Self" cards, used by the societies in Virginia, is given on page 127 as a model for any society that would like to adopt this method. A special dedication service, led by the pastor, may be held before the election of officers and the women asked to check the items they would be willing to do if called upon by the president or the nominating committee of the society. 

Do not be discouraged if the newly discovered leaders do not measure up to the ideal standard all at once. They will have to be trained in the school of experience. "We learn to do by doing." Let us use them else we will lose them.

How One Society Prepares Programs 

At a divisional W.M.U. clinic in Mississippi the W.M.S. of Newton, Miss., gave a demonstration of how they prepare and present their monthly missionary programs. It was such a splendid example it could be used as a model for many societies to follow. The story was obtained from the president and program chairman, to pass on to you. What the Newton W.M.S. has done can be done by almost any group of women in Southern W.M.U. for 

Newton is just a small town having only one Baptist church with a resident membership of 450. But let Mrs. 

Armstrong tell the story in her own way:

How We Prepare Our Missionary Programs 

Before we began giving special emphasis to our monthly missionary programs three years ago, ours was the "ebb and flow" kind of society. I've been a member of this society for twenty years. We tried various ways of having the Royal Service programs. We got into the rut of having it presented by circles in turn. Often the attendance at the meetings was made up mainly by members of the circle in charge of the program that month. 

The Program Committee 

When Mrs. White was asked to serve as program chairman she agreed on condition that I be her assistant, so you see it takes both of us to be the second vice-president. 

We had worked together in W.C.T.U. as program committee for two years and in other capacities in the W.M.S.

We had an ideal president who encouraged us and a loyal group of women to work with. But the thing that really inspired us was an article in the W.M.U. Year Book which we obtained right after we were elected. This article, The Enlisting Power of Programs, raised the question "How can we make programs live?" That was what we wanted to do, so we tried to follow every suggestion the author gave, for we felt they would work. 

SEVEN SUGGESTIONS 

1. The program chairman must be willing to give herself to the task. Adequate preparation must precede acceptable performance. The more invested the greater the returns. 

2. Use as many people as possible, in this way talent is discovered and developed and Christian character is formed. 

3. Use originality, variety, costumes, posters, placards, dramatization to add interest and color to the program. 

4. Enrich the program with poetry and music. 

5. The most essential element is prayer, definite prayer all during the preparation as well as in the presentation of the program. 

6. Remember that time is a precious commodity to worth-while people, therefore, do not waste anyone's time. Begin on time, close on time. 

7. Never forget, only living things grow. Make your program live.

I have made posters and placards for years, still I'm not very good at it. They can be read, however, and that's what counts. I use letters cut from construction paper and paste them on crepe paper streamers or posters just

as hundreds of women are doing everywhere, I suppose. 

Nothing new or difficult about that.

Getting Women to Take Part on Programs 

The way we got the women to take part on the program was by giving them short easy parts at first. A timid woman afraid of her voice will wear a robe and carry a placard long before she will get the courage to accept a speaking part. But doing even that much makes her feel she helped to put on a splendid program. 

We study the program material just as soon as Royal Service comes, talk it over together, decide on the most interesting facts to be presented. Instead of giving a whole topic to one woman we often break it up into several parts. 

For instance in our next program, I see where four women could assist in making the first topic, "Lights Within 

America", interesting the leader gives the first paragraph, then two women (in white robes) enter carrying placards cut in lamp shape with lighted words Religion and Liberty on them, they give the next two paragraphs. Then two other women with a streamer bearing these words, "Where the spirit of the Lord is there is liberty", come in and the leader gives the last paragraph of that topic. Thus five women are used instead of one and each feels she has made a contribution to an impressive program.

III. UNIQUE CIRCLE PLANS 

Many societies have difficulty in changing and increasing the number of circles. Some have had the same number of circles for years. Circles should be kept small to encourage enlistment. As the membership increases the number of circles should be increased. The main object of the circle is to develop and train every member in it. 

This cannot be done when there are more than ten or fifteen women in the circle. 

Honor Circles

This problem was cleverly solved in a certain Mississippi society by its ingenious president who felt more circles were needed. There were many unenlisted women in the church but many members thought they had enlisted about "all that would do anything", besides they had invited "those other women" many times with no response, why keep on? The president was not satisfied, however. She conceived the idea of an Honor Circle and chose four of the best workers in the society to form the Honor Circle. 

She explained her plan to them, they were enthusiastic about it and said they would like to see what could be done. This circle of four accepted an equal share of the society's obligations and activities. They had to get their

new members from the unenlisted women of the church. 

Not wanting to fall down on their appointed goals, they naturally tried to enlist the most capable women for their circle. It created a lot of interest, new life was injected into the society. It worked! 

It was a much coveted honor to be chosen one of four to begin a new Honor Circle each year. Every year, so far, the Honor Circle has excelled all others in the society and some of the finest workers the society now has were won to active membership through the Honor Circles.

Another Novel Circle Plan 

A visiting speaker attending a missionary meeting of a society in Atlanta saw a unique thing done which greatly impressed all those present. Fully a hundred and fifty women attended the meeting that day.

The circle in charge of the program featured the Girls' Auxiliary which they fostered. As the twentyfive or thirty members of this G.A. faced the audience, the W.M.U. mission study chairman presented the awards for a recently completed mission study class-not every girl received an award. The circle chairman then introduced the G.A. counselor. She in turn introduced 

G.A. members who had completed the requirements for various Forward Steps. One girl from each group outlined the work they had done to become Maidens, Ladiesin-Waiting, Queens, and so on. These girls, dressed in 

G.A. colors with their arm bands and insignia according to rank, made a beautiful picture. 

The climax of the program was when the counselor called the mothers to come from the audience and invest their daughters with the new insignia which they had won. 

Sad to relate, not every mother was present. What regret must have been hers if she had let some flimsy excuse keep her from attending the society meeting that

day! The W.M.U. president took the place of the absent mothers.

A splendid bit of fostering that was! It gave the women a new idea of the value and importance of the work and it gave the girls a stronger incentive to participate in the full program of their organization. 

One wonders what those women thought, whose young daughters were not members of the G.A.

IV. COMMUNITY MISSIONS The Need of the Hour 

A great step forward was taken in the 1942 convention when it was voted to change the name Personal Service to Community Missions. This title links up the work at our door with the whole mission program, putting community missions on the same basis as to type, quality and intensity as that being done on other mission fields. 

The need for genuine Christian living was never so great as today. The paralyzing period of depression through which we have passed, and the great world conflict which now holds us in its grip, have filled the minds and hearts of people everywhere, with fear and hate and revenge, and is undermining the whole social structure of our civilization. Home life and the care and training of children are being disrupted; standards of living and of morals, lowered; religious activities and race relationships are being seriously affected. All of these constitute a challenge to every religious group. 

Are Christian women ready to accept that challenge?

If so, the place to begin is the community around every church in the Southern Baptist Convention. There is nothing wrong with the world but the wrong attitude of its people toward Christ. Today the need supreme is for 

Christians to get a new conception of their task in their own community and go to work.

"No man liveth unto himself" is true of this day and age as of no other in history. By means of radio (and difference in time) we in America hear of events transpiring on European battle fields the day before they take place! The day after shoes were rationed in the United

States the topic of conversation on the streets of London was about the reaction of our people toward shoe rationing!

Do not be deceived, if the world takes cognizance of such a minor matter as shoe rationing, is it not aware of weightier matters also? They know just how Christian 

America really is. A Hindu poet, Tagore, said recently, "as long as lynchings go on in America she has very little brotherhood to import to the Orient."

They, here at home, know also. An Indian chief when approached by a missionary with a Bible, said, "White man had that Book long time. Done white man no good. 

Indian no time to listen." 

As has been said, "If America is ever to be recognized as a truly Christian nation it must be as visibly Christian as Russia is visibly Communist, or Germany visibly Nazi. 

No fainthearted lip service on the part of individual 

Christians will be enough to make America Christian.

Of this we may be sure, we can never transport to any mission field in the world of tomorrow any better missionary program than we have in our own home churches. 

Woman's Missionary Union, through its community missions program has a great opportunity to become a mighty factor in making our nation truly Christian.

When Christian women get this conception of their task and teach the young people to realize that they serve just as truly as those who go across the seas, when they witness faithfully to those round about them, then, and not till then, will America be Christianized. This would strength-

en the hands of missionaries at home and abroad as much as anything that could be done. 

How One Society Did It

In Their Own Community Here is an example of what wide-awake, missionaryhearted women can do when they get a vision of their own community as a mission field. 

COMMUNITY MISSIONS PROGRAM OF GUTHRIE, OKLAHOMA MRS. RALPH CRABBS 

It does not take a large city church to do community missions work. Many of the greatest opportunities lie in reach of small churches.

I will tell you about the community mission work of our society, hoping that it will inspire others to do even greater work for the Master.

We have about 170 women enrolled in the W.M.S. which is divided into circles.

We rented a house in the part of town where many underprivileged people live. We began the work by having a Vacation Bible school. The average daily attendance was sixty-three. After the Bible school closed classes were continued once a week. Later, a class for mothers and a story hour for pre-school age children, were organized, meeting each Thursday. Two members of the W.M.S. volunteer their services, one to teach the 

Sunday school lesson to the mothers (most of them do not attend Sunday school), the other to conduct a story hour for the children. Thirty to forty-five minutes are given to study in the women's class, then we have a sewing project which consists of piecing quilts, making comforts, quilting, mending, and making over clothes for the people

who need them. While the class for mothers is in progress the children enjoy Bible stories, songs and handwork.

Old clothing, quilt scraps, and so on, are donated by the members of our W.M.S. We keep them at the Center where it is handy to give them out to the needy. We have a Christmas party each year for the members of all the classes. We have found all this very interesting, enjoy. able and profitable work.

In Rural Community Nearby 

Our extension work consists of three rural circles and two Bible classes. One of our members superintends this work. She attends all of these meetings each month; teaches the Bible lesson and occasionally a mission study book. She also helps with the Royal Service program. 

The women are eager to follow the whole program of WW.M.U. including the gifts. We find the rural circles are more faithful in attendance than those in town, and one rural circle last year canned two hundred quarts of fruits and vegetables for the Children's Home. 

We have a student from Oklahoma Baptist University doing rural work also. He has four preaching engagements each month at schoolhouses in the country. In the summer a revival meeting is held in each place. 

How do we finance all this? Our preacher-missionary is paid $5.00 a week by the W.M.S. He gets the offering taken at the services, which amounts to anything from a few cents to $10.00 a week. Besides this, the country people give him butter, eggs, meat, and so on, and we assign a circle each week to have a food shower for him. 

When the tire shortage came up we had to make some plan to get our workers to the rural circles and the preacher to his appointments, as individuals could no longer use their cars, so we bought a second-hand car known as the missionary car. Each member was asked for $3.00 which

was much cheaper than using her own car. 

At the end of three months when the note came due we had more than enough to pay it.

The expense of the car, the preacher's salary, and the expense of the Good Will Center is apportioned to the circles, amounting to about $2.50 a month to each circle or less than 20c for each member.

As our women see the results of community missions we are doing, all rejoice in what Jesus is accomplishing through us.

What the Daily Vacation Bible School Means to Colored People 

The Daily Vacation Bible school provides our people with a definite plan for the worthy use of leisure time. 

Although we live in a Christian nation with Bibles in almost every home, obviously the practical application of its teachings is sorely neglected. Not only is the application lacking, but we need a fundamental knowledge of the 

Bible which the Vacation Bible school gives.

Our churches, for the most part, do not fully realize the importance of such schools. You, who have the light 

I should share it with others today. The Vacation Bible school lays the foundation for Bible study and the formation of correct habits. It opens the door to God's treas-

Weekday Bible Schools 

Several state W.M.U. organizations now employ a full time Negro missionary who works under the supervision of the state W.M.U. secretary and in co-operation with the Negro leadership of the different conventions in her territory.

An outstanding example of this type of service is that of a Negro missionary employed by the Louisiana W.M.U. to work chiefly among the Negroes of New Orleans. What this woman did in the big, cosmopolitan, Catholic city of 

New Orleans, could be done all over the Southland. 

According to the survey made by the missionary, Mrs. 

Cora Johnson, there are 149,000 Negroes in New Orleans.

Approximately 33,000 are members of Baptist churches, 18,000 are Catholics, 13,000 in other religious groups. 

This leaves 85,000 absolutely unchurched. 

Miss Hannah Reynolds, secretary of Louisiana W.M.U. gives the account of the missionary's work: 

Mrs. Cora Johnson came to New Orleans September 1, 1942. In a few weeks she secured the co-operation of the pastors of the Negro churches and won the confidence of several capable Christian women who promised to help her. Most of these were schoolteachers and students in the Negro Baptist Seminary in the city.

The Louisiana law permits children to be excused from school two one-hour periods a week for religious instructions. Catholics have long availed themselves of this opportunity so the missionary felt that in a free democracy other denominations should be entitled to the same privilege.

She visited individual after individual, schoolteacher after schoolteacher, preacher after preacher, to work out the many details such an undertaking involved. The consent of pastors to use their churches for the classes had to be secured and volunteer teachers had to be signed up for the task. Only those who would faithfully meet the children twice a week, rain or shine, hot or cold, and would make preparation to teach them, were acceptable. A course of study had to be outlined and mimeographed. Much publicity had to be given to the plan. Parents had to be contacted and their co-operation secured, as each child had to have a note to the teacher from his parents requesting that he be excused for religious instruction. 

Nothing seemed too difficult for the missionary to tackle. 

She walked many miles, rode street cars, with only an occasional lift in a car, she made the necessary arrangements for opening nine weekday schools in October.

There were fifteen regular full-time workers and four

others who agreed to help in an emergency. With the aid of the Louisiana W.M.U. the course of study was outlined and mimeographed. Material for 500 children was provided, but by the end of the month more than 1,000 had been enrolled. By the end of the session there were 

1,632 who received religious instruction in these schools. 

Graduation exercises were held in the spacious auditorium of the Booker T. Washington High School. It was thrilling to see the hundreds of precious black boys and girls, from six to sixteen years of age, led by intelligent, earnest, Christian women, read responsively, from memory, many inspiring Scriptures. They repeated the 

Ten Commandments, the Twenty-third Psalm, the books of the Bible, the plan of salvation, and so on. It was truly a soul-stirring experience for all who attended. Realizing how much had been done in so short a time we were overwhelmed by the possibilities of the future.

As a result of this missionary's efforts in behalf of her people the Protestant Ministers' Conference of New Orleans asked her to come before them and tell what she had done and how she did it. They were so impressed they immediately began to make plans for weekday Bible classes in the white schools of the city. Several were soon in operation. They hope to have many more ready to function by the time next school session begins.

V. THE RURAL CHURCH PROBLEM The Problem Stated 

Southern Baptists are just beginning to wake up to the country church problem. But few of them are even vaguely aware of its magnitude or importance. When you realize that 89 per cent of Southern Baptist churches are rural churches and that 69 per cent of them have only once-a-month preaching,¹ you begin to see the huge task 

11939 Southern Baptist Handbook-Alldredge.

confronting Southern Baptists. The crying need of the country church is for trained leadership and a more adequate church program. The absentee pastor is only one factor, though a very important one, in this problem. 

Low income and mobility of the majority of rural people further complicate the situation. The tenant farmers and day laborers which compose more than 50 per cent of the farmers of the South, move every two or three years. The average pre-war cash income for the tenant farm family was less than $200.00 per year and the average cash income of the share cropper ranged from $39.00 to $78.00 per year, according to Lowell Mellet's report to the president in 1938. Recent inflation has raised those amounts some but not in proportion to increased living costs. 

Poverty and ignorance make a hazard for any people. 

The South is 8.8 per cent illiterate as compared to 3.5 per cent the national average, because it has to educate onethird of the nation's children on one-sixth of the nation's school revenue. These conditions make the rural communities a wide open field for all sorts of falacious doctrines, sects, and schisms which are fairly taking the country.

In spite of all the handicaps it is estimated that for the past fifty years 80 per cent to 90 per cent of our preachers, missionaries and denominational workers have come out of country churches. This gives some idea of the vital importance and need for strengthening the country church.

When our circle was organized seven years ago, with a membership of nine women, only two of them had ever worked in a W.M.U. We knew little about the work and nothing about the many worthy causes which are sponsored by this organization.

Through our study of Royal Service and mission books that were taught us by competent leaders, we learned of our missionaries' great courage and sacrifices on foreign and home mission fields and we prayed that we might do more to help.

As we learned more of the need of missionary work at home and abroad, we were appalled. How could we, a small group of women, do anything worth while to help? 

We learned that by co-operating with the other circles of our church and W.M.U. of our state much could be accomplished. What we have done, though very little in itself, when added to the work of thousands of other circles throughout the Southland, has helped Southern 

Baptist W.M.U. carry on its work.

In dollars and cents, our work has amounted to only $446.07 during the seven years since we were organized. 

This has been divided among the many objects sponsored by the W.M.U., such as foreign, home and state missions, White Cross work, Margaret Fund, and others. 

Perhaps our pet project has been the Orphans' Home, to which we have contributed $184.70 through gifts of eggs, chickens, canned fruits and vegetables, at our yearly Harvest Festival (the time for the ingathering of fruits and vegetables, sponsored by our local church) and other supplies.

In our own community missions activities are valued at $73.82. We have made quilts and have given grocery showers for needy families. One time, each member gave a hen to an unfortunate family, to help them get a start of chickens. Another time, we furnished eggs for a neighbor to set her incubator, again we did sewing for a woman who had a serious operation. We have enjoyed all this work and feel that we have received a great blessing by doing it.

In February, 1941, we met and organized the R.A., the G.A., and the Sunbeam Band. In these groups the children are learning to take part in missionary programs and they learn to lead in prayer. They learn about our missionary work and missionaries. By interesting them in this work, we create a desire among the children to help and they eagerly save their money for the Lottie 

Moon Offering or the Annie Armstrong Offering.

Thus, through these groups our community is receiving a blessing and we hope to be able to do more to extend God's work during the years to come. 

VI. INTERCESSORY PRAYER LEAGUE 

The need for prayer was never so great as today. In many states the W.M.U. gives special emphasis to the

promotion of the Intercessory Prayer League. Georgia 

W.M.U. sends out these helpful suggestions and urges each society to organize a prayer league in their church or community:

Purpose: The purpose of the Intercessory Prayer League is twofold: (1) To harness the "Prayer Power" of women unable to attend the regular meetings of the Missionary Society to the cause of missions; (2) to give these women a consciousness of being intimately connected to the mission cause through their interest, prayers, and gifts. 

Many women, kept from so-called active participation in the missionary society because of age, illness, or other reasons, could be well informed through literature. 

Many of these during months or years of enforced quietness have learned how to wait upon God. They have many lessons to teach others who are "busy about many things." Moreover, their prayers can prevail where the efforts of others fail.

Members: Members will naturally be sought among women, members of the church, who but for providential causes would be active in missionary work. The church roll will provide the names. Where there is an organized extension department of the Sunday school the task of enlistment should be easier. These two plans need not overlap, but help each other. No one who wishes to unite her prayers with others for missions should be denied the privilege.

Methods: 

Choose a chairman, if it seems wise, from among the "shut-ins", but preferably from the regular members of the W.M.S. In the first case, she should have an assistant who can help her to contact the other members of the "League." In either case she must be a woman of

prayer and a member of the community missions committee. 

Enlist members. Do not disdain small membership. 

Remember "if two of you agree on earth as touching any thing that they shall ask, it shall be done for them of my Father." It is better to start with a small group of real earnest pray-ers and grow to include all eligibles than to make a big show at first with no real power of prayer in it. The pastor can be an invaluable help as he knows these "shut-ins" better than anyone else, perhaps. 

See that every member gets Royal Service, follows the Calendar of Prayer and regular monthly programs. 

The magazine may be provided from society funds for those unable to subscribe. They need the magazine more than any other members.

Regular visits by the chairman or other members of the W.M.S. should be made as often as necessary to keep members informed. The chairman should be called on for a report occasionally, perhaps through the community missions committee. Do not let this get too mechanical.

Regular gifts to the church should be encouraged. 

If the extension department of the Sunday school attends to this, well and good. An opportunity should be given for special offerings at seasons of prayer and other times.

Alabaster offering boxes may be ordered for each one. 

Have a regular prayer list for each member. Besides this bring emergency requests before the group.

Arrange some way for each one to be contacted by phone, neighbors, or mail, so that a special request for prayer may be placed before the group without delay.

VII. WEEK OF PRAYER PROGRAMS AND OF- FERINGS 

Practically every W.M.U. organization in the Southland regards the seasons of prayer for state, home, and

foreign missions as the mountaintop experiences of the year. But not yet have the full power and significance of these prayer seasons gripped the hearts of our women.

The experience of the W.M.U. of First Baptist Church, 

Monroe, La., indicates that progress is being made along this line, however. 

This thrilling story is related by Mrs. L. G. Wilkinson, the president of the W.M.U., and originator of the plans and methods used in her society to increase their offerings during the seasons of prayer: "It was in 1935, in the midst of the depression, when debts were crushing the spirit and usefulness of our mission boards and institutions, when Co-operative Program receipts were being drastically reduced, when the mission. work at home and abroad was so largely dependent upon the special offerings of the W.M.U., the women of First 

Baptist Church, Monroe, La., began to search and pray for a plan to increase interest in the weeks of prayer and the special offerings. In 1934 our Home Mission Offering was $103.00, State Mission Offering $81.00, and $241.00 for foreign missions. We felt that with God's help we could do better, much better than that, if we could get our people to realize the need. The faithful few always come and hear and give, but they represent such a small per cent of our church membership more women must be reached and all need to be inspired to greater giving.

The Beginning 

"Early in January, 1935, we began to lay special emphasis on home missions and the offering to be taken in March. We stressed it in circle meetings, urging the women to begin right then to give to the offering as they could. The amounts thus collected through the circles ranged from $1.11 to $6.75. These offerings were brought in during the Week of Prayer along with individual gifts

of those accustomed to bringing their offering at that time. 

During the Week of Prayer the president gave an alabaster box (secured from state W.M.U. headquarters, free) to each circle chairman and auxiliary counselor, to be used in gleaning gifts from those who wanted to increase their offering after hearing the programs and from those unreached by the programs. At a special evening service held the last of March these gifts were brought in. 

Knowing how little our own Sunday school gave for home and foreign missions on regular missionary days and remembering how many church members are in the Sunday school who are not members of W.M.U., we were impressed to invite the adult and young people's classes and other departments to join us in the "Alabaster Box 

Service." A few responded with small offerings. The funds brought in by circle leaders that night raised their totals somewhat, $2.35 was the lowest and $8.81 the highest. The grand total of $271.00 was a considerable increase over the $103.00 given the year before.

We were happy but not satisfied. We saw where many improvements were needed in our plans. In October when we began to plan for the Foreign Mission Week of Prayer, we challenged the women to set a goal for the whole union, not on basis of what they gave the year before, ($241.00) but on the basis of faith in an all-powerful God.

We held his many promises before them. The women accepted the challenge and adopted a goal of $400.00, much to the consternation of some. From this time on the promise in John 14:14 became our inspiration and strength, "If ye shall ask any thing in my name, I will do it." We next suggested that each woman accept $3.00 as a goal for herself, giving a dollar each month. This was for two reasons, first there was a fixed idea among our women that twenty-five or fifty cents, or certainly a dollar, was enough to give. Then, by giving a dollar a month for

three months it would not interfere with their regular gifts through the church budget. We constantly urged all who were able to give more to do so and encouraged all to launch out in faith, to make a worthy and sacrificial offering. Many did, as will be seen. 

Setting Goals 

More was necessary than setting a goal. "Knowledge begets interest" so an intensive study of missions was begun, using that splendid book, The Desire of All Nations by Smith, and we made much use of the program material for the Week of Prayer. A financial goal and study would not suffice unless they were backed up with prayer, individual and united. In every circle meeting a chain of prayers for the offering was made and each woman was asked to pray earnestly that God would help her reach her goal of $3.00 and go beyond if possible. Furthermore, the president invited those who would join her in claiming the promise in Matthew 18:19-20 to meet with her each week to pray for the Lottie Moon Christmas Offering throughout the Southland as well as in our own church. 

A number of consecrated women responded faithfully and we literally bombarded the throne of grace with our petitions.

Our Christmas Program 

As a climax to the months of study, prayer, and giving, we planned a beautiful White Christmas program when all organizations would bring their gifts-they had been asked to hold them until this time. This service was held one evening just before Christmas. Again, in December, we invited the Sunday school to have a part in this offering, missionary talks were made before classes and deconsequently the response was partments, enthusiastic, and the offering was much larger than that given in the spring. more

We advertised the evening program extensively. The church was beautifully decorated, a tall white Christmas tree illuminated with soft blue lights occupied central place in the setting prepared for the missionary program. "Christmas for Christ" was emphasized. We packed as much missionary information and inspiration into that program as we possibly could. A large crowd came. At the close of the program the Sunday school and all W.M.U. organizations brought their offerings and laid them under the tree. Our joy knew no bounds and the audience was amazed when it was announced that not $400.00 but twice that much had been given. We praised God and thanked him for hearing our prayers and rewarding our work and faith. Gifts continued to come in until the total reached $963.05. Of this $394.60 came through the Sunday school and $568.45 through W.M.U., which far exceeded our goal. How our faith was strengthened!

The Value of Goals 

The value of goals for individuals as well as for the society may be seen in comparing circle gifts. In March the lowest was $2.53 against $11.50 in December; the highest $8.81 against $82.53 in December. The young people's gifts showed an increase also.

For the Home Mission Offering in 1936 we used the same plan of setting individual goals, praying and studying. The past year's gift formed the basis to work 

An offering chairman was appointed in each circle, or elected along with chairman of mission study, stewardship, etc., at first of the year. The recommended home mission book was studied in each circle. This has become our regular policy now for both home and foreign seasons of prayer. The two missionary magazines are used as supplementary material also, especially if books are short on information from the fields as was the one

last fall, If Two Agree. Full use is made of all the Week of Prayer program material. 

These three months of work, study, prayer, and giving were climaxed in the special evening program. Again the organizations were asked to hold their offerings and bring them to this meeting. The Y.W.A. girls gave a beautiful home mission pageant at the close of which the Sunday school and W.M.U. organizations brought their gifts in alabaster boxes and placed them in a larger box covered in W.M.U. colors. The total amount of this offering was $584.09, an increase of $313.09 over that of the year before.

Two New Plans Added 

Two new things were added to the preparation plans for Lottie Moon Offering that year. The success that resulted from setting individual and society goals caused us to include the circles in this plan. The women had grown so, in one year's time, in missionary zeal and faith that all except two of the thirteen circles set goals much higher than the amount given the previous year. Some almost doubled the amount. The individual goal was continued. The rule adopted for the society goal was a 10 per cent increase over last year's gifts for both home and foreign missions.

The second new plan which also became a permanent feature was an enlistment campaign to solicit every church member not in Sunday school or W.M.U. and the unaffiliated Baptists not attending any other church. These were visited and given an offering envelope. The mission cause was presented and they were told someone would come later to get the envelope and whatever offering they cared to put in it. Each person was allowed to add the gifts she collected to her circle offering. This proved to be a great stimulus to circle members and many who had never been interested before became deeply interested in

missions from these contacts. As a result every circle exceeded the high goals they had set. Some went as high as $50.00 over the goal. The lowest amount given by any circle was $33.50 while the highest was $106.51. At the

White Christmas program, which was repeated, the gifts were brought by Sunday school and W.M.U. leaders. A larger crowd attended because they had seen or heard of last year's program and became interested in our great missionary work, its needs and triumphs. The total amount of that offering was $1,393.44.

Other Important Factors 

There is another important factor which helped to increase the offerings, namely, the increased attendance at the daily programs during the Week of Prayer. This was due to several things, first of all, to the special care given to planning and preparation of these programs, choosing the most capable and consecrated women to lead the devotional services. They were asked to work and pray over their messages so that the spiritual atmosphere for the whole program might be created. The five daily programs were given to certain circle leaders who were urged to choose women who would study their parts and give them well.

Second, we made the place of meeting just as attractive as possible, using seasonal flowers, flags of the nations, women and young people in costumes of various nations, posters, etc., anything to add interest to the occasion, to make our women feel that these were the finest and most important meetings held anywhere in our city.

Knowing that the best programs are of no value if no one hears them we sought a way to get more women to attend and were led to offer attendance awards for three circles having the highest average attendance during the week, $5.00, $3.00 and $2.00 respectively, which was to

be added to the offering of the winning circles. This created much interest and stimulated each circle to see that every member attended. Many visitors also were invited. 

We have continued using these same plans since 1936 except a few changes now and then, for instance, in 1937 we broke away from the individual goal of $3.00 because some became satisfied to give just that amount. So we asked the women to set their own goal, to let it be a matter between them and the Lord, but urged that it be high enough to challenge their faith and to call for sacrifice to meet it.

Testimonies 

We have had many wonderful testimonies from women on how they met their goals, one woman wanted to give $10.00. more than she had ever given in her life but she felt she could reach it with the Lord's help. With joy unspeakable she brought $11.50. Some of the circles had similar experiences. Circle Seven, very small and weak, only five or six active members, yet they would set unexpectedly and increasingly high goals. More than that they always met them, leaving the rest of us to wonder how on earth they did it, only to be told "the Lord made it possible." Circle Thirteen gave $120.00 to the Christmas offering in 1941 but they lost several good members before time to set the goal for the 1942 offering. Only five or six members were present when the decision had to be made, should they dare to follow the custom among the circles of a 10 per cent increase? They fell on their knees and prayed earnestly about it and rose each with the amount $135.00 in their mind as their goal. So that amount was accepted. They reached the unbelievable amount of $180.00. The Lord has truly been with us.

He has led us and blessed us far beyond our greatest hopes and expectations.

Until recently each prayer season has been climaxed with an evening program with a pageant or missionary speaker and the bringing of the offerings by all organizations. 

The decorations have always been as beautiful and appropriate to the occasion as we could make them. The offerings have grown larger from year to year as missionary knowledge and zeal have increased to the extent that it permeates our whole church and every organization within it. 

Blessing to the Church

There has been a wonderful transformation in the attitude of the men in our church toward missions since we began putting on these special programs.

About three years ago when we were decorating for the ingathering of our Lottie Moon Christmas Offering one of our fine deacons came in and stood watching us for some time. Then he came up to me and said, "Mrs. Wilkinson, I never heard of Lottie Moon and the Christmas

Offering for foreign missions until you women began to put on these marvelous programs and talk so much about it."

I wish I had time and space to tell you the story of how our Sunday school superintendent, who served from 1938 to 1942, grew from a person of practically no missionary vision, knowledge, or interest to one of the most interested, enthusiastic and missionary-minded men we had in our church. And, he gave the women credit for it.

He took up our plan of goal setting and would write the "peppiest" missionary letters to his superintendents and teachers, quoting from both missionary magazines and plead with them to set high goals and to give sacrificially. 

Our present superintendent took over when he left and no one could ask for better co-operation than he has given.

Has Not Hurt the Regular Offerings 

I want to emphasize the fact that the ever-increasing offerings and the intensive three months' preparation we made for each one did not interfere with, or lessen collections for other causes. To the contrary, our church records show that both the church budget which includes local expense and Co-operative Program and our church building fund increased through the years as the "special offerings" increased, which proves that when Christians grow in the grace of giving all of God's causes prosper. 

Some were skeptical at first and said "so many special offerings will hurt the regular contributions." But they can say so no longer.

Furthermore, we realized we were departing from W.M.U. custom and practice when we invited the Sunday school to have a part in the home and foreign mission offerings but as I stated before the needs were so pressing on mission fields and our hearts were so burdened, we felt we must do all we could to meet those needs with the greatest offering possible. At the request of the Sunday school their gifts were sent along with those of W.M.U. to Annie 

Armstrong and Lottie Moon Christmas offering, but we always kept the record of those gifts separate just as we did the junior auxiliaries, full credit being given to each organization for its gifts. No one this side of eternity will be able to estimate the value of this project, not only to our church but to the kingdom of God. 

We did not use this plan for our State Mission Offering until 1938. We just had a day of prayer and gathered the offerings. The tables given on page 158 show how our offerings jumped up when we began setting definite financial goals, studying and praying and working to reach them these eight years.

VIII. INSTITUTES FOR NEGRO BAPTIST W.M.U. 

WORK Southern Baptist Woman's Missionary Union has long been interested in helping the Negroes. In 1938 an event with far-reaching influence took place. Miss Mallory gives a brief account of it in the following paragraphs:

"In planning for the celebration of its Golden Jubilee Woman's Missionary Union decided to raise $10,000 to be used in work with and for Southern Baptist Negro women and young people. After considerable conference between the Negro and white W.M.U. leaders it was decided to use the money in holding institutes for the Southern Baptist Negro women leaders of missionary organizations. The first of these institutes was held in the summer of 1940 in 

Jackson, Miss., Macon, Ga., and Montgomery, Ala. Delegates came from many parts of the respective states. Two outstanding speakers of their own race were on the program, Mrs. Mabola Ayorinde of Nigeria, and Miss Nannie 

Burroughs, the corresponding secretary of the Woman's Convention of the National Baptist (Negro) Convention Incorporated. The next summer other states held institutes : 

North Carolina, South Carolina, and Florida. That summer a week's institute was held at Selma University in Selma, Ala. Carefully selected delegates came from Alabama, Georgia and Mississippi. In the summer of 1942 excellent results were obtained from a statewide institute of about six sessions each in the following states: South 

Carolina, Florida, Georgia, Alabama, Arkansas, Missouri, Illinois, and Kentucky. That same summer Mississippi held several regional institutes. The regional plan was effectively used in the summer of 1943 by North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Arkansas, and Missouri. Oneday institutes were held in Baltimore and Washington that same summer while the following states had sixsession institutes in Negro Baptist colleges: Florida, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana. Thus it will be seen that thirteen of the Southern states and the District of Columbia have availed themselves of the offer of the Golden Jubilee Fund to finance such institutes.

Among the blessings accruing from the institutes might be mentioned: favorable opportunities for Southern Bap-

tist Negro and white women leaders to meet for the mutual understanding of each other's problems in the promotion of their missionary work; the growing conviction that 

Woman's Missionary Union members and their organizations can more effectively co-operate with Negro Baptist women in their communities; the opportunity of hearing outstanding Negro speakers such as Rev. and Mrs. Ayorinde of Nigeria, Miss Nannie Burroughs of Washington, and the presidents of the various Negro Baptist colleges and universities in which the several institutes were held; the decision to give $15,000 from Beyond-the-Goal-Gifts of the 1942 Annie Armstrong Offering for larger help to twelve of the Negro Baptist schools in the South; to give a similar amount through the 1943 Annie Armstrong Offering as well as $5,000 from its Beyond-Goal-Gifts, to include $25,000 for them through the same offering during the 1944 March Week of Prayer for Home Mis sions and to anticipate really adequate appropriations as the years come and go."

Now that Southern W.M.U. has lead the way several of the state W.M.U.'s have a regularly employed Negro woman to work co-operatively with them and their own people. As time goes on more and more of such work will be done by local and associational W.M.U. organizations.
