Introduction 

By the Rt. Rev. Matthew Smith, Ph.D., Jour.D.,

Editor of The Register, Denver, Colorado. 

From a spiritual standpoint, man can indulge in no more profitable study than the Passion of 

Our Lord and Savior, Jesus Christ. It is with gratification, therefore, that we hail Father 

O'Reilly's scholarly discussion of the Passion, Death, and Resurrection of Christ, and the Descent of the Holy Ghost.

A writer in The Commonweal (Sept. 11, 1936) quotes the following from Carlyle: "Strange enough how creatures of the human kind shut their eyes to plainest facts; and by the mere inertia of oblivion and stupidity live at ease in the midst of wonders and terrors. But indeed man is and was always a blockhead and a dullard; more ready to feel and digest than to think. and consider."

We close our eyes to plainest facts if we do not meditate on the Passion and its effects. The inertia of oblivion and stupidity holds us entrapped. We are really, then, blockheads and dullards. We are reduced to the state of mere animals, sensuous but blind to spirituality, oblivious of our own real worth and dignity.

The Commonweal writer, Margaret Gunn, says that the modern world is bewitched by words words beautiful but meaningless when detached from their intrinsic reality: liberty, fraternity,

Introduction equality, and democracy, at first; and, later, science, race, nationalism, etc. In the midst of wonders and terrors, mankind has lost its power to think and consider. 

How will it solve its problems? It can do this only in the way the saints did it by considering the lesson of the Crucifix. St. Thomas 

Aquinas declared that he learned more from the Crucifix than from books. The world must turn again to the story of the Passion, realize that man is on earth for but a brief period of probation, that he is utterly dependent on the spiritual fruits won by Jesus Christ on the Cross if he is to be successful in this period of trial, and that he is destined to reign forever in heaven or to suffer eternally in hell, in accordance with his success or failure, as judged by God's standards, in his period of probation. 

It is an old story, often told, that nothing really matters but our eternal salvation. Yet millions deliberately turn their backs on the only means of real success and go chasing after any fool who can invent pretty catchwords or who pretends to offer them an easier way of filling the heart's desire. There is only one way to satisfy the cravings of our hearts: that is to possess God. There is only one way to reach 

God: that is by believing in and living up to what He has taught. The way to appreciate the truth and beauty of Christianity is to study the teachings of Jesus Christ, to ponder over them, to apply them to our individual problems.

If we follow such a plan, we will not be found

Introduction in the mob that goes wildly enthusiastic over beautiful words that have lost their intrinsic meaning. We will sift everything down to the fine gold of its real meaning and will be able to guard our souls against the blistering heats, the mad winds, the driving rains and blizzards, of that type of modern thought which finds its origin in the lower regions.

Our age is particularly in need of the story of the Passion. We need a yardstick by which to measure our lives. We need a set of scales with which to weigh the sophisms that are put up to us from all sides. We need the wisdom to see through the artifices of devils and fools, the courage to remain with the truth when others abandon it, the glorious idealism that comes from profound spiritual knowledge and intense supernatural love. Therefore a book on the Passion and Death of Jesus Christ, written by a scholar who has made a deep study of his subject, has a value that no man can overestimate. We greet 

Father O'Reilly's work as a contribution to knowledge that will bear fruit for many throughout eternity and as a book that will bring all of us who study it to a new realization of what, after all, "everything is about." 

Matthew Smith 

Sept. 10, 1936.

SERIES EXPLAINING THE APOSTLES' CREED 

HE fourth article of the Apostles' Creed in- 66 under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died and was buried:" viz.: while Pontius Pilate was administering the government of the province of Judea under Tiberius Caesar. The reference in the creed to Pontius Pilate brings home to our minds the time and the way in which our Lord suffered, and that He suffered till His death was accomplished, and His dead body was laid in the grave. The full history of Christ's suffering, or, as it is commonly called, His Passion, is given by the four Evangelists. From putting together what they say we get an account of nearly all we know on the subject, there being only a few additional particulars handed down by tradition. The detailed account belongs to sacred history: but to facilitate the study of what is told us in the Gospels, the several stages in the history of the Passion shall be considered separately.

Judas, having made his sacrilegious Communion, departs from the supper-room and goes to

the Pharisees, to accomplish the awful design he has conceived of betraying his Devine Master. Whilst the enemies of Christ are thus busying themselves making preparations for His seizure and imprisonment, our Divine Savior,

His heart burning with love for us, retires with His eleven Apostles from the supper-room. The door of the cenacle has closed behind them. 

Christ and His Apostles are in the street leading to the Garden of Olives, that hallowed spot that was destined to be the first scene of Christ's dolorous Passion. It was in the garden of delights, the terrestrial paradise, that the struggle of the first Adam took place. "It is also in a garden," says St. Cyril, "that the second Adam -Jesus Christ-commenced His Passion, by which He will repair the evil done to us in the garden of paradise.

Gethsemane is a sacred place: we do not wonder that our Savior was accustomed to come there to pray. The ashes of the Blessed Virgin's parents rested there, and close beside the tomb of Joachim and Anne is the grotto where our first parents found shelter, when they fled from Paradise. There, it is said, they wept bitterly for their fatal sin: to this grotto comes the second Adam, to redeem by His agony and Passion sinful man.

The Garden of Gethsemane is on the side of the Mount of Olives. The name is compounded of two Hebrew words signifying "Oil-press.

It was so called on account of its being the place where oil from the olives growing in the neigh-

borhood was made. It is made sacred and memorable by the sufferings of Christ. All we learn from the Gospel history of this locality is, that Gethsemane was a garden-which is probably to be understood as an olive-grove-on the farther side of the brook Cedron, and at the foot of the Mount of Olives. After the visit of St. Helena (the mother of Constantine) tradition places it very near Cedron and quite near to the Church of the Blessed Virgin, alleged to have been built over her tomb.

Dean Stanly gives us a description of the present state of the locality: "The Garden of 

Gethsemane is now enclosed, and in it are pointed out some traditional sites such as the Grotto of the Agony: the rocky bank of the 

Apostles the terra damnata of the betrayal. 

There are eight olive trees, which, by their manifest difference from all the others in the garden, have struck even the indifferent observers. They are now indeed less striking, in the modern enclosure, built around them by the Franciscan monks, than when they stood free and unprotected on the rough side of the garden. They will remain, so long as their protracted life is spared, the most venerable of their race on the face of the earth; the most nearly approaching to everlasting hills themselves, in the force in which they carry us back to the gospel history." 

Having given this description of Gethsemane and its localities, which is sufficient for us to form an idea of the place, we will now follow our Savior and consider His Agony and Passion

in the garden. Eight of His Apostles have not seen His garments on Mount Tabor become as white as snow. They did not hear Him talk with

Moses and Elias; nor did they hear the voice of the Eternal Father ringing from the clouds: "This is My beloved Son in whom I am well pleased hear ye Him. So in pity, lest their faith should fail, He compassionately spared them the sight of His Agony. "Sit you here till 

I go yonder and pray" (Matt. XXVI 36). The three that were with Him on Mount Tabor, He took with Him. And taking with Him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee He began to grow sorrowful and to be sad (Matt. XXVI 37). "My heart is troubled within Me, and the fear of death is upon Me: fear and trembling are come upon Me, and darkness hath covered Me" (Ps. 

IV, 5:6). 

This has been predicted and it must come to pass. But it must come to pass because as St. 

Paul says: "It behooved Jesus Christ in all things to be made like unto His brethren," that He might show them mercy. Having chosen to become man for us He must act as man and suffer as man, in order to give us a clear and sensible proof of His true Humanity. Being God at the same time, He must show that He has dominion over His passions. As God, then, His passions and His sorrows are obedient to His will. St. Augustine explains this in the following manner: "Consider the power of Christ 

Jesus. You feel sorrow without willing it. Jesus

Christ suffered hunger, it is true, only because

He willed it. He was sad, but only because 

He willed it Himself. He suffered in the Garden of Olives and died, it is true, but only because He willed it Himself. He had power to suffer these things or not as He willed. No man amongst us is born because he wills it, or dies because he wills it. Jesus Christ was born when 

He wills. He dies when He wills. All that He wills, He does because in Him there are two wills the Divine will and the human will; and the human is always subject to the Divine." 

His sorrows, therefore, are voluntary. And if we would sound the full depths of Christ's agony we must keep before our minds, and never lose sight of the fact, that it is a God who suffers. The soul and body on which the tempest beats are indeed human, but the sufferer is Divine, His sufferings are interior, and therefore intense. "The true home of pain lies within."

The pain of the body is as nothing when compared with the anguish of the mind. The soul is the seat of sufferings, the body the channel through which it passes. "He began to be sorrowful and to be sad." Great was the sorrow and sadness of Christ in the garden. It was great: (1) by reason of its material object: namely, the sins of men; (2) by reason of its formal object: namely, the knowledge which 

Christ had of the malice of sin and the offended majesty of God; (3) by reason of the principle from which that sorrow proceeded; namely, Christ's infinite love for His Father and us.

Hence Christ, with the clear Divine vision which He had, of the number of those that would be lost: at the sight of all living human beings, and especially those around Him who were persecuting Him to death: at the sight of the future: His Passion the next day: His abandonment that night Christ utters those words of intense suffering: "My soul is sorrowful even unto death: stay you here, and watch with Me."

He leaves them and goes to the grotto, which is about a stone cast. There IIe dismisses His attendant angels, and bids Lucifer, with his army, approach, and put their powers of sin against Him. "Now is the hour for the power of darkness." Gethsemane is the battleground. There all the waters of iniquity were poured upon His soul. There never was a curse or imprecation uttered, there never was an impure or venomous word spoken or a blasphemous oath hurled against God that did not cling around His soul. 

There never was open act of evil or secret deed of shame done that did not seem to stain His lips and heart.

There never was inmost thought of evil, or foul desire cherished that did not burn His soul like fire. Sin rose before Him, each single sinner came to Him, that He might atone for his sins by His agony, and win pardon for him by 

His love. Jacob of old clothed himself in the garments of Esau to secure a parent's blessing. 

So Christ put on His brothers' vesture of shame and brought upon Himself His Father's malediction. For He has taken all the sins of the

world upon Himself. Think not that because 

He is God, His sufferings are diminished: On that account they are rather intensified to an infinite acuteness. As the Omniscient God, He sees those sorrows bearing down upon Him like the hideous, destroying monsters of a feverish dream. No wonder then His body would weep tears of blood. Behold Him as He lies upon the earth, a crushed worm, and as yet no man has touched Him. But see a light break through the darkness, and the voices and footsteps of approaching men are heard. Perhaps some tender hearts are coming to pour the balsam of consolation into the Savior's ear. What, must another drop of bitterness be added to the chalice of bitterness? The light of the torches reveals to His sacred gaze the features of the traitor Judas. 

Behold Him rising from the earth full of courage, animated with supernatural strength the power of the Godhead going forth to meet those who have come to capture Him. And He said to them: "Whom seek ye?' They answered 

Him, 'Jesus of Nazareth.' Jesus said to them: 'I am He.' As soon as He said to them 'I am He,' they went backward, and fell to the ground.'

Annas-Ben-Seth, called Ananos by Josephus the Jewish historian, was at the period of our 

Lord's last days upon earth the most powerful figure in the national and religious life of Judea. 

His father, Seth, had been the head of one of the most powerful priestly families, and is spoken of by Josephus as the most fortunate. man of his time. Like most sacerdotal families theirs belonged to the political-religious party known as Sadducees, i.e., those who through the intercourse of Palestine with the Greek kingdoms of Syria and Egypt and with Rome had adopted many of the ways and ideas of pagan civilization, to the detriment of the national religion of which they should have been the guardians.

Annas, at the age of thirty-seven and about the year of 6 A.D., was elected to the office of

high priest by the proconsul of Syria, who incorporated the territories of the deposed tetrarch Archelaus. Annas was clever, bold, energetic, and he was well versed in the law, but he was cunning, unscrupulous and intensely avaricious. 

He was deposed for some unknown cause by the 

Roman procurator Valerius Gratus. Fifteen years afterwards he had succeeded in getting the office conferred upon Joseph Caiphas, his son-in-law, a man much his inferior in learning and strength of character. The people deeply resented this change in their priesthood as illegal, and looked upon Annas as their legal Pontiff.

Hence St. Luke thus couples the two names 

Annas and Caiphas. Into the presence of Annas 

Christ is first led. Annas realizes that what he has long wished for is come at last; he is face to face with Jesus of Nazareth. Our Savior stands silent amid the guards and servants and in the uncertain light of some lanterns and torches they hold; silent still through the long pauses Annas makes from time to time, inviting and waiting for reply. 

Christ wears yet the customary white linen headdress, His paled face with the traces of the agony more bitter than death, through which He had passed in Gethsemane. The cords are loosed from His neck and waist, and His hands hang at the side of His damp and mud-stained garments. Annas interrogates Him concerning 

His doctrine. Christ's answer amazes Annas and his friends as it sweeps aside his insidious

questions and confronts him with clear and unquestionable assertion.

He had taught openly: and to the world, in city and village, by the roadway and the lakeside, in the synagogues and wherever the Jews resort, at festival times in the portico of the temple. He had not taught in secret. Why should they ask Him? They should ask those who had listened to Him: surely these knew what

He had been teaching. There is an awkward silence. Annas at once sees his mistake: it mortifies and annoys him. His questioning is illegal in time and place. As Annas or servants know not what to say or do the silence grows long and embarrassing, to the high priest's humiliation. 

Then a blow is heard or slap from an open hand: a blow given with the flat of the hand across the face, in ignominy as well as pain. To the insolent words that accompany the blow our 

Lord deigns reply. If He had spoken evil to the multitude let testimony be given, but if well why this violence? Annas neither reprimands the servant, nor notices the outrage. Again another awkward silence, and Annas gives sign that Jesus be bound and conducted to Caiphas. 

The interview with Jesus of Nazareth which he had himself arranged, to which he had looked forward so keenly, and from which had expected so much is over: and for him it has ended in failure; every one in the council chamber has witnessed his defeat, and his loss of dignity.

Pilate Showed Contempt at Jewish 

Attempt to Force Hand as Governor 

HE next stage on the way of sorrows trodden by our Divine Savior brought Him to the pretorium. That stronghold was called the pretorium because it was there that the procurator or Roman governor of Judea held his tribunal. He came with his Roman legion up to Jerusalem for all religious and state affairs from his official residence, Caesarea, a splendid harbor on the Mediterranean sea. The pretorium was situated at the northeastern corner of Jerusalem and™ on a plateau higher than that on which the Temple was built. The site was that of the ancient citadel held by the Syrians in the valiant days of the Machabees. Then it was the palace of the pontiff princes of Judea. When Pompey led the Roman army from Damascus to Jerusalem (65 B. C.) the last stand of the unfortunate Aristabulus was made here, and in the Temple. But it owed the fullness of its size and strength to Herod the Great. The historian 

Josephus Flavius tells us that he saw the city and all its wonderful palaces and its great Temple in all their splendor, and that Herod employed in the rebuilding of this place all the resources of his genius and all the wealth at his command. He hewed down the rough stone on all sides of the rock; hence that stronghold was a castle elevated on high. This palace was only a part of the impressive buildings, for on the north was constructed a series of buildings sur-

rounded by thick, high walls. The remains are still to be seen.

It was close to the Temple, and from its heights Pilate with his soldiers could quickly and effectively quell any tumult. 

In order to reach this grandiose citadel, Herod the Great had constructed two massive flights of stairs cut out of the side of the rock, covering them with marble. Josephus Flavius tells us that "this marble was removed to Rome and is now known as the 'Scala Sancta.' One of these massive stairways led to the courtyard, to the forum of the palace, and to the public street. 

The other on the south side led to the square of the Temple. It was on these steps that St. Paul later stood when menaced by the Jews, and addressed them (Acts XXII). Mary's Martyrdom At Foot Of Cross Is Proof of Deep Power of Mother Love 

DENSER and denser grew the darkness on the hill of Golgotha until the crosses became almost invisible. At some distance on one side, in a cluster, appear the Sanhedrists, princes of the people, the ancients and chief priests waiting the completion of the sentence that soothes their malice. Some of the people, in frightened groups, have fled down the hill side; others are scattered here and there. The soldiers, overcome with fatigue, sit down upon the ground, having nothing more to do but to keep watch on the victims till death. Now Christ's friends are permitted to draw near Him. "Now there stood by the Cross of Jesus His mother and 

His mother's sister, Mary of Cleophas, and Mary 

Magdalen." (John XIX, 25.) 

Why was His mother there? Her name had almost disappeared from the sacred records of her Son's life. We scarcely hear anything of her after His first miracle. When His subsequent works of wonder fixed the eyes of men upon Him, His mother was not with Him. She was not there to share the honor eagerly paid Him by those whom He cured and fed, and who heard His preaching when He spoke "as one having authority." Others were with Him on the mount of His transfiguration; Mary saw not His shining face and snowy robes of Thabor.

But when the day of gloom arrived, when His friends had fled from Him, now when He was

condemned to a felon's death, then came Mary His mother and took her stand by His Cross to share His suffering and shame. "To what shall I compare thee, or to what shall I liken thee, O daughter of Jerusalem? To what shall 

I equal thee that I may comfort thee, O virgin daughter of Sion; for great as the sea is thy destruction: who shall heal thee?" (Jer. XI, 13.)

It cannot be said that the sorrows of Mary were easy to be borne because the sufferings of her Son were only part of a great design of love, which God was slowly working out. It is true she knew all this; she knew that her Son was a willing victim, that He embraced His Cross with eagerness; she foresaw His resurrection, and she knew that for three hours' suffering on Golgotha there would be centuries of thanksgiving from earth, and an eternity of glory in heaven. But all these previsions, all these anticipations did not lessen the sorrows in the mother's heart; His terrible Passion darkened all her life; she mourned His cruel death; she was horror-stricken at the view of the indignities to which her Son and her God would be subject; she deplored the blindness that would not recognize the proofs of His Divinity. For thirty-three years with every succeeding vision of His Passion, her love for Jesus was increasing. And as Calvary drew nearer, the dearer was Jesus to Mary; so that habitual vision of His sufferings did not dull the anguish of their reality; but it increased her sensibility by increasing her love for her Son and her God.

Holy writers tell us that this gentle-hearted mother was brought by God's hand to the place of execution, and there she stood to see not the swift and almost painless dispatch of these days, but the long-drawn agonies of crucifixion. Thus 

God made unto Himself a docile daughter, that He might lead her to this mount of sorrows and leave her standing in this place of the skull. "There stood by the Cross of Jesus His mother." There she offered to the eternal

Father, side by side with Jesus, the sacrifice of her heart and soul, of her own being and will. 

It was the crowning act in that thirty-three years of sacrifice.

The eternal Father in accepting this sacrifice of His cherished daughter has left her the vision of the past-the vision of a babe upon her bosom, with tender lips that spoke to her in smile, with gentle eyes that spoke to her in sunshine; the vision of the young man who stood before her with deep mystery glowing upon His features, with the mystery of the eternal Godhead shining from within His eyes. All the memories of the past He has left her, but the 

Divinity has withdrawn from her His mercy that would console her and support her while she stood transfixed, side by side with her Son, in the mid-day sacrifice. 

Oh! Mary, Queen of Martyrs, well have you earned that title. We know that whatever torments the martyrs had endured, they could always smile at their persecutor, for there was always with them a presence that soothed their

sufferings-the presence of their crucified Savior. It was this presence of her Son and God that was the chief cause of all Mary's sorrows. 

It was for Him she suffered. This is what was before St. John's eyes when he wrote: "There stood by the Cross of Jesus His mother."

Close by the Cross, pressing her forehead against His feet, burning with fever, is the Magdalen; by her side are His own great mother, the other Mary and St. John. They are all silent -their grief is too terrible for tears, for they know how their Lord and Master is suffering. 

The bowed thorn-crowned head is raised and turns slowly; His eyes search for a moment and then fix themselves lovingly on His mother and 

His beloved disciple. "When Jesus therefore had seen His mother and the disciple standing whom He loved He said to His mother Woman, behold thy Son; after that He saith to the disciple, Behold thy mother." (John XIX, 25.) Mary and John look up into the face of the Divine Sufferer. 

Their sorrow is too great to speak. Oh! Christ, there is no need of words, because the heart is speaking to the heart; and love is a clever interpreter and even the slightest sign may speak with eloquence and clearness when it is the heart that listens. 

But for our sake Jesus chose to tell us the marvel of His love for His followers by giving them His mother. He is our elder Brother; therefore Mary is our mother. He wished to say to Mary, "I see the immense love with

which you love Me; henceforth consecrate your love to the children of My Church, represented by St. John. In the person of this disciple I confide to you My Church. I put them in My placethey will be your children. Ecce filius tuus!"

To be forsaken by God is the greatest of all misfortunes: that is the worst torment of hell; but we do not feel this. Because never having known the full joy of possession, we cannot realize the full grief of loss. Not so with our Lord.

Even in His sacred humanity He had known the unutterable joy of the vision of God.

We can scarcely bring our minds to conceive that God is prepared in His divinity to go down to the lowest depths of sorrow and humiliation with the humanity of Jesus Christ. Such is the reality of God that He has taken that man's body and soul to Himself by a personal union so lasting, so indissoluble, so inseparable, that even when the human soul was parted from the human body by death-even then, while Christ's body hung dead upon the Cross and His soul went forth into that mysterious region where met the anger and the mercy of God before the expiation of sin-even then the Godhead did not depart from that dead body upon the Cross or from that human soul in limbo. You may urge, then, that His Eternal Father had not forsaken Him-could not; that our Lord with His di-

But the all sufficient comfort of the divinity and the sustaining power of His Father's love are put away from Him in that hour. A cloud comes between Jesus Christ upon the Cross, the Victim of our sins, and His Father's face in heaven; and that cloud was the concentrated anger of a just God which came upon His Divine Son, because of our sins and our transgressions. Not that the divinity quitted Him. No; 

He was still God; but by His own act and free will He put away the comfort and the sustaining power of the God-head, for a time, in order that while He suffered the pain of sense in His body, in His soul He suffered the pain of loss. 

We are startled at that cry of dereliction at the words that Jesus is forsaken by God; but we must remember that it is the damnation of sinners that our Savior is now bearing, and that the cry we hear is indeed a cry from hell, for 

Christ is suffering the pain of loss and the pain of loss constitutes one of the most terrible punishments of the damned. 

Surely even the racking pains of crucifixion fade into nothing compared with this veritable hell upon earth, this cry of a soul lost to the face of God-forsaken by Him and in the hour of death. Awful words which we shudder to hear, and which, had not our Lord said them, no man could utter. In the words of the

The Way of the Cross was a weary journey, and He took it for love of us; the Cross itself stands so far within the regions of sorrow that we scarcely thought His love could have borne Him further; but further He has gone; further than His sacred body could go; for His soul has stretched forward and tasted what is not tasted or what no man has even tasted and lived-the mortal draught of the waters of loss--the cup of grief and abandonment.

HEN our Savior died His body was separated from His soul: but His Divinity was not separated from either of them. The Godhead was with the dead body upon the Cross, and in the tomb: and the Godhead was with the soul in Limbo. The Divinity was never an instant absent from the human body or from the human soul from the moment that God and man became one Divine Person in the womb of Mary. 

We have now to consider what happened after the death of Christ upon His Cross. There was a custom among the Jews, when criminals had remained for hours upon their crosses, to break their legs and sometimes their ribs: which seems to imply that to the Jewish crucifixions this horror was added to hasten death. This was done by means of heavy hammers, with which several hard blows were struck. It brought such an excruciating agony that the poor sufferers generally expired before it was completed. 

By the law of Moses a condemned person might not be left upon a gibbet, but "must be buried the same day." "When a man hath committed a crime for which he is to be punished with death, and being condemned to die is hanged on a gibbet, his body shall not remain upon the

tree, but shall be buried the same day and thou shall not defile thy land which the Lord thy God has given thee in possession" (Deut. 

XXI, 22-23.) The Roman custom was to leave them to putrefy on the cross like the obsolete 

English custom of hanging in chains. 

Before Christ had called death to Him, some of the members of the Sanhedrin and a few of the priests went down from Mount Olivet and "besought Pilate that the legs of the crucified might be broken in order that their bodies might not remain upon the Cross on the Sabbath day" (John XIX, 31.) The Sabbath would commence at sunset, and before the first stars appeared all traces of the crucifixion must be removed: and the day now at hand was the "great Sabbath.",

Pilate gave the permission that they craved.

IX, 6-8.) "He was put to death indeed in the flesh, but quickened in the spirit; in which also coming He preached to those spirits that were in prison" (I Peter III, 18-19.) These words clearly signify that Christ descended into hell after His death: that is, into limbo. Hell is here used to signify lower regions- the place or state of the dead. And it is often used in 

Scriptures in this sense. "He descended first into the lower parts of the earth" (Eph. IV, 9.)

The word hell is derived from the Anglo- Saxon helan, to hide; therefore, according to etymology, it means the hiding place: the abode of departed spirits. There are two words in the 

Greek Testament, gehenna and hades, translated into the English word hell: signifying the hell of torments, taking this signification from the valley of Hinnom, where human sacrifices were offered to the idol Moloch. "Whosoever shall say thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire"

99 [gehenna] (Matt. X, 22.) And again: "Thou having two eyes to be cast into hell fire" [gehenna], "where the worm dieth not, and the fire is not extinguished" (Mark IX, 27-28.)

The word hades is the place of departed spirits called in Hebrew schoel. "And the rich man died and was buried in hell [hades] and lifting up his eyes, when he was in torments he saw 

Abraham afar off and Lazarus in his bosom (Luke XVI, 22-23.) Our Savior tells us that there are many mansions in His Father's house, so there are different states of those who are dead. 

The Jewish names for that place inhabited by the spirits of the just were Abraham's bosom, under the throne of glory, and paradise. "Amen 

I say to thee this day shalt thou be with Me in paradise" (Luke XXIII, 43.) Therefore when we say in the creed "He descended into hell,” we mean that lower place under the throne of glory, not gehenna nor hades-the hell of the damned-but the place of rest where the souls of those who died before Christ were detained: namely, the soul of Abel to the soul of the good thief. "Coming He preached to those that were in prison:" that is, He announced to them that their redemption was complete: the long-sighedfor hour was come. And as His glance swept over that home of patient longing, what a venerable assemblage presented itself. There were the prophets whose inspired tongues had announced Him: there was the last and greatest of all the prophets-John-the precursor, who

on the Jordan's bank lifted the veil from the picture they had painted, and cried: "Behold the lamb of God:" there was holy Simeon, whose lips had fashioned the sword for Mary's heart: and above all there was His foster-father-St. 

Joseph. What a flood of recollections now throng back to Joseph-the wrapt ecstasies of that first Christmas night; his journey into

Egypt; the solitude of that holy house in Nazareth, when he breathed his last sigh in the arms of the God-man, that now stands radiant before him! 

Dr. Lingard gives the following on the descent of Christ's soul into limbo and the purpose of that descent: "At the present day, usage has confined the name of hell and its representatives. in the dead languages to the habitation of the wicked after death; but in former times it was employed in a wider sense, for the abode and state of all departed souls whether they were in suffering or punishment or not. Now Jesus, in consequence of His having taken the nature of man, was 'tempted and tried in all things according to His likeness to us without sin' (Hebr. 

IV, 15.) He died as we die: His body was deposited in the grave like the bodies of other men : and His soul like those of others descended to the general abode of departed souls. This is plainly indicated by St. Peter when, applying the words of the Psalmist to the Resurrection of Our Savior, he says: 'God did not leave

His soul in hell'-the abode of souls-'nor suffer His Holy One to see corruption :' that is:

suffer His body to mould away in the grave. 

Heaven was not open to man till after the death, the resurrection and ascension of Jesus. The disobedience of Adam had closed heaven against all his posterity: it was thrown open to man by the obedience of Christ, 'who took away the sins of the world, and was propitiation for the sins of the world' (I John II, 2.) Hence it was the doctrine of the ancient Church that souls of the patriarchs, prophets and all good men were detained in limbo till Christ had paid their ransom by His death: and then 'ascending up on high He led captivity captive and gave gifts unto men: that is, He delivered the captives from confinement and allotted to them their respective portion in heaven.

In other words, Christ descended into limbo to bring the souls there imprisoned the glad news that the price of their redemption had been paid; that He for whom they had so ardently longed was now come to deliver them; in fine, to comfort them and set them free. "Thou also by thy blood of thy testament has sent forth thy prisoners out of the pit wherein is no water" (Zach. IX, 11.)

The Jewish Sabbath began with the appearance of the first stars, about six o'clock: therefore St. Matthew calls it the next day. Christ was buried on Friday afternoon, and He was in the sepulchre part of that day, the whole of the Sabbath and part of Sunday. Thus He rose the third day from the dead. "This is in keeping with the method of expressing time by the Jews who were accustomed to speak of any part of the day as a whole day." (Gills in loco.) 

As the Blessed Mother, Mary Magdalen, the other Mary, St. John, Joseph of Arimathea, Nicodemus and the holy women that followed Him from Galilee were lingering close around the sepulchre, they heard the heavy, measured tread of the soldiers who came with the night to guard

the tomb. The guard "made the sepulchre sure, sealing the stone and setting a watch.' 99 

It was a strange occupation for Roman soldiers to be set in guard over a dead man's grave, lest a few simple and affrighted peasants, who had fled at first sight of danger, "should come and steal Him away, and say unto the people,

He is risen from the dead. Excited by their strange office and agitated with superstitious fears they slept not, but watched diligently the whole of that night. At the peril of his life a 

Roman soldier slept on guard: and now they are guarding the seal of Caesar; on that massive rock that closed the entrance to the tomb of 

Jesus of Nazareth. But the real keepers of that sepulchre were angels from the right-hand of another throne. The grave which is naturally the goal of morals and the end of their power, becomes to Jesus the richest source of His glory: and His enemies by placing a guard of soldiers round His tomb, become reluctant witnesses of the Triumphs of His ressurrection.

Ascension of Christ Into Heaven 

Proves Our Hope Is Not in Vain

UR Savior, having risen from the dead on 

Easter Sunday, remained forty days upon the earth before He ascended into heaven. The flood was forty days upon the earth (Gen. VII, 17.) Moses was with God on Mount Sinai forty days: "And I continued on the mount forty days and nights" (Deut, IX, 9.) "Elias walked in the strength of food forty days and forty nights unto the Mount of God Horeb" (I Kings 

XIX, 8.) Christ fasted forty days in the desert: and He remained on earth forty days after His 

Resurrection. During this period our Lord instructed His Apostles in all that concerned His Church-or kingdom of God-and He gave them 

His commission: "Go and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matt. XXV 

III, 19.)

The account of our Lord's Ascension is given to us in the Acts of the Apostles, also in the Gospels of St. Mark and St. Luke. The Apostles had come to Jerusalem from Galilee and were gathered together in the cenacle or supper room, where their divine Master had instituted the 

Blessed Sacrament on Holy Thursday. And as they were assembled at table, "He appeared and upbraided them with their incredulity and hardness of heart, because they did not believe them who had seen Him, after He was risen again" (Mark XVI, 14.) "And eating together with

them, He commanded them that they should not depart from Jerusalem, but should wait for the promise of the Father, which you have heard (saith He) by My mouth" (Acts I, 6.)

Satisfied with all they had seen and heard from Himself as to the identity of their Lord and Master, and what power belonged to Him: not even yet did they correctly realize the purpose of His life and death, and the nature of the work to which He had called them: for the old thoughts, which, if ever entirely absent from their minds about the future greatness of their nation, came back again-"therefore they asked 

Him saying: Lord wilt Thou at this time restore again the kingdom of Israel? But He said to them it is not for you to know the times or seasons which the Father has put in His own power" (Acts I, 6-7.)

He then led them out to Calvary, by the way of Bethany, where they were joined by Martha, Mary and Lazarus. Having reached Olivet, He once more reminded His Apostles of the coming of the Holy Ghost, and their future mission. "You shall receive the power of the Holy Ghost coming upon you, and you shall be witnesses unto Me in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and even to the uttermost parts of the earth" (Acts I, 8.)

We may reflect here on our Lord's consoling colloquies with His Mother, His Apostles and all those that followed Him from Jerusalem, Galilee and Bethany. Neither the love of His disciples nor the love of His Mother would keep

Him one moment from the doing of His Father's will. One can hardly help a deep interest in the feelings of those that stood and knelt about 

Him: when "lifting up His hands He blessed them" Calvary becomes glorious in the light of His glorified body now ascending up into heaven. They watched Him going into heaven, and stood there looking up long after the cloud that had hidden Him had spent itself in the blue. And while they were beholding Him going up to heaven, behold two men stood by. them in white garments. Who also said: Ye men of Galilee, why stand you looking up to heaven? This Jesus who is taken up from you into heaven, shall come, as you have seen Him going into heaven" (Act I, 10-11.)

They kissed the ground made holy by His presence, prayed for resignation, and looked again and yet again toward heaven. No doubt they would have remained there indefinitely, but for the orders sent by the Master. The spot where our Lord's feet are said to have rested on this earth is still shown, being marked by a stone worn hollow by the lips of devout pilgrims. "And they adoring went back to Jerusalem with great joy (Luke XXIV, 52.) They went down from the hill with sorrow, but their joy knew no bounds; they walked as in a dream. Indeed the little Church then upon earth was joyful because it believed. And they knew that the "Man of Sorrows" was now the glorified Redeemer. Moreover, He had told them Himself that is was good for them that He should go.

"Let not your hearts be troubled, I go to prepare a place for you. I will come again and will take you to Myself that where I am you also may be" (John XIV, 13.)

We shoud here consider the difference between the Resurrection and the Ascension. No wit nesses were needed at the Resurrection because Christ's appearance afterwards showed that He had risen. But when He ascended, it was of the utmost importance that men should be assured that He had really gone up to heaven. "Therefore in the presence of many witnesses He ascended into heaven: as if it were the natural close of His divine life on earth" (Gill's in loco.) 

Dr. Lingard says in his note on the Ascension of Christ: "With His ascent into heaven our Savior closed His earthly pilgrimage. He ascended before the eyes of His disciples: and this visible ascent was calculated, first, not only to confirm them in the belief of His mission, but to disabuse their minds of the notion that His was an earthly kingdom, a notion which they had cherished to that very moment. Second, to show that the place which He was going to prepare for His faithful servants (that where He was they also might be) was not on earth but in heaven; and third, to lay a firm foundation for our hopes, since we now must know that we have for our advocate with the Father ‘One who ever liveth to make intercession for us' (Heb. VII, 25.) And as God had quickened us together with Christ, and hath raised us up

together, so He will make us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus' (Eph. II, 6.)

Our Lord was not carried up to heaven in a chariot of fire as was Elias: nor by angels, as it is believed that the Blessed Virgin was after her death. The disciples saw Him go up of

Himself, taking up His human nature, body and soul, into heaven. In this way our Lord perfected the work of our redemption for which 

He came down from heaven. Of course our 

Lord, inasmuch as He is Himself God, equal to the Father in all things, is not on the right hand of God but on the throne of God Himself.

But inasmuch as He is truly man, His sacred humanity is placed at the right hand of 

God: that is the highest place in heaven. Sitteth at the right hand of God, the Father Almighty, is a metaphorical expression often used in Holy Scriptures. "This man, offering one sacrifice for sins, forever sitteth on the right hand of

God. From henceforth waiting until His enemies be made His footstool" (Heb. X, 12-13.) 

And again, "He who is on the right hand of

God, who also taketh intercession for us" (Rom. 

XXVII, 34.) To the true follower of Christ, the Ascension must ever be joyful because it is so hopeful. His Ascension is the foundation of our hope, as His Resurrection is the foundation of our faith.

"From Thence He Shall Come to 

Judge the Living and the Dead” 

HILE they were yet looking upon Him going into heaven, behold two men stood beside them in white garments, who also said: Ye men of Galilee, why stand ye looking up to heaven? This Jesus who has been taken up from you will come again as you have seen Him going into heaven" (Acts I, 2.) He will come in His sacred humanity, "as you have seen Him going into heaven. Here the angels put the Apostles in mind of another great truth, that Christ would once more come down from heaven and visit the world; not in humility to teach and save it, but attended by angels in glory to judge the living and the dead. His coming will be glorious and terrible according to the words— "Hereafter you shall see the Son of Man sitting on the right hand of the power of God, and coming in the clouds of heaven" (Matt. 

XXVI, 64.) Coming "in His majesty and all the angels with Him, then shall He sit upon the seat of His majesty" (Matt. XXV, 31.) 

St. Paul describes the coming of our Lord to judgment, saying that the Archangel of God shall come with great command, and shall sound the trumpet, and that in a moment the dead shall rise incorruptible.

St. John saw a vision of the judgment day- "I saw the dead, great and small, stand before the throne and the books were opened: and another book was opened, which is the book of life:

and the dead were judged by those things that were written in the books, according to their works. And the sea gave up the dead that were in it and death and hell gave up the dead that were in them, and they were judged every one according to their works" (Apoc. XX, 12.) 

In the simile of the sheep and the goats, St. 

Matthew describes the last judgment. All nations shall be gathered together before Him, and He shall separate them, one from another, as the shepherd separateth the sheep from the goats. "And He shall set the sheep on His right hand, but the goats on His left. Then shall the King say to them, that shall be on His right hand: Come ye blessed of My Father, possess you the kingdom prepared for you from the foundations of the world. For I was hungry, and you gave Me to eat: I was thirsty, and you gave Me to drink: I was a stranger, and you took Me in: naked, and you covered Me: sick, and you visited Me: I was in prison and you came to Me. And when did we see Thee a stranger and took

Then shall He say to them also that shall be on His left hand: Depart from Me, ye cursed, into everlasting fire which was pre-

pared for the devil and his angels. For I was hungry, and you gave Me not to eat: I was thirsty, and you gave Me not to drink. I was a stranger, and you took Me not in: naked and you covered Me not: sick and in prison and you did not visit Me. Then they also shall answer

He shall answer them, saying: Amen I say to you, as long as you did it not to one of these least ones neither did you do it to Me. And these shall go into everlasting punishment: but the just into life everlasting" (Matt. XXV, 32-46.) 

Here we may observe the words, "ye blessed of My Father," in contrast to the words, "ye cursed." Of My Father is not added to the curse, as it is to the blessing, because the blessing comes from God, the curse is brought on by the sinner himself. This judgment shall be man's works; that is, by our thoughts, our words and our deeds, we shall be judged. "Therefore judge not [in your thoughts] before the time: until the Lord come, who both will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of the heart: and then shall every man have praise from God" (I Cor. IV, 51.) "I say unto you that every idle word that men shall speak, they shall render an account for it in the day of judgment" (Matt. XII, 36.) "And they were judged every one according to their works" (Apoc. XX, 13.) "For we must all be manifested before the judgment seat of

Christ, that every one may receive the proper things according as he had done, whether in good or evil" (2 Cor. V, 10.)

Those who are to be judged are the living and the dead. "This is not limited to the interpretation as referring only to all who are dead before Christ's coming or then alive: because all are to die first: and even those alive up to that day of general judgment will by some means or other have to depart this life; their souls must be separated from their bodies by death before judgment. By the living is therefore meant the just, who leave this world in a state of grace, that is to say, alive with the divine principle of charity which causes us to love God above all things and our neighbor as ourselves. By the dead is meant those who appear before God in sin or devoid of charity. They are dead to the spiritual life of grace: as it is written, 'He who loves not abideth in death' "' (Rev. Arthur Divine, C.P.).

It is appointed unto men once to die, and after death judgment-the one is as certain as the other. The testimonies of the law and the prophets, the predictions of Christ and His Apostles, are many and so well known that they need not be repeated. There is nothing more certain or more clear than the general judgment. 

Dr. Lingard, speaking of the judgment at the last day, says: "When all the objects which God had in view in the creation of man shall have been fully accomplished then will Our Blessed Lord put an end to the present order of things

on our earth, and at the same time will satisfactorily solve the apparent anomalies in the way of Divine Providence: by summoning the whole human race before Him in judgment and alloting to each individual according to his previous conduct in this world and ever lasting inheritance of weal or woe in another." "For God hath appointed a day in which He will judge the world in righteousness, by that Man whom He had appointed, whom He hath raised from the dead" (Acts XVII, 31.) "To whom the Father hath given authority to execute judgment because He is the Son of Man" (John V, 27.)

Besides and before the general judgment which takes place at the end of the world, each one will be judged immediately after his death. 

Therefore the Holy Scriptures say: "It is easy before God in the day of death to reward every one according to his ways" (Ecclus. XI, 28.)

This is also in accordance with the promise of Our Savior to the good thief who confessed Him on the Cross: "Amen, I say to thee this day thou shalt be with Me in paradise." And with St. 

Paul's expressed wish, "to depart and be with 

Christ" (Phil. I)

When once the soul has left the body, it can no longer gain pardon or merit. All opportunity of altering its state before God is gone forever. 

It has to stand before the judgment seat of Christ and give an account of the things done in the body. The soul will stand before the bar of justice with the works of its life. By these

deeds its fate must be decided, and how miserable these works will then appear! Nor will the account be only of what we have done, but also of what we omitted to do. For we have each one of us duties, obligations and responsibilities according to our position in life, and we cannot omit these duties or even be negligent about them without sin. And Christ Himself tells us that He will then "render to every man according to his works" (Matt. XVI, 27.)

Some Facts to Be Remembered About Third Person of Trinity 

HE eighth article begins the third part of the Apostles' Creed, which speaks of the 

Third Person of the Blessed Trinity and of what

He has done in regard to us. There are three things coming under consideration in this article of the creed: Who the Holy Ghost is: His coming on the Apostles: and how He sanctifies us.

The word Holy Ghost is applied to the Third 

Person of the Blessed Trinity-Holy because 

He sanctifies us or makes us holy: Ghost or

Spirit from the Anglo-Saxon, Gast, which signifies spirit or breath as opposed to body. In speaking of the doctrine of the Blessed Trinity we showed that each Person is truly God. We have this dogma distinctly expressed in the 

Athanasian Creed. "The Father is God, the

Son is God, and the Holy Ghost is God; yet

there are not three Gods, but one God. The

Father is not made nor created, nor begotten; the Son is from the Father alone, not made nor created, but begotten; the Holy Ghost is from the Father and the Son, not made, nor begotten, but proceeding." While there is but one God, yet in this Divine Essence or Substance there are three Persons-the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost. Now what the Church believes and teaches is this-that the Father is God, and the Son is the same God, and the Holy Ghost is the same God. The division is in the Persons, not in the substance or Godhead. All three Persons are 

Holy Spirits, but the first and second Person are distinguished by other titles.

The Holy Ghost is sometimes called the Comforter or Consoler and often the Paraclete, which has the same meaning. We use the word "Spirit" in so many different senses that we must be careful to remember that when we speak of the 

Holy Ghost as "the Spirit of God" it does not mean merely the expression of God's mind or will, but that the Holy Ghost is a Person equally with the Father and the Son; and not a mere virtue or perfection of God. Thus the Holy 

Scriptures teach that the Holy Ghost is God and one with Father and the Son. "Why hath Satan tempted thy heart, that thou shouldst lie to the Holy Ghost? Thou hast not lied to men but to God." (Acts V, 3-4.) Macedonius in the fourth century denied that the Holy Ghost is God, but he was condemned at the General Council of Constantinople in the year 381. To in-

culate this doctrine, that the Holy Ghost is not merely a power but a Person, the Nicene Creed enlarges this article as follows: "I believe in the Holy Ghost, the Lord and giver of life, who proceedeth from the Father and the Son, who is adored and glorified with the Father and Son, who spake by the prophets." "Well did the 

Holy Ghost speak to our fathers by Isaias the prophet" (Acts XXVIII, 25.) The Holy Ghost being here spoken of as one who gives life, and as having spoken to men by the prophets, shows His distinct personality. God the Son was born of the Father, before all worlds: the Holy Ghost proceeds from the Father and the Son. Yet He is adored equally with the Father and the Son. 

He is the same Lord and God as They are, and has all the same divine attributes and perfections with Them. Nor is He merely a Spirit. 

He is the One great Spirit who is God.

"I have yet many things to say to you: but you cannot bear them now: but when the Spirit of Truth is come He will teach you all truth" (John XVI, 12-13.) "They returned to Jerusalem from the mount that is called Olivet, which is nigh to Jerusalem within a Sabbath day's journey. All these

This upper room is probably the same as that in which Our Savior instituted the Blessed Eucharist on the Feast of the Passover, and appeared to His disciples on the evening after the Resurrection, and on the morning of His Ascension. The Emperor Adrian relates that he found Jerusalem and the Jewish temple utterly destroyed: (excepting a few small houses, and a Christian Church of God.) He also asserts that this church was diminutive in size and was built upon the spot where the Apostles were gathered together after the Ascension of Christ. We have it also on the authority of Nicephorus that St. Helena, the mother of Constantine, here raised a magnificent temple the vestibule of which was on the very site of the house into whose upper room the Holy Ghost descended upon the Apostles on Pentecost.

And there appeared to them parted tongues as it were of fire, and it sat upon every one of them. This is considered to be the moment when the New Law of Grace was promulgated; when the new law-giver-the Spirit of Truth-descended upon the hearts of men. Pentecost comes from the Greek word pentecostes (hemera), meaning fiftieth day.

When the children of Israel were delivered from the bondage of Egypt, they passed through the Red sea and coming to Mount Sinai received the Old Covenant on the fiftieth day after the sacrifice of the Paschal Lamb: Therefore on this fiftieth day the Jews were gathered in Jerusalem from different nations to celebrate the anniversary of the law given to Moses on Mount 

Sinai. So on the fiftieth day after Christ the

Eternal Lamb of God was sacrificed for us on Golgotha, the New Law was given to us through the coming of the Holy Ghost, who teaches and enforces the keeping of God's Commandments

in their spirit, by His presence and influence on the hearts of men.

Again as on Mount Sinai the Mosaic Law, written on tables of stone, was promulgated in the midst of thunder and lightning, so it happened that the Holy Ghost, in the midst of a shower of fire and noise of a mighty wind promulgated the New Law to be written by the finger of God upon the hearts and conscience of men. "Written not with ink," says St. Paul, "but with the 

Spirit of the living God: not on tables of stone, but on the fleshy tables of the heart. (2 Cor.

III, 3.) This was also foretold through the prophet Jeremiah: "After those days, saith the

XI, 33.)

Pentecost was also the Jews' harvest-feast, which was celebrated at the conclusion of seven weeks from the offering of the weave-sheafthe first day after the great Sabbath or Passover.

Christ is said to have been born about the time that the Jews of Judea planted their grain. He was crucified at the beginning of their harvest time: and on the first day after the Feast of the

Passover, they offered to God a weave-sheaf. 

Seven weeks afterwards, when they had finished reaping and thrashing the wheat, the Hebrews were required to offer to God on the fiftieth day two loaves made from the new wheat. "And the Lord spoke to Moses saying: Thou shalt say to them, when you shall have entered

into the land which I shall give you, and shall reap your corn, you shall bring sheaves of ears, the first fruits of your harvest, to the priest; who shall lift up the sheaf before the Lord, the next day after the Sabbath. And on the same day that the sheaf is consecrated a lamb without blemish of the first year shall be killed for a holocaust of the Lord." (Leviticus XXIII, 10, 11, 12.) "You shall count therefore from the morrow after the Sabbath wherein you offered the sheaf of the first fruits, seven full weeks. 

Even unto the morrow after the seventh week be expired; that is to say, fifty days, and so you shall offer a new sacrifice to the Lord. Out of your dwellings two loaves of the first fruits, of two-tenths of flour leavened, which you shall bake for the first fruits of the Lord." (Leviticus 

XXIII, 15, 16, 17.)

Pentecost day is a harvest-feast in the Church in the highest sense, for Pentecost Sunday witnessed the birth of the kingdom of God upon earth. Henceforth, the Holy Ghost, the Spirit of Truth, the Soul of the Church, assists and guides the Church, and guards her from error. 

On this day the Apostles offered to God the first fruits of their Christian zeal and ministry. 

By the sermons preached by St. Peter, immediately after the descent of the Holy Ghost, about three thousand persons were converted to the faith. By the promulgation of the New 

Law, the Levitical Law was entirely abrogated, and on the establishment of the Christian Church God abandoned the temple and synagogues of

the Hebrews. The Sabbath became the Lord's day, and was consecrated to the commemoration of Christ's Resurrection and the coming of the

Holy Ghost. 

What a marvel to hear those same Apostles speak in divers tongues and to be clearly understood by Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and by people of all different nations who had come to Jerusalem to celebrate the solemnity of Pentecost! How wonderful to hear the name of Jesus, up to this time only heard within the boundaries of Judea and its immediate surroundings, all at once preached and proclaimed in every tongue, and made known to the whole world, so that the words of St. Paul may be said to be verified on that day: "That every tongue should confess that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the Glory of the 

Father." (Philip II, 11.) According to Cornelius a'Lapide, "the Apostles received the gift of tongues in a twofold manner: they could speak in all languages: and often, when speaking only in one language, they could be understood by people of different tongues and nations." 

Pentecost is called in England Whitsunday.

Whit stands for white, and the name White Sunday was given to the Feast of Pentecost on account of the white garments worn by the neophytes, who had been baptized on Saturday, the vigil of Pentecost. The liturgical name for the first Sunday after Easter is White Sunday.

Though the Apostles were taught by His own Divine lips, nevertheless He would not have them come before the people until the power of the Spirit of Truth had come to them to stamp

His own seal of eternal truth upon their every word. This power and personal communication of the Holy Ghost that came upon the Apostles was essential to the effectiveness and success of the Christian dispensation. For from that upper room in Jerusalem a power went forth such as man had never seen before: and their success on that day was only the first of a series of successes that continued to the end of their lives. 

In tracing the results of the great transforming event in the moral history of the world, the first and most important consideration is the influence that the power of the Holy Ghost had on the Apostles themselves.

Our Divine Savior had foretold His death, His 

Resurrection and His Ascension; but the minds of the Apostles, not yet dispossessed of their

ideas of a temporal reign of the Messias, and occupied by wonderful events then occurring, seem scarcely to have thought of these predictions till their fulfillment recalled them. Their conduct during the last days, before the death of Christ, and even after His Resurrection, is as natural as the narrative of it is artless. 

In those conversations recorded by St. John, Our Redeemer prepares them gradually for it: 

He exhorts them to mutual love, and to undoubting reliance on God: He prays with them and for them, in words of such tenderness as the ear till then had never heard. He makes His last will and testament, and these fishermen of Galilee are to be not only the inheritors, but the executors of all the treasures of His love. The 

Lord Jesus has been for days beleaguered by watching enemies; His own words grow more sadly affectionate. And now He whom they had expected not to die, but to reign, with farewell words of tenderest love leaves the Cenacle, and goes out to the Garden of Gethsemani, to the agony of its midnight hours, to His betrayal, crucifixion and death.

At His death they are dispersed, strangers in the city of Jerusalem, timid, powerless, selfdistrustful; like frightened sheep without a shepherd they are scattered in dismay. "I will strike the shepherd and the sheep of the flock shall be dispersed." (Zach. XIII, 7.)

Through all these events His disciples are as men that are stunned-walking in some strange and terrible dream. And such we might expect

to be the case. For who were they? 

A few poor fishermen of Galilee, not one of them at that time possessing a commanding spirit, who came up to the great city of David. There was the temple of the Jewish worship; there they beheld the 

Roman legionaries defiling through the streets, and garrisoning the stronghold. They had followed the Savior expecting, as they themselves say, according to the Jewish notion, that He would establish a temporal kingdom. And now a dark shadow comes across their hopes. It begins to be evident that Jesus of Nazareth is not to be a temporal prince for the present. And when the Magdalen informed them, "as they were all assembled together, that she had seen her risen Lord, they were slow to believe. Again on Ascension morning, only a short time after the risen Christ "had upbraided them with their hardness of heart, because they did not believe them who had seen Him after He was risen again" (Mark XVI, 14), while on the road from 

Bethany to Mount Olivet, their thoughts about the future greatness of their nation came back to their minds "Lord, wilt Thou at this time restore again the kingdom of Israel?" (Acts I, 6.) 

But on the fiftieth day after His Resurrection, as soon as they receive the Holy Spirit, their faith is quickened and strengthened; and there is suddenly a wondrous change. These ignorant men, lately so timid and dependent and selfdistrustful, are no longer ignorant or cowardly but suddenly wise, eloquent, filled with holy zeal

and courage so that to each of them might be applied what was said of the prophet Elias: "And Elias the prophet stood up, as a fire, and his words burnt like a torch." (Ecclus. XLVIII, 1.) They leave their hiding-place and come boldly forward—not one or two, but all of them, not one recreant. They are unappalled by the power of the government or the fury of the mob; ready to devote themselves to the propagation of the holiest truths and salvation of men from sin at every personal loss and hazard, even that of death. Surely, of all miraculous days, that was the most wonderful which saw so amazing and instantaneous a change in many persons, and such various characters. 

Peter especially took the leading part, preaching Christ, whom they had so recently crucified, but who was now risen and glorious and pointed out, quoting the old Scriptures, how the prophecies referring to the Messias were verified in Jesus of Nazareth. Thousands were converted to the faith that same day, and many were so affected that they sought advice from the Apos tles. "Now when they heard these things they had compunction in their hearts, and said to 

Peter and the rest of the Apostles: What shall we do, men and brethren? But Peter said to them: Do penance and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ, for the remission of your sins, and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost." (Acts II, 37-38.) "They therefore that received the word were

baptized, and there were added in that day about three thousand souls." (Acts II, 41.)

In this account of the events of the first Christ ian Pentecost we have evidence of what suffic ing help Our Redeemer had in readiness for His Apostles when He entrusted to them His Gospel. 

Wherever this Gospel was carried, the first great topic, which embraced everything else, was the death and Resurrection of the Lord Jesus-with them it was not merely Jesus who died, but 

Jesus who now lives in heaven and upon earth.

Before the high priests and the Sadducees, Peter and John preached and taught the people that Jesus rose from the dead. On the Mars

Hill, and at Athens, the great doctrine that St. 

Paul insisted on was that of the Resurrection. "If Christ be not risen then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain. Yea, and we are found false witnesses of God; because we have testified of God, that He rised up Christ. 

If Christ be not risen your faith is vain, and they which are fallen asleep in Christ are perished. But He is risen from the dead, and becomes the first-fruits of them that sleep." (I Cor. 

XV, 14, 15, 17, 20.) Not only did the Apostles preach the death and Resurrection of Jesus of

Nazareth, but they told the chief and fundamental truths of the Church, in the simple words of men—the Incarnation and birth, the life and death, the Resurrection and Ascension of the 

Messias. They wrote these truths sincerely, cherished them reverently, lived for them heroically, and died for them triumphantly.

But we should very poorly appreciate the Holy 

Ghost's kindling in their hearts the fire of faith, if we regarded it merely as producing a change in their speculative opinions. For the inward life of the disciples is changed even more than the outward. The object for which they labor is Christ Jesus our Lord. The joys for which they look are on the other side of the grave. The rewards of effort, save those that come from an approving conscience, the consolation of sorrow, the unbounded compensation for all sacrifices are all on the other side of the grave. 

Nor was it any sensual heaven to which they looked. The heathen poet found admission to the realm of shades by bearing in his hand the branch of a sacred tree. But the Apostles preached that the only road that led up to the 

Christian heaven was the road of justice, mercy, love and truth. All their ideas of heaven centered about their ascended Christ. That heaven for which they hoped-He was the image of its spirit-was a heaven of love, purity and peace. 

They wanted to join their loving Savior and be with Him evermore. 

The dying Stephen, as he looked up, implored 

Him, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit." One brief moment of agony and he would be with his God and Savior. The aged John, who had seen all his fellow Apostles receive the glorious crown of martyrdom, who was left alone of them to bear the ministry of reconciliation, in the midst of his tender exhortations, to his followers

to love one another, breathes for himself the touching prayer, "Come, Lord Jesus, come."

Holy Ghost Works Not Only in 

Church but in Individual Souls PRO

ROM the commencement of the world the Holy Ghost spoke to the prophets and inspired sacred writers. He was the life of the society of the children of God. On the day of Pentecost He became the living principle which animates and directs the Church that Jesus Christ had founded. Even as God the Son, the second Person of the Blessed Trinity, abode and abides in the sacred humanity which He took from the Blessed Virgin Mary, so God the Holy 

Ghost, the third Person of the Blessed Trinity, on Pentecost morning took up His dwelling in the Catholic Church and abides in her as the guardian of her sanctity, holiness and truth. He moved the sacred writers to write events communicated to them, and certain truths revealed to them, or known by experience, and guarded them against error in transmitting these truths to others. He remains with the Church by His special assistance, by which she is infallible in her teaching in all matters of faith and morals. 

He remains with her forever according to the promise of our Redeemer, to keep her invincible in the midst of her enemies, and fruitful in the good works of her saints.

We have shown before that to the Holy Ghost is specially attributed the giving of life: "I believe in the Holy Ghost the Lord and giver of life, who proceedeth from the Father and the Son . . . who spoke by the prophets'" (Council of Constantinople Creed). Thus when the Holy

Spirit came upon the Apostles and "filled the whole house" where they were, the breath of life was breathed into the Church. It became a living body through the indwelling of the Holy Ghost, who abides in her, and speaks not now through the prophets, but through her, who Christ Our Lord had framed. But when the 

Holy Ghost came down, He not only filled the whole house, but "sat on each one;" thus signifying that He works our sanctification not merely through the whole body of the Church, but in the soul of each one in particular. When we speak therefore of receiving the gift of the Holy Ghost, we do not mean merely that the Holy Spirit imparts a gift or grace or influence, but that the Father sends the Holy Spirit who Himself enters into us, dwells in us, as the heat which ever flows from the sun falls on us and enters into our system. The Holy Ghost sanctifies each soul and acts on it; the soul lives under 

His influence. This is no figure of speech, but what a supernatural life actually consists in. It is no more a figure of speech than when we speak of the sun making trees and plants grow. It is by their being in communication with the great source of light and heat that their inward powers are called forth into life and energy: and with-

out this they could not put forth leaf, flower or fruit. So when left to our own natural powers we can do no good towards our own salvation: that is, we are powerless to do anything supernatural, or deserving a supernatural reward, except under the influence and by the movements of the Holy Spirit, which, like the sun, is not cut off from its source, yet enters into the soul and dwells there. This supernatural or spiritual life is begun by Baptism, as our Lord told Nicodemus: "Unless a man be born again of water and the Holy Ghost he cannot enter into the kingdom of God." (John III, 5.)

Again when the soldier of Christ is anointed in Confirmation, when the repentent sinner appears before his crucified Redeemer imploring pardon for his sins, there is the Holy Spirit descending, operating, cleansing, and sanctifying the soul for His abode. The Holy Spirit therefore sanctifies men by His own presence and influence in the soul. This is the reason why our bodies are said to be the temples of the Holy Ghost, who is cast out of our souls by mortal sin: for the Holy Spirit "will not abide where iniquity cometh in." (Wis. I, 5.)

The presence of the Holy Ghost in the soul is called habitual grace. It is this grace that makes us the sons of God and pleasing to Him and which sanctifies the soul: hence this grace is also called sanctifying grace.

The expression "gift of the Holy Ghost" has two different meanings which we must take care not to confuse. As our Savior spoke of send-

ing the Holy Ghost, who came down on the Apostles on the first Christian Pentecost, He is spoken of as being Himself given to us. But when we speak of the gifts of the Holy Ghost, we mean the gifts the Holy Spirit Himself gives us-the effects of His coming to us-the different ways in which He works in us. His influence on the soul shows itself in seven different ways spoken of by the prophet Isias: "And the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him: the spirit of wisdom and of understanding, the spirit of knowledge and of piety. And he shall be filled with the spirit of the fear of the Lord." (Isa. II, 2, 3.)

St. Paul enumerates twelve fruits of the Holy 

Ghost "charity, joy, peace, patience, benignity, goodness, longanimity, mildness, faith, modesty, continency, chastity. (Gal. V. 22.) These twelve fruits are the results of the influence of the Holy Spirit, and are shown in man's life by his words and deeds.

In administering the sacrament of Confirmation the Bishop begins by invoking the Holy 

Spirit, asking that He may impart His seven gifts, or effects of His presence. And we should daily invoke the Holy Spirit of truth, love and holiness to give us His seven gifts and His twelve fruits by saying the following prayer: "O, Spirit of wisdom, govern all my thoughts, words and actions from this hour till the moment of my death. Spirit of understanding, enlighten and teach me. Spirit of counsel, direct my inexperience. Spirit of fortitude, strenghthen my weakness. Spirit of knowledge, instruct my ig-

norance. Spirit of piety, make me fervent in good works. Spirit of fear, restrain me from all evil. Holy Spirit, make me persevere in the service of God, and enable me to act on all occasions with goodness and benignity, patience and charity, joy and longanimity, mildness and fidelity. 

Let the heavenly virtues. of modesty, continency and charity adorn the temple Thou hast chosen for Thy abode. O, Spirit of peace, impart to me Thy peace. O, Spirit of holiness, by Thy all powerful grace, preserve my soul from every sin.”’

Besides these twelve fruits and seven gifts, the Holy Ghost gave to the Apostles and to others, who in those times were called on to do special work, some special miraculous powers to enable them to do it: such as the gift of tongues, of working miracles, of prophecy and of casting out devils.

These gifts differ from the seven gifts in this: that they are not given for the sake of the person who receives them, to sanctify him, but for the sake of others. Therefore when the Holy

Ghost sanctifies us now by His presence and gifts, He does not manifest His coming by appearing in the form of tongues, as He did on the first Christian Pentecost. Nor does He impart to those who receive Him the gift of tongues, as He did on the first days of Christianity. Those extraordinary gifts or graces were necessary to the infancy of the Church, to prove to the world that she was the work of God, and to strenghthen the faith of the new disciples. But they are no longer needed as Christ's kingdom on earth is

firmly established. In the same way a young tree when first planted needs to be occasionally watered, until it takes root: but these helps are withdrawn when the tree is firmly embedded in the soil. (Matt. XIII, 31, 32.) This tiny seed, planted in Judea by our
