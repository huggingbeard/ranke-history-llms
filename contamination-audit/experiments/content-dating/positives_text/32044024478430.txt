Foreword 

I am sure no one will read these gripping, illuminating chapters without a clearer understanding of applied Christianity. A mysterious, subtle power and an unseen dynamo transmits its supernal elements of radiance and warming cheer and transforming influence through the medium of a rare personality. Only the power of the Holy 

Spirit, operating through especially prepared lines of transmission, can possibly explain the marvelous and stupendous service this practical, hard-hitting, red-blooded, Spirit-charged man of God performed and is still demonstrating in the name of Jesus Christ and the Kingdom. During the last twenty-five years a splendid work for God and the city has been done at the Sunshine 

Chapel on West Fortieth Street, New York 

City. No one can learn even a small percentage of the facts concerning it without obtaining a new comprehension and appreciation of applied Christianity.

And the successes won for the welfare of a community the greater part of which had small regard for the laws of either God or man, can

only be accounted for by the power of the Holy Spirit of God operating largely through the vigorous personality, the large fund of "horsesense," and the far-seeing, fore-seeing and resourceful mind of its devoted and consecrated religious engineer. During this period of unparalleled material achievement in the great metropolis of the Western hemisphere, a determined, resolute, modern Greatheart has engaged in this mighty enterprise. Harry W. Murphy has performed a service to society, to civic and political emancipation and to the Kingdom of 

God, that transcends in permanence any mighty works that are measured by human yardsticks. 

Inexhaustible benefits follow the application of the dynamic life and spirit of Jesus Christ to practically impregnable fortresses of evil, the breaking of flinty hearts, the transforming of rough, unlikely carbon into jewels that will forever be invoiced among the precious gems of 

Heaven's treasures. No book within my knowledge provides so complete and comprehensive a picture of persistent, patient, indefatigable industry in God Almighty's business on Manhattan

Island, of how the strongholds of Satan crumble and "Hell's Kitchen" has been cleansed of its 10

deadly products through the laboratory processes of Sunshine Chapel, directed by that analytical chemist of human souls, Harry W. Murphy.

There are certain perfumes that cannot be hid, though invisible to the passing multitudes. This book teaches us that qualities, powers and graces can and do assert themselves through all obstructions, filling the very atmosphere of earth with the fragrance of Heaven. 

May the spiritual glow of Sunshine Chapel and the zeal of its torch-bearer continue to make manifest the Light of the World; the sinister name of this district become only a memory; while happy homes, the smiling faces of children and the orderly habits of men proclaim to all observers that the great Creator hath wrought wonders far greater in the fashioning of character out of Satan's garbage refuse and picked from his "Hell's Kitchen" of filth, than the workers in stone and steel have builded in their monuments of skyscraper dimensions.

Introduction 

This is a book of truth. The sketches are taken from real life. They are facts and not fiction. The only element in them as to which any erroneous impression can possibly arise lies in the fact that they are merely sketches, by no means containing all that could be written concerning persons mentioned. But I believe they contain a fair and sufficiently accurate representation of each story.

The incidents here mentioned occurred during the ordinary ministrations of a very humble servant of the Church of God, during twenty-five years of service in "Hell's Kitchen," in connection with the work of the Sunshine Chapel in 

New York City.

It is far from my purpose to create the thought or impression that all the people of this section were persons of ill-repute and belonged to the underworld. No, no; by no means. I have always admired the splendid courage and fortitude and faith of many of the people of this community. I have never seen anywhere courage to be compared to that of many whom I have known in the years of the past.

I have seen them face to face with the most difficult problems that could possibly be con 13

ceived. I have beheld them successfully sur- 'mount obstacles in an apparently unending succession. I have seen a devoted mother caring for her invalid husband and family of small children by night, and leaving the house in the early hours of the morning to earn her bread by day-working ceaselessly to maintain a humble home.

I have seen the love and devotion to God and their loved ones of broken-hearted parents as they would go time and time again to the rescue of children who had heedlessly gone with the multitudes to do evil, only to reap the bitter harvest of that which they had sown. 

In my pastoral visitations I have found many quaint characters along the river front who, so to speak, had been washed up on the waves of time and left behind; men and women, homeless, friendless, and what was far worse, hopeless.

Such unfortunates lie down at night to sleep as best they can, maybe in an empty box car, a stable, or in the hallway of a tenement house. They wake and find that the hours have passed, they know not how; yet perchance the interval may have been filled with dreams. They have been busy, during their slumbers, achieving great triumphs; they have made long journeys, and happily escaped old troubles. 

But as the morning has dawned, they have 14

. sprung up, and then at the rising of the sun have faced once more the problems of life as usual, and then dim and distant and unreal appear all these visions of the night. We have endeavored to help these men and women understand that time is the material out of which life is made, and have told them that though they may be stamped as the Forgotten by their fellows in the world, God in 

His great love and mercy waits to lift them out of their lives of sin and shame and change their whole life and make them new men in Christ 

Jesus; and, more than that, to send them forth as bright and shining lights in the kingdom of God.

In the words of an old Salvation Army chorus:

Diamonds in the dust we are seeking, seeking. 

Diamonds in the dust we are finding, finding; 

If shining when they die, Shall we see them by-and-bye

In the New Jerusalem on high. 

Among the population of "Hell's Kitchen" I have met many persons of fine old German, Irish, and New England stock, and these good people have formed the nucleus for a great mission work. 

Yes, we have found many good people here who became interested in our work; and this, in years gone by, was a source of great comfort to a young minister who was working under the most trying 15

circumstances against heart-breaking and almost overwhelming odds.

It was always a means of great encourage. ment to the writer to visit the homes of these people. I recall one family in particular, where there was an old lady who was totally blind: but while the great Book of Nature had been closed to her and she could not see the beautiful things of this life any more, or look into the faces of her loved ones because of this deep affliction, she had a calmness, a joy, and a happiness unspeakable and full of glory which permeated not only her life but the life of all in the home as well. There were a number of children in that family, and all of them became interested in the work of the Sunshine Chapel. Strange to say, the father was in the liquor business; but he conducted a very orderly place and would not tolerate disorderly conduct at any time. He would never take money from a man who had a family to support, but would advise him to take his wages home to his wife and children. He was then and is today, a friend to all and an enemy to none. 16

"Hell's Kitchen" Itself 

Let us go back fifty years or so to the days of the old horse cars and the beer signs on nearly every street corner announcing the "Biggest Schooner" in town and "Free Lunch" thrown in for good measure, and all for five cents. Many of the street cars in those days were equipped with carpets and easy chairs for the rich and wellto-do. 

The section between Thirtieth and Sixtieth Streets on the West Side was then inhabited by people of Irish and German extraction. Farming was a part of their occupation, and they had very little trouble finding purchasers for their produce, as there was a large public market at 

Thirty-fifth Street and Eleventh Avenue. 

"THE FORTY THIEVES" 

We have read of a character in "The Arabian 

Nights Entertainments," who made his way into the secret cave of the "Forty Thieves" by the use of the magic words "Open Sesame," this last word being the name of a certain kind of grain. In the days of which we write, "Hell's Kitchen" was 17

proud of a club which was called the "Forty

Thieves," with headquarters under the dock at the foot of West Thirty-ninth Street. No one ever made his way into the secret chamber of this group of gentlemen unless he were a member in good and regular standing; indeed, if one attempt ed to climb up some other way to gain admittance it was very doubtful if he would live long enough to tell his experience to anybody.

This club did not make a practice of holding up people at the point of a revolver, taking their money and very often their lives if any resistance was offered, as is the practice of certain sinister groups today. It has been said, however, that on one occasion the leader or the president of the "Forty Thieves" opened a box car which was loaded with turkeys for the Thanksgiving season, and was kind enough to supply everybody along Eleventh Avenue with a turkey for the holiday if they cared to accept it free of cost. It is further stated that when an officer of the law approached and attempted to place his man under arrest he drew his gun and killed the officer. then ran to Ninth Avenue and Thirty-ninth Street where two policemen stopped him; thereupon he snatched the club from one of the officers and knocked both of them down. Another officer came along whom he knew very well, and because He 18

of their friendship, he submitted to arrest and was taken to the station house and lodged in a cell. I have been told that during the night some one unlocked his cell; that he had made a set of springs and put them in his shoes, and that he jumped from the third floor into the yard adjoining the station house and escaped. It seems that he was caught later, but not until after a careful search of the entire community. At last it was found that the prisoner had jumped into the sewer and crawled to the Hudson River. He was finally captured and sent to prison, and while waiting for the time when he should expiate his crime, he died. 19

THE NEGRO INVASION 

About this time the negroes began to move into the neighborhood and locate right in the heart of the "Kitchen" on Thirty-ninth Street, between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues. As might be expected, the white people resented their coming to live among them, and were much displeased with their new neighbors.

It is not hard to imagine what this invasion meant, and the situation it created. The "Kitchen" now contained gangs with these names: The 

Irish, the Dutch, the Negro, the Macks, the Minutes, the Sittin' Bulls, the Wrens, the Black 

Jokes, the Hudson Dusters, the Gophers, the "Forty Thieves." There was also the Queen of "Hell's Kitchen," who was a woman of unusual physical strength who ruled with an iron hand and was never known to be defeated in a battle with either fists or clubs. It is, therefore, not surprising to the writer that this section was first named "Hell's Kitchen" by "Dutch Fred, the 

Cop," for there were riots and fighting going ou day and night on every front, and frequently the innocent citizens would have to run for shelter to keep from being struck by stray bullets. There 20

surely was action and plenty of it in those days and a bullet-proof vest and an asbestos suit of clothes were not at all out of order.

I have been told that during one of the riots a negro was tied to a lamp-post and put to death, and that on another occasion an officer of the law was trapped on the roof of one of the tenement houses by a group of negroes and almost cut to pieces. At another time the fire was so hot during a riot that the Allerton Hotel at Fortyfirst Street and Eleventh Avenue was set on fire and burned to the ground. More than that, those who were responsible for this fire would not allow the Volunteer Fire Department to approach near enough to attempt to extinguish the blaze. Not being satisfied with even this destruction, they went to the river front and set the old Weehawken ferry house on fire, and destroyed that structure also.

About the year 1903, the negroes began to leave the neighborhood and locate up in the "Fifties." They carried the idea and the spirit of "Hell's Kitchen" with them into that community, and today the entire West Side from Thirtieth to Sixtieth Street is known as "The Kitchen." 

This is not strictly correct, for "Hell's Kitchen" proper was Thirty-ninth Street between Tenth and Eleventh Avenues. 21

THE GOSPEL OF PEACE 

The devotional meetings of our earlier years were not characterized by dullness at all. In fact they were frequently exciting. At the signal of "Rough house" from one of the gang, the lights would suddenly be extinguished and a general smashing of chairs, dancing, and war-whoops would be heard in the darkness. 

I well remember the first night I attempted to hold a prayer service. There were about twenty-five boys and girls and two women present, who constituted the congregation for a part of the evening only. A crowd on the outside lined up and marched through the long, dimly lighted hall which led to the room we called the Chapel, and where the service was being held. There was a large stove in the corner of the room and a stove pipe ran across the entire length of the ceiling. The leader of the gang carried a club in his hand about the size of a baseball bat. He walked to the stove, and with one stroke of the club ho knocked the stove pipe all over the room. The congregation left the place quicker than it came in. I stood and looked on for a moment with amazement and wondered what it was all about. 22

I at once realized that if a man was to do any preaching here he must be a fighter as well, and that this was about as good a time as any to begin. 

I took hold of the leader who had broken up my service, and handled him with considerable roughness and finally threw him out of the place.

The rest of the gang followed him without any assistance from me. These fellows had probably taken advantage of the opportunity to make trouble for me because I had just told the policeman who had been assigned there to keep order in the Chapel that I would have no use for his services; and that if I needed help I would call him. I said: "You go and look after things on the outside; I think I am able to handle any eventuality which may arise within."

When the lights were relit we found the hymn books and the Bibles were in a very bad condition because of the rough handling; the chairs were broken, and the piano had lost two of its legs and many of the strings The attendance was mostly the boys and the girls of the neighborhood, with a sprinkling of a few women who were brave enough to risk the tricks that might be played on them by the crowd which usually gathered about the entrance. 23

The title "Sunshine," however appropriately it may have applied to the spirit of the work and the workers, seemed to savor somewhat of irony when applied to the old quarters of the Mission; for we then occupied the ground floor of a crowded tenement, and the entrance to the chapel room where the Friday and Sunday services were held, was through a dark, narrow, and often ill-smelling hallway, while the chapel itself and the adjoining club rooms were dark, dingy and unattractive. 

At this time the Sunday School was small and irregular, with but little system in its workings, and many of the scholars were possessed with the idea that the sole object of the work was to amuse them and fill their hands with loaves and fishes.

Some little interest was shown by a few of the people in the social part of the work, but there seemed to be a general indifference in regard to the spiritual side of it, until the writer began a systematic visitation of all the houses within range of the Mission, and with the incoming of a new and more earnest element and the positive enforcement of good order, rough-house tactics and boisterousness rapidly became unpopular, as far as the religious meetings were concerned. 24

Our next step was to get our clubs and social part of the work into proper working order. 

There was a club of boys ranging in age from sixteen to twenty, known as the "American Eagles."

This club was having what they called a "blowout❞ one evening and a gentleman from the Home Church at Fifth Avenue and Twenty-ninth Street, a very good friend of the Mission then, and who still is today, was giving this party. The Mission friend came and there were several ladies in the group who had come to see the sights, and this they certainly did. The ice cream had been delivered, games were played and a general allaround good time was enjoyed by the boys. But the one big event was yet to happen, and everybody looked forward to that with the usual joy of anticipation, namely the serving of the refreshments. Everything was set in readiness to serve the ice cream and cake, when suddenly one of the boys threw an egg, and it was not of the choicest variety. The thrower was a perfect shot, for it landed on top of the head of one of the ladies. 

The thrower said he thought an egg shampoo was in order. The Mission friend, who was master of ceremonies on this special occasion, arose and demanded that he be told who threw the egg. The information was not forthcoming and the guests were told that unless the name of the boy who did the throwing was revealed, they would receive 25

neither ice cream nor cake. In the meantime the lady was working frantically to get the egg contents out of her hair. The master of ceremonies went to the telephone and ordered a wagon to be sent over at once to take the ice cream and cake back to the store.

Thus the boys spoiled a good party for the night; and even today the question is sometimes heard: "Who Threw the Egg?" 26 26

A BLOW IN THE DARK 

On another occasion the Hudson Club, composed of young men, were having a pool tournament, and the contest was getting hotter and hotter by the minute. Suddenly the lights were extinguished and a free-for-all fight started, and the pool cues were used to good effect, many of them being broken over the boys' heads. I decided to act as peacemaker, and on entering the darkened room where the scrap was in progress I was met with a clout on the jaw from one of the boys. 

The blow caused me to stagger around for a few seconds, and when my head cleared sufficiently so that I was able to get my bearings, I turned on the lights and viewed the wreckage. All the cues had been broken, the chairs smashed and the cover torn from the table. 

I knew the young man who was responsible for the trouble, and who had struck me. I said to him, calling him by his name: "If you are in earnest about fighting and you really intended to strike me, the fight has just started and you will have to prove yourself a better man than I am before you leave this room." He excused himself and made the usual apology, and we clasped 27

That hands and were good friends thereafter. young man, who at times was very hard to handle, is now the agent in one of the South American Republics for an automobile company and is carrying on a very large and prosperous business. 

Another member of the same club who was present that evening is now a physician and praeticing medicine in New York City. 28

A FAVORITE FORM 

One of the means of enjoyment for the "boys" twenty-five years ago, was to "initiate" anybody and everybody who came to work among them: and the writer was no exception to the rule. At least that was the idea of the "boys;" but this ceremony did not meet with my approval, by any means.

I had only been in charge of the work three days when they thought it was about time to "give me the works," to use "Hell's Kitchen" vernacular, and Monday night was the time set apart for this "initiation" or induction into this crowd of "rough necks," as they were sometimes called. There were about thirty boys present, ranging in age from sixteen to twenty-two years.

When they thought they had me in a position of disadvantage they rushed me. First they went into a huddle and gave the signal, "Rough house." 

All the lights were suddenly extinguished, and we all had a very strenuous time together in the dark. 

I, however, frustrated their strategy by backing into a corner. I beat them at their own game.

Not a few of them were struck by the ten-pins, Indian clubs, chairs and other missiles thrown 29

about the room, and when the lights were turned on again, my attackers had fled, but not until many of them had been roughly handled. I must have hurt some of the boys myself, but I am glad to say not seriously.

During the next few days, many letters were sent to the Home Church at Fifth Avenue and Twenty-ninth Street, complaining of the treatment the writers had received on this occasion. 

As a result, Mr. D. came over at the suggestion of my dear friend, the late Dr. Burrell, to look the situation over, and to learn what was going on. 

When he arrived, he called the young fellows together in a special meeting and heard their con:plaints. I did not attend the meeting and did not wish to do so, but at the close Mr. D. sent for me. My attention was called to the fact that the boys had been handled very roughly, and some of them had been hurt. I was asked if I cared to say anything. I said: "If these boys want to play any game and have some fun I shall be very happy to take part in anything that is reasonable and fair, but when these fellows want to roll me about the floor like a football, they are up against the wrong proposition." Mr. D. spoke very briefly, but what he said had a good and lasting effect upon his hearers. He said: "We have engaged

Mr. Murphy to look after this work, and you boys will have to do as he says, or suffer the conse- 30

quences." And from that time to the present there has been no attempt to hold any more initiations of this kind. Most of those boys in after years became members of the church and did their full part and duty in carrying on the work of the Lord. 31

Athletics and Religion Ex-President Coolidge writing about the im portance of sport in the development of the young people, said, in the New York Herald-Tribune of 

September 13, 1930: "All the varied sports activities take people out of doors where they relax, recuperate, and gain new interests that broaden and sweeten life. 

They afford an outlet for primitive instincts which otherwise tend to turn in upon themselves, with disaster to the normal development of the individual and at cost to society. Plenty of playgrounds and games is the best cure for youthful delinquency. Plenty of outdoor sports is a wise investment in good citizenship."

We brought together a large number of boys and youths who had been brought up in the crowded tenement district where there were not many opportunities for them in the line of sports. 

So we set to work to get these fellows out into God's open country where they could breathe pure fresh air, enjoy the great works of Nature and find outlets for their natural instincts and develop physically, mentally and morally. We 32

gathered together an organization known as the 

Sunshine Athletic Association; the first thing we did was to organize a baseball club, and the boys conferred the very great honor upon me of election as their manager.

What a task! What a job! No manager of a 

Major League club ever had half the difficulties to contend with that I had in handling these live wires. An important part of my work was to select the best players and assign them to the positions on the club that they were best fitted to fill, and also to arrange for games with other clubs for Saturdays and holidays. We had a clear understanding that there would be no Sunday baseball as far as our team was concerned.

This was in 1904. We arranged the schedules for the season, and purchased the uniforms and other equipment. We played our first game at

Dumont, N. J. This was something new to the boys, as none of them had ever been members of a regular club before. The moment we alighted from the train my team scattered about the town, but all reached the ball field in time to play the game. We have all heard of the play "Ten Nights in a Bar Room." Well, sir, these fellows visited ten bar rooms in one hour and my ball club 33

played a shaky game; also during the innings, some of the players would slip away and visit the corner saloon across the road. If my memory serves me correctly, however, we won that ball game.

When we reached the dressing room after the game, I called a special meeting and talked very solemnly to the boys. I told them what embarrassment they had caused me personally by their actions and said: "I want you fellows to promise me that from this time on you will not visit any saloon in any town before or after the game. We represent a religious organization, and the people of the town realize that fact. We must set a good example and be real sports. The boys took my admonition in very good part, and from that time on none of our players ever darkened the doors of a saloon. The people were always anxious to have the Chapel club visit their town, year after year, and in two years' time we had developed one of the strongest amateur clubs traveling out of New York City.

We all looked forward to these games, from week to week, with a great deal of pleasure. Many of the players would take their wives and children along and make the occasion a family out- 34

ing. Our team was known as the Chapel A. C. and was very popular in New Jersey, Long Island and upper New York State for their good behavior and the fast snappy baseball the team played.

For many years our club competed against the 

Sing Sing prison and the Randall's Island clubs.

These games provided very impressive object lessons for the boys and they never evidenced any desire to remain very long after the game, at either of these places. Incidentally we became acquainted with many of the inmates, and when their time of imprisonment expired these men would always come to us if they were from New 

York City. We would always give a hearty welcome to these "boys," and if possible make a place for them on the team if they could play baseball.

We also did all we could for them in securing employment and guiding them in the right path. 

We organized several basketball clubs and many of the young men developed into expert players. Our Senior Club one season lost the championship of New York City by only one game and nearly always every year finished up among the leaders. About fifty of our fellows participated in an Athletic meet held at the Seventy-first Regiment Armory twenty years ago, 35

and many of them won medals in Relay Race, High Jump, Broad Jump, Shot-Put and Half- 

Mile Run.

Although we think that the salvation of God is an even better "cure for youthful delinquency" than athletic sports, we know by long experience that these certainly retard the development of criminal tendencies, first by filling the mind with other interests, and second, by keeping the individuals away from association with persons whose influence and example would certainly be pernicious. 36

SOME OF OUR "OLD" YOUNG PEOPLE 

This seems to be a good place to speak of some of the young men and women who were associated with us twenty-five years ago in our Sunday School, clubs and classes. 

Our Boys' Clubs were: the American Eagles, West Side Club, Citizens' Club, Burrell Club, Hudson Club, Young Knickerbockers, Payne Social Club, Denison Club, Iroquois and the Victory Association.

Our Girls' Clubs were the Acorn Club, Blue 

Bird Club, Girls' Friendly Senior, Girls' Friend ly Junior, Sunshine Club, Girls' Sewing Circle, and the Martha and Mary Society. The membership of these various clubs and classes included hundreds of young people, and it is not only interesting but important to note that nearly all of them, at various times, not only professed con:version and united with the church, but became active not only in the social but also in the relig. ious work.

Any one who has had experience in Social, Educational and Religious work can realize the many difficulties met with in getting together 37

two or three hundred people many of whom have little or no training at all along these lines, and bringing them up to a point where they comprehend what it all means and understand the high purposes we have in view for them and for others through them.

We were very fortunate in having many kind friends from the Home Church at Fifth Avenue and Twenty-ninth Street, who not only rendered us necessary financial assistance, but also gave us the benefit of both their time and talents freely and readily. They came to the Chapel week after week, and year after year, as leaders and teachers of the various clubs and classes. They came in all kinds of weather; and though they were often insulted and were at times the targets for missles of different kinds, they never faltered or evidenced the least fear or justifiable anger. 

With the blessing of God we led the men and wo: men and young people of "Hell's Kitchen" to a place where they realized that we were trying to help them and do them good and that our institution was erected and supported for the benefit of all the people of the community. 

The clubs and classes and other branches of the work were all created for the purpose of leading the members into the evangelistic part, and bring them into touch with the Saviour. This contact, after all, is he main and important fac- 38

tor in transforming the life and moulding the character. And this experience did send many of them out into the world properly equipped to deal successfully with the difficult problems of life, and take their places among men and women who are engaged in doing the work of the world. 39

WHY OUR TEAM DID NOT PLAY 

I became greatly interested in a young man in Sing Sing, who always pitched against our club when we played up there. He was an excellent ball player, and was serving a term of fifteen years. His mother, whom I knew very well, lived in the neighborhood of the Chapel. She was a good mother, too. The young man's time of imprisonment was drawing to a close, and one day 

I said to him: "When your time expires I want you to come to the Chapel and see me; we want to take you under our care and help you to go straight."

He was sent home in the early winter. The next summer we were scheduled to play the Prison team as usual, and this man had been assigned to our club. When we reached the institution the Warden asked me in his office. As I entered he said: "I notice you have So-and-So, (calling the boy by name), on your club and I want to tell you I shall not permit him to enter this place."

THE "HOUSE OF ALL NATIONS" 

Some years ago there was a tenement house known as the "House of All Nations," located on Eleventh Avenue, between Thirty-sixth and Thirty-seventh Streets, noted for the many nationalities represented among the tenants. I think a better name would have been the "House of Confusion" or the "House of Babel," for not a day passed without something to cause excitement—some one would start an argument and a freefor-all fight would follow. These arguments might begin in a mild form, usually by some remark which perhaps an Italian would make about an Irishman; of course the Italian would have to defend himself, and in a very short time the Irish, the Greek, the Pole, the French, the Austrian, the German, the Spanish, the Dane, and the Mexican, were all engaged in a veritable typhoon of words. The war would be waged on every front at once; also in the back yard and across the airshaft. Cooking utensils were brought into play with telling effect, and when the smoke of battle cleared away and an armistice was agreed upon, many were ready for the hospital and not infrequently one or more went to the Morgue. 78

The most shocking of all the crimes that I remember as having occurred in this building was committed by a young Greek who lived on the top floor with two brothers younger than himself. This man went home one evening from his work; the youngest of the three was doing his best to prepare the meal, while the other brother slept on the couch. The older one went into the kitchen, and, picking up a carving knife, walked to the couch where lay his sleeping brother and stabbed him to death. He at once began dismembering the body. He cut and sawed both limbs off, then the arms, and finally the head. 

The task now confronted him of getting rid of the dismembered body so that he would not be detected. He proceeded to carry the pieces out of the house; the arms were thrown into an excavation across the street, the limbs were hidden in the cellar, the trunk placed in the hall, and the head thrown into the Hudson River. The youngest brother of the three, only twelve years of age, was compelled to witness the awful crime, and also looked on while his brother dismembered the body and kept the door of the apartment locked while the murderer was carrying the pieces of the mutilated body to a safe hiding-place. 

I visited the room with a detective where this cold-blooded murder had been committed, 79

While I do not recollect clearly the final disposition of the case, I think the defence had little difficulty in making out a good case of insanity taking the form of homicidal mania; and that the homicide was sent to an asylum for the Criminal insane.

From the day that the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution was enacted, and the Volstead Act for its enforcement became a law, I have been an ardent supporter of the measure; and I was happy, as all other right-thinking people were, to see the corner saloon pass out, and to see the grocery, clothing, and furniture stores, and other centers of legitimate business taking their place.

I have spent a quarter of a century in this community, and I well recall the days of the old saloon which meant drunkenness, debauchery, suffering, and lack of food for many women and children. I have seen a man take the coat off his back and sell it for a few drinks. I have seeu men and women take the last cake of soap and can of condensed milk and dispose of them for booze, while the little children cried for a piece of bread. Many times I have been called into these so-called "homes" to protect the mother and children from a drunken and cruel husband and father. And often have I gone to the Court to plead with the judge to give the father or mother another chance. I have carried food and cloth- 107

ing into the homes for the hungry and unclad children, and have paid the rent to keep a roof over their heads. 

But since the Eighteenth Amendment has been written into the Constitution by the people of this country we are not called upon to perform such duties. I would say that Prohibition is not only a "noble experiment," as some have called it, but it has been an untold benefit which has greatly improved the moral and economic life of the nation.

The rampant violation of the Eighteenth Amendment is caused very largely by the laxity of those who have to do with its enforcement; and this laxity is caused by the manipulation both of appointments and of officials by politicians of both parties. These so-called Federal agents know where almost every "place" in every community is located, and they permit these speakeasies to operate in violation of the law. I have seen them time and again coming out of these places under the influence of drink, get into highpowered and expensive cars with a chauffeur at the wheel to drive them to the next place possibly to drink more and collect again. 

My observation of the situation is, that nearly all the bootleggers, rum-runners and racketeers are aliens and never intend to become citi- 108

zens of the country whose laws they openly violate for personal gain. They take advantage of every privilege and opportunity to further their own interests, but have little or no knowledge of their civic responsibilities and duties, have no conception of Governmental affairs, and are not interested in anything that would improve the life of the nation. It is here they live and move and have their being, and it is here they raise their families, use our public institutions, and aecumulate fortunes. They take every material benefit they can, but contribute nothing toward the advancement of American civilization or to help to keep the country in a state of prosperity and stability.

Let us both hear and heed the words of Lincoln as he speaks on law enforcement: "Let every American, every lover of liberty, every well-wisher to his posterity, swear by the blood of the Revolution never to violate in the least particular the laws of the country, and never to tolerate their violation by others. The fight has passed all stages of skirmish and preliminary maneuver, and is now concentrated upon a few vital issues. JUVENILE DELINQUENCY 

One of the problems which is causing a great deal of discussion today throughout the nation, especially in the larger cities, is that of juvenile delinquency. Much money has been spent and is being spent in the study and handling of this important matter, and also much time and thought. 

I doubt if ever, in the history of our country, have the laws of the land been treated with as much contempt by so many citizens-and aliens as we see today. When we see this same contempt manifested by responsible newspapers and "prominent" citizens, juvenile delinquency should not greatly surprise us.

My long and intensive study of this subject, and my wide and diverse experience of it at close range, have established the conviction that the origin of the problem lies in the homes from which these delinquents come. And in those homes, the most conspicuous figure is the absence of the Bible. Home training and discipline have largely disappeared from American life. Many parents seem to have no sense of responsibility for the welfare of their children beyond the supply of their physical needs and their education. 115

Sons and daughters are allowed to go their own way, plan the future for themselves and mould their own characters, within certain wide limits, of course, without any worthwhile parental guidance or direction.

To make matters worse, the example set by many parents even of means and standing in the community, is harmful, if not worse. The widespread violation of the Sabbath for pleasure or sport, involving the loss of the spiritual benefits of the church service and the Sunday School must react for evil on the children. And from contempt for the laws of God to contempt for the laws of man is but a short distance. 

It is time for the Nation to face the fact that the great army of young people is on the march, however well or ill equipped its individuals may be. In a few years they will head great industrial, financial and commercial establishments which will demand men and women who must be trustworthy as well as capable. The Legislative halls will call many young people, for both State and Federal governments must enact laws for the government of all the people. Yes, our youth will soon become the real active leaders in the business, political, religious and social life of the Nation. It is therefore important, nay vital, that the Church and the home co-operate in imparting valuable information to the rapidly growing 116

army of young people. Through the multiform social and economic agencies connected with the Church, many of them will realize how indispensable these divine insitutions are in the building of Christian character. If we can turn their faces, their minds and their interests toward the Sunday School and the Church we have accomplished much for the individual boy and girl and the general welfare of the community as well. 

We think this is a sure remedy for juvenile delinquency and a deterrent to crime, that I believe, will prevent the young from presenting a problem of delinquency of any kind.

The religion of Jesus Christ has an antidote for every poison, a balm for every wound, a cure for every malady. It is a river of life flowing full and free from a Divine fountain-head, for the healing of the nations. 117

A FINAL WORD 

Many centuries have passed away since a man of Macedonia appeared to the Apostle Paul in the watches of the night, and demanded of him that he cross the sea into Europe to "Come over into 

Macedonia and help us!" 

Cecil Rhodes, England's great empire builder, is quoted as having said, shortly before his death: "So little done, so much to do!" And as we look around us today in "Hell's Kitchen" we realize that in spite of all the good results that have come from the Divine blessing on our work during all these years, this section today is filled with men, women and children of "Macedonia" whose needs make the same demand on the children of God everywhere that was presented to the great Apostle during that memorable and historical Asiatic night.

Our Chapel is surrounded by people who are very different in many important respects from those with whom we worked in past years, but they are poor and needy indeed. Of course the greatest need of these people is the glorious Gospel of the grace of God. Their next greatest need is the knowledge of ways and means to help 118

themselves in the building of character and the making of the best use of such industrial and mental abilities as the individuals possess. 

Scores of our converts have very naturally departed to "better" neighborhoods, where they and their children are enjoying surroundings much more favorable both to their spiritual and temporal well-being. So that they no longer shine as spiritual lights in this spiritually and morally dark section.

It has been our aim all these years to make the Chapel a stronghold of righteousness for the people who are knocking at our doors, and whose only social life is in a place like our Chapel. We have been contending against the saloons, the speakeasies, the common dance halls and other places of ill-repute that attract the young people and lead them into sin and degradation. We have tried to make Christian men and women out of these people, and with a large degree of success, for which we thank God and take courage. 

We have taught and are teaching them the principles and practice of right thinking and right living.

Thirty years ago our work was started in the rear of a small store on Eleventh Avenue, with a few people who were looking for something better 119

Twenty-Five Years in "Hell's Kitchen' 

than life held for them, and a few workers who were trying to help these people from motives of social helpfulness rather than selfish aggrandisement.

During these thirty years we have grown from a struggling handful to a congregation of good and honorable people who seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness. This has been accomplished in no small measure by the generous financial help of the friends of the Chapel at the Marble Collegiate Church, New York City, and by the untiring efforts of a small but conscientious band of workers. The efforts of these workers have been effectively aided by the whole-hearted co-operation of the people themselves.

Our chapel is a two-story brick and limestone building, with a large basement where two bowling alleys have been installed. The ground floor contains an office, a small club room, the chapel itself and a large Bible-class room. The second floor has a girls' club room, a coat closet, a kitchen and a large assembly room where the kindergarten is conducted daily. All rules and regulations of the Board of Health as to sanitation and ventilation have been carefully carried out. 120
