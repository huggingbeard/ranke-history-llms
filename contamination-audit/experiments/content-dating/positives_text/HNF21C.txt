This summary gives the main points of Tax and Expenditure Policy for 1952, a Statement on National Policy issued April 21, 1952, by the Research and Policy Committee of the Committee for Economic Development. Full text of the statement is available free from CED, 444 Madison Ave., New York.

CCORDING TO THE PRESIDENT'S BUDGET, the federal government will incur a deficit in the cash-consolidated budget of about $10.4 billion in the fiscal year 

1953.1 The prospective deficit confronts the nation with this choice: Should expenditures be reduced below the budget proposals, should taxes be increased, or should a deficit be permitted?

This question is as difficult as it is important because: 

1) The present world situation seems to require large expenditures to promote the security of the 

United States. 

2) Present tax rates are extremely high, especially on individual and corporate incomes, and further increases would threaten the efficiency and growth of the economy. 

3) Despite the fact that inflation has been checked during the past year, a budget deficit would increase the risk of further inflation, with serious injury to the nation.

It is possible that a combination of good luck, vigorous monetary policy and direct controls would enable the nation to squeak through the next two years without further inflation despite annual deficits of, say, $7 billion. But this is a risky proposition in view of the serious consequences of being wrong. The objective should be not merely to skirt the edge of inflation but to reestablish confidence in the value of the dollar with the briefest possible reliance on price and wage con- 1All budget figures in this statement refer to the cash-consolidated budget. 

The federal fiscal year 1953 runs from July 1, 1952 to June 30, 1953. trols. Therefore, we must not add to the risk of inflation by incurring a deficit in fiscal 1953. 

The problem is how to finance the expenditures necessary to our national security and to other important objectives of national policy without inflation and without repressive taxation.

Analysis of the budget in the light of our present domestic and international situation leads us to the following conclusions: 

1) The cash budget should be balanced. 

2) Expenditures should be held down to the amount of revenue that will be obtained from present taxes, by operating all government programs more efficiently, and by reducing or deferring some programs not directly related to national security. This will require reducing the cost of the military and foreign assistance programs, but will not require reducing their effective size. 

3) If expenditures are not held down to the revenue from present taxes the budget should be balanced by enacting a temporary tax on retail sales except sales of food, housing and items already subject to direct federal excise taxes. 

The recommendation to hold down federal expenditures raises two questions: 

1) Can it be done without impairing provision for the national security and other essential government functions? 

2) Is it realistic to expect that expenditures will be held down, even though it be agreed that they

can be? We believe that the possibility of success in the effort to balance the budget is indicated by a growing determination on the part of the public, the Congress and certain government agencies. 

The short period of extremely high expenditure is likely to be followed by a long period of somewhat lower but still very high expenditures. Reforms in our fiscal procedures are necessary if we are to get through this period without inflation, without retarding the rate of economic growth, and without an elaborate harness of direct controls.

THE BUDGET PROBLEM FOR 1953 

How Big Is the Problem? The $10.4 billion cash deficit indicated in the President's budget message would result from estimated expenditures of $87.2 billion and estimated revenues from existing taxes of $76.8 billion in fiscal 1953. The estimate of expenditures appears somewhat high, even assuming adoption of the whole presidential program. For example, the rate of 

Defense Department expenditures to date indicates that military expenditures could fall short of the $58 billion budget estimate by $5 to $7 billion. If the program is vigorously pushed, it seems reasonable to believe that actual expenditures will still be about $3.5 billion below the estimate.

A reduction of this size in total estimated federal expenditures would leave a deficit of about $7 billion.

This $7 billion may be taken as a measure of the budgetary problem. 

Lower Expenditures or Higher Taxes? There are compelling reasons for balancing the budget by hold- "It is time to begin a national reconsideration of the principles of taxation. It would, in our opinion, be one of the most valuable contributions that any group could make to the health of our economy”. -from Tax and Expenditure Policy for 1952. ing down expenditures rather than by raising taxes. It is obviously desirable at any time to reduce expenditures to the extent that this can be done by more efficient operation of the government. Beyond this, we believe that an item-by-item analysis of the budget reveals many programs that can be reduced or deferred with less cost to the nation than would be involved in either raising taxes or running a deficit to pay for them. 

The benefits of an expenditure program must be very great to justify an increase in our already very high tax rates. Also a smaller budget is safer, from the standpoint of preventing inflation, than a large one. We believe that the budget can be balanced by achievable gains in government efficiency and by reduction of government programs that are, at least during this emergency period, less important to the nation than avoiding either a deficit or a tax increase.

CUTTING THE BUDGET Military Programs and Foreign Assistance. Of the 

$87.2 billion of expenditure proposed in the budget, $65.2 billion is for national security programs. The great bulk of this-$58.0 billion-is for the military functions of the Department of Defense and military aid. 

Two and a half billion dollars is for other foreign aid. The programs that make up the remainder of the national security total include atomic energy, civil defense, stockpiling and defense production and economic stabilization. Even after allowance for the $3.5 billion by which we estimate that the Department of Defense and military aid programs will fall short of the budget estimates, the $54.5 billion of expenditures in these two categories will still be 65 per cent of the whole budget. 

Obviously, the decisions made about these expenditures are critical for budget policy. 

We believe that the world situation requires a rapid build-up of our own military forces and assistance to friendly nations in building up their military and economic strength. But, with much of the necessary information not publicly available, we are unable to form a judgment about whether the programs should be of the size contemplated in the budget or, say, 10 per cent smaller or larger. Accordingly we are assuming here that the objective is to carry out military and foreign assistance programs of the scale contemplated in the budget. We believe, however, that more economical operation can substantially reduce the expenditures required for the programs contemplated in the budget without reducing the programs themselves. We also

I believe that foreign assistance-military and non-military-is as necessary to our own security as our own military program.

Economizing Without Loss of Security. Substantial economies in the security programs can be achieved in the following ways: 

1) More rigorous screening of procurement requirements. 

2) Reduction of inventory requirements through standardization and integration. 

3) More unified procurement. 

4) Design of military equipment to permit use of the most economical production methods. 

5) Design of military equipment to eliminate unnecessary performance characteristics. 

6) Elimination of specifications that unnecessarily limit sources of supply. 

7) Use of foreign sources of supply when they are cheaper. (In this connection, we believe that the 

President should report to Congress each year on the budgetary costs of compliance with the 

Buy-American and shipping preference statutes.) 

8) Economy in construction.

We believe that with vigorous and discriminating effort a saving of about $4 billion could be achieved in the annual expenditures of the Department of Defense (including foreign military aid) without impairing the effectiveness of the security programs. This = would be a reduction of slightly less than 10 per cent in the expenditures of the Department exclusive of military pay. This rate of saving applied to the Department's program for the next few years would add up to many times $4 billion.

The Department of Defense has made progress towards economy in recent years. Congress can also stimulate economy in the military establishment by requiring the Secretary of Defense to report to Con gress on, say, December 31, 1952, on the status, progress, problems and opportunities in those specified areas where the greatest possibility of economy is believed to lie. And the Secretary should appoint a commission of competent civilians to conduct a survey, advise on improvements and help prepare his report to Congress.

We believe that the proposed expenditure of $4.7 billion for security programs other than the military and foreign assistance programs can be reduced about "The idea that we are living in an Age of Inflation is gaining currency. Expenditures Outside The Security Field. Outside the national security programs the budget proposes expenditures of $22 billion. We believe that this figure can be cut by about $2.4 billion. About $1.1 billion of the expenditures proposed in the budget are new programs. There is a strong presumption against the introduction of new programs at this time, and we believe that expenditures under new programs should be held to not more than $100 or $200 million in fiscal 1953, unless larger reductions in existing programs than are recommended in this statement are achieved. 

With expenditure reductions of the magnitude recommended in this statement, the interest payments of the government would be about $150 million less than the figure in the budget. We recommend also that public works expenditures be cut $300 to $400 million below the budget figure of about $2 billion. 

About $7 billion of miscellaneous expenditures not already discussed are included in the budget. Curtailment of expenditures for the Federal National Mortgage Association, aid to agriculture and marginal programs, and savings through administrative economies could account for a cut of $900 million to $1 billion. 

IF EXPENDITURES ARE NOT REDUCED 

The expenditure reductions recommended and estimated above would approximately balance the budget for fiscal 1953. If expenditures are not sufficiently re-

duced to balance the budget, we believe that a temporary general retail sales tax, exempting sales of food, housing and items already subject to federal excises, would be the "least bad" way of financing an excess of expenditures over the yield of present taxes. Other sources of tax revenue cannot be exploited further without serious economic consequences. If it becomes necessary to enact a sales tax, the legislation should provide for its automatic termination on a specified date. 

It is extremely important that the budget be balanced by expenditure reductions rather than by additional taxation; there is no source of additional revenue to which strong objections cannot be raised.

The recommendation to hold down Federal expenditures raises two questions:

2) Is it realistic to expect that expenditures will be held down, even though it be agreed that they can be? We recognize the administrative difficulties and political pressures that stand in the way of economy. 

Adequate reductions will not be achieved unless the public, the Congress and the Administration have an unusually strong determination to achieve them. This determination appears to be strong and growing, at least on the part of the public, because of the heavy and growing burden of taxes. It is reflected in Congress by the cuts already made in appropriation bills and by the efforts of Congressional committees to find opportunities for saving. There are also indications of growing cost-consciousness in certain government departments and agencies. 

The House of Representatives has passed a bill that would hold 1953 expenditures by the Department of Defense $4 billion below the amount estimated in the budget. The acute budget problem of fiscal 1953 is largely an emergency and temporary problem. It results from the rapid increase in security expenditures initiated after the outbreak of the Korean fighting in 1950. 

Under present plans security expenditures will apparently begin to decline in calendar 1954, and the character of the budget problem will change. But the planned reduction in security expenditures to a maintenance level will not necessarily usher in a period of budgetary ease. 

The short period of extremely high expenditure is likely to be followed by a long period of somewhat lower but still very high expenditures. And in this uncertain world we must reckon with the possibility that unforeseen political and military developments may require large spurts in security expenditures.

Certain reforms in our fiscal procedures are necessary if we are to be confident of getting through this period successfully-which means without inflation, without retarding the rate of economic growth and without an elaborate harness of direct controls. We need to develop: 

1) Principles for making sound decisions about the relation between the amount of taxes and amount of government expenditures, and procedures for carrying those decisions into effect. These principles should recognize that the appropriate relation between revenues and taxes depends upon the level of economic activity

and upon the size of the budget. One aspect of this problem is the treatment of large temporary expenditures, especially recoverable expenditures such as loans and purchases of commodities for stockpiling. 

2) A tax structure that is capable of providing the necessary flow of revenues with minimum impairment of incentives to efficiency and enterprise and minimum discrimination among individuals and businesses. As the basis for such a structural reform we need a fundamental reappraisal of our existing tax system. 

3) Government procedures for keeping the rate of expenditures under firm, flexible control, for preventing the Topsy-like growth of expenditure commitments and for determining and executing large defense programs in an economical manner. 

4) Policies and techniques for managing the public debt so as to promote economic stability rather than instability. One aspect of this problem is the maintenance of a fluid and orderly securities market, to meet the needs of the Federal Treasury and private investors, without inflationary expansion of bank credit. Another aspect is the integration of savings bond policy into an overall stabilization program. 

Federal budget policy and tax reform have been the subject of a number of CED Policy Statements beginning in 1944.¹ The problems of expenditure control and debt management are now being intensively studied by CED. We do not propose to elaborate on these matters here but wish to call attention to the urgent need for forward-looking thought and action in these fields.

I. THE BUDGET PROBLEM FOR 1953 How Big is the Problem? 

The $10.4 billion cash deficit indicated in the President's Budget message would result from estimated expenditures of $87.2 billion and estimated revenues from existing taxes of $76.8 billion in fiscal 1953. It appears, however, that the estimate of expenditures is somewhat high, even assuming adoption of the whole Presidential program. Though small in relation to the total of expenditure, the adjustment has a substantial effect on the size of the deficit-reducing it by about one-third. 

The budget estimates that $58 billion will be spent by the Department of Defense in 1953 from its own appropriations (for military functions) and from the military aid share of the appropriation for the Mutual Security Program. Whether this amount will actually be spent will depend largely upon the rate of increase of production and procurement for military purposes. At the end of calendar 1951, Defense Department expenditures (including its expenditures for mutual security) were running at the annual rate of about $41 billion. Thus expenditure of $58 billion in fiscal 1953 implies an increase of about $17 billion in the rate of expenditure in one year. The rate at which actual expenditures have been increasing since the summer of 1951 would not produce an increase of more than $10 to $12 billion in a year. Moreover, the monthly figures show no sign of acceleration in the rate of increase. On this basis expenditures could fall short of the budget estimate by $5 to $7 billion. 

Deliveries and expenditures can be expected to flow more rapidly as the military program passes out of the design and tooling-up stage. On the other hand, expenditures in fiscal 1953 will be concentrated more heavily on payments for hard-to-produce equipment than was the case in fiscal 1951 and fiscal 1952. This may retard the growth of expenditure.

Just how much allowance to make for lag in expenditures behind the budget estimates is uncertain. If the program is vigorously pushed the lag probably will not reach $5 to $7 billion, but it seems reasonable to believe that actual expenditures would still be about $3.5 billion below the estimate.

In recent years tax receipts have consistently and substantially exceeded the budget estimates. This may again be the case in fiscal 1953. 

However, there is at present no strong evidence to support an estimate significantly different from the figure contained in the budget. When information on actual collections in fiscal 1952 becomes available, some revision in the estimates for 1953 may be indicated. For purposes of the present analysis we shall use the budget estimate of receipts. 

A reduction of $3.5 billion in the estimated expenditures for fiscal 

1953-which is suggested as probable-would leave a deficit of about $7 billion ($6,911 million). This $7 billion may be taken as a measure of the budgetary problem.

Why Not Have a Deficit? 

Unless strong affirmative action is taken there will be a deficit. This is the easiest course, but it is also the most dangerous-for two reasons: 

1) A deficit would increase the risk that inflation will start up again.

The Federal cash budget is now about in balance. That is, the Federal 

Government is now withdrawing about as much from the stream of private purchasing power, in taxes, as it is withdrawing from the stream of private supplies, by its own purchases. If we now switch to a deficit this balance will be upset. The government will be absorbing more output without absorbing an equivalent amount of private incomes. Private incomes available for expenditure will rise more (or fall less) than output available for private purchase.

2) The principle of balancing the budget is one of the main restraints on government expenditures. This principle means that if the 

Government is going to do the easy and popular thing of spending money it must also do the difficult and unpopular thing of raising taxes. This force for economy should not be weakened by abandonment of the payas-you-go principle under present circumstances. 

If the budget were smaller, this Committee would recommend a budget surplus under present conditions of high production and employment. A year ago we did recommend a budget policy of expenditure reductions and tax increases that would have yielded a surplus in this fiscal year (1952), instead of the balanced budget that now seems probable. In view of the large size of the 1953 budget we believe it wise that a stronger program of credit restraint and savings promotion should take over some of the burden of preventing inflation that would ordinarily be borne by a budget surplus. But at a minimum the budget should not become a positive inflationary force.

Lower Expenditures or Higher Taxes 

There are compelling reasons for balancing the budget by holding down expenditures rather than by raising taxes. 

It is obviously desirable at any time to reduce government expenditures to the extent that this can be done by more efficient operation of the government. When the budget is as large as it now is, the possible gains from greater efficiency become very large, and when tax rates are as high as they now are it becomes more urgent than ever to achieve the possible gains.

The possible savings in expenditures through greater efficiency, although large, do not seem to be large enough by themselves to balance the budget. If the budget is to be balanced without raising taxes, some of the

proposed programs must be reduced or postponed. The benefits of the proposed programs must be weighed against their costs. The weighing of benefits against costs must be done item by item. The outcome of this process of analysis is described in succeeding sections of this report. However, two considerations of general application to the weighing of benefits and costs should be pointed out here. 

1) If a deficit is to be avoided the question that must be asked about every item in the budget is whether its benefits are sufficiently great to justify an increase in taxes above present rates. Present tax rates are very high, much higher than the taxes prevailing when most of the non-security expenditures programs contained in the budget were first adopted or proposed. Any general increase in the rates of present taxes would have adverse effects upon incentives to work and upon the efficient organization and operation of production. Some government expenditure programs that were desirable when taxes were lower may not pass the test of worthwhileness when the cost of retaining them on the present scale is an increase of taxes above the present level. 

2) From the standpoint of preventing inflation, a smaller budget is safer than a large one. In general, higher taxes to meet higher expenditures will either reduce private saving or risk setting off a round of price and wage increases and thereby reducing the willingness or ability of individuals to save. In either case, the higher taxes fail to offset completely the inflationary influence of the higher expenditure. A balanced budget of $85 billion is likely to be more inflationary than a balanced budget of $80 billion, although less inflationary than a budget with $85 billion of expenditures and a deficit.

We believe that the cash budget can be balanced by achievable gains in government efficiency and by reduction of government programs that are, at least during this emergency period, less important to the nation than avoiding either a deficit or a tax increase. We must, however, recog-

nize the possibility that Congress, on the basis of different evidence or a different evaluation of the same evidence, may come to a different conclusion. Should this be the case, with expenditures remaining substantially in excess of prospective revenue, we recommend balancing the budget by a temporary tax on retail sales, except sales of food, housing and items already subject to direct Federal excises.* This way of financing the excess of expenditures would be less inflationary and less injurious to production 'than any alternative. The reasons for choosing this particular tax are discussed in the final section of this statement. *See footnote, page 3. 10

II. CUTTING THE BUDGET Military Programs and Foreign Assistance 

Of the $87.2 billion of expenditure proposed in the budget, $65.2 billion is for national security programs. The great bulk of this-$58.0 billion-is for the Department of Defense (military functions) and military aid. Two and a half billion dollars is for other foreign aid. The programs that make up the remainder of the national security total include atomic energy, civil defense, stockpiling and defense production and economic stabilization.

We have estimated, as explained above, that expenditures of the Defense Department and foreign military assistance would fall about $3.5 billion below the budget figure. Even after allowance for this, the $54.5 billion expenditures in these two categories would still be 65 per cent of the whole budget. Obviously, the decisions made about these expenditures are critical for budget policy. 

There are two main questions about this part of the budget. 

1) Is the scale of the program about right? Does reasonable provision for our security require the numbers of men, airplanes, naval units and so on for our own forces and the volume of deliveries to foreign countries that would be paid for by these expenditures? 

2) Can the program contemplated in the budget be carried out at less cost?

With respect to the first question—the real scale of the programs-the answer must be that we, the Research and Policy Committee of CED, do not know. This Committee is now preparing a separate statement about the scale and character of our national security programs that we hope will contribute to national discussion of this vital subject. 11

We believe that the world situation requires a rapid build-up of our own military forces and assistance to friendly nations in building up their military and economic strength. But we are unable to form a judgment about whether the programs should be of the size contemplated in the budget or, say, 10 per cent smaller or 10 per cent larger. The issues involved in answering such a question are complex; much of the necessary information is not available, at least to the public; and even after painstaking analysis with the best information available the answer would be a judgment not capable of proof.

It is our belief that if the proposed programs are necessary for our reasonable security they should not be curtailed by the thought that our national economy cannot bear them. But we wish to emphasize the importance of developing security programs and policies that meet our national objectives with the least possible cost. Also it is obviously essential to avoid waste in the execution even of necessary programs. 

We also believe that foreign assistance is as necessary to our own security as our own military program. The components of both the foreign assistance program and our own military program should be objectively examined from the standpoint of usefulness and efficiency. But this examination should not assume that a dollar spent for foreign assistance is less essential to our security than a dollar spent for our own forces. 

Similarly, there is no fundamental difference, from the standpoint of our own security, between foreign assistance given in the form of military end items and foreign assistance given in some other form. The form of the assistance is a matter of efficiency to be decided in each instance on the merits of the particular case.

For the purpose of this statement on budget policy, we assume that the objective is to carry out military and foreign assistance programs of the scale contemplated in the budget. But we believe that more economical operation can substantially reduce the cost of these programs reducing their effectiveness. programs without 12

The Department of Defense has made progress toward economy in recent years. We do not wish to detract from the credit due to the Office of the Secretary, the Defense Management Committee and other branches of the Department for what has already been achieved. The Department has had to work toward economy despite unusual difficulties. It has been responsible for carrying-through a large build-up while completing the fundamental organizational change to unification. And it has had to strike a balance between speed and durability in the build-up. 

Recognizing the achievements and difficulties of recent years, we nevertheless believe that large opportunities for further savings exist, especially now that the adjustment to unification and to the new defense program has been largely accomplished. 

In recent months a great deal of evidence has been revealed to indicate inefficiency in the operation of the security programs. Some of the evidence has been trivial; some has been subject to a variety of interpretations. But it definitely appears that substantial economies can be achieved in the following ways: 

1) More rigorous screening of procurement requirements. The decision as to the scale of the military programs is a decision as to the general size of the military forces to be in existence at a certain date-expressed in divisions, air wings, active ships and other units. Once this basic decision has been made it must be translated into requirements for the hundreds of thousands of things needed by forces of that size-from guns to soap. 

The requirements include not only the supplies and equipment that will actually be in the hands of active units but also reserve stocks. The calculation of requirements is extraordinarily difficult. Most of the material will, we hope, never be used in combat; if it has to be used, the time and conditions of use cannot be foreseen. The calculations involve not only an estimate of the total amounts needed in some future period but also an estimate of the supplies that will be available from current production 13

in that period. In the absence of effective review by higher authority there is a natural tendency on the part of the services to overestimate requirements. While major items, accounting for the majority of the expenditures, are reviewed by the Munitions Board and other supervisory bodies of the Defense Department, the items not so reviewed amount to a very large sum. Moreover, there is some question whether the Munitions. 

Board staff is large enough or sufficiently independent to conduct an adequate review of the items that come before it. 

The overstatement of the requirement for gas masks, which has now been corrected, is a classic case of the need for more rigorous review. The initial requirements of the three services, calculated from their standard tables of allowances, totalled nearly 13 million gas masks. This requirement was checked, outside the regular channels of review, and upon reconsideration a figure of less than 3 million was accepted as reasonable. 

The incident reveals the importance of reviewing not only the requirements but also the underlying formulae from which the requirements are computed. 

2) Reduction of inventory requirements through standardization and integration. There have been some notable recent cases in which the standardization of equipment so that one item can serve many uses—for example, one engine part can be used in several makes and types of engines --has reduced the amount of space and reserve stocks that have to be carried. There has also been evidence that the military services still have a long way to go in this direction. The necessary steps are (a) cataloging, to identify common items, (b) standardization, to eliminate unnecessary differences among items, (c) provision for interchange of common items among different uses and users, by methods ranging from exchange of information to establishment of common supply pools and procedures. 

3) More unified procurement. Even before unification the savings that could be effected by unified procurement of common items were recog- 14

nized by such practices as central procurement of coal and certain Quartermaster supplies for both the Army and the Navy. Since unification further steps have been taken, medical supplies being one instance. Congressional investigations have called attention to other opportunities for saving by better integrated procurement of common items, such as clothing. 

4) Design of military equipment to permit use of most economical production methods. Instances have been reported in which changes in the specifications required by the military services would permit more economical production of equipment without sacrifice of its performance characteristics. It seems to be true that in the design of military equipment insufficient attention has been given to the problem of getting the desired military performance-in terms of speed, fire-power, durability, etc.at the least cost in money, materials, machine hours and manpower. Some beginning steps have been taken to make fuller use of industry's production skill by specifying military products only in terms of their performance characteristics, leaving design of production to the supplier. This seems to us a promising approach.

One aspect of this problem is the design of equipment to make maximum use of our existing production capacity. The great civilian-type productive capacity of the United States, and its convertibility to military production, have been the basis of our military strength. Serious attention needs to be given to preserving this advantage of convertibility as far as is consistent with a technological trend toward more specialized military equipment requiring more specialized production capacity. 

5) Design of military equipment to eliminate unnecessary performance characteristics. This is a different point from the one just mentioned, which referred to the achievement of given performance characteristics at lowest cost. The question raised here is whether all the performance characteristics specified for military equipment are necessary or important. What 15

proportion of non-combat vehicles need to be equipped to operate under combat conditions? Do military garbage trucks need automatic gear shifts?

There is almost nothing that cannot be justified as a factor of insurance or morale, and great weight needs to be given to expert military judgment on such matters. But we believe that critical examination of these judgments could result in economies. 

6) Elimination of specifications that unnecessarily limit sources of supply. 

Congressional investigations have disclosed a number of cases in which military contracts for civilian type items specified incidental features irrelevant to performance characteristics, thereby restricting the number of suppliers who could successfully bid on the contract. In many cases low bids have had to be rejected because of failure to meet such irrelevant specifications. 

7) 'Use of foreign sources of supply when they are cheaper. By Executive 

Order issued under the President's Emergency powers the Department of Defense has been exempted from the provisions of the "Buy American" Act which require preference to domestic suppliers. However, the Department has in general continued to give preference to domestic suppliers except where the foreign supplier's bid is at least 25 per cent below the lowest American bid.

When the low foreign bid is rejected there is a possible triple loss to the U. S. Government. We lose the amount by which the foreign bid is low, plus the amount of import duty that would in some cases have been paid on the foreign supplies, plus the amount of foreign aid that may be necessary because of the inability of the foreign country to earn dollars. We believe that in the interest of economy, as well as in the interest of a consistent foreign economic policy, purchases should be made from the lowest competent bidder, except where there is a clear national interest in maintaining a source of supply other than the lowest bidder. 

For similar reasons we believe that present laws requiring prefer- 16

ence to American ships in exports under government programs should be relaxed. Also, the President should report to the Congress each year on the budgetary costs of compliance with the Buy-American and shipping preference statutes. 

8) Economy in construction. Congressional investigators have recently reported waste-allegedly involving millions of dollars-in overseas military construction. Earlier reports pointed to apparently unnecessarily expensive standards of construction in parts of the program. The full story on these matters is not yet in, but at the least disturbing questions have been raised. The importance of economy in planning and execution of military construction is suggested by the fact that the budget included $3.5 billion of expenditures for this purpose in fiscal 1953. 

This is not an exhaustive list of the ways in which expenditures can be held down, although we believe that it includes some of the more important possibilities. In addition mention may be made of such other possibilities as eliminating unnecessary flight pay, eliminating research projects of marginal value, and eliminating payments to unnecessary middlemen.

We wish to emphasize that the objective of economy in the defense program is not only to achieve a given military force with less expenditure but also to increase our potential military strength by improving the efficiency with which the military establishment uses resources. If we should ever again have to mount an all-out military effort, the size of the military force we can produce will depend largely upon the efficiency of military management. The difference between 10% more or less efficiency then would be the difference between 10% more or less fighting strength. This greatly increases the importance, as a preparedness measure, of developing the most efficient possible organization and procedures in the military establishment now. 

There is, in our opinion, sufficient evidence to demonstrate that 17

savings can be made. However, the evidence leaves one question of great difficulty. How much can be saved?

It is literally impossible to say how much could be saved. The cases that prove the existence of situations in which savings are possible do not tell just how extensive these kinds of situations are. The total amount of saving that is possible could be definitely estimated only in the process of actually making the saving. As Congress goes through the detailed hearings on the military and foreign aid appropriations it will be able to make a firmer judgment about the amount of possible saving. But even this judgment will be subject to a considerable margin of uncertainty.

Estimates of the amount that can be saved are required for two purposes. First, we need such estimates in order to decide how much tax increase, if any, is required to balance the budget. Second, such estimates are needed in order to decide how much to appropriate. The use of the estimates of savings for these two purposes involves different risks. If the possible saving is overestimated in calculating tax requirements there will be an unintended deficit. If the possible saving is overestimated in deciding on appropriations there will be an unintended reduction in the real program. The effort to cut fat out of the program would unintentionally cut into the muscle. 

We suggest that for purposes of calculating tax requirements the Congress should make a reasonable estimate of the total possible saving, including allowance for savings that cannot be specified and measured but which evidence indicates can be achieved by Administrative action. 

For purposes of determining appropriations we suggest that allowance should be made only for those savings that Congress can specify, measure and require.

We believe that with vigorous and discriminating effort a saving of 18

around $billion could be achieved in the annual expenditures of the Department of Defense (including foreign military aid) without impairing the effectiveness of the security programs. This would be a reduction of slightly less than ten per cent in the expenditures of the Department exclusive of military pay. This rate of saving applied to the Department's program for the next few years would add up to many times $4 billion. As already noted we recognize the uncertainties with which such an estimate is surrounded, and expect that Congress will be able to form a more confident judgment after the appropriation hearings. 

The procedure suggested would leave in the Department responsibility for achieving a large part, probably most, of the economies believed possible. However, Congress has made, and can continue to make, a valuable contribution to military economy by the investigations of its various committees. to stimulate economy in In addition Congress can take another step to stimulate the military establishment. It can require the Secretary of Defense to report to Congress on, say, December 31, 1952, on the status, progress, problems and opportunities in those specified areas where the greatest possibility for economy is believed to lie-such as requirements, inventories, specifications and procurement practices. The Secretary should appoint a commission of competent civilians to survey the operations of the Department, advise him on improvements, and assist him in preparing his report to Congress. This Commission and report would, we believe, stimulate action within the Department and lay the basis for further action by Congress if necessary.

New Expenditure Programs 

About $1.1 billion of the expenditures proposed in the budget are new programs, in the sense that new authorizing legislation must be enacted before funds can be appropriated for them. The main new items proposed are Federal aid to general education, extension and expansion of Federal defense housing, and increased benefit rates for old age and survivors' insurance, public assistance and veterans' benefits.

There is a strong presumption against the introduction of new expenditure programs at this time, which necessarily leads to a recommendation for much deeper cuts in proposed programs than in existing programs. Part of the expenditures under existing programs is required by legal commitments. In other cases the long existence of a government program has created a kind of moral commitment to persons who have arranged their affairs in the expectation that it will continue. In still other cases a temporary reduction and subsequent restoration of an 21

existing program may be inefficient. For these reasons a much larger proportion of the "cuttable" expenditures is concentrated in the new expenditure programs than is suggested by the fact that they account for less than 2 per cent of the total budget. 

Moreover, the introduction of new programs at this time will increase commitments for expenditure in 1954 and subsequent years. In order to regain some flexibility in control of expenditures, assumption of new commitments should be deferred until some of the existing commitments have been reduced.

Public Works 

Of approximately $2 billion of civil public works expenditures provided in the budget under existing legislation, $1.1 billion is for land and water development projects. Most of these are power projects or 22

In view of the need for additional power as an element in the national security program only a relatively small cut of $100 to $150 million is suggested in these projects. However, we believe that Congress should review these projects to determine their relation to the security program. Expenditures for development of land and water resources had been rising sharply for several years, even before the beginning of the present defense program. It is not clear to what extent the present rate of expenditure is necessary for defense and to what extent defense needs are used to rationalize continuation of programs initiated for other reasons. Moreover, it is not clear whether the projects contained in the budget are economical ways of providing power as compared with private projects or public projects less heavily weighted with flood control and irrigation. 

Such a study may reveal the possibility of greater reduction than the $100 million recommended here.

The remaining $200 to $250 million cut proposed in public works expenditures would be achieved by reductions in recommended expenditures for new hospitals (mainly veterans' hospitals) and schools, in starts of new units under the public housing program, and in expenditures for transportation facilities, including rivers and harbors. With respect to new hospitals, note should be taken of the incomplete utilization of many existing Federal hospitals and of the difficulty of obtaining medical staffs for new hospitals. The largest item in the transportation category is almost $400 million for Federal aid to state highway construction. This amount cannot be touched in 1953 under existing commitments to the states. We recommend that future authorizations for this program give the government greater flexibility in controlling the timing of its own expenditures.

The Federal National Mortgage Association serves as a secondary market for purchase of FHA and Veterans Administration guaranteed housing mortgages. Reduction of this portfolio to at most $2 billion by curtailing purchases and increasing sales would reduce budget expenditures by a minimum of $250 million. Neither of these programs can be curtailed in fiscal 

1953, short of quite drastic action. Most of the expenditures under this program are payments to commercial farmers for engaging in such practices as the use of fertilizer that are profitable to them and which they would continue without subsidy.

III. IF EXPENDITURES ARE NOT REDUCED THE EXPENDITURE REDUCTIONS recommended and estimated in the preceding sections of this statement would approximately balance the budget for fiscal 1953.

It is extremely important that the budget should be balanced by expenditure reductions. How important can only be appreciated by canvassing the tax sources from which any substantial amount of additional revenue could be obtained. There is no source of additional revenue to which strong objections cannot be raised.

Nevertheless, if expenditures are not reduced sufficiently to justify reasonable confidence that the budget will be kept in balance, additional taxes should be imposed. If revenue needs above the yield of present taxes are large, this should take the form of a temporary tax generally applicable to retail sales, except sales of food, housing and items already subject to Federal excises.* Such a tax would be the "least bad" way of financing an excess of expenditures over the yield of present taxes. 

There are four general sources from which substantial additional tax revenue might be sought-corporate profits taxation, individual income taxation, selective excise taxes and a fairly general sales tax. 

Corporate profits taxes have been increased three times since mid- 1950. The standard rate of tax, applicable to all but small corporations, is 

52 per cent of net income. In addition, excess-profits tax of 30 per cent is payable on income exceeding 83% of the average income in the three best years of the base period 1946-49. On the average for all corporations almost 60% of net income is taken in Federal taxes while the figure is as high as 70% for some corporations. Moreover, a great many corporations are now in the position where they retain for savings or dividends only 18 cents out of a dollar of additional income. 

See footnote, page 3. 26

There seems to be general recognition now that this source of tax revenue cannot be exploited further. Corporate taxes have long since reached the point where they seriously impede efforts of business management to promote efficiency and restrain unnecessary expenditure.

Moreover, additional corporate taxes would come almost entirely out of corporate saving, and would either reduce investment or fail to achieve the anti-inflationary result that should be the main objective of additional taxes.

The position is similar with respect to the individual income tax, especially in the upper brackets. The total amount of income remaining after tax in the upper brackets is now so small, and the rates so high, that significant amounts of revenue could be obtained only by nearly complete elimination of income incentives to work and investment. 

Moreover, the revenue so obtained would be of little value from the anti-inflationary standpoint since it would mainly come out of saving. 

The possibility of obtaining revenue in the lower brackets is somewhat better because the aggregate incomes are larger and the present taxes lower. But the lowest bracket tax rate is already 22.2 per cent of income after exemptions. There is danger that higher rates would reduce incentives to work, especially the incentive of a wife or other second worker in a family to seek employment. Moreover, higher rates would intensify discrimination against wage earners, whose incomes are fully covered by withholding, as compared with self-employed persons whose incomes may not be fully reached by the income tax. 

Further increases in selective excises should, in our opinion, be ruled out on the ground that there is no reasonable basis for selecting the objects of such taxes. The purpose of additional taxation at this time is to exert a general restraint on private expenditures, and for that purpose a general tax is indicated.

Mainly because of the defects in the alternatives, a fairly general 27

retail sales tax is best suited to our needs as a temporary revenue source if expenditures are not held down sufficiently to balance the budget. 

The chief problem in such a tax is to avoid the imposition of undue burdens on persons with very low incomes. This can be minimized by exempting food and housing from the tax. We also recommend that items now subject to Federal excises, like tobacco, automobiles, etc., should be exempted from the tax.

Such a tax would be effective in restraining private expenditures and thus in helping to prevent further inflation. Moreover, it would have much less effect upon incentives to work than an increase in income tax, which bears much more heavily upon marginal income, such as overtime earnings or the pay of a working wife. 

If it becomes necessary to enact a sales tax, the legislation should provide for its automatic termination on a specified date, as has been done in the case of the excess-profits tax. 28

IV. IMPROVING THE TAX STRUCTURE 

AS ALREADY EMPHASIZED we believe it is both possible and necessary to balance the fiscal 1953 budget without any general tax increase. However, even if this course is followed there are urgent tax questions deserving national attention.

Since 1944 this Committee has repeatedly pointed out the need for reform of the Federal tax system to make it more equitable and less repressive of productive efficiency and economic growth. Some forward steps have been taken in the past eight years. Equal tax treatment of married couples throughout the nation has been provided by permitting all married couples to compute their tax as if their income was equally divided between them-as could only be done in the community property states prior to 1948. 

Another improvement was the extension of the period for which business losses could be carried forward to offset against subsequent profits. But on the whole, progress has been slight. In a sense tax reform is a more urgent problem now than it seemed even in 1944 when we issued our first statement on this subject. In 1944 we could expect that the end of the war would bring a radical reduction in revenue requirements, which would automatically soften the impact of the deficiencies in the tax system. Now the prospect of getting back to what was generally regarded as a "normal" peace-time tax burden is remote. 

Many of the basic reforms needed in our tax system would involve a loss of revenue. These can only be undertaken as part of a general tax reduction or as part of a general tax overhaul in which better revenue sources are substituted for inferior ones. This means that the possibilities of action this year to improve the tax system are narrowly limited. Nevertheless some steps can be taken to improve the tax structure and also increase the revenue, or at least not reduce it. 29

While the immediately possible reforms are important, as steps to equity and efficiency, it would be a mistake to expect any substantial revenue from them. There have been suggestions that large sums, running sometimes as high as $5 billion, could be obtained by closing socalled tax "loopholes". Such calculations rest upon including in the category of "loopholes" certain basic aspects of established tax policy that are not "loopholes" in any definition. One important example from the point of view of revenue yields is the present provision for equal tax treatment of all married couples.

Though basic tax reforms cannot be enacted this year, it is time to lay the groundwork for such reforms. The post-war experience showed that even when the opportunity for tax reform came the opportunity was not taken because the basis of study and understanding had not been laid.

Fundamental decisions about our tax structure will have to be made in the next few years. Many of the emergency tax increases enacted in 

1951 and in 1952 will automatically expire in fiscal 1954. Even if that were not the case, the passage of time will make continuation of the present tax system more and more obviously undesirable. More important, the philosophy of taxation underlying not only our emergency tax legislation but also the tax legislation of earlier years is not likely to lead to basic reform when the occasion for revision comes. 

It is time to begin a national reconsideration of the principles of taxation. A period in which we are freed from the necessity of immediately raising an additional X billion dollars should provide an opportunity for objective thought about basic questions. How can taxation of corporation profits and individual incomes be related so as to avoid discrimination in favor of or against profits as a source of income? What is the function of excise taxation in the Federal revenue system, and what are the relative advantages of general and particular excises? What limits 30

upon the progressiveness of the rate structure are set by considerations of economic efficiency and growth?

We believe that it would be appropriate and desirable for the Joint Committee on Internal Revenue Taxation of the Congress to initiate a study of our tax structure. A study conducted by this Committee could obtain information that could not otherwise be assembled and arouse more public interest than could be attracted in any other way. It would, in our opinion, be one of the most valuable contributions that any group could make to the health of our economy. Our goal should be the smallest budget consistent with our national responsibilities, financed by the best possible tax system. A start should be made now upon the necessarily slow process of improving the tax system. And immediate action should be taken to hold down expenditures. We believe that a determined and discriminating policy by Congress and the Administration can hold Federal expenditures for next year down to the revenue from existing taxes without reducing the effectiveness of the planned security program. It is imperative that this be done, if we are to have not only the military tools of security but also the economic stability and growth upon which our national security and welfare depend. 31

The Committee for Economic Development 

HE COMMITTEE FOR ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT is a non-profit organization of 

130 leading businessmen and educators who are devoting their time, experience and resources to objective economic research and education. It is sup- ported by voluntary contributions from business and industry and is non-partisan and non-political.

CED was incorporated in September, 1942, with Paul G. Hoffman as its first chairman. It grew out of an idea advanced in 1939 by Mr. Hoffman and other leaders in business and education that "we should enlist the services of the best brains from our universities and from business and put them to work in solving the problems of how high productive employment can be attained and maintained in a free society."

BASIC OBJECTIVES 

CED is dedicated to these basic objectives: 

1) To develop, through objective research and discussion, findings and recommendations for business and public policy which will contribute to the preservation and strengthening of our free society, and to the maintenance of high employment, increasing productivity and living standards, greater economic stability and greater opportunity for all our people. 

2) To bring about increasing public understanding of the importance of these objectives and the ways in which they can be achieved. 

The first objective is the responsibility of CED's Research and Policy Committee; the second, the responsibility of the Information Committee and the Business- 

Education Committee.
