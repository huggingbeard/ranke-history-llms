PART ONE Before Taking the Bench

'ederal judges exercise wide authority and discretion. They determine civil cases and bankruptcy cases filed under federal law, adjudicate prosecutions of federal crimes, hear state law suits removed from the state courts, and review the constitutionality of criminal convictions imposed for violation of state laws. Although the jurisdiction of federal courts is defined and limited by Article III of the United States Constitution and by statute, it is impressively broad and touches upon many of the most significant issues affecting the American people. 

But these weighty adjudicative responsibilities do not encompass the entirety of a judge's task. Judges must balance their daily caseload demands with the managerial oversight of their courts and supervision of their chambers and staffs. Before they can try their first case, appointment paperwork must be processed, the oath of office taken, and chambers staff hired. For new judges, especially those without prior experience in government, these tasks can be daunting. 

Part One of this book addresses the administrative steps that new judges face before assuming their duties in the judiciary. The clerk of court or circuit executive will be able to assist with most of the details, and offices within the Administrative Office and the Federal Judicial Center that offer further assistance are referenced throughout the text.

Part Two addresses issues relating to judicial branch administration. It describes the decentralized court governance structure that vests most administrative authority with the judges who make up each court.

ARTICLE III, TERRITORIAL, AND COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS JUDGES 

A vacancy in an Article III, territorial, or Court of Federal Claims judgeship is created by the death, disability, elevation, impeachment, resignation, or retirement of an active judge of the respective court, or it results from the creation of a new statutory position. From the time a vacancy on the federal bench is created, it takes on average over 15 months until a new circuit or district judge is confirmed. Approximately 50 judges are confirmed in a typical year, joining over 

1,100 presidentially appointed judges.

The first step in the appointment process involves identifying persons suitable to fill judicial vacancies. Under the Constitution and by statute, Article 

III, territorial, and Court of Federal Claims judges are appointed with the advice and consent of the Senate. Home state senators have historically been consulted by the President concerning candidates for vacancies on federal district courts. For vacancies on the courts of appeals (which cover several states), the 

President exercises more discretion in identifying candidates, but frequently

confers with elected officials from the state where there is a judicial vacancy, and may consult with elected officials from each state within the circuit. 

The next step in the nomination process entails a background investigation conducted by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. Concurrently, the 

Department of Justice sends the potential nominee a questionnaire, the contents of which may be made public. The Justice Department also generally arranges to interview the nominee candidate. 

In addition, the potential nominee is interviewed by the American Bar 

Association's Standing Committee on the Federal Judiciary. The Committee pro vides its evaluation to the Department of Justice. The evaluations consist only of ratings of "well-qualified," "qualified," and "not qualified." Other information relied upon and conclusions reached by the committee are kept confidential. 

When the background investigation, the questionnaire, and the interviews are complete, the information is forwarded to the White House staff. At this point, the President makes a selection, and the nominee is notified. The process from vacancy to nomination has on average taken over a year during the last decade.

The nomination is then transmitted to the Senate, where it is referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee, which normally holds a hearing to question the nominee. Occasionally, other witnesses may also testify in favor of or in opposition to the nomination. Next, the nomination is reported out of the 

Senate Judiciary Committee and sent to the Senate floor for consideration. 

Usually within a couple of weeks, the full Senate votes on the nomination. If the nominee is confirmed, the Senate notifies the President in writing. The 

President will then sign the commission appointing the nominee as a Judge of the United States. In recent years, the process from nomination to confirmation has taken, on average, a little over 70 days.

The federal bench that has been shaped by this appointment process reflects the preferences of both the President and the Senate. The following statistics - which cover Article III judges appointed by Presidents Carter, Reagan, Bush, and Clinton (through December 31, 1994) — reveal some of the characteristics of recent appointees to the United States courts: • The average age at nomination of the 189 circuit judges appointed between 1977 and 1994 was 50-1/2; for the 747 district judges appointed during that time period, the average age was just under 49 years. Women represented 14% of all circuit judges and 16% of all district judges appointed.

Slightly over 12% of the circuit judges and 16% of the district judges were ethnic minorities.

To assist in their deliberations, the judicial councils may, by majority vote, appoint a merit selection panel as part of the process of screening and reviewing the qualifications of applicants for the bankruptcy bench. The panel may have no fewer than three members, including the chair, and it should be composed of an odd number of persons. The governing Judicial Conference regulations do not prescribe qualifications for service on the merit selection panels, except that panel members must be residents of the circuit within which the appointment is to be made. Panelists receive no compensation for their services but may be reimbursed for travel expenses. Procedures used by the merit selections panels vary. Many panels choose to interview applicants for bankruptcy judgeships. In addition, Judicial Conference regulations require that, with the exception of its report to the circuit judicial council, the panel keep confidential all information it receives in the course of evaluating candidates. The panel's report to the circuit judicial council, which is due within 90 days of the panel's creation, lists from 5 to 10 persons it deems best qualified for the bankruptcy judgeship; the report may or may not rank the candidates. Some circuits require the nominees listed by the panel to file a limited financial disclosure statement prior to their consideration by the circuit judicial council. 

After receiving the panel's report and concluding its deliberations, the circuit judicial council forwards a list of at least three persons that it recommends to the court of appeals for consideration. The court of appeals may conduct a final round of personal interviews. Finally, the court of appeals selects the new bankruptcy judge by majority vote. If a majority cannot agree, the chief judge of the circuit is authorized to make the selection. The court of appeals will then notify the nominee and obtain the nominee's acceptance.

THE FIRST DAY ON THE JOB 

ecoming a judge involves taking on a whole new set of relationships and routines. This chapter discusses a variety of issues relating to the primary steps that need to be taken on or before the first day of judicial duty, including making arrangements for the investiture ceremony, establishing relationships with the court's leadership and with mentor judges, receiving initial case assignments, and determining residences and official duty stations. 

INVESTITURE 

The investiture ceremony is an important milestone for all new judges. In many cases, a judicial nominee takes the oath of office at the ceremony and formally accepts the commission of a federal judgeship. Often, though, a judge takes the oath of office privately and holds a formal investiture ceremony at some point after assuming the bench.

Policies and practices for the investiture ceremony and the receptions that follow vary among courts and individual judges. The clerk of court or circuit executive will discuss the court's policies and traditions and may assist new judges with planning the event and making the necessary arrangements. 

Oath of Office 

Every federal judge must take the statutory oath of office before exercising the judicial authority of the United States. The oath is actually a combination of the judicial oath of office and the general oath administered to federal government officials, set forth in two sections at 28 U.S.C. § 453 and 5 U.S.C. 

§ 3331:

The oath of office may be administered by any official authorized by federal or state law to administer oaths and affirmations, including a justice or judge of the United States. There are no statutory limits on who may administer the oath or where it may be administered. New judges should consult with the chief judge of the court on any local court traditions and select an appropriate individual to administer the oath. 

Payment of judges' salaries begins on the date that they enter on duty, which in most cases is the same date that the oath is administered. See Chapter 

3 for more information on salary and benefits. 

Ceremony and Reception 

The oath of office is often administered at a formal investiture ceremony held in the court's ceremonial courtroom, if the court has one. Many courts invite their congressional delegation, local officials, state court judges, and members of the bar and academia. A reception is usually held for the new judge, family members, and invited guests after the ceremony. 

Official court funds may be used to pay for certain expenses relating to the investiture. For example, stationery, envelopes, and postage stamps may be purchased with the court's appropriated funds to extend invitations to attend the ceremony. See vol. III of the Guide to Judiciary Policies and Procedures. 

Catering expenses for the reception, however, generally must be paid by the new judge. The clerk of court or circuit executive can discuss in greater detail the use of court funds for expenses associated with the investiture. 

Occasionally, a judge's former law firm or bar association will offer a contribution toward the cost of the reception or will present a gift, such as a gavel. 

A judge may accept the contribution in certain circumstances under the "gift" provisions of the Code of Conduct for United States Judges and the Ethics 

Reform Act of 1989. See § 5.4-2 of the Compendium of Selected Opinions (1997), vol. II, ch. V of the Guide to Judiciary Policies and Procedures.

ADVICE AND ASSISTANCE FROM COURT PERSONNEL 

The officials directly responsible for administering each court and circuit - the chief judge, other judges, the clerk of the court, the circuit executive, and other court executives are often excellent sources of information for new judges.

Payment of Salaries 

Judges are paid on the first day of each month for services performed during the previous month. Judicial salaries are computed on the basis of a 30-day month. A new judge's first paycheck will include compensation beginning the day on which the oath of office is taken. (If the oath is taken on the 31st day of the month, that day is not counted.) Thus, a judge who enters on duty in the middle of the month will be paid a regular monthly salary less one-thirtieth of that amount for each day of the month prior to the date the oath of office is taken. 

The salary earned for the month of December is paid on January 1 of the following year. Thus, salary received during the tax year reflects work performed beginning on December 1 of the previous tax year and ending on the following November 30 of the current tax year. W-2 forms provided by the

Administrative Office reflect salary paid during this period. (Retirement compensation is reported in the same manner on the 1099 form.)

Article III Judges 

An Article III judge, regardless of age or length of service, may retire from regular active service and take senior status if the judge becomes permanently disabled from performing judicial duties. A judge must certify the disability to the President in writing and must also furnish the President a certificate of disability signed by either the Chief Justice (in the case of an associate justice of the Supreme Court, chief circuit judge, or chief judge of the Court of International Trade), the chief circuit judge (in the case of a circuit or district judge), or the chief judge of the Court of International Trade for judges of that court. 

A judge who voluntarily takes senior status on disability and has at least 10 years of Article III service will continue to receive the full salary of the judicial office, including any post-retirement cost-of-living allowances and other salary increases. If a judge voluntarily takes senior status on disability and has less than 10 years of Article III service, the judge receives one-half the salary of the judicial office, including any post-retirement cost-of-living allowances and other increases.

Thrift Savings Plan The Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) is a tax deferred retirement savings plan available to all federal civilian employees, including judges. Participation is voluntary, and contributions made to the TSP are separate from any contributions made toward retirement annuities under any of the retirement plans available to judges.

The TSP offers the same type of savings and tax benefits many private entities offer their employees through 401(k) plans. It is treated as a trust qualified under 26 U.S.C. § 401(a) and is deferred from current taxation. See 26 U.S.C. § 7701(j). 

Employee contributions are deducted automatically from salary before federal income taxes (and in most cases, state income taxes) are calculated. As a result, TSP contributions are excluded from the taxable income reported on a judge's W-2 form. 

The TSP is a defined contribution plan. Each covered employee has a personal account, and retirement income received from the account will be based on employee contributions and accrued interest. All the money withdrawn from a TSP account is taxed for federal tax purposes as ordinary income in the year it is received. 40

New judges often have questions concerning how their new position may affect their relationships with former clients and lawyer colleagues. Both the Code of Conduct and the disqualification statute (28 U.S.C. § 455) provide guidance on these matters.

Judges are selected from those attorneys who have achieved a measure of prominence in society through government service, private practice, and/or academia. Thus, most judges have built strong personal and professional ties within the legal community by the time of their appointment to the federal bench. Judicial decisionmaking cannot be made in a vacuum by judges without ties to family, friends, neighbors, or the community. Judicial Discipline 

Judicial discipline is a statutory responsibility of the judicial councils of the circuits and the Judicial Conference of the United States. Section 372(c) of title 28 of the United States Code establishes the process by which complaints alleging judicial misconduct or disability are resolved. Interpreting this section, the Judicial Conference Committee to Review Circuit Council Conduct and 

Disability Orders has held that Congress intended to establish an administrative, non-judicial process for determining complaints against judges. See In re 

Complaints of Judicial Misconduct, 9 F3d 1562 (U.S. Jud. Conf. 1993). This statutory process thus supplements the formal impeachment proceedings provided for by the Constitution.

Many of the familiar features of federal court proceedings under Article III (such as the requirements that plaintiffs have standing and that parties be accorded full adversarial rights at all stages of a proceeding) do not apply to judicial discipline proceedings. Instead, any person can file a complaint alleging judicial misconduct or disability, and the level of the complainant's participation is generally within the discretion of the circuit judicial council. 

Filing a complaint. The statute provides that complaints against a judge may be filed with the clerk of the appropriate court of appeals for transmission to the chief judge of the circuit (unless the complaint is against the chief judge, in which case the complaint is forwarded to the next most senior active circuit judge) and to the judge named in the complaint. The chief judge is authorized to dismiss the complaint if it does not allege conduct that comes within the 60 

Getting Started as a Federal Judge

statutory definition of misconduct or disability, if the complaint relates to the merits of a judicial decision, or if the complaint is frivolous. The chief judge is also authorized to "conclude" the complaint if corrective action has been taken or if intervening events have made further action unnecessary. Even if no written complaint has been filed, the chief judge may, based on information available to the chief judge, initiate a formal judicial discipline inquiry sua sponte. 

If the chief judge does not dismiss the complaint, the chief judge is required to appoint himself or herself and equal numbers of circuit and district judges to a special investigatory committee. The chief judge is also required to provide written notice of these actions to the complainant and to the judge named in the complaint. The special committee is then required to examine the allegations and prepare a written report and recommendations to the circuit judicial council.

Formal remedies. After consideration of the special committee's report, the circuit judicial council is empowered to investigate the allegations further or to take appropriate actions, including: requesting an Article III judge to retire voluntarily on full salary; certifying the disability of an Article III judge (thereby creating a vacancy on the court); directing the chief judge of a district court to take action with respect to a magistrate judge; ordering that no further cases be assigned to a judge for a temporary period; issuing a public or private reprimand of a judge; and ordering other action as appropriate. 

The removal of an Article III judge from office is not permitted under the statute. In order to remove a bankruptcy judge, the circuit judicial council must follow the procedures specified in 28 U.S.C. § 152(e), including providing the bankruptcy judge with notice of the charges and an opportunity to respond. Magistrate judges may only be removed by the appointing district court after following the process mandated by 28 U.S.C. § 631(i), which also requires notice and an opportunity to respond. 

If the circuit judicial council determines, however, that an Article III judge may have engaged in impeachable conduct or that the complaint is not amenable to resolution by the council, the council must forward the matter to the Judicial Conference of the United States. In addition, the circuit judicial council has discretion to refer any complaint to the Judicial Conference. If a matter is referred to the Judicial Conference on the ground that a judge may have engaged in impeachable conduct, the Conference must determine by

JUDICIAL IMMUNITY 

awsuits against federal judges generally fall into two categories: those arising out of a judge's performance of judicial duties and those arising out of the performance of administrative functions. The vast majority of lawsuits filed against federal judges relate to a judge's performance of judicial duties. They include complaints filed by disgruntled litigants who seek to attack a judicial decision collaterally through the institution of a new lawsuit against the judge. 

Judges acting within their jurisdiction are immune from liability for damages for judicial actions.

On occasion, however, lawsuits are filed challenging a judge's performance of administrative functions such as employee terminations and court management decisions. With respect to administrative functions, judges are, in general, effectively immune from suits based on common-law tort theories. 

There is, however, a modest risk of liability for administrative actions that are challenged by so-called Bivens or "constitutional tort" suits. 

The risk of liability for judges is discussed and an overview of applicable law and policy is provided in an Administrative Office paper, "The Risk of 

Personal Liability for Federal Judges," available from the Article III Judges Division, (202) 273-1860.

Administrative Liability 

Although judges possess absolute immunity while performing strictly adjudicative duties, they may be subject to constitutional tort claims for administrative actions they have taken, including employment decisions. In Bivens v.

THE CHAMBERS SUITE 

The chambers suite is a group of offices and other work areas designed for the judge and support staff. Most suites (especially at the district court level) are conveniently and privately accessible to a courtroom. All suites are restricted to access by the public.

Ordinarily, new judges receive chambers in a United States courthouse or other federal building. Depending on local conditions, it is not always possible to locate new judges in existing federal facilities. In those circumstances, the General Services Administration (GSA) — the judiciary's landlord - seeks available leased space in commercial office buildings as near to the primary federal court facility as possible. In many cases, GSA relocates other federal entities from the federal courthouse to make room for the court's expansion. 

The clerk of court, circuit executive, Administrative Office staff, and property managers from GSA work together to resolve temporary and permanent space and facility needs by the time a new judge is appointed. In some locations, interim solutions to facility requirements must be sought until permanent quarters are secured. For example, existing office space, space formerly occupied by a visiting judge, or the chambers for a vacant judgeship may be modified to accommodate the new judge and staff. 78 a s a Federal Judge

ESTABLISHING COURTROOMS 

A significant portion of a judge's work occurs in the courtroom. The general public often associates the use of a courtroom with the actual trial of a civil or criminal case. But most pretrial and post-trial proceedings that do not require confidentiality are also held in open court. In addition to traditional judicial activities, courtrooms are used for ceremonial purposes like naturalization proceedings. This chapter discusses the judiciary's policies governing courtroom assignments and the guidelines for courtroom size used in new construction or renovation. 

POLICY ISSUES 

The Judicial Conference has promulgated several policy statements addressing courtroom construction and assignment. Courtroom assignments are subject to circuit judicial council and local discretion. 

The judiciary's general practice has been to provide one trial courtroom for each district judgeship. The same practice has generally been extended to bankruptcy judges and magistrate judges. In some courts, however, courtrooms are shared. The "one judgeship, one courtroom" policy is presently under study by the Judicial Conference.

As construction costs for new court facilities have increased in recent years, many of the newest courthouses in larger, urban courts have fewer courtrooms than judges. The clerk of court or the chief judge will be pleased to discuss the court's policies regarding courtroom assignments for new judges. 90

RECORDINg of ProceEDINGS 

The recording of proceedings in United States district courts is governed by 28 U.S.C. § 753, which gives each judge the discretion to determine the means for recording court proceedings. Section 753(b) requires that: 

Each session of the court and every other proceeding designated by rule or order of the court or by one of the judges shall be recorded verbatim by shorthand, mechanical means, electronic sound recording, or any other method, subject to regulations promulgated by the Judicial Conference and subject to the discretion and approval of the judge. 

Thus, judges may elect the method by which the court proceedings are recorded. Presently, there are four methods of recording court proceedings that have been approved by the Judicial Conference: (1) manual shorthand; (2) machine shorthand (stenotype with or without realtime reporting); (3) stenomask; and (4) electronic sound recording. A description of the types of court reporting services and the means by which proceedings are recorded can be found in Chapter 14 at pages 139-41. 

Most district judges use salaried official court reporters, supplemented where necessary by contract reporters and recording equipment. Court reporting methods used by bankruptcy and magistrate judges often differ from those

nfortunately, there are a few members of the public who may attempt to there aut a few. In fact, there several in recent years. For that reason, it is important for all members of the court family to think about and practice effective security precautions. 

When discussing court security, it is useful to understand the function of the court security committee. Each judicial district has a court security committee consisting of the United States marshal for the district, who serves as principal coordinator of the committee; the chief district judge (or judge designee); a magistrate judge; a representative of the bankruptcy court; where there is a court of appeals or the chambers of a circuit judge within the district, a circuit representative; the United States attorney; the clerk of court; and a representative of the General Services Administration, where appropriate. The responsibility of the court security committee is to develop and implement a district-wide security plan, to request sufficient resources via the annual budget process to support their security plan, and to meet periodically to identify security problems and recommend solutions. The local court security committee is responsible for addressing the security concerns of the entire judicial family, including judges, chambers staffs, clerks, probation and pretrial services officers, librarians, public defenders, and other members of the court family. 

This responsibility includes protection for judicial areas within federal buildings as well as any leased space that has court staff on site. 

In the federal courts, security issues can be examined in two contexts security inside the courthouse and security outside the courthouse. This chapter also addresses how to respond to a threat and recommends personal security practices.

SECURITY INSIDE COURTHOUSES 

The United States Marshals Service, a bureau of the Department of Justice, is responsible for providing security for judges wherever they are located. A major part of its mission is to ensure that courthouses and courtrooms are safe. 

It accomplishes this task in two ways: First, the district's United States marshal and deputy marshals work closely with the members of the court and court staff, as well as with the Federal Bureau of Investigation and local law enforcement, to ensure the security of judges and court facilities. 

Second, the United States Marshals Service, using funds provided by the judiciary, contracts with various private security firms to provide court security officers (CSOs) to assist with routine security functions. The CSOs, who are readily recognizable by their blue jackets and gray slacks, provide guard services at entrances to the courthouse and court parking lots and operate magnetometers and x-ray security screening equipment. Like deputy United States marshals, CSOs are armed. CSOs are deputized by the Marshals Service, but their authority is limited to areas within the courthouse. 

The Marshals Service has been successful in providing a safe and secure environment for the operation of the federal courts through the development of effective court security measures. The use of magnetometers, closed-circuit television monitors, electronic alarm systems and other devices within court facilities has resulted in the confiscation of thousands of weapons at the entrance to courthouses, the interception of explosives, and the avoidance of physical assaults on judges.

In the event of an incident or threat to the courthouse or member of the judicial family, the United States marshal, in cooperation with other law enforcement agencies, will take appropriate action to provide required security.

RESPONDING TO A THREAT 

What should judges do if they or family members receive a threat?

If the threat is made in the courthouse, the United States marshal should be called. Outside the courthouse, the judge or family member should contact the local police immediately by calling emergency services (911 in most communities). Then, the marshal should be notified. Local police, in cooperation with the marshal, will immediately take action to provide protection and neutralize the threat. 

What if the threat is more subtle; that is, what if something does not seem to be an overt threat but still causes concern? The matter should be reported to the marshal, who will analyze the situation and take action, if warranted.

PERSONAL SECURITY 

Judges and their families should develop and practice good personal security habits. These habits involve two simple concepts: "anonymity" and "situational awareness."

Anonymity 

Outside the courthouse, judges and their families can blend in with the rest of the population. Anything that draws particular attention to them as members of the court family can provide assistance to persons who may wish to harm them.

Home addresses have been a matter of particular concern. Judges are encouraged to remove their home addresses from the telephone directory or any place that may be accessible to the public. The Judicial Conference Committee on Security, Space and Facilities recommends that judges have unlisted telephone numbers. Recognizing that for some families this may be difficult, an alternative is to provide a telephone number in the telephone direc-

tory without an address. Another option is to provide only the chambers address for the telephone directory.

In many places, the public may discover the address listed on a judge's drivers license by checking with the state's Division of Motor Vehicles. While practices vary from state to state, it may be possible to have the drivers license list only the chambers address.

Another suggestion to preserve anonymity is to avoid personalized license plates that might draw attention. Parking places with names and titles should also be avoided.

Some judges have all personal mail delivered to the courthouse. Mail arriving at the courthouse is screened through an x-ray device to provide an additional level of security.

Use of Travel ENTITLEMENTS EARNED ON OFFICIAL TRAVEL 

Travel entitlements concern promotions or bonuses that airlines, hotels, and rental car companies give to their customers to encourage loyalty to a specific company. These kinds of bonuses include frequent flyer miles, reduced-fare coupons, companion (Two-for-One) airfares, and other kinds of promotional discounts. This area of travel reimbursement policy is constantly changing as new promotions are created and frequently raises questions of interpretation. 

With respect to the personal use of frequent flyer miles earned during official travel, the Comptroller General has ruled that the personal use of officially earned frequent flyer benefits is prohibited. Promotional items obtained by redemption of frequent flyer mileage accrued during official travel are government property and thus cannot be used for personal travel. When a traveler has both official and personal travel that result in bonuses, it is the traveler's responsibility to keep detailed records of the bonus source and to use them accordingly. It follows from the general prohibition against personal use of offi- Travel 123

cial frequent flyer benefits that a spouse is not able to use official frequent flyer benefits. 

The government does not prohibit employees from using frequent flyer miles to upgrade seating into first-class during official travel. The regulations in vol. III of the Guide to Judiciary Policies and Procedures do not prohibit a judge from traveling first-class under certain circumstances, and it is possible to travel in this manner without additional expense to the government by using accrued frequent flyer benefits.

A special rule applies to the receipt of cash or credit rebates on credit cards used to pay for official travel expenses. In contrast to the rule for frequent flyer benefits, the Comptroller General has ruled that a government employee's use of a personal credit card in the course of official travel is a matter of personal convenience and is not directly related to the official travel. Such rebates therefore may be kept by the credit card holder. 

For companion airline (“Two-for-One") tickets, the judiciary is following a recently issued ruling of the Comptroller General. Under defined circumstances, a government employee may personally use a free promotional companion airfare received as a result of official travel. The Comptroller General has drawn a distinction between a companion airline ticket and other travel entitlements. Unlike most other travel entitlements, a companion ticket is of limited temporal use. It attaches to a specific employee for a specific flight and is of no value to the government after the flight is completed. Thus, where the agency is unable to use the companion ticket for the official travel of another employee, and the use of the ticket will not result in additional cost to the government, the traveler may make personal use of such a ticket.

CHIEF JUDGES 

There is a chief judge for each court of appeals, each district court, each bankruptcy court with more than one judge, the Court of International Trade and the Court of Federal Claims. In the district courts, the courts of appeals, and the Court of International Trade, the chief judgeship is determined on the basis of seniority; by statute, the chief judge is the judge senior in commission who, at the time of appointment, has served at least one year and is under the age of 65. Those chief judges serve for a maximum period of seven years but not beyond age 70. The chief judge of the Court of Federal Claims is designated by the President and serves until age 70 or another chief judge is designated, whichever occurs first. Bankruptcy court chief judges are chosen by majority vote of the district court judges. 

The statutory authority of the chief judge of the court of appeals and the chief judge of the district court is limited primarily to presiding at court sessions, 28 U.S.C. §§ 45(b) & 136(b), and certain other narrow duties. While chief judges have no authority over the actual decision of cases by other judges, the chief judge by tradition has considerable responsibility for court administration. 

The chief judge represents and speaks for the court in institutional matters. For example, delegations of authority to spend money flow from the Director of the

Chief circuit judges are responsible for certifying to the Chief Justice the need for temporary assistance from judges from other circuits and for assigning judges in the circuit to temporary duty on other courts in the circuit. ARTICLE III, TERRITORIAL, AND COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS Judges 

Judges in active service of the courts of appeals, district courts, Court of International Trade, and Court of Federal Claims constitute their respective courts. Although the roles of circuit and district judges are well-delineated today, those two positions have undergone significant evolution over the years. (128) 

Getting Started as a Federal Judge

Circuit Judges 

Although the Judiciary Act of 1789 created circuit courts, the office of circuit judge as we now know it was not created until much later. Circuit courts were originally staffed by one district judge and two Supreme Court justices (who rode circuit). These courts were primarily trial courts and handled major criminal trials and diversity cases, as well as appeals from the district courts. Needless to say, it was a significant burden for the justices of the Supreme Court to attend circuit court sessions around the country (especially in an era of slow and difficult travel). Although changes were made to reduce the frequency with which the justices needed to attend circuit courts, it was not until 1869 that Congress permanently authorized the appointment of circuit judges, who possessed the same authority as an associate justice of the Supreme Court sitting on a circuit court. 

In 1891, the present appellate model was instituted when Congress established the circuit courts of appeals, which were created exclusively to hear appeals from the district and circuit courts. The circuit courts continued in existence until 1911, when all of their trial functions were formally assumed by the district courts. By statute, the circuit courts of appeals became known as the courts of appeals in 1948. Because of the history of the circuit court, it is thus technically inaccurate to refer to, say, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit as a "circuit court" instead of as a “court of appeals." 

Although this was not always the case, circuit judges now typically sit in panels of three, presided over by the most senior judge. By majority vote of the active circuit judges, some cases may be heard or reheard by an en banc panel of the court of appeals as a whole. See 28 U.S.C. § 46. Justices of the Supreme 

Court are still assigned certain responsibilities for the various courts of appeals, but the modern roles of the "circuit" justices are mere vestiges of their former responsibilities. 

District Judges 

The office of district judge has existed since the Judiciary Act of 1789 created the position. At that time, one district court was created in each state, and one district judge was appointed for each district. The district courts had exclusive jurisdiction only over admiralty and revenue cases and had concurrent jurisdiction with the circuit courts in a few other areas, including minor criminal offenses. The district courts were gradually granted greater authority, culminating in their merger with the circuit courts in 1911. 

Today, all district courts save a few territorial courts have more than one judge. In contrast to trials in the former circuit courts, district judges now try cases alone, with certain statutory exceptions (such as voting rights cases, The Court Family 

129

which are heard by three-judge district courts). See 28 U.S.C. §§ 132(c), 2284.

District courts do not typically sit en banc, except on ceremonial occasions.

SENIOR ARTICLE III JUDGES 

Article III judges may elect to become senior judges if they are at least 65 years old and meet the requirements of the "Rule of 80," that is, if the total of a judge's age and years of service equals or exceeds 80. (More information about the Rule of 80 and retirement programs for Article III judges can be found in Chapter 3 at pages 24-26.) When a judge takes senior status, an immediate vacancy is created on the court, even though the senior judge may continue to work. Senior judges continue to receive their full salary at the time they left active service and any cost-of-living allowances provided to active judges. If they are certified by the chief circuit judge as meeting certain minimum workload requirements, they may also receive any base salary increases provided to active judges. Senior judges who perform substantial services for the court are also generally entitled to chambers and to such assistants as their work may require. 

By statute, the membership of the circuit judicial councils and the individual Article III courts are limited to judges in active service (but see note on page 155). Thus, senior judges have relinquished their right to have a formal vote in judicial governance decisions. Many courts, however, keep senior judges fully informed about such matters, include them in discussions, and rely on their experience for guidance.

Without the considerable efforts of senior judges, the federal judiciary would need substantially more judges to handle its caseload. In 1995, there were nearly 400 senior Article III judges, most of whom continued to perform judicial duties. For the 12-month period ending September 30, 1995, senior judges accounted for about 17% of all judge participations in appellate cases and about 19% of all trials conducted in district court. These totals are equivalent to the services of approximately 100 active judges, which represents a major contribution to the administration of justice in the federal courts. 130)

28 U.S.C. § 151. They are appointed by the courts of appeals for 14-year terms. They adjudicate cases arising under or relating to the Bankruptcy

Through standing orders, district courts routinely refer all bankruptcy cases to the bankruptcy courts but may withdraw cases, in whole or in part, on motion or sua sponte.

Once a case has been referred, the role of the bankruptcy judge depends on whether the proceeding is a "core" or a "non-core" proceeding. Core proceedings exclude those that would require the bankruptcy court to exercise the judicial power of the United States, which is reserved to judges having the tenure and salary protections of Article III. See Northern Pipeline Construction Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co., 458 U.S. 50 (1982). Core proceedings are defined by 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2) to include 15 specific types of proceedings. 

Bankruptcy judges make initial determinations as to whether proceedings are core or non-core.

If the matter involves a core proceeding, the bankruptcy judge may make final determinations and issue orders, subject to appeal to the district court or to a bankruptcy appellate panel. If the matter involves a non-core proceeding that is related to a bankruptcy case, the bankruptcy judge may make determinations and issue orders if all of the parties and the district court consent. If such consent is absent in a non-core proceeding, the bankruptcy judge is only authorized to make recommendations (including proposed findings of fact and conclusions of law) to the district court.

MAGISTRATE JUDGES 

A United States magistrate judge is a judicial officer of the district court appointed by a majority vote of the active judges of the court. The number of magistrate judge positions is determined by the Judicial Conference, based on recommendations of the respective district courts, the judicial councils of the circuits, and the Director of the Administrative Office. A full-time magistrate judge is appointed for a term of eight years; a part-time magistrate judge serves for a term of four years.

The office of United States magistrate judge was created by the Federal Magistrates Act of 1968. Its foundation is the commissioner system, which was first established in 1793. Commissioners were previously used in federal courts to try petty offense cases committed on federal property, to issue search warrants and arrest warrants, to determine bail for federal defendants, and to conduct other initial proceedings in federal criminal cases. 

The Federal Magistrates Act of 1968, as amended, was enacted by the Congress to create a federal judicial officer who would be a flexible resource for the district courts, who would assume all the former duties of the commissioners, and who would conduct additional judicial proceedings to expedite the disposition of the civil and criminal cases of the United States district courts. The authority of magistrate judges was expanded by statute in 1976 and 1979 to include a wide range of pretrial and trial duties. Reflecting the expanded role magistrate judges play in the district courts, the title of the position was changed from "magistrate" to "magistrate judge" in 1990. 132

The staff attorneys office is an operational unit of the court of appeals. 

The staff attorneys constitute the court's central legal staff, as opposed to "elbow" law clerks who work in the judges' chambers. Pre-argument conference attorney programs now exist in some form in each court of appeals. COURT REPORTERS 

The Judicial Conference has approved four different methods for reporting proceedings. There are three types of professionals who provide court reporting services in the federal courts - - official staff court reporters, contract court reporters, and electronic sound recorder operators. 

Reporting Methods 

The Judicial Conference has approved four methods for reporting court proceedings in federal courts. Written transcripts can be generated from each method, subject to Judicial Conference guidelines set forth in vol. VI of the 

Guide to Judiciary Policies and Procedures. 

Stenotype reporters employ a stenotype machine that translates keystrokes into symbols that correspond to the spoken word. A typewritten transcript is made from the machine tape after trial. Over 95% of court reporters use stenotype reporting to record proceedings in the federal courts. Recent advances in technology allow the use of computer-aided transcription (also known as "realtime" court reporting) to provide a nearly instantaneous translation of the reporter's keystrokes for use by the judge and counsel on courtroom monitors.

Pen writing involves a shorthand specialist who takes down the proceedings manually and later transcribes them into typewritten form. This method is used only on a limited basis, mostly by senior court reporters. 

Steno mask reporters repeat everything said in the courtroom into a mask linked to a tape recorder. The reporter later transcribes the recording into a typewritten record of trial. Only a few court reporters continue to use this method to record proceedings.

FEDERAL DEFENDER SERVICES 

Historically, authority for the appointment of counsel in federal criminal proceedings has rested with the federal judiciary. Before the enactment of the 

Criminal Justice Act of 1964 (CJA), however, there was no statutory provision for compensation of appointed counsel, and judges relied mainly on the professional obligation of lawyers to provide pro bono services to indigent defendants.

The CJA authorized payment of hourly compensation and out-of-pocket expenses for lawyers appointed by the courts to represent criminal defendants. In 1970 the CJA was amended to authorize federal public defender organizations and community defender organizations as additional options for providing defense counsel in those districts where at least 200 CJA appointments are made annually.

Each judicial district is required to have a plan for furnishing representation under the CJA. The plans generally provide for representation by private panel attorneys in a substantial proportion of cases (at least 25%) and, in 142)

addition, may provide for representation by a bar association, legal aid agency, or (for qualifying districts) by attorneys furnished by a federal public or community defender organization. 

Private attorneys, drawn from a panel of attorneys approved by the court as qualified to handle federal criminal cases, are appointed on a case-bycase basis by the district court or court of appeals. The court approves attorneys for membership on the panel after receiving recommendations from a panel selection committee. The membership of the panel should be large enough to provide a sufficient number of experienced attorneys to handle the CJA caseload, but small enough so that panel members will receive an adequate number of appointments to maintain their proficiency in federal criminal defense work. LOCAL GOVERNANCE 

he individual appellate and district courts are the basic building blocks of the federal judiciary. Reflecting and supporting the jurisprudential independence that is the hallmark of the federal judiciary, the courts operate with considerable autonomy under national guidance, with only the essential elements of court structure, governance, budgetary allocations, and procedures and plans set at the regional or national levels. The following sections discuss each of these elements as they apply to the individual courts. 

COURT STRUCTURE AND GOVERNANCE 

The courts of appeals and the district courts, through largely autonomous selfgovernance, perform many of the basic tasks of judicial administration in the federal court system. Local rules of court, administrative decisions, and personnel appointments (except for chambers staff) are made collegially by all the active judges of the court. In a sense, each active Article III judge of a particular court functions as a member of the "board of directors" of the court. The chief judge, by analogy, is the "chair" of the board and is ultimately responsible for seeing that the court is administered effectively and efficiently. Many courts develop court policies and oversee court operations through a system of court committees or liaison judges appointed by the chief judge to address specific subject areas (e.g., budget, space, or local rules) or to supervise court units (e.g., the clerk's office or the probation office).

The Long Range Plan for the Federal Courts includes a recommendation that "there should be broad, meaningful participation of judges in governance activities at all levels." The Plan's commentary further notes that "[i]t is essential that the different perspectives and experiences of trial and appellate judges, (146) 

Federal Judge Getting Started a s

and of life-tenured and fixed-term judges, be reflected in the decision making process." By tradition, many courts involve their senior judges, bankruptcy judges, and magistrate judges in judicial governance. But by statute, the Article 

III judges in active service constitute their respective courts and are therefore responsible for most decisions concerning hiring, local court rules, and other policy matters. 

The governance structure of the judiciary is often described as decentralized. This means that Article III judges, as independent judicial officers enjoying salary and tenure protections, retain considerable autonomy over matters of court administration. The judiciary governs itself primarily though local mechanisms, with the Judicial Conference and the circuit councils providing guidance and setting policy when specific needs arise. Further information concerning national and regional governance mechanisms is provided in 

Chapters 17 and 18. 

Judges at the local level enjoy considerable latitude to structure their court operations to suit local conditions. Workload, while monitored on at national basis, may be structured and divided based on the needs of the individual court and its judges. Courts may issue local rules and operating procedures to address situations that may be unique to the local court. Moreover, personnel policies are set locally, and hiring is done by individual judges or courts. Similarly, bankruptcy courts, while units of the district courts, are generally administered autonomously. The following sections describe several features of local governance in greater detail.

Each federal court develops a number of individualized procedures and plans to conduct its business. Procedures and plans relating to local rules, case assignments, case calendaring, alternative dispute resolution, and long-range planning are developed by local option; others—such as those pertaining to jury selection, indigent representation, and civil and criminal case disposition are mandated by statute.

Case Assignments 

Every court with more than one judge must utilize procedures for assigning responsibility for cases to its judges. Common methods of case assignment in the district courts include random assignment, rotational assignment, subject matter assignment, and assignment by geographic division of the court. (148) 

Getting Started a s a Federal Judge

Using computer software provided by the Administrative Office, most federal district and bankruptcy courts randomly assign a case to a particular judge at the time it is filed, or soon thereafter, and that judge has complete responsibility for the case until it is terminated. Random assignment generally helps to ensure an equitable distribution of workloads and also prevents “judge shopping." Under a rotational assignment system, a judge or judges are allotted a “shift” during which all incoming cases are assigned to them. 

Some courts have successfully implemented case assignment systems in which the civil part of the docket is allocated randomly while the criminal docket is assigned rotationally. Such systems help to clear time for judges to handle civil matters without the frequent interruptions occasioned by criminal cases. Subject matter and divisional assignment systems are used less frequently, as they tend to create workload disparities and may also lead to improperly close relations between the bench and the bar. Courts, however, often use the subject matter of certain types of cases such as Social Security appeals and prisoner petitions — to determine assignments to magistrate judges.

In the courts of appeals, cases are normally assigned by random selection to three-judge panels. If a judge is unable to attend one or more sessions of the panel, he or she will normally trade places with another judge not assigned to the panel (subject to the approval of the chief judge). 

Many courts have also implemented special procedures for reassigning cases in which a judge is disqualified, for assuring that related cases are all assigned to the same judge or panel, and for special assignment of unusual and protracted cases. The local rules of the court or the court's civil justice expense and delay reduction plan (see infra, pages 150-51) will normally detail these special procedures.

In addition to receiving new cases through the court's case assignment system, new judges will frequently inherit "old" cases from judges who have left the court or who have taken a reduced caseload. In such situations, new judges may face the unenviable position of starting out behind. Nevertheless, the number of old cases a new judge receives may be subject to negotiation with the other judges of the court.

Jury Selection Plans 

Each district court is required by 28 U.S.C. § 1863 to formulate a plan for random selection of grand and petit jurors. The statute sets out a number of requirements for jury selection plans. Plans must designate either a jury commission or the clerk of the court to manage the jury selection process. Alternative Dispute Resolution Mechanisms 

Courts have developed several forms of court-annexed alternative dispute resolution (ADR) programs over the past decade, primarily to promote settlement. (Litigants may, of course, agree to avail themselves of private ADR programs that are not affiliated with the court.) The Civil Justice Reform Act directs all courts to consider the adoption of an ADR referral system (28 U.S.C. § 473(a)(6)), which has led to an increased use of these programs. Some ADR programs may be more suitable for certain types of cases than others. The following is a brief description of the principal court-annexed programs, which include court-annexed arbitration, mediation, early neutral evaluation, nonbinding summary jury trials, and nonbinding summary bench trials. (152) 

Getting Started as a Federal Judge

In arbitration, the litigants present their cases to an outside neutral (or panel of neutrals). Attorneys from the local community generally serve as the arbitrators. The presentations are generally less formal than at a trial, and rules of evidence are suspended. The arbitrator issues a nonbinding decision. If the parties accept the decision, the case is terminated with no right to appeal. If the parties do not accept the decision, they may proceed to trial de novo or may agree on an outcome different from the arbitrator's decision. Arbitration may promote settlement, even when the parties reject the arbitrator's decision, by suggesting the likely outcome if the parties were to proceed in court. At present, 10 federal district courts are authorized to utilize presumptively mandatory arbitration, and another 10 courts are authorized to offer voluntary arbitration. See 28 U.S.C. §§ 651-658. The statutory authority for this model arbitration program ends in 1997. The Judicial Conference of the United States has adopted policies in support of permitting all courts to offer voluntary arbitration.

Mediation involves meeting with an outside neutral, either courtappointed or selected by the litigants, for in-depth settlement discussions. 

Mediators are often experts in the subject matter of the case and are usually attorneys; magistrate judges sometimes serve as mediators. Mediators do not render a decision but instead assist the litigants in identifying key issues and developing a settlement package. If the case does not settle, the parties may proceed to trial.

Under "early neutral evaluation," the court refers the litigants early in the case to an outside neutral, who is generally an attorney with expertise in the subject matter of the case. The parties each present their side to the evaluator, who then offers an opinion about the case's likely outcome. Settlement is facilitated by enhancing communication and providing litigants with a more realistic assessment of their prospects. Even if settlement does not occur, the issues for trial may be narrowed and the cost and duration of litigation may be reduced. 

A nonbinding summary jury trial, in which the court conducts an abbreviated trial before a regularly impaneled jury, is another strategy for reducing the costs of traditional courtroom litigation. The jury offers a nonbinding, advisory verdict, and the lawyers are generally permitted to question the jurors about their decision. Settlement may be promoted by giving the parties an idea of the likely outcome if they go to trial. If the case does not settle, the parties may proceed to trial. This strategy is generally only useful when a lengthy trial is expected. The nonbinding summary bench trial is a related approach, except tried to the judge.

Article III Judgeships 

Since 1964, the federal judiciary has conducted periodic surveys to determine the need for additional Article III judgeships in the appellate and district courts. Since 1980, those surveys have been conducted every two years. The Judicial Conference in March 1996 approved a recommendation to include in the survey process reviews of the need to eliminate judgeships or to leave positions unfilled. In March 1997, the Conference approved an amended process that will take into account the need to eliminate judgeships as part of the biennial surveys of judgeship needs in the district courts. 

The Judicial Conference's Committee on Judicial Resources initiates the surveys by sending a questionnaire to each chief judge. Based in part on the responses to the questionnaire, the Subcommittee on Judicial Statistics applies weighted caseload indices developed by the Federal Judicial Center and makes preliminary recommendations as to changes in the number of judgeships required by each court. These preliminary recommendations are then distributed to the chief judges and the circuit judicial councils. After comments are received, the Subcommittee's final recommendations are reported to the full Committee on Judicial Resources and to the chief judges and the judicial councils. After review and approval by the full Committee and then the Judicial 

Conference of the United States, the judiciary proposes draft legislation to Congress to alter the number of judgeships. 

To determine the number of judgeships required in each court, the Subcommittee on Judicial Statistics first considers numerical information. For example, in the district courts beginning with the 1994 survey the Subcommittee used a general standard of 430 weighted filings per judgeship as an indication of the need for additional judgeships. In the case of a court that is requesting additional judgeships, the Subcommittee also considers whether the situation is temporary or whether there are other factors in the caseload which would make the situation unique. Even if the court does not meet the numerical criteria for additional judgeships, the Subcommittee will consider other factors including whether there are senior judges or active judges who will soon be eligible for senior status or whether there are unique circumstances justifying the request.

ADMINISTRATIVE PROGRAMS 

he judiciary forms a separate branch of the federal government with explicit powers under the Constitution's system of checks and balances. These powers guarantee that federal judges maintain judicial independence from political pressures and heated public sentiment as they administer justice. And judicial independence entails a certain amount of administrative independence to allow the federal courts to operate with the requisite autonomy.

At the same time, the federal courts are accountable to the Congress and the people for the efficient management of their resources and the responsible oversight of public monies. This chapter discusses the judiciary's roles and responsibilities in five areas that influence the administrative independence of the federal courts the budget, the personnel system, contracting and procurement, financial regulations, and space and facilities. 

THE JUDICIARY'S BUDGET 

The Congress has the sole authority under the Constitution to raise revenue and appropriate funds. Thus, the judiciary is dependent upon Congress to enact an appropriation that will provide the courts with the necessary resources to discharge their constitutional and statutory responsibilities. 

The judiciary retains a significant amount of autonomy over its budget after an appropriation has been enacted. The federal courts have managed their own financial affairs since 1939, the year Congress transferred most administrative and budget functions to the newly created Administrative Office of the United States Courts and placed the agency under the supervision of the Judicial Conference. Prior to that time, budget development and implementa-

Under the supervision of the Judicial Conference, budget estimates for the federal courts (the Supreme Court and two other Article III courts with nationwide jurisdiction are exceptions) are prepared at the national level by the Administrative Office with the input of the program committees of the Judicial Conference. Budget execution, however, is performed locally by each individual court under a process known as "budget decentralization." As a result, the clerk of court and other court unit heads manage and spend court funds under the supervision of the chief judge, a committee of judges, or the court as a whole, within procedural guidelines for the use of decentralized budgets pro vided by the Administrative Office.

Budget Decentralization 

Budget decentralization places the responsibility for planning, management, and control of allotted funds at the local court unit level, where reaction to local needs can occur quickly. Courts have the discretion to reprogram funds between most accounts and between units within their district courts. (Not all funding is fully decentralized, however. For example, salary and travel funds for judges and chambers staff are held centrally by the Administrative Office and are not included under budget decentralization.) 

The Judicial Conference and the Administrative Office decentralized budget management several years ago in order to provide courts with greater autonomy in long-range planning, improved cost control, flexibility in meeting local needs, and an enhanced sense of cooperation between court units. Under budget decentralization, each court unit (including the district clerk's office, the bankruptcy clerk's office, and the probation office) is responsible for requesting and managing funds that are allotted to different budget categories, such as salaries, travel, consumable supplies, and office automation equipment. 

Each spring, the Administrative Office analyzes the judiciary's budgetary needs for the fiscal year beginning the following October. A proposed financial plan, with several alternatives, is developed for review and approval by the Executive Committee of the Judicial Conference based on the estimated level of funding from Congress, current services program requirements, and necessary program increases. Detailed court requirements are obtained from a "budget call" distributed to the courts of appeals, the district courts, the bankruptcy courts, and the probation and pretrial services offices. The courts provide projections for the next fiscal year for staffing, travel, tenant alterations, equip- (178)

Judiciary Salary Plan (JSP) The Judicial Conference implemented the Judiciary Salary Plan in the 

1960's. Under the JSP, most decisions on job classification and salaries are made centrally through the Judicial Conference of the United States, its committees, and the Administrative Office. 

The JSP grade structure and compensation system are patterned on the civil service system used for most federal employees in the executive branch. 

The JSP has 18 grades and defines most jobs in the courts in "landmarks” (more than 180 in number), prescribing job title, listing typical duties and responsibilities, and showing the JSP grades available for the job. 

Court Personnel System (CPS) In 1989, the Judicial Resources Committee of the Judicial Conference directed the Administrative Office to undertake a study of the JSP, in cooperation with court managers and employees. Based upon the findings of that study, a recommendation was made to create a new personnel management system. After a development effort that included court managers, the Court 

Personnel System was approved by the Judicial Conference in September 1993. The first courts converted to the new system in March 1995, and all courts were phased in by October 1996.

The Court Personnel System simplifies the job classification system and grade structure used in the JSP. The CPS also contains a wider pay range for each position, giving the court manager more flexibility in setting and adjusting an employee's pay. No limitations are placed on spending for promotions or hiring decisions, as long as the court unit is able to fund its personnel actions from the salary dollars provided under the Cost Control Monitoring System. 

Many elements of the personnel system did not change with the implementation of CPS, including employee leave policies, retirement systems, disability and survivors benefits, health insurance, life insurance, and other employee benefits and protections. Conversion did not result in a reduced salary for any employee.

(This most often occurs with after-hours receipt of bail or forfeiture of collateral in petty offense cases held at remote locations.) Absent exceptional circumstances, however, judges in situations without clerk's office support should use a “lockbox" for receipt of collateral forfeitures, fines, and assessments. (The lockbox is a post office box established by a financial institution in Atlanta under contract with the Department of the Treasury.) Offenders mail their payments directly to the lockbox using preaddressed envelopes provided by the judge. 

Judges and other federal employees having custody or possession of public monies are required to safeguard the money, deposit it in the Treasury promptly and without deduction, and keep accurate records of each amount received, transferred, and paid. See 31 U.S.C. § 3302.

It is essential that judges follow the judiciary's depositing and transmitting procedures for public funds if they accept fines or other monies on behalf of the clerk of court. See vol. I, ch. VII 

of the Guide to Judiciary Policies and Procedures. 

Judges, like other federal employees having custody of or responsibilities for public funds, are personally liable for improper payments or shortage or loss of funds. See 31 U.S.C. § 3527.

Registry Funds 

The federal courts hold registry funds in an assortment of interest-bearing and non-interest bearing accounts on behalf of litigants in pending litigation. Registry funds may be invested in Treasury bills or deposited with local 

FDIC-insured financial institutions, the Treasury, or depositories designated to accept funds for the Treasury. Separate accounts are maintained for each case by the clerk's office in ledgers captioned with the title of the case and its docket number. Types of funds held include settlement funds, interpleaded money, unclaimed funds for persons whose whereabouts may be unknown, bail deposits, and fines pending appeal.

These deposits are held in trust by the United States for whomever is ultimately adjudicated to be their owner. See 28 U.S.C. § 2041. The court's responsibility over these funds extends to the parties to the litigation and to the United States. If the funds are held outside the Treasury, they must be collateralized if the amount deposited exceeds FDIC insurance limits. 

Generally, registry funds are deposited with the court under procedures set forth in the federal rules of practice and procedure, but they may also be placed into the registry by specific statute (e.g., 11 U.S.C. § 345). See Fed. R. 

Civ. P. 67; Fed. R. Bankr. P. 7067. Parties depositing funds with the court are required to serve a copy of an order to deposit with the clerk's office to ensure that the clerk is aware of the court's responsibility with respect to any special handling of the deposit. Funds deposited in the court's registry may not be removed without a court order. See 28 U.S.C. § 2042.

Further information on court registry deposits and withdrawals can be obtained from the clerk of court and in vol. I, ch. VII, pt. J of the Guide to

SPACE AND FACILITIES PROGRAM 

The federal courts are located in over 750 separate facilities that are either government-owned or leased, occupying over 19 million square feet of space. 

As with most other federal entities, the judiciary has no direct authority to acquire facilities for its own use. By law, that responsibility lies exclusively with the General Services Administration (GSA), an executive branch agency. 

The judiciary, like other GSA tenants, pays rent to GSA. (In fact, 19.2% of the judiciary's fiscal year 1997 budget is applied to rental payments.) It is 

GSA's responsibility to plan for the space needs of its clients. 

Many of the federal courthouses currently in use were built in the 1930's as combination post offices and courthouses and are on the National Historic Register. Over the subsequent 50 years, most critical space needs were addressed by the construction of makeshift facilities in existing federal buildings, which in many cases were not built to accommodate modern-day court operations. Large increases in caseloads, and the staff to do the new work, have combined with the age of many of these structures to render them obsolete. 

Dramatic growth in the federal court system during the 1980's has resulted in a proliferation of court facilities with varying degrees of functional and security deficiencies. In 1991, GSA began a major program to replace aging court facilities by approving nearly 90 major renovation and construction projects. 

This is the largest public works project authorized by Congress since the 1930's. 

Recent efforts by Congress and the executive branch to reduce federal spending have resulted in cuts to the courthouse construction and renovation program. 

Statutory and Regulatory Responsibilities 

The statutory and regulatory relationship between the judiciary and the General Services Administration is the basis for the acquisition of court accommodations for the judicial branch. Under the Federal Property and 

Administrative Services Act of 1949 (the Property Act), GSA serves as the landlord for virtually the entire federal government, including the judiciary. See 40 

U.S.C. § 751 et seq. As the landlord for the federal court system, GSA is charged with providing space in either public buildings or leased facilities, and with providing certain levels of services in these accommodations. 

To supplement the provisions of the Property Act and other statutes, the Administrator of General Services has promulgated the Federal Property 

Management Regulations. See 41 C.FR. subch. D, pts. 101-16 to 101-21. The regulations apply to "Federal agencies," which term encompasses “any executive agency or any establishment in the legislative or judicial branch of the 

Administrative Programs 

(185)

Government ...." 40 U.S.C. § 472(b); 41 C.FR. § 101-17.003-23. Thus, these regulations are applicable to the judiciary. 

The regulations set out in detail how government-owned or leased space in GSA and Postal Service facilities is to be assigned and utilized (41 C.FR. pt. 

101-17), how real property is to be acquired (41 C.FR. pt. 101-18), how public buildings are to be constructed and altered (41 C.FR. pt. 101-19), how buildings and grounds are to be managed (41 C.FR. pt. 101-20), and how monies for these projects are to be collected from agencies and deposited in a special account for future construction projects called the Federal Buildings 

Fund (41 C.FR. pt. 101-21).

Complementing the responsibilities given to GSA for acquiring space are statutory provisions imposing duties on the Director of the Administrative Office. The Director has a statutory role to provide accommodations, including chambers and courtrooms, to the courts. See 28 U.S.C. § 604(a)(12). The 

Director is generally authorized to provide accommodations only at places where regular sessions of court are authorized by law to be held, subject to a determination by the circuit judicial council that the accommodations are necessary. See 28 U.S.C. § 462(b). These responsibilities are carried out through the Administrator of General Services. See 28 U.S.C. § 462(f). 

In 1984, the Judicial Conference approved the United States Courts Design Guide and other documents to provide guidelines and standards to the Administrator of General Services and to design architects for the construction and furnishing of federal courthouses. GSA has adopted these standards and guidelines for the design, construction and furnishing of federal courthouses and continues to work closely with the Administrative Office. 

The Committee on Security, Space and Facilities provides nationwide policy recommendations to the Judicial Conference on matters relating to the space and facilities program. One of the Committee's responsibilities is to recommend revisions to the Design Guide, copies of which are available from the clerk of court or circuit executive, and from the Administrative Office's Space and Facilities Division at (202) 273-1230. 

Maintenance of Court Space 

As landlord, it is GSA's responsibility to provide levels of standard operating and maintenance services equivalent to those available commercially, including periodic repainting, repairs and alterations, and physical protection and security of the building's perimeter. (Note that GSA provides perimeter security only as distinguished from the courtroom security and personal security services for judges provided by the United States Marshals Service.) (186)

Requests for New Construction or Renovation 

Requests for new construction or renovation projects should be presented to the chief judge. Many controls are in place to monitor courthouse construction and renovation projects, and no appropriation may be made for a public building project that exceeds $1.7 million unless the prospectus of the proposed building has been approved by the Congress. See 40 U.S.C. § 606. 

After approval by the chief judge, the judicial council must approve each request made within the circuit. See 28 U.S.C. § 462(b). Next, the request is reviewed by Administrative Office staff for compliance with judiciary design standards before it is submitted to the appropriate GSA regional office. GSA's regional office determines whether to pursue alterations or an extension to existing facilities or new construction. The request is reviewed by GSA's central office and the Office of Management and Budget before it is submitted to the Congress. (The Director of the Administrative Office has no statutory authority to propose funding directly to the Congress for construction projects.) A space request is examined by four congressional committees before legislation. authorizing construction and appropriating the necessary funds is passed by the Congress and sent to the President to be signed into law.
