FOREWORD 

This is a Supplement to Herr's Law of Marriage, Divorce and Separation in New Jersey published in 1938. 

It contains all the statutes affecting the subject enacted since the publication of the parent work, as well as all new or amended rules of court.

All New Jersey cases decided during the past six years down to 

March 23, 1944, are incorporated.

Another feature that will be found particularly valuable is the forms which it contains. These forms are especially useful in connection with new legislation, such as the Soldiers' and Sailors' Civil 

Relief Act, see pages 9-14.

The supplement is keyed to the parent work by means of page references. Page references appearing in bold-face type such as those on page 1 of the supplement refer to pages of the parent work, thus the material on page 1 of the supplement refers to pages 1-2 and pages 5-6 of the parent work. 

Always consult the parent work first and refer to corresponding pages of the supplement to ascertain whether there are any changes or new developments.

The law of marriage, divorce and separation has never been static. 

This is particularly true now in view of changed social and economic conditions, to say nothing of the problems growing out of the war and wartime marriages and divorces. 

Advisory Master Herr's work is one of the greatest treatises on this subject ever written and this supplement increases the efficiency of the parent work by bridging the gap that has elapsed since its publication. 577036

CAUSES FOR NULLITY OF MARRIAGE 

Pages 1-2 The provision at the end of section 1, to the effect that "no decree of nullity shall be made where a child of the parties has been born, either before or after the marriage, or is likely to be born, unless the court upon an examination of all the facts of the case shall be of the opinion that such decree will not be against the best interest of the child" has not been judicially construed. The provision was added by the amendment of 1937 (N.J.S.A. 2:50–1). 

It seems clear that this amendment affects only statutory causes, not those brought under the general equity jurisdiction. 

PUBLICATION-EFFECT OF APPEARANCE Pages 5-6 

The statute, N.J.S.A. 2:50–12.1 [L.1938, c. 191], has proved to be of little, if any, practical use either in simplifying or accelerating the procedural requirements for securing jurisdiction over an absent defendant. It rather confirms and emphasizes the strictness of those requirements. It applies only when the absent defendant has been personally served with a copy of the petition and notice of order for publication. Until the defendant actually files his answer or appearance the terms of the order for publication must be complied with strictly. Only where defendant's answer or appearance is filed within the first twenty days following the date of the order for publication can publication be dispensed with entirely.

SOLDIERS' ANd Sailors' CIVIL RELIEF ACT 

The federal statute for the protection of defendants who are engaged in the military service of the United States touches divorce and matrimonial litigation only incidentally. Protection is extended to all officers and enlisted men in all branches of the service. 

The statute will be found in U.S.C.A. Tit. 50, §§ 101-165; 510-585.13

In matrimonial litigation this statute requires that if the defendant (male or female) be in the service and defaults, whether he or she has been served personally or by publication, no adverse order or decree may be made until the court shall have first appointed a solicitor to represent the interests of such defendant. The solicitor so appointed may not in any wise compromise the defendant's rights or interests in the suit, but may otherwise represent him. 

In practice the solicitor so appointed corresponds with the defaulting defendant, and experience shows that in most cases he is advised that defendant has no desire to defend the action. The cause is then brought on for final hearing ex parte. The appointed solicitor appears and states on the record the advice or instructions which he has received from the defendant, putting the correspondence in evidence, whereupon the cause proceeds ex parte. However, if defendant advises the solicitor that he wishes to defend (although he has previously defaulted) it would seem that no decree nisi can be entered against him unless he declines to avail himself of a reasonable opportunity to appear and defend, if there be such reasonable opportunity.

If the defendant defaults and the cause is moved ex parte, proof must be adduced that he is not in fact in the military service, as of the date of the entry of the decree nisi. Such proof may be by affidavit, by evidence furnished at final hearing, or by certificates secured from each of the four branches of the service. 

The affidavit (or certificates) must speak as of the date (at least approximately) of the adverse order or decree. No affidavit will be accepted unless it legally sustains the fact of affiant's present knowledge of defendant's military service.¹ 14 In respect to a defendant whose classification is unknown it is to be observed that a negative type of certificate may be obtained in pursuance of section 601 of the statute. Rule 20 was amended in 1940 to read as follows: 

20. In matrimonial causes no non-matrimonial cause of action shall be joined without leave of court. A non-matrimonial cause of action may be so joined if it involves the accounting for and division of property or funds to which the parties have conflicting 15

Pages 19

claims, or if it be such as to affect the matter of alimony or maintenance, although it may require impleading third parties (as, for example, a cause of action to set aside a fraudulent conveyance), or if, in the discretion of the court, such joinder is advisable to avoid multiplicity of suits or otherwise in the furtherance of justice and equity. In case of such joinder the causes of action shall together be regarded as a "matrimonial cause", within the definition of Rule 128(e).

Under this rule, as amended, proceedings against a third party for sequestration and accounting, in conjunction with separate maintenance proceedings, are within the province of an advisory master to adjudicate.¹ la In practice the advisory masters are loath to assume the authority given them by this rule. Many issues which might be joined with a strictly matrimonial count under the rule are properly triable before a vice chancellor. Joinder may be ordered, and two orders of reference made, one to an advisory master on the strict matrimonial issue and another to a vice chancellor on the other issues so joined.¹ 1b In such a case the original joinder is ordered to avoid expense to a litigant who appears to be unable to bear the expense of two suits. The better practice is to litigate the extraneous issues in a separate suit, referable to a vice chancellor. 

No joinder of a non-matrimonial cause of action can be permitted after the adjudication of the matrimonial cause. Nor can an independent suit be consolidated with the original matrimonial cause after decree nisi therein. After the advisory master has made his adjudication of the matrimonial issue referred to him, and has advised the decree nisi (or decree of dismissal), his authority under the order of reference ceases, except for control of the original issue referred to him. To try a new issue after decree a new order of reference would be required. Such an order should then be made to a vice-chancellor, rather than to the advisory master who determined the matrimonial issue.10

Prior to 1942 alimony was not deductible from the husband's, or former husband's, taxable income, nor were payments under an order for separate maintenance deductible from the husband's taxable income. Consequently they were not included in the wife's or former wife's taxable income. 

But by the Revenue Act of October 21, 1942, (c. 619, 56 Stat. 798) alimony and separate maintenance paid periodically (whether or not at regular intervals) under final decree are taxed to the payee and may be deducted by the payor. Alimony and maintenance pendente lite are still governed by the old rule. Where the decree provides for a lump sum payable in installments such payments may be regarded as periodic (so that the new rule will apply) only if they are payable over a period of at least ten years and only to the extent that they do not exceed 10% of the principal in the taxable year.

The wife need not include in her income, nor may the husband deduct, payments made for the support of children. If separate sums are ordered to be paid to the wife and for the children's support, and less than the full amount is paid, the amount paid will be treated, for tax purposes, as paid first to discharge the obligation to the children. For instance, assume wife is to receive $200 and children $50 monthly. If in a particular month $175 is paid, only $125 ($175 — $50) will be income of the wife and a deduction of the husband. 

To the extent that a wife is required to include a payment in taxable income, it is not considered payment by the husband for a dependent's support.
