Of the 1938 farm household heads in Brown county, 1738 or 89.7 percent expressed a preference for one of the various denominations. The Lutheran denomination was pre… ferred by 49.5 of all farm operators more than three times as many as expressed preference for any other church. Fourteen and seven-tenths percent named the Methodist church as the church of their choice; 13.7 percent expressed preference for the Catholic church; 4.4 percent chose the Presbyterian denomination, and 3.5 preferred the 

Congregational church. Other churches were preferred by 39 percent of the farm families while 10.3 percent expressed no church preference.

In a comparison of farm owners and renters it will be noted that these denominations rank in the same order in both groups although there is some variation in the proportions. The Lutheran church was preferred by 53.8 percent of the farm owners as compared to 47.1 percent of the renters. On the other hand the Methodist and Catholic churches each drew somewhat larger proportions of the renters than of the owners in their preference groups. Five and three-tenths percent of the farm owners preferred the Presbyterian church as compared to 3.8 percent of the renters. The proportion preferring the Congregational church was practically the same in both groups while there was a slightly larger percentage in the "Others" preference group among the renters than among the owners. The proportion of household heads with no denomi national preference was lower for farm owners than for renters. 1

Nationality background remains one of the chief factors influencing denominational choice. Brown county was settled by large numbers of foreign born persons who, along with their children, have shown a vigorous tendency to cling to the dominant denomination, or denominations, of their native land. Almost four-fifths (79.2 per cent) of the household heads of Scandinavian descent and about three-fifths (57.7 per cent) of those of German descent expressed preference for the Lutheran church. Thirty-nine and two-tenths per cent of the American group and 30.5 per cent of the British group proferred the Methodist denomination. The Catholic church was preferred by 17.9 per cent of the British group and 15.4 of the German group. 

Since the Finns were included in the "Others" group, a large proportion (60.8 per cent) of this group named the Lutheran church as their preference.

The increasing numerical importance of the "American" and "Mixed" groups reflect a tendency on the part of second, third, and succeeding generations no longer to identify themselves with the nationality of descent German, Scandinavian, etc. 

Foreign culture patterns, including denominational preference and religious practices are greatly modified through the continuous process of assimilation. It is significant to note that denominational preference of the "American" and "Mixed" groups is quite evenly distributed among the several leading denominations. It will be noted also that the proportion having "no preference" is comparatively large in these groups.

Not all of the farm household heads of Brown county who expressed & church preference held church membership. Of the 1938 farm household heads, 1502, or 77.5 per cent were members of some denomination. The five leading denominations, Lutheran, Methodist, Catholic, Congregational, and Presbyterian accounted for 96.4 per cent of the total membership. More than one-half (58.7 per cent) of all members belonged to the Lutheran church. The Catholic church ranked second with 16.8 per cent; the Methodist church followed with 14 per cent while the Congregational and Presbyterian churches had 3.5 and 3.4 per cent respectively. Three and sixtenths of the farm church members were affiliated with other churches.

The Lutheran, Congregational and Presbyterian churches ranked high among the owner group in membership, just as they did in denominational preference. Sixty-one and five-tenths of the owners were members of the Lutheran church as compared with 57 per cent of the renters. Eighty-six and four-tenths of all farm household heads who expressed denominational preference were members of some church. It is evident that certain denominations enroll a considerably larger proportion of their preference group as members than do others. Comparison of the five leading denominations shows that the Catholic church ranks highest, recruiting as members 95.1 per cent of all farm household heads who expressed Catholic preference. The Lutheran church follows closely with 91.8 per cent of its preference group listed as members. The Congregational and Methodist churches enrolled 78.8 and 

73.8 per cent respectively, of their preference group as members. Sixty per cent of those who preferred the Presbyterian church were on the membership rolls.

The chief explanation for the superior recruiting power of the Catholic church lies in the indoctrination programs which it promotes. Children are brought into church membership at an early age. 

Emphasis is placed on the learning of the catechism and in developing loyalty to the church. In certain of the Protestant denominations less effort is put forth in influencing children to become church members. 

As a consequence, many who are reared in these churches drift away because their loyalties have not been cemented by membership. Others continue to express a preference for the church which they attended during childhood, but as they grow older they are less and less likely to become members. The Lutheran church which carries on an intonsive confirmation program among children likewise ranks high in the ratio of membership to preference.

Figure 8 shows the per-centage of farm household heads in each township of Brown county who expressed a denominational preference, along with the per-centage who were members of some church, It will be noted that some variation exists although the general average for both preference and membership is relatively high throughout the county. In two townships, Allison and Greenfield, 100 per cent of tac farm household heads expressed church proference. However, a number of other townships outranked these two in membership. Lincoln township ranked high in membership with all of the 

95.1 per cent who expressed a preference also listed as members of some church. 

A number of townships---principally Gem, Cneots, Putney, and Lansing---appear to be rather seriously unchurched by virtue of the low pcr-centage of membership among household heads. Farm families in these areas are apparently not being adequately served at present. This situation does not call for the organization of new churches in open country areas. In fact, the existence of weak country churches with necessarily inadequate programs often serves as a deterrent to church membership and participation. There are very few farmsteads in Brown county which are farther removed than eight miles from a village center---therefore it would seem that all rural areas could be served by existing church organizations. The vastly improved transportation facilities of the present day have pushed back community boundaries and rendered the. neighborhood church an impractical unit. As previously noted, about four-fifths cf the farm families in Brown county already attend village churches. It is probable that village congregations with a deliberate program in operation could bring in many of the remaining farm families who do not belong to, or attend, any church.

Suggestions for Solving the Problems of Over-churched and 

Unchurched Areas in Brown County 

Brown county is distinctly over-churched in that is has a considerably greater number of churches than the population can adequately support. The National Home 

Missions Council recommemds a ratio of one church per thousand of the population. On this basis, Brown county, with a population of 29,676 in 1940, should have approximately 30 churches. The actual number of churches, however, is 67, or more than twice as many as the recommended figure. At the same time, large numbers of Brown county residents are not availing themselves of church membership or attendance, thus indicating the existence of unchurched areas. The following suggestions are made for solving the problems of over-churched and unchurched areas in Brown county. 

I. Town Areas 

Paradoxical as it may seem, over-churching in towns is partially responsible for an unchurched condition there. Either over-churching within a denomination or denominational overlapping has the effect of reducing adequate financial support. Without sufficient finances it is impossible for a church to maintain a program sufficiently vital to attract the unchurched. As C. Luther Fry has expressed it, "A weak and ineffectual church, like the abandoned church, is a burden upon religious faith. It is a feeble symbol (a dying epistle one might say, known and read of all men) that religion as there exemplified approached impotence." Furthermore, the existence of a large number of small sects,cach with a conflicting creed is apt to have a tendency to produce contempt for the Christian religion on the part of the unchurched. The first step, therefore, would be to cut down denominational overlapping by one of the following methods of comity. Denominational Exchange This is an arrangement whereby two denominations agree to an exchange of churches in two different localities. 

Members of a weak church in a given locality agree to merge with a strong church in another denomination. In another locality the opposite process may be carried on within the same two denominations. b. Undenominational In this type of union the uniting churches sever all connections with denominations. c. Federated This is an arrangement whereby two or more denominations maintain a joint local worship but each of the united bodies continues to keep affiliation with its own denomination. d. Affiliated When this type of union takes place, a loose connection is maintained for certain purposes with some one denomination. 

Since practically all farm families in Brown county live within eight miles, or easy driving distance, of town churches, the solution to the problem the problem of reaching unchurched farm families will probably not be found in organizing additional open country churches. Town churches, which already serve about four-fiftas of the county's farm families, are in a better position by virtue of the better equipment and superior programs to interest the unchurched than are open country churches. However, it will probably be necessary for them to make special and well-planned efforts to appeal to the unchurched farm people of the latter are to be brought into the church. This in rural school buildings, country evangelistic services, and special "Rural Life 

Sunday" programs.
