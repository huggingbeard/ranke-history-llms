COMMENTARY BACKGROUND 

The principal function of the Committee on State Buildings has been its service as the legislative group for visitation of state-owned property and institutions, and for recommendations regarding priorities in appropriations. This is a continuing project of the Legislative Research Council, carried out during every interim since 1955. Two other topics assigned to the Committee have been study and analysis of the feasibility and advisability of procuring commercial insurance on state buildings against loss by fire and other hazards, and an examination of specifications for state construction projects.

STATE BUILDING, CONSTRUCTION, AND REPAIR Introduction 

The Committee has devoted thorough research and careful deliberation to the buildings and land needs of the state for the next biennium. This function of the Committee was a continuation of previous studies of buildings needs and construction practices in the state. The Temporary State Building and Planning Commission, established by the 1955 South Dakota Legislature, completed a very comprehensive study in this field. During the 1957-59 biennium, the State Legislative 

Research Council established a Subcommittee on State Building and Construction, which through its functions emulated the 1955-57 Commission, and attempted to provide the continuity which was demanded by the needs of the state. Again, during the 1959-61 interim, a Council subcommittee was established to provide the necessary continuity. Unfortunately, in neither of the latter two biennia were time nor funds available to allow a first hand review of more than a small portion of state buildings--although consideration was given to all. During the current interim, the Committee was fortunate to be able to visit all state-owned buildings and land which are financed by legislative appropriations.

Functions of the Committee 

In its study of state building needs, the Committee followed these steps. First, it has visited the existing state buildings and has studied their condition and suitability for the constructed purpose. Second, the requests of all state agencies and boards of control which intend to ask appropriations for capital improvements in the 1963 legislative session have been considered. Third, the Committee has calculated the relative degree of necessity for the requested projects. The Committee's careful determination of

-6- priority recommendations has been included as the first recommendation in this report. Fourth, the Committee has, through its recommendations, attempted to contribute to the formulation of a state building and construction policy which will be in the best interests of the state. It is the earnest hope that these recommendations will aid in the development of a comprehensive, long-range plan for financing, rehabilitation, building, repairing, improving and developing all present and future state-owned buildings, facilities, and land of South Dakota. And fifth, the Committee has caused the staff of the State Legislative Research Council to update and expand the 1955 inventory of state-owned buildings and land, excluding those not financed by legislative appropriations. Although the size of the inventory precluded its inclusion in this report, an overall recapitilation is presented herewith. The entire document is, of course, available in the office of the Council. It is hoped that this expanded inventory will provide a good basis for future studies of building needs, and also provide interested legislators with a basic reference work. 

Building Recommendations Priorities The Temporary State Building and Planning Commission, created by Chapter 378, Session Laws, 1955, was directed to study thoroughly the most equitable means and the benefits to be derived from the construction, reconstruction and repair of all state-owned buildings. The report of the Commission had a two-fold purpose. First, it provided the appropriations committees and the Legislature as a whole with a reference manual about the various institutions operated by the state. 

Second, it recommended priorities for the enactment of building requests. This priority listing was of great value to the 

1957 appropriations committees.

Many members of the Legislature have never had the opportunity of visiting all state institutions to view firsthand the adequacy or inadequacy of present physical plants. 

For this reason, appropriations for buildings are often predicated on two items of influence: the persuasiveness of the head of the institution and political practicality, i.e., "log-rolling.' In order to put special appropriations for construction on a more sound footing, through actual visitation and study of the building needs of those institutions, the present Committee has, through its first recommendation, attempted to emulate the success of the 1955 Commission. 11 

Accordingly, the Committee asked all state agencies, except those whose construction and repair expenses are met other than by special appropriations from the general fund, to submit a listing of the building requests which will be presented for considertion by the 1963 Legislature. The directors of the appropriate agencies were also asked to categorize the requests, according to need, as follows: emergency, necessary or desirable. The classifications were

predicated on the same bases as those established in Chapter 

378, 1955. (1) Desirable. The term "desirable" refers to a classification describing a need of a given institution as one that is of importance, but not of prior importance in relationship to the classifications of "necessary" and "emergency" as defined herein. (2) Necessary. The term "necessary" refers to a classification describing a need of a given institution as one that is not only desirable, but is considered to be essential to the immediate welfare of the institution in relationship to its long-range use and development. This indicates a classification more urgently needed than that of being merely desirable, and one that is needed now pending the availability of funds, but not so urgent as to be deemed an absolute emergency or as to indicate an immediate hazard of inability to continue operations for the next biennium. (3) Emergency. The term "emergency" refers to a classification describing a need that is an absolute "must" insofar as the Legislative budget is concerned for which the failure to provide might conceivably endanger the functioning of a given institution during the ensuing biennium.

In order to provide continuity, the Committee has used the same terminology in this report. A listing of the recommended priorities, and amounts for each, is shown in Appendix I. 

Need for Continuous Review

South Dakota's building needs, and especially the needs of her institutions, will present problems for years to come. 

Special building requests will undoubtedly continue to exceed available funds in future sessions. For this reason, a definite need exists for the governing boards to establish priority ratings on building requests for the benefit of the Legislature. It is in this light that it becomes imperative that appropriations for construction receive the most exacting scrutiny and review. Sound long-range planning and good maintenance practices are of equal importance to the state's building program. 

The need of the South Dakota Legislature for reliable and independent information is increasing, particularily in the field of appropriating funds for special projects, such as institutional buildings. The Committee believes that in a 60-day session, time does not permit adequate review and analysis of requests for such appropriations. The building

requests presented to the Legislature every two years often amount to many millions of dollars. And the Committee anticipates that they will continue to be high, in light of the state's needs.

It seems logical that sums of this size should demand a more thorough screening and continuous review by lawmakers. 

INSURANCE OF STATE BUILDINGS Introduction 

The 1961 Legislature adopted House Concurrent Resolution No.14, which charged the State Legislative Research Council to study and analyze the feasibility of procuring commercial insurance on state-owned buildings against loss by fire and other hazards. The topic was assigned by the Executive Board for study by the Committee on State Building (see Appendix IV for text of the resolution).

The Committee has conducted an intensive study into the various aspects of securing commercial insurance. In doing so, it has consulted with representatives of commercial insurers the National Board of Fire Underwriters, and others, and has directed the Council staff to poll the several states to determine their practices concerning commercial insurance, with especial emphasis on a basis for estimating the costs of such insurance.

Brief Analysis of the Problem 

Today state government is big business in many ways. 

The State of South Dakota, although small in comparison with some states, has a very substantial investment in its state buildings. The estimated replacement value of buildings under the supervision of the Board of Charities and Corrections, Board of Regents, and Division of Employment alone totals nearly $116 million (see Appendix II to this report for a recapitulation of the elements making up this figure). It should be noted, at this point, that buildings under the supervision of Game, Fish and Parks located outside Pierre, Highway Department buildings outside Pierre, State Fair Buildings, and armories have not been considered in this report, and that, due to the responsibility of the Committee on Health, only cursory attention has been paid to buildings located at State Sanatorium. 

Many of these buildings, particularily the newer ones, are of fire resistant construction, and, in a few isolated instances (i.e., buildings under construction and dormitories being built with HHFA bond funds), buildings are insured. Yet the great bulk of this $116 million investment is not protected by any casualty insurance.

Until the present time the state has provided its own limited insurance by appropriating money to replace

or repair damaged buildings. While there is little doubt that this has been a wise practice, the rising cost of building replacement and recent losses have led to a request that this policy be examined with a view toward modification. 

One of the chief factors behind this request is the steadily rising cost of construction. For example, a building constructed in 1913 for $1 million would cost $5.72 million to replace in 1961. The new building might be more functional and modern, but the physical size and functions performed would remain the same. 

A second chief factor behind the study request was the number of recent building losses--particularily fire losses.

During the past six years over $2 million damage has been done to state buildings by fire. Nor does the value figure quoted accurately reflect the true loss because replacements have cost much more than the original buildings. 

At the present time, building damage in South Dakota is covered by an "Emergency Fund" appropriated biennially.by the Legislature. The report of the 1955 Temporary State 

Building and Planning Commission did not make recommendations concerning this policy of replacing or repairing damaged buildings. 

This emergency building fund was set up in 1941 with an annual appropriation of $100,000. The 1957 Legislature reduced this amount to $100,000 per biennium, the amount which has been appropriated since. 

During the 1957-59 biennium, the Legislative Research.

Council created a State Building and Construction Subcommittee to carry on the work of the 1955 committee. This group noted that although the cost of construction and furnishings had increased greatly, the appropriation for the emergency fund had not been increased. The Subcommittee observed that South 

Dakota, without insurance on any of its public buildings, was in an extremely vulnerable position. The present fund was termed inadequate to replace any but the smallest state buildings, and a request was made that the fund be increased to at least $400,000 each biennium. Neither the 1959 nor the 

1961 Legislatures accepted this recommendation. 

Since mid-1955 eleven major state-owned buildings have been damaged or destroyed by fire, the estimated loss being more than $2 million. The replacement cost of buildings completely destroyed, however, was much greater. Two of the eleven fires were minor, those damaging the Agronomy Seed Laboratory at the State College in 1955 and Central Building at Northern State Teachers in 1959. The other nine fires, shown in the table below, each did at least $4,500 damage.

Although in most instances cited above the emergency fund would have been adequate to cover the loss, the loss of Central Building at Northern State Teachers College during 1961 points to the need for a better method of state insurance. Through chance this building was destroyed while the Legislature was in session, and it was possible to provide $70,000 to raze the old structure and provide temporary classrooms until the building could be replaced (Chapter 373, Session Laws, 1961). Had the loss occurred during a period when the Legislature was not in session, however, the state would have been left with only $30,000 in the emergency fund for the remainder of the biennium--scarcely enough to raze a fire damaged building with no provision for replacement space except by an appropriation bill passed by a special session of the Legislature. 

Feasibility of Commercial Insurance 

Although the purchase of commercial insurance on stateowned buildings would in no way lessen the possibility of damage by fire and other hazards, the practice would allow the costs of a major capital outlay to be spread over a number of years rather than during one biennium, should major damage occur. Several states have seen such a purchase, therefore, as a feasible and advisable approach to the problem.

During late 1961 the Council staff circulated a questionnaire to the various states to determine whether or not they purchased commercial insurance. The replies indicate that twenty-seven states purchase commercial insurance although the amounts and nature of this insurance vary. Twenty-three states provide their own insurance--twelve simply appropriating money for losses, one through a limited insurance fund, and ten through a cumulative selfinsurance fund, The practices of the various states are shown in Appendix III to this report. It is notable from the table resulting from the questionnaire that South Dakota is the only state shown as having a special or limited fund (Although Hawaii also has a $100,000 "emergency fund" which can be used for a variety of purposes, including the repair of damaged buildings).

The questionnaire also attempted to determine the costs of commercial insurance. The costs, of course, are determined by the amount and nature of the coverage, but the figures quoted might roughly indicate the costs of such a system. 

Montana replied that $100,675,000 coverage cost $240,815 in annual premiums, or, the insurance cost about 2.3 percent of the insured coverage. Rhode Island insured $92,000,000 of buildings for ninety percent coverage for a premium of $147,237 annually, or at a cost of about 1.6 percent of the insured coverage. West Virginia did not indicate the total coverage purchased, but the state did indicate that commercial fire insurance on state buildings cost $281,416. 

Maine reported that the annual premium for $108,360,000 coverage was about $203,000. This insurance included fire, extended coverage, vandalism, and malicious mischief. 

Although the figures quoted above can be used as a general guide to costs, a final determination of the cost of providing commercial insurance for state owned buildings in South Dakota can be determined only after rates are quoted by commercial insurance groups. Consequently, the rates would be negotiated due to the magnitude and unique nature of the coverage. In addition, the state could purchase insurance on all or any portion of the buildings, the. coverage could be for total value or a percent of value, the policy could have a deductible clause at almost any amount desired, or other modifications could be made.

A portion of the costs of commercial insurance, however, would be recovered through claims for damage against the insurance companies. Maryland reports that during the 195059 period 36.5 percent of premiums paid were recovered from claims. During the 1947-60 period, Georgia recovered 39.6 percent of premiums paid through claims. Louisiana reported that the average loss ratio during 1955-60 was 56.4 percent, or, stated differently, that 43.6 percent of the premiums paid were recovered through claims. Using the average of these states, South Dakota could probably expect to recover about 40 percent of the premiums paid to commercial insurance agencies through claims.

Self-Insurance 

Assuming that the major purpose of insuring buildings is an attempt to remove the risk of high expenditures during any one biennium, and spread them over a long period of time, self-insurance will do the job as well as commercial insurance. 

And there is evidence that self-insurance might be less costly than purchase of commercial insurance. Wisconsin, a state that maintains a self-insurance fund, stated in response to the staff questionnaire:

The operation of the fund has been similar to company operation with a deviation from bureau rates beginning at thirty percent and reaching the present fifty percent deviation which has been in effect since 1934.

Surplus in the fund has accumulated steadily and in 1955 the Legislature transferred $5,000,000 to the state general fund. The 1961 Legislature voted to discontinue premiums on state property at any time the surplus on December 31 exceeds $5,000,000. Our present surplus being in excess of $6,000,000, there are no state premiums, but the usual fifty percent premium is still effective on non-state properties of county and municipal units. 

North Dakota replied to the questionnaire that:

The state fire and tornado fund, chapter 26-24 of the North Dakota Century Code, insures all public buildings owned by the state and its political subdivisions. 

Premiums are charged at a rate of not more than fifty percent of the rates set by the Fire Underwriters 

Inspection Bureau. Policies may be assessed when the reserve fund falls below $8 million. 

Kentucky and Oregon have funds which are increased until specified maximums are reached, $2 million and $3 million respectively. Alabama, Florida, and South Carolina have funds but do not have an specified limits on the amounts which can accumulate. Finally, as indicated above, the funds can be created either by direct appropriations from the Legislature or by creation of a state insurance department which would collect premiums from the various divisions of government.

SPECIFICATIONS FOR STATE CONSTRUCTION PROJECTS 

South Dakota statutes make only brief reference to building standards for state-owned buildings. Any building constructed for the permanent housing of human beings must be fireproof, but this control does not extend to buildings at the State Sanatorium nor to buildings for employée housing at institutions under the control of the Board of 

Charities and Corrections (SDC 1960 Supp. 55.1815). Some control over bidding and contractual procedures is also provided (SDC 1960 Supp. 65.07). Additional control is

provided through regulations of the State Fire Marshal and the State Health Department. The result is, however, with minor exceptions, that the state in fact has no building code. 

Yet, the Legislature is requested to appropriate about $5 million each biennium for the construction and repair of buildings. It seems illogical that the state should invest such sums of money in buildings with so little control over the specifications for such projects. 

In reality, the situation is not quite so bad. For a small portion of total state building, where federal contributing funds are used, the standards of various federal agencies are used. In addition, the State Engineer has attempted to follow standard contruction codes recommended as minimum standards including: the National Building Code, the Uniform Building Code, the National Electrical Code various professional journals, standard handbooks for civil, mechanical, and electrical engineering, and fallout protection guides issued by the Office of Civil Defense. While it is laudable that the State Engineer is now using codes as standards, there is no guarantee that they will be used in the future, nor has the State Engineer any explicit statutory authority to enforce the use of such codes. 

In addition to providing control over construction projects, a further benefit accrues from the adoption of a building code. At the present time a good deal of misunderstanding exists regarding the standards required for state building. Legislators and taxpayers alike have questioned whether the state has required excessive standards for state buildings thus dissipating funds on needless "frills." The Committee on State Buildings has examined the standards and costs of state building projects. 

In fact,

The standards do not appear to be excessive. careful review of building plans and prudent changes have resulted in a savings of nearly $500,000 on five major projects at the time the initial contracts were awarded. Later change orders may have reduced the savings to zero on some projects, but this is indicative of what can be accomplished by using a uniform standard for construction. There have also been notable savings effected in construction costs. Information supplied by the State Engineer indicates that nationwide construction costs have increased an average of 4.5 percent per year during the past nine years. During that same period of time, construction costs for state building projects have increased only 1.41 percent per year. The difference is due chiefly to intensive review of plans and specifications to insure competitive bidding and omit any so-called "ginger bread," and by intensive advertising and solicitation of bids.

Adoption of a building code might not eliminate all misunderstanding and complaints, but it would lead to a better understanding of building specifications, provide a standard measure for both legislators and taxpayers, and provide a statutory basis for determing building standards.

If Most governmental officials who are in contact with construction agree that some type of building code is essential to insure that proper building standards are met, especially for safety and sanitation. However, the field is complex and building codes are frequently outmoded. the State of South Dakota is to invest $5 million each biennium in capital improvements, it seems imperative that the state keep abreast of advances in the construction field to insure that building is governed by necessary and appropriate regulations in the interest of public health, safety and welfare. At the same time it must be certain that building regulations are reasonable and not arbitrary or unduly restrictive. The State Engineer, as early as 1925, recommended the adoption of some sort of building code. taxpayers' money is to be expended wisely, realizing maximum benefit for each dollar expended, a standard code for building seems necessary.

RECOMMENDATIONS 

The Committee recommends that consideration be given to the building and land requests listed by the Committee, which are classified according to need (see Appendix I). 

Using the classifications assigned by the various agencies and institutions as a base, the Committee on State Buildings, through careful deliberation augmented by visitations, has developed a priority list of building and land needs. The list is presented in Appendix I to this report, which the Committee submits and recommends to the 1963 Legislature.

It should be noted that the priority listing was drawn up within the framework of the state's needs and ability to provide physical plants for the various institutions.

The recommendations were also made at a particular point in time, based upon the information then available. 

As such, these recommendations do not preclude the possibility that a project now assigned a desirable category listing could become emergency within a very short period of time under certain circumstances. The Committee has attempted to keep the "emergency" recommendations approximately within the generally estimated amount which revenue predictions will permit. Due to this limitation, some requests from the institutions have not been included in this listing. This does not, however, indicate that the Committee necessarily feels such projects are undesirable.

2.. The Committee recommends that all state institutions place increased emphasis on maintenance of their present physical plants. The Committee recommends that attention be given to the budget item for building maintenance. 

Although the buildings at most state institutions appeared well maintained, in some instances it was noted that even minimal maintenance had been neglected. The Committee believes that deferred maintenance, for even a short period of time, is false economy. Small defects in buildings can rapidly become major problems, and the costs of major renovations very often exceed the cumulative costs of providing proper maintenance. In some cases the Committee members were informed that major repairs had become necessary because the limited maintenance budgets would not allow proper upkeep. Proper maintenance will not only be more economical, but will also insure that the buildings can provide the functions for which they are intended.

4. The Committee recommends that serious consideration be given to the construction of a judiciary building in Pierre due to the crowded conditions in the State Capitol Building. 

At the present time the judicial branch of state government is housed in the East wing, first and second floors, of the State Capitol in Pierre. During the years, the functions of the branch have been expanded at a steady rate and the law library has acquired an inventory far in excess of what the present spaces can properly store. There is no additional space in the State Capitol building which can be assigned to the judicial branch to alleviate the crowded conditions. Conversely, there is great pressure for additional space within the Capitol for use by other departments of state government. 

The construction of a judicial building will not only provide adequate quarters for the judicial branch, but will also provide badly needed additional office space in the Capitol building.

This proposal has been under consideration for some time.

The state has now acquired property directly north of the present Capitol which could be used as a site for the judiciary building. It is predictable that state government will continue to grow in size and highly probable that construction costs will continue their steady rise. 

The Committee feels, therefore, that construction of a 'udiciary building should not be delayed further.

5 The Committee recommends that the Board of Regents give careful consideration to a plan whereby parking permits would be issued at the various educational institutions. A nominal to moderate rate could be charged for parking and the proceeds used for improvement of streets and parking.

During the inspection tour, the Committee members noted that a need exists for additional parking areas, particularily at educational institutions, as well as street improvements. 

The Committee members feel, however, that the costs of improving streets and parking should be assigned to the users of those facilities. A good deal of discussion was devoted to whether or not such a practice would bear heavily on the costs of education, but the Committee feels that with reasonable adjustments--such as a lower charge for students who must commute--the charge will not be a hardship on students.

The Committee recommends that the 1963 Legislature give serious consideration to provision for insurance on stateowned buildings, and that the alternate possibilities of establishing a self-insurance fund and procuring commercial insurance be examined with a view toward providing protection at a reasonable cost to the taxpayers of the state. 

After an examination of the replacement value of stateowned buildings, the Committee on State Buildings has concluded that some method of insuring these structures against loss by fire or other hazards is necessary, and that the present, biennially reverting "emergency fund" is inadequate, since it covers minor losses only. The Committee therefore believes that some form of more adequate insurance is essential.

Adequate protection can be supplied by several methods. 

One such method would be a permanent, non-reverting, selfinsurance fund. This method was discussed in detail by the 

Committee, and, if the fund is set up, the Committee would suggest that $250,000 be appropriated each biennium to a non-reverting insurance fund until this fund reaches $1,000,000. 

After the fund has reached $1,000,000, the Committee would recommend that $100,000 be appropriated each biennium until the fund reaches $1,500,000, and that money thereafter be appropriated as necessary to maintain the fund at this amount. 

The Committee would also recommend that the Legislature authorize expenditures up to $100,000 from this fund upon the authority of the Governor, but that expenditures in excess of that amount be authorized only by the Legislature itself.

A second means of providing protection would be the purchase of commerical insurance. The Committee has attempted to ascertain the costs of such an approach to the problem. 

In cooperation with the office of the State Engineer, representatives of commerical insurance agents examined the buildings at Southern State Teachers College at Springfield and State College at Brookings. The rates quoted on these two institutions appeared attractive to the members of the Committee. Unfortunately, the rate which would be used if all buildings owned by the state were insured, and hence the total cost of commerical insurance, was not available to the Committee. Such a rate would be negotiated between representatives of the state and commerical insurance agents. 

Because the total cost figures were not available and also because the Committee did not have the authority to negotiate for the state nor commit it to a particular course of action, the Committee on State Buildings requested that representatives of commerical agents compile figures showing the total cost of commercial insurance and be prepared to present these to the 1963 Legislature. With these figures available, the Legislature will be able to compare the costs of self-insurance with the costs of commerical insurance. 

In addition, the Legislature will be able to negotiate a rate schedule if this approach seems feasible and economical.

7. The Committee recommends that consideration be given to the adoption of a building code for the State of South 

Dakota. 

One means of accomplishing this purpose would be adoption and incorporation of the National Building Code into the laws of South Dakota. Because of its length and the need for uniformity, the Committee suggests that incorporations by reference be considered, but also warns that danger of miscontruction is incurred by such incorporation. 

The incorporating statute should be re-enacted to include future changes in the code, when occurring. Precedent for incorporating such a code by reference was established in 1951, when the National Electrical Safety Code was so adopted (SDC 1960 Supp. 31.04B03).

HOUSE CONCURRENT RESOLUTION NO. 14, 1961 

Introduced by Nauman and Pommer

A CONCURRENT RESOLUTION 

A CONCURRENT RESOLUTION citing legislative interest in, and the necessity for study of, the possibility of commercial insurance against fire and other hazards for buildings the property of the departments and institutions of the State of South Dakota, and charging the State Legislative Research Council with the conduct of such study and the making of a report thereof to the Thirty-Eighth Legislature. 

BE IT RESOLVED BY THE HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES OF THE STATE OF SOUTH DAKOTA, THE SENATE CONCURRING THEREIN: 

WHEREAS, The Legislature of the State of South Dakota is responsible for the construction, reconstruction and replacement of buildings the property of the departments and institutions of the State;

WHEREAS, There is at the present time no insurance against the loss of such buildings by fire or other hazards except through appropriation by the Legislature:

NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT RESOLVED, By the House of Repre sentatives of the Thirty-seventh Legislature of the State of South Dakota, the Senate concurring therein, that this Legislature declare its interest in the possibility of securing commercial insurance against such losses;

BE IT FURTHER RESOLVED, That necessity exists for a study and analysis of such possibility; and 

BE IT FINALLY RESOLVED, That the State Legislative Research 

Council be charged with the duty of making such study and analysis, and submitting a report of such study, analysis and any recommenda tions resulting therefrom to the Thirty-eighth Legislature of the State of South Dakota

Facts about the Legislative Research Council The State Legislative Research Council is a permanent, joint committee composed of every member of the South Dakota Senate and House of Representatives. Its purpose is to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature. To achieve this purpose, the 

Council, during the interim between sessions, gives advance study and consideration to problems expected to confront the next Legislature. 

The work of the Council is conducted by committees and subcommittees and by a research staff, under the direction of an Executive Board. The Council staff serves the Executive Board and committees and subcommittees in development of a program for consideration at the next legislative session, and serves every legislator in an impartial and objective fact-finding capacity. 

In its work, the Executive Board: 1) acts as a clearing house for ideas on current legislative problems through receipt of proposals for study; 2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all topics accepted; 3) disseminates advance information on these problems to legislators and the general public by means of reports and newsletters and by discussion at public meetings; and 4) reports directly to the Legislature in advance of regular sessions, making recommendations in the form of bills or otherwise, and summarizes the material prepared for use of the Legislature in considering the Council program. 

Since 1951 the State Legislative Research Council has provided an open forum to which legislators, administrators, citizens, and organizations have submitted their proposals for consideration. 

As a result of Council activities, problems of South Dakota state and local government receive continuous attention and study by members of the Legislature.
