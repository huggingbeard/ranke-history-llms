INTRODUCTION 

HE letters of Joseph Benson and Sally Thompson were discovered

Tam among the manuscripts of the Thursfield Smith Collection of Wesleyana, which was acquired by Emory University in 1914, and seem never to have been published before.¹ It is a small lot of letters, but the completeness of the collection and its singleness of purpose are sufficient to suggest publication.

That the purpose of courtship was the real aim of every letter is evident. But the ramifications of this courting bring forth interesting influences which were at work in the late Eighteenth Century, and the connection of the two principals with the beginnings of the Methodist Church makes these letters of some significance in a study of the psychology of the period.

People then were much as they are today, and what the family thought of a suitor was important even in that period when the unmarried state was considered as anything but a blessed state for woman. The fear of gossip and its stimulation is evidenced in several letters. But the dominating influence of the Methodist Societies in the actions of individual members, their devoutness, and their deference to Mr. Wesley in even such personal problems as marriage, so clearly shown in these letters, are the main purposes in presenting these letters as A Methodist Courtship. 

Little biographical data concerning Sarah Thompson can be found. In a preface to one of John Wesley's letters2 Mr. Telford has written: "Sarah 

Thompson was the youngest daughter of Thomas Thompson of Knottingley, near Ferry Bridge. She and her two sisters were called "The Three 

Graces.' Her father was dead, and her mother had married S. Dawson of

Leeds, at whose house Miss Thompson lived. Benson married her on 

January 28, 1780." In the Memoirs of the Rev. Joseph Benson only brief mention is made of Miss Thompson, and as Mrs. Benson she is mentioned only when accompanying her husband. That Sally was poorly educated is shown by her spelling, and by the awkwardness and selfconsciousness of her epistolary style. From her frequent apologies she

Joseph Benson, the son of John Benson and Isabella Robinson, was born on January 25, 1749, at Melmerby in the parish of Kirk Oswald,

Cumberland, England. His father intended him for the ministry, and early records show that his own original intention was to become a minister of the Established Church. 

Joseph Benson was educated in the village school and later was placed under a Mr. Dean, a Presbyterian minister, with whom he lived and studied for several years. While under the tutelage of Mr. Dean he became well grounded in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew, and read widely in the divines and the other religious writings of the day. At the early age of sixteen he became a teacher in a school at Gamblesby, where he taught for about a year, during which period his interest in the Methodist Society was aroused by a cousin, Joshua Watson, who had already come under its influence. In his seventeenth year he became personally acquainted with John Wesley, and so strong an impression was made by each on the other that Joseph was appointed to the office of Classical Master of Kingswood School, where he continued his private studies. 

That Mr. Wesley maintained the high regard which he first entertained for Joseph Benson is attested to by an extensive correspondence, from 1768 to 1790, which discloses real esteem and affection throughout the years. In 1769 he entered Oxford, and in 1770 he was appointed by the 

Countess of Huntingdon as head master in the college at Trevecca, which she had established in South Wales. 

Although he had come into direct contact with, and under the influence of both Presbyterians and Methodists, Benson did not separate himself from the Established Church, but returned to Oxford to prepare himself further for ordination. "He applied for ordination, bringing with him a testimonial from the bishop of St. Davids, but the bishop of 

Worcester refused to ordain him. He alleged the absence of an academic degree as excuse, but the real reason was his intimacy with the methodists. Thereupon Benson went over to methodism, and he exercised his ministry in successive circuits." In later years he expressed himself as

As early as the period of his teaching at Kingswood School he was persuaded to engage in public prayer and, because of the difficulty of obtaining the services of a regular preacher, occasionally to preach to the colliers in the neighborhood. He was untrained in public speaking and suffered from great timidity in preaching. However, his sincerity, plain speech, and true religious warmth gave him power in the pulpit in those early days and he came to be one of Wesley's most influential and forceful preachers. "Mr. Benson, as a Preacher, had perhaps fewer faults, and more excellencies, than ordinarily fall to the lot of one servant of 

Christ, however gifted."8

He was a voluminous writer, and his numerous publications show him to have been a man of thorough scholarship as well as a great preacher. 

He wrote a number of pamphlets in defense of the Methodists, who were so much attacked in the early days of the Society, and his Life of the Rev. 

John W. de la Flechere (1804) and his Commentary on the Holy Scriptures, begun in 1809 and completed in 1817, are works of permanent value. His Sermons, collected and published after his death, are still to be found in libraries and in the private collections of ministers. Throughout his life he possessed the dignity and self-command, simplicity and devoutness which these letters exhibit. He died February 16, 1821, aged seventythree years, and his remains were buried in the City Road Chapel, London.

A METHODIST COURTSHIP Rochdale July 29, 1779 

Dear Miss Thompson,

I have often thought on you since I had the pleasure of being in your company at Armly¹ above a year ago, & have wish'd to see you again; but as often as I have been at Leeds since that time, I have not been so happy as to meet with you. I did not care to enquire for you, unless in a distant manner, for obvious reasons & providence never threw you in my way, so that how much so ever I desired another interview, I was denied that satisfaction. Last summer indeed, I was told you were at H-- & had I known how to direct to you, you should there have received a letter from me. For I wish'd to know how your soul prosper'd, remembering you were in no little distress about your spiritual state when I saw you at Armly. And it seem'd to me you hurt yourself (as I have often done) with vain reasonings & groundless fears, wch malady I hoped might be remedied by dealing tenderly with you & giving you proper encouragement. This office I should have been glad to have sustain'd, that I might have had the happiness of administering comfort to one for whom I had conceived a very great regard. But hitherto I have not been able to do more than wish you well & pray for you, being quite uncertain whether you were returned to Leeds, or if you were how to see you or direct a letter to you. And I should have been uncertain still had I not (I hope providentially) enquired of Miss Ritchie,² whom I saw at Kighly last week concerning you & obtained your direction from her. I was glad to

Dear Sir,

I am Much obliged to you Dear Sir, I will endeavour to tell you something of the state of my Soul, that I may have your prayers & kind advice. He yet strives with me, though, I am very disobedent to him. Since I see you I have at times laboured under sore temtations, & by them, I think the Lord has often let me, in some measure, know what a weak creature, I am, in myself, & if he did not mercyfully interpose for me, I have been ready to fear, I shou'd destroy myself for ever. O what wou'd my heart not be capable of doing, did not God mercyfully keep me. The temtation I am most frighted with is concerning the sin against the Holy Ghost. Dear Miss Thompson, Manchester Augst. 11, 1779

I expected your answer a few days sooner but find, upon the receipt of it, I am not to blame you but the distance to wch providence had cast you for the delay. I think myself obliged to you for the confidence you have put in me & the free & unreserved manner in wch you have diclosed the state of your soul. And I shall rejoice if the Lord shall direct me to say any thing by way of reply, wch may, thro' his blessing, be of any service to you.

The situation of your mind, according to the discription you have given of it, is indeed not the most comfortable or desireable. And yet it has its use in the mean time, & out of this darkness light shall arise to your comfort & establishment. By the various exercises that you speak of, fightings without & fears within, you certainly learn to know two things viz. your own weakness & where your strength lieth, & to know them, as [11]

you cannot know them from any other means so effectually viz. from experience. Now if you are truly in earnest (as I trust you are) the consequence will be, you will die to all dependence upon yourself & every person & thing, & will look only to that grace for help wch is alone sufficient for you. And then the Lord will help you indeed & will perfect his strength in your weakness. When you have thoroughly learnt that of yourself you can do nothing, that you are not sufficient of yourself to think any thing as of yourself, & when you see too, that the best books, sermons, counsels, ordinances can do nothing for you, without God: when weary of a life of alienation & distance from the Father of your spirit & the Bridegroom of your soul, you pant for God even the living 

God, wishfully crying Oh! when shall I appear before God; & when in & thro' all means & creatures you look only to him & long only for him: then shall you know, following thus on to know the Lord, for his goodness is prepared certain & reviving as the morning, he will come unto you chearing as the rain, yea as the former & latter rain upon the earth, to water, refresh & enrich your soul with all the graces of his Spirit. And then "Sing ye to her, a vineyard of red wine. I the Lord do keep it, I will water it every moment lest any hurt it I will keep it night & day.- 

In the mean time permit me to advise & entreat you not to rest satisfied in your present state: you are undoubtedly called to enjoy greater & better things even to live & walk in the Spirit, experiencing his witness & bringing forth his fruits day by day. Now be you fully persuaded of this: settle in your very heart, that you are called to be an habitation of 

God thro' the Spirit; & be satisfied also God alone can put you in possession of this blessing, & then go to him for it. With desire and confidence go to the throne of grace & go again; pray & persist to pray: Be instant in prayer for this one thing that he would lift up the light of his countenance upon you & baptize you with the Spirit of his grace & rest assured, if we [that are] evil are inclined to give good things to our children, [he] will much more give his holy Spirit to you who thus ask him. Only if the blessing tarry wait for it: For it will surely come & will not tarry long.

I shall only add at the peril of your soul do not reason, do not trifle: but in simplicity & faith draw nigh to God, reconciled to you in Christ, & he will draw nigh to you. His compassion [s] are moved toward you, his bowels yearn over you & he will by no means cast you out. Trust in the 

Lord with all your heart & you shall not be confounded. 

You have not told me when you return to Leeds, or how to direct this to Scarbro'. I shall perhaps be in Leeds within a few weeks, when & where can I see you? Do let me know & write soon & particularly to 

Your most sincere friend & obedient Servt.

My dear Sally 

Halifax Octr. 27, 1779

You know I told you we must write to each other every day. I have but a few moments, & employ them in telling you I most affectionately love you & long for your happiness in body & soul. I was sorry that I was obliged to take such an abrupt leave of you, but I could not help it: my master's work required it. And, my dear love, I can tell you for your encouragement, he is indeed a good Master & his service is perfect freedom. Blessed be his dear name he never fails the expections of those that trust in him. Last night I experienc'd much of his goodness in preaching at Bradford. The congregation was very large & I went into the pulpit hardly knowing what text I shd. preach from: but he was with me & enlarged my heart & open'd my mouth & made the word spoken a blessing to many. And in our affair, my dear, I have seen most manifestly the leadings of his providence: & have had the most remarkable answers to prayer. And I want you my Jewel, to trust in him & to cast all your care upon him, for most assuredly doth he care for you. Oh! dare to believe his love his infinite & unbounded love to your soul & you will be set at perfect liberty: Look not at yourself but at the love of God in Cht. Jesus. 

You will smile when I tell you, I got this paper &c & retired a moment till dinner was ready to write to another person: but I no sooner sat down than my heart constrain'd me to drop you a line & in return I expect you to tell me all your heart & if you direct to me at Mr. Lavender's Stockport, near Manchester, I shall get your letter on Sundy. & then I sh'll write you more particularly. Put en [tire] confidence in me & believe me

Rochdale Decr. 18, 1779 

It is now two long days since I parted with you, during wch time, however, you have not been often absent from my thoughts: and it is with pleasure that I now sit down to converse with you in the only way yet in my power. I hope by & by to have my dear love with me & then I shall be able to converse with her in a sweeter and more endearing way, without the medium of Ink & paper. In the mean time I send you as much love as my paper can carry, persuaded I shall not be inclined to cease writing (tho' I am straiten'd for time) till my paper can hold no more. 

I wonder at you, my Jewel, for saying you find such a reluctance to write: but it must be (& tell me is it not?) because you feel but little of the love 

I bear for you, my precious creature. But I excuse you, my love; I do notcannot expect you to find as much love for me as I find for you: because I am unworthy of it. But you--stop my heart & dare not to commend the object of thy affection lest she shd. be temtped to think too highly of herself & being forsaken of the Lord shd. become less worthy of thy regard. You, my dear, like me, are a poor weak & wretched sinner, unworthy to be regarded by God or man: but nevertheless you are regarded by both. Yes my Sally, the Lord regards you & has given his only begotten

Son to die for you, & because, with a penitent heart, you believe in him therefore are you justified from all things. And I entreat you, my love, for your own peace & for the honour of God, dont reason & fear, but confide in the Lord your Father & friend & trust in his faithful promises, for be assured, they are all yea & amen in Cht. Jesus & they are all yours. You have hurt yourself, my dear, exceedingly, (I dont say by being too conscientious, for that you cannot be, but) by being too scrupulous respecting little things. Remember, my love, that Love is the fulfilling of the Law & that the main things in love are 1st. giving the heart to the 

Lord & 2d. intending to please him in every thing. Only secure these two points & you may rest assured you can do nothing amiss but are accepted in every thing you do. Ask therefore in faith nothing doubting, & you shall have the petitions that you ask. - You can [not] think how often I have been ask'd since I parted [from] you at Armly - Branly - Bradford, Hallifax, &c whether we were not married on Mondy. last.
