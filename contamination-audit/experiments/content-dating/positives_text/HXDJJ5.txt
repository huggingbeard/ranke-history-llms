INTRODUCTION , THE FARM BOY 

HIS story depicts the life of a farm TH boy, from the farm and "THE

LITTLE RED SCHOOL HOUSE" to the thriv ing village, and finally to one of the greatest of cities, with its allurements and numerous pitfalls. In these three phases of life are expressed four incentives, which motivate human careers: psychology, tragedy, romance and telepathy. 

The tendency of the story is to show the value and protection of prayer, not only for the youth, but also for the adult. For without sincere prayer it is impossible to live in harmony with God.

It is only in service to God that we can obtain peace, happiness and prosperity

in this life, and unshaken faith in a life hereafter.

CHAPTER I  

ID was a young boy whose parents lived on a farm near a little village called Carysville. It was so named after a great land owner, Mr. George Cary, who many years ago obtained a charter from the king of England for large tracts of land in this and other counties of New York State. Mr. Cary owned a number of farms in the vicinity of Carysville, and also owned a part of the little village itself.

Sid's father worked one of Mr. Cary's farms for several years. Sid and his brother, Joe, became of school age while their parents lived on this farm and

they got their first schooling in a large one-room school house that stood on the edge of the little village. The school teacher at the time was a Mrs. Esmond. 

She was one of the old class of fifty years or more ago, who never believed in spoiling a pupil by sparing the rod. 

In her very few rules of conducting a country school, the one at the top was "Strict Discipline." She insisted on the keeping of this rule regardless of other school exercises. Sid and Joe got the rather bad habit of throwing away bread or plain food that they might have left in their dinner basket. One day, when their father was going to the village, he spied a couple of slices of bread at the roadside. He picked it up and brought it home to mother. Mother recognized at once that it was her bread, and mother did make excellent bread-large oldfashioned loaves. Right there the two boys were taught a timely lesson in economy and frugality.

In those days there was an old minister by the name of Richard Rouse, familiarly known as "Dickey," who went about the country on horseback preaching. As I heard him say once, his "circuit embraced Otsego and one or two other counties." He could be seen riding along on his old horse, and if men were working in the field nearby or he met people on the road, he would stop, recite a passage of scripture accompanied with a few religious instructions. Everyone who knew him admired and respected the old preacher. One day, when making his rounds, he called at Mrs. Esmond's school. She was well acquainted with the old gentleman. It was his custom, whenever he called at people's houses, to begin his visit by making a prayer. And this he did in

Mrs. Esmond's school, and, as I remember, it was quite a long and good one.

While he was visiting with the

teacher, the boys were let out for afternoon recess. It was late in the fall and there was a fleece of soft snow on the ground. At once the larger boys began packing a good supply of snowballs intended, of course, for Dickey when he came out. Soon the old gentleman left the building and walked across the street to the place where he had tied his old horse to a tree. No sooner had he untied the horse and got into the saddle, than the boys let go a fusilade of snowballs, hitting the old man in the head and face and all over his body. Many of them plugged the old horse in the side.

One would naturally have thought the old preacher would have whipped up his old nag and gotten away from the sudden attack as soon as possible. But instead, he wheeled the horse around and charged straight for the group of boys. Immediately there was a wild scramble for the front door of the 10

school house. Sid, half frightened to death for Dickey did look dangerous coming on with a big whip in his hand ready to bring it down on the back of the first miscreant he could reachrushed for the door, too. He was caught in the jam and knocked down in the entry, where some of the larger boys scurried over the top of him, treading on him with their coarse boots.

Mrs. Esmond ran to stop the rush, but it was impossible. It was an actual stampede. So she grabbed him by the arms and pulled him out of the frightened crowd. I can see her now, wiping Sid's hands and face with a towel and looking him over to see how badly he was hurt. I am not sure how many of the boys in the rear of the rush got a wallop of Dickey's whip. And it came pass that Dickey Rouse, the old traveling preacher, taught the mischievous boys of Mrs. Esmond's school that he to 11

had more than one way of imparting moral instruction.

At this time there lived in the neighborhood nearby an old Irish woman, whom we will call Mrs. Hannigan. She was one of the old rugged sort and had lived a good part of her life in Ireland. 

Mrs. Hannigan was quite fond of Sid's mother and came frequently to visit her; many of her calls were made in the evening. The old lady had stored up and brought over from the old country a wonderful collection of ghost stories. 

One would have thought to have heard Mrs. Hannigan tell ghost stories that a considerable part of the population in 

Ireland was ghosts. They might be seen in hedges, dark alleys, along lonesome roads. Every old vacant house had at least one, and sometimes a ghost was discovered dwelling in a house with the living. It would seem that the guardian saint who, it is said, rid the Emerald

Isle of much of its evil, for some rea- 12

son spared the ghosts. Perhaps he thought they might help keep the young folks in their own homes at night.

Sid listened to these weird tales with boyish interest, and they seemed to do him no harm, at least not at the moment; for he never went away from home alone at night, and quite often three of the boys slept together in one bed. But a change was in store for 

Sid, and he was soon to meet new experiences. 

Just over the hill in the next valley there lived a farmer by the name of 

Hiram Brown. Mr. Brown owned a small farm which he worked mostly alone. He had no boys of his own. One day he called to see Sid's father to ask him to let Sidney come and help him with chores and light work through haying and harvesting. His father consented and Sid went home with Mr. 

Brown. At the end of the first day, when the chores were done and supper 13

was over and darkness had come, the farmer lighted a lamp and said, “Sidney, I will show you to your bedroom now." 

Sid followed him upstairs and into a sleeping room in a wing of the house. Mr. Brown set the lamp on the stand, bade Sid good-night and walked out.

Sid looked about the room and saw there was a large closet in one corner with the door open; also an open door of an adjoining room. Suddenly there came a strange feeling over him, such as he had never experienced before. Old 

Mrs. Hannigan's ghosts began to pour into his mind and the most horrible stories he had heard her relate were vivid before him. He thought to himself, "There may be a ghost in that closet and one in the bedroom with the open door," and he began to feel most uncomfortable. He knelt down by the bedside to say his prayers, a duty his mother had taught him never to neglect. 14

He said his evening prayer with such speed as never before, all the time trying to look through the back of his head, for he felt sure there was a ghost in the closet and another standing in the open door of the adjoining room, both looking straight at him. 

He got up, undressed as quickly as possible, piled into bed and covered himself up, head and all, with the comfortables to make sure, if there should be any ghost prowling about in the night, he would not see it. The same procedure was followed every night for nearly a week. In haying and harvesting the weather is usually the hottest of the summer, and Sid, covered up so snugly with the bed clothes, was uncomfortably warm and perspired profusely.

Mr. Brown was a widower and his mother, quite an old lady, kept house for him. She noticed that the bedding showed that Sidney was sweating a 15

great deal each night, and about the end of the week she said to Hiram, "I don't see what makes that boy sweat so nights." Hiram decided he would investigate Sid'sleeping quarters. A half or three-quarters of an hour after 

Sid had gone to bed, Farmer Brown went upstairs and very quietly entered the room. He found Sid covered up, head and all, and curled up like a squirrel in its nest. He took hold of the covering and began pulling it off Sid's head very carefully.

Sid was just dozing off to sleep when he felt the bed clothes moving. He was frightened immediately, for he fully believed that one of old Mrs. Hannigan's ghosts was surely after him. He gripped the clothes with all his might and tried to keep them over his head.

The ghost pulled and Sid hung on, but after a little the ghost got the better of the struggle. When the comfortables were gone, Sid was frightened almost 16

into hysterics, for he expected to see something far more horrible than the skull and cross-bones peering right into his face.

He was about to give a scream that would, no doubt, have aroused the whole house, when he heard Mr. Brown say in a reprimanding voice, "Sidney, what are you covering your head up like this for? What is there about here that will hurt you?" Sid was one glad boy when he found out it was Farmer Brown who stood by his bedside, and not one of 

Mrs. Hannigan's frightful ghosts. Sid plucked up courage and tried to shake off the nonsensical idea of ghosts, and he slept more comfortably afterward. 

He finished his stay with the Browns and in the fall began going to school for the winter with his brother and two sisters.

There lived in this same neighborhood a farmer by the name of Smith

Harding. He was well acquainted with 17

Sid's father and they had dealings together as farmers are wont to have. He knew Sid quite well, for Sid went occasionally to visit his boy who was a few years older. Smith Harding had a brother, Daniel, who lived in a little hamlet called Locust Corners, several hours' drive from Sid's home. Smith had told his brother Daniel about Sid and that he thought he would make a good boy for doing chores and light work on his farm. 

Mr. Daniel Harding had no children.

About the middle of March Mr. Harding came to see Sid's father and have 

Sid come to live with him. It was bargained that Sid should work for Daniel the ensuing summer for thirteen dollars a month. Sid was not yet thirteen years old, his birthday happening on April 

13th, a date which was near at hand. 

On the last day of March, Sid's father took him with a horse and cutter to what was to be his home for a while at 18

least. In those days it was customary for people who hired out to farmers, to begin the season's work on the first of 

April. 

On their way they stopped at Uncle 

George Ganoe's at Carysville for a few moments. Uncle George, as he was familiarly called by the little community was one of the pioneers of that small settlement. He saw that Sid had his few extra clothes in a newspaper under his arm, and he said to his wife, "Jane, haven't we a satchel in a closet upstairs that Sid could have to put his clothes in? He hasn't anything to carry them in but a bit of newspaper." Mrs. Ganoe went upstairs and came down presently with a satchel and handed it to Sid. His clothes were at once deposited in it and 

Sid said to himself, "I will surely make a much better appearance going into my new home with my clothes in a good-looking valise than if I had them in a newspaper under my arm." 19

Uncle George was one of those bighearted, whole-souled men, found frequently in those early days, who, if you came traveling along hungry to his home, would gladly give you a meal; and if you were in need of lodging, he would take you in and keep you all night, and send you on your way rejoicing in the morning.

It was the longest journey that Sid had yet taken, and it was a very interesting trip, for he observed intently everything he saw along the way. It was on Sunday and they arrived at 

Locust Corners about noon. Sid's father stayed and visited with the Hardings for a couple of hours or so, and, after partaking of a good farmer's dinner set off for home.

Sid busied himself looking about the place, for he realized now that he had some new things to learn, until it was time to do the chores. Then he went out with Farmer Harding to get ac- 20

quainted with the stock, horses, hens, and the work he would be expected to do. The chores were finished and, after their Sunday evening lunch, they sat by the dining room stove; Mr. Harding, a big fat, good-natured man of about sixty years, and Sid on one side, and his wife, Martha by name, a medium-sized woman of about the same age as her husband, on the other side.

By Mrs. Harding's side was a small table on which were a few papers, magazines and a large book. Mrs. Harding was a good reader and a woman of intelligence. Sid had very little to say during the evening, for his thoughts were back in his home at Carysville. 

He was wondering what games his brothers and sisters might be playing, for they usually played some game before going to bed. 

Eight-thirty o'clock came, and time for bed. The old lady looked over the top of her spectacles at Sid and said, 21

"Bub," for this was the name by which

Sid was to be called by the old couple, "did you ever hear the Bible read?" Sid replied, "Yes, ma'am, my mother reads the Bible to us quite often." Then she inquired again, "Did you ever hear it all read?" Sid replied that he had not. 

Then she said, "I read a chapter in the 

Bible every morning and evening, and for your benefit I shall commence at the beginning of the book, and perhaps while you are with us, I can read the most of it for you. 

Then she opened the large book which he saw lying on the small table, and read the first chapter of Genesis. When she had finished she lighted a candle and said again to Sid, "Now, Bub, I will show you to your room." Sid followed her into a short front hall at the back of which was a wide, old-fashioned staircase. Up the stairs they went and at the top he found himself in a large, empty room, at the farther end of which 22

were some small sleeping rooms and one or two of the doors were open. 

Sid glanced about the big room, andespecially at the bed rooms at the farther end, and there came over him a feeling of suspicion, for old Mrs. Hannigan's ghosts came pouring into his mind and again a shudder went through his whole body. He said to himself, "Oh, dear me! have I got to go through this big, empty room every night on the way to bed?" Whatever pleasure he might have anticipated in his stay with the Hardings, right then was pretty much spoiled. In after years, when Sid became a man, if he could have had an office in an immigration bureau, beyond all doubt he would have barred all old Irish women and other women, too, who had their heads full of superstition and nonsensical ghost stories from ever coming to America. It is likely, indeed, that he would have banished them all to some lonely island where they could 23

live in the company of their self-made ghosts. 24

It was weeks before Sid could pass through one end of the old ball room on his way to bed without some feeling of dread. He invariably cast a glance of suspicion at the bed rooms in the rear, especially if the doors were open. 

Sid was now at the end of his second week at Locust Corners and on the eve of his thirteenth birthday. It was Saturday evening, and, as he was eating his supper, Harry Williams, a boy of about his own age, came in and said, "Sid, I want you to come and visit me 25

this evening and stay all night." The invitation sounded good to Sid, for although the old couple were treating him very kindly; much the same as if he were their own boy, yet he had been somewhat lonesome during the two weeks. "All right, Harry," he replied, "as soon as I finish my supper and do a few chores, I will be right over." He

When the chores were done, he hurried off to spend the night with Harry. 

Old Squire Williams, Harry's grandfather, lived in a large white house, a few minutes' walk from the inn. was a well-to-do man and Harry had a comfortable home with the old Squire.

He wore nice clothes and had many toys, playthings and picture books that boys of such families usually have. 

The two boys had a royal fine time with Harry's good things until about 9 o'clock, when Harry got up from the floor where they were sitting, looking over some picture books, and walked 26

over to his grandmother, a rather stern old lady of about sixty years, and said something to her in a low tone. Whereupon she arose from her chair, stepped over to Sid and said, "Sidney, it will not be convenient for me to have you stay with Harry tonight. You had better come some other time." If she had poured a bucket of cold ice water on 

Sid's head and down his back, she could not have chilled him worse. He bade Harry good-night, accepted his cap and walked out. 

It was quite a little step over a narrow path across the big Williams' dooryard to the highway. So intent was

Sid in pondering in his mind why 

Harry's grandmother did not let him stay all night, and at the same time watching the narrow path, for it was quite dark, that he did not look up until he came to the edge of the road. Then he looked over to the old inn which was on the opposite side of the highway 27

and, lo, and behold! everything about the house was as dark as a pocket. 

Not a light to be seen.

He stood for a moment or so and said to himself, "Now, this is a nice pickle that I am in. Those old people have gone to bed and I shall have to wake them up and get in. What will they say when I have to tell them that 

Harry's grandmother would not let me stay all night?" He was not very well acquainted with the Hardings yet. It seemed to Sid a very uncomfortable position for a boy to be in. But there was nothing left to do, except to face the music.

Sid went in through the woodhouse to the kitchen door and knocked several times, but no reply came. Then he went around to the front door and knocked on it as loud as he could, but not a sound from within. Next he went to the window of the old couple's bed room in the rear of the house and 28

rattled and banged on it and shouted several times, but no response. All seemed as dead as a graveyard within. "Well," said Sid to himself, "those old people must be sleeping like logs, if they can't hear all the noise I'm making." Then he went around to the front of the house again and sat on the steps to counsel with himself as to what he should do.

After thinking his plight over for a few moments, he decided the only thing left for him was to sleep in the barn. 

So he went to the horse barn, where the cutters and sleighs were kept and where he knew there was a robe and some horse blankets, and, after groping about in the dark for a few minutes, he found a robe and a blanket and made himself a bed in an empty horse stall. He had been lying on the robe, wrapped up in the blanket, but a few minutes when he was in the land of dreams. 29

In those days, about fifty years ago, we had what are called "old-fashioned winters," when there were much heavier falls of snow, and which, it now seems, lasted much later in the spring. On this night, April 12th, there was still a great deal of snow on the ground. 

The old farmer and Sid were drawing wood and gathering sap on a sleigh. 

Sid slept soundly in his horse stall bed (farm boys are almost always good sleepers) until late in the night, perhaps 1 o'clock, when he awakened from a sense of cold. His feet were cold and he was getting quite chilled. He got up, stamped his feet and walked back and forth in the barn until he felt quite warm again. Then he lay down. to try and sleep once more. But he had become thoroughly chilled and sleep was banished for the night.

So Sid gave up the idea of more rest and walked about, wrapped in the blanket, and kept himself as warm as 30

he could until morning. At the first peep of daylight, he began doing the chores, glad to have something to do to keep warm. Mr. Harding had several head of young stock in a barn some distance away on another part of the farm. Sid went and fed this stock first, then came back and fed the cows and horses, cleaned the stables and curried old Ned and Bill, a pair of Canadian ponies used on the farm.

He was just finishing brushing old Ned when Farmer Harding walked into the stable. The old gentleman was surprised to find Sid in the barn, for he supposed he was sleeping snug in bed with Harry and would not be out for some time yet. So he said, "What, Bub, you haven't got the chores done already, have you? We never get up so early on Sunday mornings." Sid replied, in a rather shy tone, that he had them all done. Mr. Harding, surmising that something was wrong, began ques- 31

tioning Sid closely until the boy had to acknowledge, somewhat reluctantly, that Harry's grandmother would not let him stay with Harry all night and that he had slept in the barn. 

Then the old gentleman said, "Bub, that is too bad. Let us go to the house." Sid and his employer went to the house and into the kitchen where 

Mrs. Harding was preparing breakfast, and he said to her in a voice somewhat peeved, "Martha, Granny Williams did not let Bub stay with Harry last night and he had to sleep in the barn.” "Well," she said, "how did that happen, Bub? I hope you haven't caught a bad cold." Then Sid replied, "When I came back to the house, you people had gone to bed, and I did not seem to be able to wake you up." "Why, Bub," said the old lady, "we were not in the house.

We went to Mr. Ames', (a farmer close by) to spend the evening and did not get home until after ten." 32

But Sid had taken a severe cold nevertheless, and came very near to having a run of pneumonia. The old lady doctored him up at once. She gave him a hot foot bath with plenty of mustard in the water and hot herb tea to drink together with other old remedies of grandmother's, and in a few days he was out again helping 

Farmer Harding do the chores and gather the sap, apparently none the worse for having slept in a horse stall on the eve of his thirteenth birthday. In after years, when Sid became a man, he thought quite often that it was a bit queer that his father should have hired him out to the old farmer at 

Locust Corners for thirteen dollars a month and that he should have met such an episode on the night of this particular birthday. But these were only ripples in the young stream of life.

A number of weeks passed by, and 33

the winter was gone, the maple sugar season was over, the warm weather came and they began to work in the fields. One morning as Sid sat at the table, the old gentleman said to him, "Bub, after you finish your breakfast,

I want you to take Ned to the blacksmith shop and have him shod." Mr.

Ames, who lived a short distance away, was the smithy for the communty about 

Locust Corners. Sid finished his breakfast, then went out and got old Ned and led him by a halter to the blacksmith. He sat in the shop and watched the smithy do the shoeing.

The horse was shod and Sid was starting out to lead him home in the same manner he had come. Whereupon the smithy said, "Sidney, get on and ride; there is no use walking." Then he gave him a lift on to the horse's back, and at the same time he gave Ned a good whack on his hind parts and said, "Get up." Ned trotted down the 34

lane to the road and as soon as he reached the highway immediately changed the trot into a gallop, and the farther he went, the faster. One would have thought to have seen old Ned flying along the road with Sid clinging desperately to the halter with one hand and clutching the mane with the other, that the horse had suddenly regained his youth, for he was said to have been twenty-one years old at the time.

On the opposite side of the old inn was a barn constructed with an open shed for teamsters, in bygone years, to drive under. In the rear of the shed were a number of horse stalls and a wide, but rather low door to go in.

As old Ned came to the front of the inn, lunging along, it occurred to Sid that if he went plunging in through the low doorway, he might be banged against the top casing. So, as soon as 

Ned came to the edge of the shed, Sid 35

crouched as low as he could on Ned's back in the hope that he might get through the door without being hurt.

Ned never slackened his speed until he came right to the door. Then he stopped dead, apparently waiting for Sid to get off. 

Sid slipped quickly to the ground and stood holding the halter a moment, thinking, "I wonder if old Ned knew that he might have jammed me against the door casing if he had gone in galloping as he was? I believe horses know more than folks think they do." 

Sid was settled in his new home now and quite attached to the old couple, for they treated him kindly and were interested in his welfare. Mrs. Harding was a splendid cook and she tried to make things that she had learned that 

Bub liked. Usually for breakfast they had large, square cuts of old-fashioned ginger bread with their coffee, and quite often she would make large, fluffy, light 36

biscuits with a chunk of maple sugar in the center. These good things appealed to Sid's keen appetite. It is said that "good digestion waits on appetite and health on both." Farm boys don't need tonics for their appetites.

The exercise in the fields among the new hay and grain and crops and fresh air supplies all this. 

Three months had gone by. It was now the first of July and Sid was promised a visit home. Saturday morning 

Old Squire Williams fitted up Ned with a good looking saddle, and after breakfast Sid and Ned set out for Sid's home. About two miles from the village there was a by-road that ran diagonally across to the highway near his home. 

Sid chose the near cut because it would save a mile or more. Part of the byroad ran through some woods, at the further edge of which it was shut off the public road by a gate. 

Sid jumped off the horse to open the 37

gate. From where he stood he could look across just one large field and see his dear, old home. He stood gazing intently at it for a few minutes to see if it was really the same old place; for it seemed to Sid that he had been gone for a very long time. Just then he saw his mother come out of the house and go to the old square well curb with its wooden reel, chain and bucket and draw a bucket of water. She poured the water into her pail and returned into the house. Just as she was disappearing through the door, unaware and scarcely knowing why, tears began to stream down his sun-burned cheeks. 

Just then Sid heard the rumble of a wagon coming down the road on his right and, looking, he saw it was a team, democrat wagon and a family who lived in the neighborhood, probably going to the village for their week-end shopping. He turned the horse around and let him back into cover of the trees 38

and shrubs, and for a few minutes gave away to his feelings. (And here we see a characteristic of the boy. Perhaps later on we shall see the characteristic of the man.) Presently he saw that Ned seemed to be looking straight at him, and he said to himself, “I wonder if old Ned knows what a silly boy I am." At that he dashed the tears from his eyes, turned Ned around, led him through the gate, closed it quickly, jumped into the saddle, and in a few minutes was riding up the lane to the rear of the house. 

His father was at the barn and came hurriedly to shake hands with Sid and take the horse. His brothers and sisters came running gladly to see him and were very much interested in the Canadian pony. Mother stood in the back door all smiles, ready to greet Sid when he came in. It is needless to say that Sid Granger had one royal weekend visit. In the joy of being home 39

once more with the family, the old couple away in the inn were almost forgotten. The week-end passed quickly, altogether too soon for Sid, and on 

Sunday afternoon toward evening Sid and old Ned set out on his return trip to his new home. They arrived at 

Locust Corners just as the old couple were sitting down to their evening meal.

Sid joined them in the repast, soon after which the chapter in the Bible was read and they retired.

It was the custom of Mrs. Harding, as she said to Sid on his first night with them, to read a chapter in the 

Bible every morning and evening. It mattered not if clouds were rising in the sky and threatening rain, and there was ripe hay and grain cut in the fields that should be put in the barns or other crops waiting to be gathered, the help had to remain in the house and hear the chapter from the Bible immediately after breakfast. Often when Sid was 40

working about the buildings or in the fields alone, he would be pondering in his mind the strange things that the good old lady had read from the Bible.

The Old Testament is, indeed, a strange, and in some parts a rather weird story. At times, when the occasion admitted, she would kneel by her chair and say a sincere, intelligent prayer in which there were always a few words for "Bub." She was a Methodist.

The time had passed pleasantly with 

Sid at Locust Corners. The fall had come and his father arrived to settle with Mr. Harding for his summer's work. Sid had worked something over six months and earned a snug little sum for a boy of thirteen, especially in those days, fifty years ago, when working people did not get fabulous wages they have been receiving in these later years.

After Mr. Harding had paid Sid's 41

father, the old gentleman drew a crisp new bill from his purse, and, handing it to Sid, he said, "Here, Bub, is five dollars for you. After you have had a visit home, I want you to come back and stay with me this winter and help do chores and go to school." There was a small district school close by the little hamlet. The man on watch at the front door, who was no other than Mrs. McHenry's husband, had given him a sign by a string hanging from the window above, or a similar device, that Sidney was here and it was the proper time to rush out and give him a horrible fright. Sid had neither seen nor heard anything from Ruth, when one bright morning about the first of May, when all nature seemed to be putting on its broadest smile, as he was going down Broadway, to work, as usual, who should step out from a side street on the right of him, and pass directly in front of him, only a few feet away, but queenly Ruth.

She did not raise her eyes to look at him, and walked leisurely across Broadway. As she stepped up on the walk on the opposite side, she turned partly around and gave him one, long, lingering look, and the dispatch that came from her said as clearly as if spoken in loud words, "Good by; I am leaving the city for home." She was on her way to the depot to take a train. Sidney gave a deep sigh and his whole being seemed to relax, and he said to himself, "Is the curtain now down on the last act?"
