GLANCE AT THE PAST The Franciscans from the beginning of their Order have been closely associated with Lithuania and Lithuanians. As early as 1245 we find them working in areas populated by 

Lithuanians. Henry of Luxembourg, a Franciscan who in 1257 was appointed Bishop of Žiemgala by Pope Innocent IV can rightly be said to be first apostle of Lithuania. Later

he was appointed to the Diocese of Kuršas. Because most of the boundary of Lithuania was within the limits of the Diocese of Kuršas, he is acclaimed first Bishop of Lithuania. His zeal for these people was boundless and his episcopacy is marked

The Franciscans had great influence during the period if the creation of the state of Lithuania in the beginning of the thirteenth century. The Franciscans were present during the Baptismal and Coronation ceremonies of Lithuania's first King, Mindaugas (1251).

On many documents we find the signatures of the Fathers. Henry of 

Luxembourg was appointed by the Pope to be protector of Lithuania. 

In 1263 when King Mindaugas was brutally killed, the Catholic church and the Franciscan Order felt this blow keenly, since in him they had found a bulwark to ward off the enemies of the Catholic religion which the Lithuanians had recently embraced.

It was only during the reign of Vytenis in 1298 that a treaty was made between Ryga and Lithuania to work jointly to fight the inroads of the Order

of Livonia which was set to molest the nation. When a Franciscan, Frederick of Persteins became Archbishop of Ryga, the relations between the ruler Vytenis and Ryga were strengthened. In 1312 Vytenis built a Franciscan monastery and church in the city of Naugardukas.

During the reign of the Grand Duke Gediminas (1316–1341) Franciscan Fathers were counselors and secretaries to him. The 

Grand Duke showed his appreciation for the work these Fathers were doing by building a new monastery and church in Vilnius and renewing the one destroyed in Naugardukas, and requested the Father Provincial of Saxony to send more Franciscans to labor in Lithuania. 

The Grand Duke of Lithuania Jogaila and his household were prepared, instructed and baptized by the Franciscans in Cracow. In 1385 these religious men jointly with Vytautas went to Lithuania to baptize the Lithuanians and bring to them the light of Faith. In 1387 when the first Diocese in Lithuania was established at Vilnius, a Franciscan, Andrew Vasylius, who figured in the baptism of the Lithuanians, was made its first bishop. It was this great apostle who founded the first schools in Lithuania, built churches and monasteries, and by educational religious influence made the Church stronger. When he died, his work was carried on by Jacob Plichta (1398-1407) another Franciscan, the second 

Bishop of Vilnius.

After an increase in the number of members in the monasteries, in 1530 the first Lithuanian Franciscan Province was established.

In the beginning this Province had only thirty-one monasteries but at the close of the nineteenth century it had under its jurisdiction 60 monasteries and a thousand members. Their specific duties were administrating to the spiritual needs of the Lithuanian faithful and teaching in the schools.

The first elementary schools in Lithuania were founded by the Franciscans. These were usually near the larger monasteries.

Another wave of Church persecution reached Lithuania when Soviet Russia occupied Lithuania June 15, 1940. All the monasteries and religious educational institutions were closed and all property confiscated. The religious were once more scattered, with some going to their own homes and others living privately. Some others were arrested and deported to 

Russia. Those who were more fortunate fled to other countries.

The Fathers who remained in Lithuania were deported or killed on the scene of their labors when the Russians occupied Lithuania for the second time in 1944. The last superior in the monastery at Kretinga was publicly tortured and then thrown into a truck and carted to an unknown destination. After so many efforts and sacrifices and hard labors, the Commissariat of St. Casimir was once more dispersed at one Russian gesture.

Because the number of Franciscan Fathers increased and the scope of their work widened, plans were made to build a larger monastery and chapel in Greene, Maine. These plans could not materialize because of the high cost of building material at the time. 

The next best move was to purchase a large house suitable for their religious activities.

Such a house was round in Kennebunk, Maine, near the Atlantic Ocean, the former William Campbell's estate, which was purchased after securing a loan on the feast of the Nativity of B. V. M., September 8, 1947.

ST. CASIMIR'S MONASTERY, BROOKLYN, N. Y. 

After establishing themselves and adding more members, the Franciscan Fathers of St. Casimir's Commissariat began to spread their cultural activities. The Fathers realized the necessity of keeping alive the Faith of our fathers which could well be done through the press, and so they began the realization of strengthening the Lithuanian press. 

It was through the efforts of the Very Reverend Justin Vaškys, O. F. M., that in December of 1941 "Varpelis" (The Bell of St. Francis) began to be printed. This religious magazine had eighteen thou-

​sand readers in Lithuania, but as all the press was suppressed by the Communists in June 1940 in Lithuania, so too, this magazine received its death stroke. It is now the tenth year since its publication has been resumed in America. During all these years the articles have continued to give the Lithuanian Catholics inspiration and a continuous urge to fight for human rights.

When it became impossible to continue in Germany the publication and printing of the cultural-educational magazine "Aidai" (The Echoes) due to financial difficulties, the Franciscan Fathers undertook to continue the publication of this magazine. October 4, 1949, they began to work on it and are trying to make it an outstanding publication. The best writers in the various fields of culture are contributing their writings to this magazine. Entering its third consecutive year under the able direction of the Fathers, "Aidai" shows a great promise to be a widely read publication. 

One of the great steps undertaken by the Franciscans was the combining of several papers into one. For a long time there were discussions to concentrate upon one solid newspaper rather than have several with small circulation of each and too many localized news of no interest to the Lithuanians living in other cities. The newspapers in question were the semi-weekly "Darbininkas" (The Worker) of 

South Boston, Mass., the weekly newspaper "Amerika" (America) of 

Brooklyn, N. Y., and "Lietuvių Žinios" (Lithuanian News) of Pittsburgh, Pa. The well-thinking publishers of these newspapers thought it well to centralize all these papers into one which would meet the wishes of the demanding readers. The Franciscan Fathers took up this idea of consolidating these three newspapers into one and began its publication twice a week under the name of "Darbininkas" (The 

Worker) in their printery in Brooklyn, New York.

With the invaluable assistance of the pastors Rev. N. Pakalnis, Very 

Rev. Msgr. J. Balkūnas, Rev. J. Aleksiūnas. Rev. J. Petrauskas and with the permission of His Excellency Thomas E. Molloy, D. D., Archbishop of Brooklyn, the Fathers established a foundation and printery at 680 Bushwick Avenue, Brooklyn, New York, and on the 16 of April 1951, work was begun here. The solemn blessing of this established 

Franciscan Monastery took place on December 9, 1951.

The editorial staff is comprised of choice men of letters and a group of well chosen associate editors who by their contributions give appeal to the readers. It stands stauchly on Catholic principles and the readers get the best matter at a minimum amount of words. 

Besides the newspaper "Darbininkas" the Franciscan Fathers print the "Aidai" (The Echoes), "Šv. Pranciškaus Varpelis" (The Bell of St. Francis), "Ateitis" (The Future), a magazine for youth, "Karys" (The Soldier) a magazine for Lithuanian Veterans. A fine line of books. is being printed, and in the near future they are contemplating to print also some much needed Lithuanian text books for schools. 

To the Franciscan enterprise belong also the pastoral works. As missionaries they are always going to various Lithuanian parishes in the States conducting missions, retreats and helping the pastors. Special interest is taken in working among the Lithuanians in the State of Maine. Prior to the establishment of the Lithuanian Franciscan monastery in Maine, there were no organized Lithuanian parishes, and therefore the religious status was at low ebb. 

Catholic Action is not left in the background but greatly activated by the interest in Tertiary Fraternities throughout the United 

States and Canada. Under an independent St. Casimir's Province, 85

Lithuanian Fraternities have been rejuvenated and made more active. 

Lastly it is noteworthy to state that the Franciscans did much for the Displaced Persons even while these were in Europe. They wrote many affidavits and when they came to the United States, they found work for them, and also a place where these people could start life anew. In order to spread their activities even more and prepare for future labors in Lithuania, the Fathers are working for more vocations. A number of clerics are studying for the priesthood, and some younger priests are attending Universities to prepare themselves for pedagogical work. When Lithuania will regain her independance, the 

Fathers will be prepared to step into the work of reconstruction, and also bring the light of Faith to the vast lands of Russia. Turning the historical pages of St. Casimir's Province, it is plainly seen that its paths were thorny and painful. Looking back to the monasteries destroyed by the Communists, the members scattered and wandering throughout the world, it appeared that the road of St. 

Casimir's Province led to death.

But the ways of Divine Providence are marvelous. After living through the agonies and torture, the Lithuanian Franciscan Fathers of 

St. Casimir's Province began to see the brighter future. In God's country of Freedom - America, the Province arose to new life.
