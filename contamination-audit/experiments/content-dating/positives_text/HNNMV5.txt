F I KNEW Dr. Johnson would not mind, I should change it to read thus: "...words are the daughters of earth, and thoughts are the sons of heaven." Probably that is what he really meant, anyway. 

You often hear people say that words are poor things; that they stumble and fall, as our poet Riley says, and are unworthy to be the bearers of our human messages to each other, whether of love, or sorrow, or heartening joy. I do not think so at all. I think that words are beautiful things - that they have gathered unto themselves, through ages and ages, the most marvelous qualities as servants to our souls. 

One day I asked a young painter which he would rather do if he had his choice-paint great pictures, or compose great music, or write great books? He regarded me intently for a moment, a look of tense interest replacing the baffled expression habitual to his face; "Well...write great books, of course," he answered

This young painter has crossed over now to the Other Side, where he can probably come much nearer to the articulate expression of feeling toward which he struggled.

As servants of expression, words have colour and flavour and odour. They sparkle with humour, they tremble with emotion, they can crackle with anger or thunder with indignation; they are alive with meaning!

I always admired them immensely, and have feared them also, with a wholesome kind of fear, ever since the day I really read for the first time that verse in Proverbs which says, "Death and life are in the power of the tongue."

It is a truism, of course, that reading with the eyes only is not reading at all; my eyes had encountered that verse in Proverbs a hundred times. We simply brush past the words as we do the crowds in a busy street. 

I never realized until lately how much I missed by this habit. Now, I look as I go, and am beginning to see with a better understanding the faces I pass. Walking in earthly loneliness myself, I am learning to see loneliness in the faces around me, as well as that peace of the spirit which God gives to those of us who need it so greatly! Many a time lately I have been tempted, as doubtless you have also, to stop short on a crowded street, accost some perfect stranger in trouble, put out my hand and say, "I'm sorry...can I help?"... But I've never done it... have you? Did you ever try weighing and testing and measuring words against each other to catch their scintillating facets of exact meaning, as a jewel expert does his gems?

All words are worthy in their place and requirement, for every one of them is a faithful conveyor of some meaning intrusted to it by generations of usage and perhaps through centuries of word evolution. But just as there are blue white diamonds and commercial whites, and blue-blooded sapphires and humble turquoise, so are some words more distinctive than others and more beautiful. There are those whose pronouncement is a trumpet call to head up and foot forward; there are those that soothe like honeyed flattery; there are those that sting to resentment or conviction. There are those that bless and caress us!

There is mere physical courage, which rushes in where angels fear to tread, and is hollow as a gourd when you come to examine it, but that kind is only a step-cousin to moral courage.

We once had a little Swedish seamstress who divided her time among half a dozen families, and was known to the group as Gentle Annie.

She had never been taught to sew, nor served an apprenticeship. Her first two years in this country were spent as a nurse-maid, and she had graduated from that into mending, and then making over, and plain sewing. We never thought of her as anything but a seamstress until the day we discovered that Gentle Annie was in reality one of those intrepid souls who would try anything once, as the phrase goes.

There it was! The spirit of those who march forward, the builders-the empire makers. "I try" was the standard Gentle Annie had nailed to her masthead, and she never hauled it down. 

Of course she got her chance, then and there. I held my breath as she cut into an expensive piece of broadcloth, but what mistakes she made, she corrected, and very soon I left her to herself. She bought and studied a pattern, as one who adventures into a trackless wilderness would study the chart of an experienced traveller who had gone before. She measured and basted. She used her brains; I could positively see the birth of creative thought, deduction, and balancing judgment in her mild blue eyes.

And in the end she turned out a coat which no one need have been ashamed to wear, not even a fussy person spoiled by a perfect Scotch tailor. The fact was, a designer had been born, and Gentle Annie's status ceased to be that of a mere seamstress.
