INTRODUCTION 

Blackstone in his famous Commentaries says that science and law should in some manner and in some degree be the study of every free citizen. Progress of civilization has long since established science as an important factor in everyday life, but for various reasons many people overlook law. Yet in business, trade and in the management of personal affairs law is the concern of every individual. 

A well-established legal maxim is that ignorance of the law excuses no one. It is not until they have some unfortunate experience that the average man and woman discover how completely ignorant they are of their rights and liabilities under the law. 

Is it not, therefore, an act of wisdom to acquire a useful knowledge of the legal rules which govern those personal and business relations most likely to affect one's daily life? Buying and selling property, the making of a will, the requirements for a valid contract, marriage, and divorce, interest and usury are but a few examples. 

The Home Law Book was compiled by a layman for the use of laymen and does not, in any sense, purport to be a textbook. Standard reference works were consulted in its preparation. 

Because there is wide variation in State laws, the treatment of each topic must, of necessity, be general, except in special instances where tabulations of data are given for each State. These facts have been checked as of 1943, but since laws are constantly changing, it is wise to consult a competent lawyer when in doubt or to write to the proper State or local official for the latest information. A lawyer's business is not so much to get people out of trouble as to prevent their getting in, and no law book can take the place of expert advice when the need for it exists.

JOHN Contracts 

OHN MARSHALL defined a contract as an agreement in which a party undertakes to do or not to do a particular thing. To this may be added that it is an agreement for the violation of which a court will award damages.

Contracts are the basis of all modern business activity ranging from transactions covering only a few cents, such as buying a newspaper, to those involving the outlay of millions of dollars. 

To be binding a contract must have the following essentials: 

1. It must be made by competent parties. 

2. The subject matter must be lawful. 

3. The parties must consent mutually to the terms. 

4. There must be sufficient consideration. 

Competent Parties. Certain persons are incapable of entering into. a binding contract for the reason that they do not possess sufficient mental capacity. These include lunatics, idiots, drunkards. A minor, or person under legal age, may enter into a contract but, with certain exceptions, is not bound to fulfill his contract with an adult, though the adult is bound by the agreement. Minors are bound on contracts for necessities to maintain their well-being in their particular station of life. Such necessities generally include food, shelter, clothing, medical attendance. and education. They are also liable for fraud, i.e., obtaining benefits by misrepresenting their age.

Under the common law married women could not contract. The majority of States have removed such disability now and married women can contract almost as freely as their husbands. 

Subject Matter. Illegal contracts include those in direct violation of law and those against public policy, i.e., a contract to do anything which, though not forbidden by policy, is injurious to the peace, good order, health or morals of the community. Such contracts are unenforceable.

Mutual Consent. This means that the minds of the two parties must meet. They must agree to be bound by the terms of the contract of their own free will. Consent is not real if obtained through misrepresentation, fraud, mistake, duress or undue influence. Such defects may cause a contract to be unenforceable.

Consideration. A simple promise to do or give something is not a contract. There must be a consideration for the promise, some benefit to be derived from the performance of the contract. It may consist of money or other property or services. A promise is sufficient consideration for another promise when both are made at the same time, i.e., if a person makes a promise in return for another person's promise, the law 580583 3

will require him to make good his promise when the first party has fulfilled his.

Negotiable Instruments CHECKS 

A CHECK is a written order by a person upon his bank, directing it to pay a specified sum of money upon demand, to a certain person named thereon, or to bearer.

When a person deposits a sum of money in a bank in a checking account, he receives a check book and thereafter can draw checks against his deposit. All checks that the depositor draws are charged against his bank account, and when paid and canceled are returned to him.

The use of checks is a great convenience in business and a check also serves as a receipt. 

Every check should be made out accurately, for carelessly drawn checks may lead to errors for which the maker is responsible. Name, amount and signature should be written in ink, the amount of the check in both words and figures. If there is a discrepancy between the two, the written words prevail, i.e., the sum indicated by the words is the sum payable.

A check drawn to bearer is payable to anyone who presents it. 

It is advisable to number checks and to keep a record of each one that is drawn.

When a check is received it should be presented for payment within a reasonable time. This generally means the next business day after delivery of the check.

Indorsement. Before presenting a check for payment the person to whom it is drawn endorses it, i.e., signs his name on the back. 

There are various forms of indorsement. The one in common use for checks is known as blank or general indorsement, consisting merely of the signature of the indorser.

Certified Check. A bank is not liable to the holder of a check unless and until it accepts or certifies it. A certified check bears the words Certified, Accepted or Good affixed to it by the teller or other proper bank official. This means that the bank has honored the check and will be responsible for it. It guarantees the drawer's signature and indicates that he has sufficient funds in reserve to meet the check. 

Post-Dated Check. When a check is dated ahead payment can be demanded only upon the day of its date or on a succeeding date. The 

Negotiable Instruments Law provides that an instrument is not invalid for the reason that it is ante-dated or post-dated provided that this is not done for any illegal or fraudulent purpose.

Lost Checks. If a check is lost, the person who drew it may notify the bank to stop payment. The notice may be given verbally but to be binding the order must be in writing and describe the check accurately. 

When a bank receives such an order each of the depositor's checks presented for payment is scrutinized carefully until the lost check is found or the order is withdrawn. This means time and expense so that stoppayment orders should not be given without just cause. It is bad practice to have checks stopped merely to escape the consequences of drawing against insufficient funds or for other petty reasons. If payment is stopped wrongfully the maker of the check will be liable for damages. 

Overdrafts. A person who keeps careful record of his checking account is in little danger of overdrawing. If an overdraft does occur by mistake, the bank may honor the check if it is not for a large amount and the depositor is not in the habit of overdrawing. A depositor, however, has no legal or moral right to expect the bank to pay such checks or to notify him of shortage in his funds.

Joint Accounts. For the sake of convenience joint banking accounts are often maintained by husband and wife, different members of a family or any two persons, whether related or not. These accounts may be checking, savings or thrift and can be opened by the two persons either as joint tenants or as tenants in common. 

As joint tenants either party may, during the lifetime of both, deposit or withdraw the whole or any part of the funds. If one dies, the survivor is entitled to the balance. 

As tenants in common either party may deposit, but must act jointly in withdrawing. If one dies the balance is divided equally between the survivor and the estate of the deceased. 

PROMISSORY NOTES 

A promissory note is an unconditional written agreement by which one party undertakes to pay to another a specified amount at a fixed or determinable future date. It may be to order or to bearer, and must be signed by the maker.

Unless the note reads with interest it will bear no interest until the day of maturity. If it contains these words, interest will be computed from the date of the instrument. Where no rate is specified, the legal rate fixed by the statute of the State in which the note is payable, will apply.

The words to order or to bearer or their equivalent must be used to make a note negotiable. A note payable to a named person and not payable to order is not negotiable.

A note drawn "without interest" is legal. However, if said note is not paid when due, then interest may be collected from that date to date of payment at the legal rate. Interest

INTEREST is the amount paid for the use of money. The rate that may be collected is usually fixed by State laws. Nearly all the States provide for a legal rate of interest that may be collected on those claims that properly bear interest but in which no rate is stipulated. The lawful rate is any rate agreed upon up to the maximum which is fixed by statute.

Compound interest is not favored by the courts and will not be allowed unless the State law authorizes it, or unless the contract expressly provides for or implies it. 

In computing simple interest the United States Rule is commonly used except where the contracts of the parties or the usage of trade indicate other method. Payment is applied first to interest due. Any balance is applied to the principal. If the payment falls short the

balance of interest is not to be added to the principal so as to produce interest.

The Mercantile Rule is permitted when it is agreed upon by the parties or is the custom of the trade in which they are engaged. By this method the balance of the debt is obtained by calculating the interest on each item of the debt on one side and allowing interest on each payment on the other.

Usury. A rate of interest in excess of the maximum rate permitted by statute is usury. Penalties vary in the different States. In some, only the excess of legal interest is lost, in others the entire loan is forfeited. In certain States the taking of usurious interest is a misdemeanor subjecting the lender to prosecution.

Buying Property 

TITLE. The word title refers to the ownership of property. It means the right to possess and enjoy the property as well as the right to transfer it to another.

In buying and selling land the customary procedure is to sign a

contract of sale. The Statute of Frauds requires this contract to be in writing. It constitutes an agreement to convey the land at some future date and provides for two things, first that the seller will deliver the deed on a given date and second, that the buyer will pay an agreed price. 

The contract specifies what kind of title is to be conveyed as well as many other important details. When these things are accomplished the transaction is completed and the title closed. Buyer and seller are entitled to exactly what was specified in the contract. 

Searching Title. Before closing title the purchaser is advised to search the records affecting the property in question or to have a competent attorney or title company do this for him. This is to make sure that the seller has the right to convey what he agrees to convey and to determine whether or not there is any defect in the title or existing claim against the property.

Deeds. A deed is a written instrument by which the title to reall estate is transferred from one person to another. 

The laws of the States prescribe certain formalities in the execution and delivery of deeds. In most instances the deed must be signed, sealed, witnessed before a notary public or other authorized individual, acknowledged, recorded and delivered. A deed is not valid unless delivered to the person to whom it is granted (grantee) or to his duly authorized agent. The deed may be delivered conditionally to a third party with the understanding that he is to deliver it to the grantee upon fulfillment of a certain condition. This is delivery in escrow. 

Unless the contract of sale provides specifically for a full covenant and warranty deed, the purchaser is entitled only to such a deed as is sufficient in law to pass title. 

The most important deeds are warranty deeds and quit claim deeds.

In a warranty deed the grantor warrants that he is the owner of the land and has the right to convey it and that the grantee shall have peaceable enjoyment, i.e., shall not be put out by anyone having superior title.

A full warranty deed provides in addition that the lands are unencumbered by mortgage or other encumbrance (unless otherwise stated); that the grantor will defend the grantee in these rights and if necessary execute a further instrument to perfect the grantee's title. 

Recording the Deed. The first thing to do after title is closed is to have the deed recorded by giving it to the proper person in the county in which the property is situated. This may be the recorder of deeds, county clerk, clerk of the circuit court or other person having charge of such matters.

The object is to give notice to the world and to protect the purchaser against the possibility of adverse claims by a subsequent purchaser.

Wills 

LAST will and testament or, more briefly, a will, is an instrument executed by an individual during his lifetime to take effect at his death, and by means of which he disposes of his property. 

A person who leaves a will is said to have died testate, otherwise intestate. The person who makes the will is the testator; those who receive benefits from it are the beneficiaries. When a person dies without leaving a will (intestate), the court appoints someone to administer his estate. The executor is the party named in the will to carry out its provisions.

Formalities of a Valid Will. The law relating to wills differs in the 

States but the general rule is that a person must be of sound and disposing mind and not under restraint.

In a majority of States the required age for making a valid will is 21, but a common provision is that persons over 18 may dispose of personal property by will but only adults of real property. 

The will should be in writing, i.e., typewritten, printed or handwritten. It should state specifically the disposition and the name of the beneficiary of each piece of property, real or personal, which it is desired to bequeath.

It should be signed by the person making it and be witnessed by at least two (in some States three) credible witnesses of the age of majority, who shall sign in the presence of the testator and of each other, affixing their names and preferably their addresses. There are 

States, New York for example, which impose a penalty for witnessing a will without adding one's address. Witnesses should not be beneficiaries under the will.

The laws of New York provide that a will must be subscribed by the testator at the end of the will, in the presence of the attesting witnesses or must be acknowledged by him to have been so made, to each of the attesting witnesses, and, at time of making and acknowledgment, must declare the instrument to be his last will and testament. 

The usual attestation clause reads: Signed, sealed, published and declared by.. the above named testator as and for his last will and testament in the presence of us and each of us who, at his request and in his presence and in the presence of each other have hereunto subscribed our names as attesting witnesses this..... day of. . . . . 19....

Shortened Form of Will. A will may be brief and still comply with the requirements of the law. An example is the will of the late 

Chief Justice White of the Supreme Court of the United States which read as follows:

Washington, D. C., June 26, 1915.

This is my last will. I give, bequeath and devise to my wife, Leita M. White, in complete and perfect ownership all my rights and property of every kind and nature, whether real, personal or mixed, wherever situated, appointing her executrix of my estate without bond and giving her Seisin thereof.

Edward D. White. 

Signed, sealed, published and declared by the said Edward Douglass White as and for his last Will and Testament, in the presence of us who, in his presence, and at his request, and in the presence of each other, have subscribed our names as witnesses. 

Richard H. Nugent, 1231 T St., N.W. 

Bertram F. Shipman, 1745 Oregon Avenue.

Holographic Will. This is a will written entirely in the handwriting of the testator. Such wills carry more weight than others as indicating the testator's wishes. Because it is difficult to forge such a will Louisiana, and probably other States, hold such a will valid without witnesses.

Nuncupative Will. This is an oral will, such as may be made by soldiers and sailors on active duty or at sea.

Codicil. Any testator of sound mind can alter or modify his will whenever he pleases. In most States he can do so by adding a supplement or codicil to it. The codicil requires the same formalities as the will. 

Revoking. A testator can revoke a will by destroying it or by making a new will. When a testator makes a subsequent will it operates as a revocation of the prior will.

Probate. This is the proceeding whereby the will is proved and established by law. When the testator dies, his executor or administrator applies to the court for authority to administer the estate and distribute the property. The court may be the probate or surrogate court. Statutes in the different States regulate the time and manner in which the will must be presented. Where there is no statute it can be proved at any time after the death of the testator.

Contesting a Will. The various States provide by statute as to the method of contesting a will, generally specifying a time limit in which such action can be commenced. Descent

WHEN a person dies without having made a will (intestate), the law determines to whom the property shall go. As a rule real property 10

descends to the heirs, subject to the estate of dower or curtesy in surviving wife or husband, and personal property to next of kin. 

Statutes of the various States define who the heirs shall be, but generally provide that real property descends substantially as follows: 

1. To the children, or if they are dead, the grandchildren. (In some States where dower and curtesy are abolished the widow or widower is counted an heir and takes one-third to the children's two-thirds.) 

2. If there are no children, grandchildren or great-grandchildren, real estate goes to the parents of the intestate if living, or to the brothers and sisters, or, if the latter are dead, to their descendants. 

3. If none of the above-mentioned are living, then other relatives inherit, such as uncles, aunts, etc., and their descendants. 

Statutes differ to some extent concerning the disposition of personal property. The administrator first pays all the debts, and the remainder is distributed among the next-of-kin, or nearest blood relations, substantially as stated above except that a widow usually receives a con siderable portion.

A person under legal age is known as an infant under the law. He gains his majority the day preceding his twenty-first birthday.

In some States a legal marriage contracted by a female relieves her of the disabilities of infancy.

Eminent Domain 

EMINENT DOMAIN is the power of a nation, state or province, or of public service corporations to whom the power has been lawfully given, to appropriate private property for public use. The ownership and possession of such property is then appropriated to the announced public use, after the owner is paid due compensation, to be ascertained according to law.

According to constitutional law of the United States, no person can lose his property by eminent domain except it be taken (1) for public use, (2) by due process of law, and (3) for just compensation. The first condition has been held by the courts to include not only public improvements carried on directly by the State but also private or semi-public undertakings, such as railroad bridges, etc. There is some difference of opinion as to what constitutes "due process of the law." The usual method is by condemnation proceedings. Just compensation means payment of the full value of the property taken or of any interest therein. 12

Marriage 

'N THE eyes of the law, marriage is a civil contract entered into by two persons of the opposite sex to live as husband and wife. Like other contracts it has certain essential conditions that must be fulfilled to make it legal. Lack of them renders it void. 

A marriage is regulated by the laws of the State in which it takes place. Laws differ widely and efforts to adopt a uniform marriage law have thus far proved unsuccessful. Information upon any point in doubt can be obtained from the nearest marriage license bureau or from a competent attorney.

Essentially, marriage is a contract between husband and wife involving mutual consent, concluded publicly and with the approval of society.

Consent. This is necessary to a valid marriage. It means that the parties must be willing and free from duress or undue influence. 

Competent Parties. If one or both parties are not competent to give legal consent no valid marriage can result. Incompetence may mean mental incapacity, want of sufficient age, a previous marriage not legally ended, physical incapacity, consanguinity or other disability defined by state law. 

License. All States require a license and prescribe certain formalities to obtain it. The license is issued by a designated official who must satisfy himself that no legal impediments to the marriage exist. 

The issuance of a formal license gives publicity to the proposed marriage. 

About 30 States also provide that the ceremony be performed in the presence of witnesses by a specified civil official or clergyman. Some 

States permit one witness; the majority specifies at least two.

PREMARITAL EXAMINATION LAWS 

The following States require a medical examination with physician's certificate or a sworn statement or declaration of freedom from syphilis, venereal, or other communicable disease before a marriage license may be issued.

Thirty states now require a blood test for syphilis of both bride and groom: Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Virginia, North 

Carolina, West Virginia, Ohio, Kentucky, Tennessee, Michigan, Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin, Iowa, Missouri, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Wyoming, Colorado, Idaho, Utah, Oregon and California.

Three states require examination by physician of groom only for freedom from venereal diseases: Alabama, Louisiana and Texas.

Three states prohibit marriages of persons with venereal diseases: 

Delaware, Oklahoma and South Carolina.

Twelve states grant marriage licenses without regard to venereal disease infection: Arkansas, Arizona, Florida, Georgia, Kansas, Maryland, Mississippi, Nevada, New Mexico, Montana, Minnesota and Washington.

Age. The age at which persons can marry differs in the States. 

In the case of minors, consent of the parents or guardians is usually required.

Consanguinity. All States forbid marriages between blood relations in the direct line of descent and some prohibit marriage of first cousins. Marriage between first cousins is prohibited in all States except * Judges empowered to waive waiting period in whole or in part. 14

the following: Alabama, California, Colorado, Connecticut, District of 

Columbia, Kentucky, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Mexico,

New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island (with exception of Jews), South

In North Carolina marriage is prohibited between any two persons nearer of kin than first cousins (double first cousins may not marry). 

In Wisconsin, marriage is prohibited between persons who are nearer of kin than second cousins, except that first cousins may marry when the female is 50 years of age or over.

Second and third cousins are not prohibited from intermarriage, with the exception of second cousins in the State of Oklahoma. 

If there is doubt about any matter it is always advisable to obtain the specific law of the State in which one intends to marry. 

Race. Intermarriage between races is forbidden in many States. 

Southern States as a rule prohibit marriages between whites and negroes and some Western States those between whites and persons of other races beside negroes. 

Persons married to near relatives or to those of other races are advised to consult the laws before removing from one State to another. 

While valid in the home State, such a marriage may be forbidden in another. In some States it is a crime for such parties to live together.

ANNULMENT 

Annulment differs from divorce in that the decree of annulment, barring legislation to the contrary, is a judicial declaration that no valid marriage existed. Divorce on the other hand, puts an end to what was a legal marriage. There is considerable difference in respect to State law concerning this matter. Some States regard the relationship as null and void from the beginning, others only from the date of the decree. 

Theoretically, since the marriage was invalid, neither party acquired the usual marital rights. This involves ownership of property and legitimacy of children. Some States provide for the restoration of property owned at the time of the marriage, others for a division of property owned at the time of the decree.

A few States grant alimony to the wife. 

Any cause rendering a marriage wholly or partly invalid from the beginning is held to be ground for annulment. 

The children of certain annulled marriages are declared legitimate. 

A suit for annulment in general resembles one for divorce. In some 

States distinction between annulment and divorce has been lost and divorce is the remedy for ending a marriage illegal from the beginning. 18

Husband and Wife 

NDER the Common Law the personality of the wife was merged into that of the husband. Blackstone wrote in 1765, "By marriage the husband and wife are one person in law the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during the marriage or at least is incorporated and consolidated into that of the husband under whose wing, protection and cover she performs everything." The husband, under the law, might give his wife moderate correction. He acquired absolute title to all his wife's property not held in trust, but he also became responsible for his wife's torts and all her debts, even those contracted before the marriage.

In an effort to avoid the assumption of antenuptial debts many so-called "smock marriages" are recorded. These resulted from the extraordinary idea that if the bride came to her husband clad only "in her shift" a creditor could follow her person no further, and the husband was, therefore, exempt from liability for her debts or engagements. 

Such liability on the part of the husband has almost entirely disappeared. Present day legislation has established the separate entity of husband and wife and the statutes uniformly permit a married woman to hold property and conduct business as though unmarried. Real property of a married woman now remains her separate property, generally free from control or interference of the husband and not liable for his debts. In most States the same is true of personal property. 

In this country, as a rule, their interest in each others' real property is an estate by curtesy to the husband, if surviving, and a right of dower to the wife, if surviving.

Certain western States have established what is known as community property. This includes three classes: separate property of the husband, separate property of the wife, and common or community property.

It is everywhere the duty of the husband to support his wife in a manner justified by his means, as long as she lives with him. This applies even if the wife has an independent income. If the husband fails to support her, the wife can compel him by bringing action. She may also charge to him such things as she needs, but the obligation of the husband is only to provide necessities, e.g., food, clothing, medical attention and the like. What actually constitutes necessities is determined largely by the husband's position. 

In a few States the wife is, under certain circumstances, required to support her husband.

The wife commonly assumes her husband's name though she is under no legal obligation to do so. 19

It is the duty of husband and wife to live together and while it is the husband's privilege to choose the family domicile, he is obliged to show a reasonable cause for removing in order to force his wife to follow him. An unreasonable refusal by the wife to do so may be construed as an act of desertion.

The husband may require his wife to perform certain services as are necessary to conduct his household. Both husband and wife have the right to each other's society. 

At Common Law the husband was responsible for the torts of his wife, even those committed before marriage. In most jurisdictions the husband's liability is now limited to those committed in his presence. 

Parents and Children

THE FATHER is, in all jurisdictions, required to support his child. 

If he neglects to do so he can be compelled by the court. 

He has a right to the child's services and to any wages earned by him while he is a minor. The father may compel obedience of the child by punishment provided it is not applied in a cruel or vindictive manner. 

Should a third party injure the child in any way, the father has the right to sue for damages for loss of the child's services. 

In most States, the father's rights during his lifetime are superior to those of the mother, in some States their rights are equal. Upon the death of the father, his authority and duties vest in the mother. 

Adoption. For legal adoption, papers must be filed with the court. 

The exact procedure is regulated by the statutes of each State. When legally adopted, such children have the right to use the adoptive parent's name and to share in his property. They stand in the place of real children in so far as the parents are concerned. 

Guardianship. The father is the natural guardian of his child. In some States both father and mother are so regarded. Two kinds of guardianship are possible for the person of the minor child and for his estate or property. If either parent is living, he or she has first claim to personal guardianship unless considered unfit. When the child is an orphan a close relative is usually appointed. In some States a child over 14 may name his own guardian provided a fit person is chosen. 

Anyone over 21 is eligible to become a guardian and has the same rights as a father.

When a guardian is appointed to administer the child's property a bond is usually required. Such a guardian must see that food, shelter, clothing and education are secured to the ward, and must give a proper accounting. He can sell property only under order of the court. 

Guardianship of a minor is terminated when the child becomes 21. 

In most States if a girl marries before she is 21, the law recognizes her as being emancipated. 20

Divorce 

THE problem of divorce is not new but goes back as far as the history of the human family. In primitive society the husband had the right to repudiate his wife but with the development of Greek and Roman . civilization special provisions were made for divorce. 

After the spread of Christianity and the increased authority of the Church, marriage came to be regarded as a sacred bond, not lightly broken.

In England, until 1857 divorce could be granted only by the ecclesiastical courts. These courts followed the canon rule and could allow only a partial, not an absolute, divorce. Such power lay with Parliament. Thus the Old English custom of wife selling evolved, to permit a quasi-legal separation, divorce being well-nigh impossible to obtain. 

Many references exist to the sale of wives by auction. The Annual 

Register of 1832 describes the sale of the wife of one Joseph Thomson. 

After three years of marriage, Thomson came to the conclusion that his married life was not advancing his happiness so he brought his wife to the auction and having seated her in a large oak chair with a halter of straw about her neck, he spoke as follows: "Gentlemen, I have to offer to your notice my wife, Mary Anne Thomson, otherwise Williams, whom I mean to sell to the highest bidder. I took her for my comfort and the good of my home but she became my tormentor, a domestic curse, a night invasion and a daily devil. May God deliver us from troublesome wives and frolicsome women!"

In spite of this frank exposé of Mary Ann Thomson's shortcomings, she was sold to Henry Means for the sum of 20 shillings (c. $5) and one Newfoundland dog.

Ecclesiastical courts were never established in America. The power to legislate on divorce was the prerogative of the separate colonies and now belongs to the States. Two forms of divorce are recognized, limited and absolute.

Limited Divorce, or divorce a mensa et thoro (from bed and board) is a judicial separation merely giving the parties in question the right to live apart. It does not dissolve the union and neither is permitted to remarry. During the period of separation the wife can claim support from her husband. Such limited divorce may be annulled at any time by the parties living together again. 

Absolute Divorce, or divorce a vinculo matrimonii (from the bonds of matrimony) is complete severance of the marriage tie and either party is usually free to marry again, although some States impose restrictions.

There is no uniform divorce law and a wide diversity exists in the 21

statutes of the several States. Grounds for divorce range all the way from none in South Carolina, and only one in New York to ten and more in other States.

This diversity of code sometimes results in a couple finding themselves holding a divorce decree in one State that is challenged in another. 

Attempts to secure a divorce by temporarily taking up residence in another State or country may lead to serious legal entanglements. 

Divorce is not granted when there is collusion, that is, consent or agreement between the parties. Collusion may be grounds for denying the decree.

The plaintiff is always required to prove the existence of the alleged grounds of divorce by satisfactory evidence, even though it is not contested. When the offense has been condoned by the parties living together as husband and wife after its occurrence, such offense will not be considered grounds for divorce. 

The term separation applies to persons living apart by mutual consent and who have neither limited nor absolute divorce. Their legal status remains unchanged. Some States provide for separation agreements relating to support, custody of children and property.

Garnishment 

ARNISHMENT is a legal process by means of which any property, credits, or effects of a debtor that are in the hands of a third party (garnishee) can be held for payment of debts. 

A writ of garnishment can usually be obtained against a person after a judgment has been rendered against the debtor, or in a pending suit to recover on a debt. To obtain such writ, a creditor must file with the clerk of the court an affidavit stating the amount due him and designating the persons that have property or other effects belonging to the debtor. The writ is served upon the garnishee. 

Garnishment is often used for attaching wages. When the debtor is employed and receives a salary a writ can be obtained requiring the employer to pay the creditor a certain per cent of the debtor's wages. 

As a rule only a limited percentage of wages or salaries may be applied in garnishee proceedings. Ten per cent is a common limit though State laws differ in this matter. 

It is generally recognized that an interest in real estate cannot be reached by process of garnishment. 

In some States a debtor may secure release of garnished property by giving a bond in the amount prescribed by statute. 

Riparian Rights 

RIPARIAN RIGHTS are the rights to land on the bank or shore of a body of water.

Boundaries on the ocean extend to high water mark unless statute provides otherwise. The same is true of navigable rivers generally. 

Such owners may use the waters but may not encroach upon navigation. 

In some States boundaries of navigable non-tidal rivers extend to the water's edge, in others to midstream.

In non-tidal unnavigable streams the boundary generally extends to the middle line of the stream.

The right to flowing water is common and equal to all those through whose property it flows and no one can obstruct or divert it. In most instances each proprietor has the right of reasonable use of the water. for domestic or other purposes as it passes through his land, provided such use is just and reasonable and does not interfere with the rights of others whose property bounds the same body of water. 

In public waters the soil beneath is also public property. The public has the right to the use of such waters also any rights incident to such ownership, e.g., fishing, gathering seaweed, etc. All tide waters, such as the sea and its arms, belong to this class. 29

There is some diversity in State laws relating to riparian rights and information on specific problems should be sought from the Secre tary of State at the State Capital. 

How to Change a Name 

IT IS NOT necessary to go through any legal procedure in order to change one's name provided there is no intent to defraud which can be proved. It is, however, advisable to obtain a court order for such a change because of the fact that this affords a means of positive identification should the question ever arise in respect to inheritance of property, transference of deeds, etc.

The process is a simple one and any lawyer will draw up the petition to present to the local court for a nominal fee. If there is no opposition the petition will be granted by the judge and the change of name made a matter of public record.

How to Secure a Patent 

PATENT gives the inventor the right to exclude all others from making, using, or selling the invention. In the case of an invention other than a design, the protection lasts for the term of 17 years. 

The grant of a patent is authorized by law for a new and useful art, machine, manufacture or composition of matter, or any new and useful improvement in one of these. An applicant is not entitled to the grant of a patent if the invention has been known or used by others in this country before the invention or discovery thereof by the applicant, or has been patented or described in any printed publication in this or any foreign country before his invention or discovery thereof, or more than two years prior to his application, or has been in public use or on sale in this country for more than two years prior to his application unless the same is proved to have been abandoned.. 

No patent is granted upon a mere idea or a suggestion. 

Patents are not granted for useless devices, for printed matter, for methods of doing business, for improvements that are only the result of mechanical skill, nor for machines that will not operate, particularly for alleged perpetual motion machines.

There have been issued over two million patents covering every possible line of inventive endeavor, and the Patent Office, therefore, says that it is advisable, before applying for a patent, to have a search made by an experienced searcher to make certain that the device is not already disclosed in some existing patent. Such disclosure will stand in the way of the grant of another patent. 

A patent is granted only upon a regularly filed application, complete in all respects, and only after a determination of utility and completeness of disclosure of the invention, and a search upon payment of fees to determine its novelty. 

The preparation of an application is a highly complex proceeding and generally can not be conducted properly except by an attorney trained in this specialized practice. The inventor, therefore, is advised to employ a competent patent attorney who is registered, as without skillful preparation of the specification and claims a patent grant is of doubtful value. A register of attorneys is kept in the Patent Office. 

Application for letter patent must be made to the Commissioner of Patents, Washington, D. C. An application for a patent for an invention other than a design must comprise (1) the first Government fee of $30.00, with in addition, a fee of $1 for each claim in excess of twenty, (2) a petition, giving name, residence, post office address of the inventor, and title of the invention, (3) a specification containing a complete description and also containing a claim or claims setting forth 35

what is regarded as new, (4) an oath, and (5) a drawing illustrating the invention if the invention is one that can be illustrated. A model will not be admitted except when called for. If the application is allowed, a final Government fee of $30.00 (with the addition of $1 for each claim in excess of twenty) must be paid in order to secure the actual grant of a patent. In the case of designs the requirements and fees differ somewhat.

An application for patent must be signed and sworn to by the inventor himself. A person who makes a financial contribution merely, cannot apply for a patent in his own name, but the invention, and the right to the patent applied for, may be assigned to him. A patent issued to more than one inventor, where only one has actually invented the device, is invalid.

Personal attendance of applicants at the Patent Office is unnecessary. The assignee of the entire interest of an invention is entitled to hold correspondence with the office to the exclusion of the inventor. 

When an attorney has been appointed, the correspondence will be held with him only. .

The Patent Office says that the inventor must judge for himself of the propriety of making an application for a patent. The records are open to inspection by him or his attorney, and further than this the Patent Office can render him no assistance until his patent application comes regularly before it. Pending patent applications are preserved in secrecy, and no information will be given .without written authority respecting the filing by any particular person of an application for a patent.

No protection is afforded by the patent law prior to the actual issue of a patent. The terms "Patent applied for" and "Patent pending" have no effect in law but give information that an application has been filed in the Patent Office.

Trade-Marks and Their Registration 

A TRADE-MARK is a distinctive name, word, mark, emblem, symbol or device, used on goods actually sold in commerce to indicate or identify the manufacturer or seller of the goods. 

No mark which is identical with that used by another on the same class of goods, or so nearly resembles it as to be likely to cause confusion in trade, can be registered. These limitations do not prevent the registration of a trade-mark because it is the name of the applicant, provided it is distinctively written or printed. 

The trade-mark laws authorize registration in the Patent Office of a trade-mark which actually has been used in interstate or foreign commerce, or in commerce with the Indian tribes, but only provided the mark has been used exclusively by the claimant who applies for the 4. 36

registration. There are various classes of trade-marks which the laws state shall not be registered.

The Patent Office, ordinarily, does not pass upon the applicant's right to use the mark, but determines only the right to registration. 

Registration does not create the right to use the mark, but gives prima facie right to prevent others using it. Ownership of a trade-mark arises from its use, so it must be used before it can be registered. 

Trade-marks in general are registered under the act of February 20, 1905, but no mark can be registered under this act which consists merely of the name of an individual, firm, corporation, or association, unless it is written or printed in a particular or distinctive manner, or in association with a portrait of the individual. Nor can mere words or devices descriptive of the goods with which they are used or the character or the quality of such goods be registered; nor may a mere geographical name or term be registered under the act of 1905. These marks, however, may be registered under the act of March 19, 1920, if they have been in actual, bona fide use as a trade-mark in interstate, foreign, or Indian commerce at least one year prior to the date of filing the application for registration. An application filed under the 1905 act may be changed to the 1920 act and vice versa. 

Registration of a trade-mark is obtained by filing an application in writing, addressed to the Commissioner of Patents, Washington, D. C., specifying the date when the mark was first used and a number of other particulars required by law, accompanied by a declaration under oath, five specimens or facsimiles of the trade-mark, a drawing of the latter prepared in a specified manner, and the government fee of $15. Personal attendance of applicants at the Patent Office is unnecessary. 

There is much that is difficult and highly technical in connection with trade-mark practice in the Patent Office. The latter advises the employment of competent attorneys in all trade-mark matters. 

To avoid conflict it is advisable, before adopting a trade-mark, to employ an attorney to make a preliminary search of the records of trademarks already registered. Many of the states have their own trademark law which it is necessary to consult. 37

The application for First Papers must give the correct name of the applicant, his place of residence, occupation, age, description, date and place of birth, full information about his marriage and children, residence before coming to this country, place and date of entrance, and some other information.

Legal Words and Phrases 

Days of Grace are days immediately following the day when a bill or note comes due on its face, allowed to the acceptor or maker, in which to make payment. The period is three days except in California, Connecticut, Idaho, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Jersey, New York, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Utah, Vermont, Wisconsin and the District of Columbia, where no grace is allowed on time paper although in some three days are given on sight drafts.

Property. Real property is land or anything attached to it; for example, a house or fence. The term covers such things as are permanent, fixed or immovable.

Personal Property includes such things as money, clothes, tools, cattle, sheep and the like, in fact, all movable chattels and belongings or everything that is neither land nor a right that is inseparably connected with land and its improvements. 

Property, Lost. Lost property belongs to the true owner if he can be discovered. If not, the finder has the right. A finder of lost property who keeps it without making an effort to locate the owner may be guilty of larceny. An owner of lost or stolen property may reclaim it from its possessor even if the latter paid for it. It is not wise to buy personal property if the seller's title is in doubt. 

SS is an abbreviation of the Latin word "scilicet" used in legal documents to stand for "to wit." 

Caveat Emptor. Latin, meaning "Let the buyer beware," that is, let him examine the article he is buying. This is a legal rule in sales of personal property to the effect that the purchaser buys at his own risk when there is no express warranty, or unless the law implies a seller's warranty or rules there has been fraud in the transaction. 

Nolle prosequi. Latin, meaning "I am unwilling to proceed." An entry on the record made when a prosecutor or plaintiff declares he will proceed no further either with the suit as a whole or as to some count. 

This may occur either in a civil or criminal case. 

Lien is a hold or claim by one person upon the property of another as security for a debt or charge.

Incorporation. Requirements for forming a corporation differ in the States and before proceeding it is wise to consult the statutes or seek the advice of a lawyer. The procedure under general laws is substantially as follows:

To secure a charter application must be made to the proper officer, usually the Secretary of State, and the required fees paid. If accepted the articles of application constitute the charter of the new corporation. 44

The application must include: "(1) the name of the proposed corporation; (2) its purpose; (3) the amount of capital stock with number and par value of the shares of which it is to consist; (4) city, village, town and county where located; (5) duration; (6) number of directors (there must be at least three); (7) name and post office address of the directors until the first meeting of the stockholders; (8) name and address of each subscriber of the certificate of incorporation and 'the number of shares he is to take; (9) statement that all subscribers to the certificate are of full age; that at least two-thirds are citizens of the 

United States and that one is resident of the State; (10) location of the principal office.

The more prominent States for incorporation purposes are Delaware, Maine, Maryland and Virginia, because their laws relating to outside corporations are liberal. Before choosing a name for a new corporation it is necessary to consult the Secretary of State or other proper official who keeps a record of names already adopted. Such names may not be used again.

Subpoena. A writ issued by a court commanding a person to appear at a designated place on a certain day and date under penalty of failing to comply.

Lease. An agreement whereby the owner of property gives to another the right of exclusive possession either for a fixed period or indefinitely. Tenancy may be month to month, for a year or longer. If indefinite or continuing at the will of either party it is assumed to be a self-renewing agreement. In such case notice to vacate is usually required on the part of both landlord and tenant. 

Where no specific agreement is made, the landlord is not under a duty to keep the leased premises in repair. It therefore falls upon the tenant when he gives up the premises to deliver them in as good condition as he received them except for reasonable wear and tear. 

Common Law. That body of rules, principles and forms developed through the centuries, embodied in decisions and opinions and enforced by the courts in England.

In the United States, the Common Law of England was adopted as the basis of jurisprudence in all the States except Louisiana. It is distinguished from statute law or the enactments of State legislatures. 

Common law, however, constitutes the basis of the State law except as modified or repealed by statute and is applied by the courts where no statute covers the situation.

In Chattel Mortgage. A lien given upon personal property usually as security for a loan or other obligation. As a rule it must be recorded in the city or town where the mortgagor resides in order to be valid. the event of non-payment, sale of the property is usually provided. 

Chattel. Every species of property, movable or immovable, which is less than freehold.

Plaintiff. One who complains. The person who starts a civil suit 45

against another (the defendant) seeking a remedy for injury to his rights. 

Libellant. The party who files a libel (written statement of cause of action in a divorce action. 

Libel. Injuring a person's reputation and.causing public hatred, contempt or ridicule in a written, printed, pictured or some other permanent record. Slander is similar defamation by word of mouth.

Tort. A private or civil wrong or injury. The law recognizes certain rights as belonging to every individual, e.g., the right to personal security, liberty, reputation, property, etc. Any violation of such right, such as assault, libel, slander, trespass, nuisance, etc., is a tort. 

Contempt of Court. Refusal to comply with or wilful disregard of an order of the court. Also any act tending to impair the dignity of a court.

Attachment. A writ issued by a court authorizing the seizure of a person or of designated property.

Domicile. The true, fixed and permanent home to which one has the intention of returning after absence therefrom.

Blue Laws. 

Laws for regulating religious and personal conduct in the Colonies of Connecticut and New Haven. Hence, any rigid Sunday laws and regulations. 

Dower. Provision which the law makes for a widow after her husband's death. At common law, dower consisted of a one-third interest, for the life of the widow, in all the land owned by the husband during the marriage. Many States now have abolished dower rights, other provisions being made for the widow. The right to a dower interest in land is generally determined by the law of the State where the land is - situated. 

Curtesy. The husband's life estate or tenure in the wife's property after her death, which by common law takes effect where he has had issue by her, born alive and capable of inheriting. 

Habeas Corpus. Latin (you) have the body. This is "the great writ of personal liberty." Habeas corpus is a court order directing one who holds another, to produce the body of the person detained before the court to determine whether there exists sufficient cause for his detention.

Duress. Any unlawful use of force which deprives à party to an agreement of the exercise of free will and thereby illegally forces him to do or forbear some act.

Void. Destitute of legal effect; invalid. Voidable means capable of being avoided; not void unless made so by a legal or inherent defect or a decree of a court. 46

Discipline was severe in Colonial days and the authority of the father was absolute. No young man might pay his addresses to a young lady without her father's permission. Even so, the younger generation rebelled occasionally as is shown by the amusing episode of Jacob Murline of Hartford and Sarah Tuttle. On May Day, 1670, Jacob “inveigled" Sarah without her father's permission. It all began by Jacob's seizing Sarah's gloves and demanding the usual forfeit, a kiss. "Thereupon," says the scandalized chronicler, "they sat down together, his arm being about her and her arm upon his shoulder or about his neck, and he kissed her and she kissed him, continuing this posture about half an hour." Hearing of this, the angry father haled Jacob into court for inveigling his daughter's affections. But the crafty Sarah calmly informed the court that nothing of the kind had happened, for she had wished Jacob to kiss her, had, in fact, inveigled him. Thoroughly baffled by this unusual feminine behavior the court fined Sarah, but, of course, her father had to pay the fine. She got off with a severe lecture from the magistrate who accused her of being a "bould virgin." 47
