Introduction 

Good citizenship has been set up as an important objective of public education. It implies, not only a knowledge of the acts performed by a good citizen, but also an emotionalization of this knowledge sufficiently intense to induce action directed toward good citizenship.

It is obvious that a textbook in civics can contribute directly only to the knowledge aspect of good citizenship. It can, however, develop a favorable attitude toward acts of good citizenship by its method of presentation and its materials. It is the hope of the authors that such a favorable attitude will result from a study of this text.

Service for common welfare through governmental organization is the keynote of the text. Legal governmental organization of a school district is of value only because of the service of education which it renders. The township, village and the city governments are presented as creatures of the people rendering services which individuals cannot well perform for themselves. The state through its boards, commissions and departments is performing the acts of a good government when it carries out the will of the people and performs services for the common welfare. Protection of life and property, education, improvement of health, safety and sanitation, good highways, care of the physically and mentally handicapped, the dependent, the orphans and the poor, contributions to the material welfare of its citizens-the efficient performance of these and many other services are evidences of good government. 

An informal method of presentation has been used throughout which should develop an interest on the part of the pupil. In order that the book should be intrinsically interesting to read, the authors have sacrificed formal organization of materials. The pupil is constantly faced with situations which require him to decide how a good citizen will act. Not the least important aspect of the text is the wealth of fine pictures showing government in action performing services for the people. vii

The school district board. These pupils learned, also, that the annual school district meeting did not perform all the school business that is necessary during the year. board of a common school district consists of three members, a director, or secretary, a moderator, or chairman, and a treasurer. This board represents the electors of the school district and carries out their wishes expressed at the annual school meeting. The control of public education in cities. If Peter and 

Mary had lived in a city, it would not have been possible for them to attend an annual meeting of their school district. 

No hall or auditorium would be large enough to hold all of the voters of a city. It is not possible for them to come together to vote taxes and decide on other matters relating to their schools. Pure democracy is not possible where large numbers of people live together. The people in cities must elect representatives to carry on school business. Instead of gathering together at an annual meeting, as has been described above, the voters of the city school district usually go to the election polls on the second Monday in July and vote by ballot for a school board to act on school matters. 

The city school board. The school board is usually elected for a term of three or four years, part of the members being elected each year. The number of members of the city school board is usually seven, although under certain conditions a greater or smaller number is permitted. CHAPTER THREE The Town or Township and Its Government 

OHN and William were on their way to school one morning. At the Four Corners, they saw their neighbor, Mr. Smith, nailing a paper to the telephone pole. They stopped and inquired if his son James had already gone to school. Mr. Smith informed them that James had ridden part of the way with him and had walked the rest. John then asked Mr. Smith what he was doing. "Putting up a notice of a special town meeting," said Mr. Smith.

The boys nodded their heads pretending to know what the town meeting was and said nothing further. As they walked along toward school, however, William said, "John, what is a town meeting and what is a special town meeting?"

John replied that it was a meeting which had something to do with the government of their town, or township as it is also called, but he wasn't sure what that "something" was. After a few minutes of silence, John said, “I don't know very much about the government of our township, and I believe you don't either. Let's try to find out something about it!" "Fine," said William. "As soon as we get to school, let's ask some of the other fellows." "No," answered John, "let's ask the teacher today in class."

William suggested that they might try to find out for themselves and be ready when the town government was discussed in their Civics class. It was finally decided that they would say nothing to anyone else during the day. 16

On the way home that night, the two boys again came to the notice at the Four Corners. They read it carefully and found that a special meeting of the electors of the township was to be held at the township hall at two P.M. on the twelfth of December for the purpose of raising money for the building of a bridge.

This bridge, located on one of the township roads, had recently been washed away by violent rainstorms and it was necessary that a new bridge be built immediately. 

After having read the notice, the boys started toward home. In their discussion several questions arose, such as: 

What is a town? What is a town meeting? How does a special town meeting differ from the regular one? Who may attend the town meeting? What business is transacted? Several other questions came to their minds as they walked along the road, and when they arrived at John's home, they parted with the understanding that they would make a complete study of the town.

He said, "When most of the land in a neighborhood has been included in farms, there are certain things that the people can do better by joining together than by individual action. The town clerk. William's father said that the town clerk was elected for one year and that he had many important duties to perform. The most important are given below:

The town treasurer. As William was already familiar with the words, treasure, treasurer, and treasury, he had some notion of the duties of the town treasurer. His father explained to him that it was the treasurer's duty to keep an itemized account of all money received and paid out by the town, and that he could not pay out money except upon an order signed by the town clerk. He collects the taxes of the town. In order to keep an accurate record and to know just how much tax each individual has to pay, the town treasurer has a tax roll which has been furnished him by the town clerk. 

It is also the duty of the town treasurer to receive from the county treasurer the money which has been apportioned to the town for schools. This money, together with that

which the town treasurer has collected in the town for the support of schools, he turns over to the treasurers of the school districts.

William's father then told him that, because the treasurer had to handle large sums of money during the year and to be responsible for it, he was required to give a bond, so that the town would not lose any money in case he was guilty of fraud or misconduct in office.

This list is called the assessment roll.

Form of government. One of the most important things which the members of the constitutional convention had to determine was the form of governmental agencies which they should establish in the state. In this they were limited only by the national constitution which required that each state should have a republican form of government. Other state governments and the national government provided for a division of the powers among legislative, executive and judicial departments.

The departments of government. When the constitution was completed, it contained provision for these three departments. Since the legislature makes the laws, it was necessary to have it as representative as possible. For this reason it was provided that our state should have a Senate and a House of Representatives. In the state at that time there were no classes of people to be represented, but it was thought best to make the membership of the Senate smaller than that of the House of Representatives, and, therefore, the senatorial districts were made larger. It was also believed that in establishing two houses one would act as a check on the other.

The constitution provided a number of ways by which

The limitations of the powers of the legislature. The legislature is limited in its power. Its acts must not be contrary to the state or the federal constitution. 

The legislature is prohibited from passing any special or private laws for the following purposes or provisions: 

1. For the allowance of any private claim or account. 

2. For granting divorces by special act. 

3. For establishment of a state paper. 

4. For authorizing a lottery or permitting the sale of lottery tickets. 

5. For granting corporate powers or privileges except to cities. 

6. No local or special act shall be passed by the legis-

lature where a general act could be used. Local acts do not become effective until approved by a majority of the voters in the district affected. 

The legislature must pass general laws for all of the above cases-laws which will apply to all people and in all cases. 

The legislature is given the power to create subdivisions in the state for governmental purposes and to delegate to them such authority as it sees fit, so long as it is not contrary to the constitution. That is, it may create towns, villages and counties and may issue charters to cities.

The source of executive powers. The executive department is defined and certain powers of the governor are enumerated in the constitution. The legislature may confer additional powers upon the governor. He is limited to the powers conferred by the constitution and by the legislature. 

The establishment of courts. The constitution provides a system of courts for the state and gives to the legislature power to create such other inferior courts as may be deemed necessary. The judicial power of the state, both as to matters of law and equity, is vested in these courts. The power of the supreme court to declare a law unconstitutional is not given in the constitution, but it has been exercised by that court so long that it is regarded as one of the powers. 

The administrative work of the state is entrusted to the secretary of state, the treasurer, the attorney general, and a great portion to the county officers. Most of the duties of these officers are defined by the legislature.

The methods of raising and expending money. The services rendered by our government are so numerous, and so many people must devote their entire time to do the work which we wish to have done, that the question of the amount of money to be spent is a very important one. That the government may not become extravagant and that there may be a means of compelling each one to pay his share, we find a number of fundamental laws in our constitution that deal with taxation and debts. Taxes:

3. Incomes, privileges, and occupations may be taxed.

Debts:

It was voted upon and passed by a vote of 21 ayes and 1 no. The Senate, then, sent the bill back to the House of Representatives which sent it to the Committee on Enrolled Bills. This committee reported that it was correctly enrolled and the bill, signed both by the speaker of the House and the president of the Senate, was sent to the governor. The governor studied the bill for a week and then approved it. It was published in a newspaper and became a law just ninety days after the adjournment of the legislature.

The rather detailed account of the legislative history of one bill was given to the Lincoln school pupils by their own committee in order that they might know with what care bills are studied and checked before they can become laws.

Petitions of the people. Attention has been called to the opportunity given to the people of the state to express their opinions about proposed laws at hearings before the various committees. The governor of the state. At the head of the great organization set up to perform these services for the common welfare is the governor of the state. The executive power of the state is given to him. This centers the responsibility for the general welfare in one person. The duties and powers of the governor. When one reads over the long list of services performed by the state, it is quite clear that the governor must be given wide powers and many duties. It will help the pupil to understand the work of the governor if these are grouped into classes. 

Law making duties. While the governor himself does not make laws, he does have certain powers and duties which have to do with the making of laws. He calls special meetings of the legislature. The only business that can be carried on at a special session is that mentioned by the governor when he calls the special session. At each regular session of the legislature the governor reads his message in which he recommends the passage of certain laws. The governor, also, has the duty of approving or vetoing bills which have been passed by both branches of the legislature. 

The judicial powers and duties of the governor. Persons who are charged with crimes against the state laws are given a trial in a state court. If such a person is found to be guilty, i.e., if it is proved that he has committed the crime, he may be sentenced to prison or to pay a fine, or both. The governor is given power to reprieve, that is, to postpone or delay the punishment; to commute the sentence, that is, to shorten the period of punishment; or to pardon the prisoner, that is, to forgive him his crime and free him. Since the use of any one of these powers changes the decision of the judge in the court, it is clear that the governor has judicial or judging power. 

That the governor may not abuse the power of pardon, the law requires that at each regular session of the legislature, he must make a report of each reprieve, commutation

or pardon which he has granted and state the name of the convict, the crime for which he was convicted, the sentence and its date and the date of the commutation, reprieve or pardon, together with his reasons for granting it. (Courtesy of the University of Michigan)

State administrative board. The general supervisory control of all functions and activities of the state administration, boards, commissions, bureaus, officers, and state institutions is vested in the state administrative board. The governor is ex-officio chairman of the board. It is made up of the secretary of state, state treasurer, auditor-general, attorney-general, highway commissioner and the state superintendent of public instruction. Being the chief administrative board of the state it is vested with the power to adopt such rules and regulations as seem necessary to govern its procedure. The board fixes the salaries of the

State department of health. The state advisory council of health was created in 1919. It assumed the duties of the former state board of health which was created in 1873. 

The advisory council is composed of five persons who are appointed by the governor with the consent of the Senate. 

They are required to meet four times each year, and also whenever they are called by the order of the state health commissioner. The state health commissioner is appointed by the governor for a term of four years.

Bureau of engineering. The bureau of engineering examines and approves the water supply of cities, state institutions, railroad stations, airports, summer resorts, and roadside wells. It requires that sewage from cities and state institutions is properly disposed of. It examines plans for swimming pools, inspects the water used, and supervises their general operation. It tests the water on numerous streams and if it finds it unhealthful, posts signs forbidding bathing in order to prevent the spread of disease. 

Bureau of communicable diseases and rural hygiene. It is the duty of this bureau to locate the source of outbreaks of communicable diseases, advise local health officers on how to control the disease, and to stop its spread. It also investigates rare and unusual diseases in an attempt to gain scientific data for future use. Campaigns for immunization are also carried on.

Bureau of laboratories. The bureau of laboratories aids the state by assisting physicians, health officers, and nurses in diagnosing the true nature of the various cases with which these health officers come into contact. The bureau also analyzes sewage and water samples. It aids the law

Bureau of records and statistics. This bureau collects statistics on births, deaths, marriages and divorces. From these records statistics are compiled which tell us whether we are winning or losing our fight against disease, which diseases are taking the greatest toll in human lives and whether or not certain diseases are being eliminated.

Kinds of courts. Because there are many people to be served and numerous laws to be interpreted, and in order that this service may be had without too much expense and inconvenience, there are a number of courts. Since there are so many different kinds of services to be rendered, it is necessary to have the courts arranged in a regular system with the work of each clearly defined. This system is composed of the supreme court, circuit courts, probate courts and justice courts, besides many special courts for cities and particular cases.

The supreme court. At the head of this system is the supreme court which has general supervision over all other courts in the state. It is not only the highest and most important court in the state, but it is the most powerful department of our state government. All acts of the school, township, village, city, county, legislative and executive departments of the state, the decisions of all inferior courts and the relations of individuals are subject to review by this court. Its decision on all acts of these bodies is final. It is limited only by the constitution of the state and the federal constitution. Because of this the name, supreme court, is well chosen.

Composition. This court is composed of eight members called justices. This number was set by the legislature in 1903. 

Seven of these are called associate justices and one, the chief justice. One of the two justices whose term soonest expires is made chief justice. The one having served the longer is chief justice the first year. In view of the importance of its decisions, the makers of our constitution

thought it best to entrust the power of this court to eight justices instead of one. And since its decisions are important to the citizens of all sections of the state, the constitution provides that the voters of the entire state shall take part in the election of each justice. Such election is held in the spring, two justices going out of office every two years.

Qualifications. To be a member of this court, a justice must be twenty-one years of age and a qualified voter in the state. No member of this court is permitted to hold any other office of public trust during the time for which he is elected.

Powers of the Supreme Court. The constitution provides that the supreme court shall have both original and appellate jurisdiction. Original jurisdiction means that the first hearing of any case may occur in the court. The framers of the constitution intended that the original jurisdiction should be very limited and specified that it should be confined to the few cases mentioned in the constitution. 

These cases are brought before the court by giving it the power to issue the following writs: (a writ is an order issued by the court) habeas corpus, mandamus, injunction, quo warranto, certiorari and other original and remedial writs.

In such cases, the court has the authority to hear and determine the case.

The writ of habeas corpus directs an officer who is holding a citizen to bring him before the court for the purpose of determining whether he is legally held. This writ has long been one of the main factors in the protection of individual rights and is particularly valuable as a protection against tyrannical power by officials. Its importance is shown by the fact that it may not be suspended except in cases of rebellion or invasion when public safety is endangered. 

An injunction is a writ from a court ordering an officer, a private citizen, or a corporation to do or refrain from doing a certain act. An injunction is usually temporary and is used to stop some action until the case may be tested in court. If the state should lay out a highway through a farm and the owner wishes to have such action tested, he may ask the court to issue an injunction against the highway

commission. If the court grants the injunction, no work may be done until the case has been decided in regular court.

Quo warranto is a writ requiring an officer, either public or private, to appear before the court and explain by what authority he holds his office.

Mandamus is a writ issued by the court directing a public official, a public or municipal corporation, or inferior court to do what is directed in the writ. If the person or corporation does not do as directed in the writ, he must appear before the court issuing it and give reasons why he does not perform the act.

The writ of certiorari directs that an inferior court shall send all records of a case to the supreme court for review. 

The writ may be issued while the case is in progress in the lower court or after it has been decided. This writ is not used to any great extent.

The authority to issue these writs places in the hands of the supreme court a powerful instrument. By their use the supreme court may exercise general supervisory control over the inferior courts; it may prevent an injustice being done to a citizen; it may hasten the rendering of justice; it may hasten decision in cases of immediate importance. 

This power is very rarely used, probably in not more than twenty-five or thirty cases a year.

Criminal cases. One of the most important services which the state performs is to protect the lives and property of its citizens or of anyone who may be in the state. Another is to preserve the peace of the community. For these purposes, the state and its subdivisions have many laws. When an individual violates one of these laws, he is guilty of an offense against the state. Such an offense is a crime, and cases of this nature when brought before the court are called criminal cases. It is the duty of every citizen to help in the protection of all and in preserving the peace, but we have officers whose duties are to see that the laws are obeyed. 

When a person violates a law, some one makes a complaint before a judicial officer. This is usually done by a policeman, constable or sheriff, but any citizen may make the complaint. The judge then issues a warrant for the arrest of the person charged with the violation of law. The officer, after reading the warrant to the person, brings him before the judge. The prisoner is probably not ready for a trial and the judge releases him on bail. This may be given by depositing a sum of money or by the guarantee on the part of friends to pay a sum of money if the prisoner does not appear at the time set for trial.

If the punishment for the offense is light, that is three months in jail, or $100 fine or both, the case may be tried before a justice of peace. If the punishment is greater than this, it goes in most cases before the circuit court. 

On the appointed day, Mr. Palmer, the prisoner, appears with his attorney for trial. He is first given the opportunity to plead guilty. If he does not, the trial proceeds.

The first step is the selection of a jury, unless the prisoner is willing to leave his guilt or innocence to the decision of the judge. In the circuit court the county clerk has prepared

a list of all tax payers in the county who are eligible for jury service. From this list the clerk of the court draws twentyfour names. From these twenty-four, twelve are drawn for the jury which shall hear the case and make a decision. If the attorneys object to some of these, others are drawn until a jury is selected which is satisfactory to both sides. 

The state, represented by the prosecuting attorney, then examines its witnesses who testify to the acts of the defendant. When the prosecuting attorney has finished, the attorney for the defendant cross-examines these witnesses.

When all witnesses for the state have been examined and cross-examined, the witnesses for the defendant are examined by his attorney and then cross-examined by the prosecuting attorney. When all testimony has been given, each attorney makes an argument for his side of the case. 

The judge then instructs the jury informing them of the law governing the case. The jury then retires to come to a decision. In criminal cases the decision must be unanimous. 

If their decision is "guilty," the judge pronounces sentence and orders the sheriff to execute it. If the jury finds the prisoner "not guilty," he is set free.

Kinds of law. The decisions of the courts of our state are based on one of three kinds of law, common law, statutory law, constitutional law.

The constitution of our state recognizes the authority of the common law. The Schedule, Section I, says: "The Common Law and the Statute laws now in force, not repugnant to this constitution, shall remain in force until they expire by their own limitations or are altered or repealed.' 

The common law is a long line of decisions in cases where there is no statutory law. These precedents have the effect of law.

Statutory law is any law enacted by the legislature of this state, and constitutional law is the law as established in the constitution and to which all statutory law must conform. 

Impeachment. Another judicial service is that of impeachment. This, however, is rendered by the legislature and not by the regular courts of the state. Any public official may be impeached for corrupt conduct in office or for crimes and misdemeanors. Any member of the House of 

Representatives may prefer charges against an officer and introduce a resolution of impeachment. If this resolution is passed by the House, the case is then tried in the Senate. 

It requires a two-thirds vote of the Senate to convict. If the public officer is convicted, he is removed from office. 

In case the charges are against the governor, the lieutenant governor is not permitted to preside at the trial.

WHY WE have elections. In your school there are times when you do not all wish to do the same thing. On the playground, some of you may want to play one game while others prefer another. To get the most enjoyment out of the game, all should take part. Perhaps you have a club in your school. You choose one of your members to be president of your club. In both of these cases, you take a vote, that is, you give each one an opportunity to express his preference for one game or the other, or his choice for a president.

So far in the study of our state, we have learned how different units of government serve the people by doing things which it would be difficult for the people to do for themselves; how through these services the government is working for the best interests of all its citizens; and how this work is divided among a great number of persons called officers. These officers are our representatives and the services are those which we wish to have done. We select these men and women to perform this work because we do not have the time or the ability to do it ourselves. For the best interests of all, we feel that it is better to have some person or persons whom we can hold responsible for performing these services. In selecting these officers, we wish to choose those who are honest and capable so that we may feel that the work will be well done, and so that we may devote our time to the performance of other things for our own welfare. 

In most cases the work which these officers do has been outlined by our representatives in the legislative depart- 179

There are some services, however, which are so important and require the expenditure of so much money, that we feel they should not be performed until the voters have had an opportunity to express themselves directly. In amending the constitution, for example, or when it is a question of issuing bonds for a public building in a city, we wish to have the approval of the voters. On these and many similar questions, we prefer to give all of the voters an opportunity to decide the matter and not to entrust the decision to representatives of the voters. 

When you decide which game to play or when you make a choice for president of your club, you hold elections. The method by which the voters choose officers in the state and decide questions is also an election. 

In arranging for elections the state renders a service by making rules or laws to regulate them, but in choosing officers and in deciding questions we ourselves are performing a service when each individual voter does his part. It is our duty to take part in these elections. Voting is an obligation which each one owes to his government.

Who may vote. The most important question in a democratic form of government is to decide who may vote. The voter determines either directly or indirectly the kinds of schools we shall have, the amount of taxes we shall pay, how our lives and property shall be protected, what laws shall be made, who shall enforce those laws, who shall administer justice and many other important kinds of services. He is able to decide these questions by making a wise choice of those who are elected to the various offices of the state. It is probably not an exaggeration to say that the success or failure of our government depends upon the voter.

We all know that there is a large number of voters, but there are some who may not vote. We shall now learn what qualifications a person must have to permit him to exercise this privilege and why some are disqualified. 

The Constitution of the United States prohibits any state from denying the suffrage, or right to vote, to any person on account of sex, race, color or previous condition of servitude. That is, our state cannot deny this right to women, or to any person who is of a different race unless he fails to meet the other requirements. The other qualifications are fixed by the constitution of our state and the laws passed by our legislature.

Things guaranteed to the voter. The right to vote is so important that the voter must be given every protection so that nothing may interfere with his freedom in exercising this right. He may not be prevented from going to the polls except in case of imprisonment. When he enters the polling place, he is protected in his right to secrecy in marking his ballot. He is free to vote for any candidate on any ticket except that he may not vote for two candidates for the same office. If he desires to vote for any person whose name does not appear on the ticket, he may write that person's name on the ballot. If he is unable to read and write, he may have assistance in marking his ballot. He may not be questioned in regard to his vote.

Absent voting. It is intended in our system of government, that every qualified voter shall have an opportunity to cast his vote. Sometimes a voter may be prevented from coming to the polling place because of sickness or other disability; or he may have an important business engagement on the day of election which requires that he be away; or there may be other good reasons why a voter cannot go to the polls on election day. In such cases there is a method by which he may cast his vote. To do this he must make an application in writing for a ballot to the county, city, village, or town clerk of the governmental unit in which the election is held. When he receives the ballot, he marks those for whom he wishes to vote and sends the ballot back

to the clerk, who then gives the ballot to the inspectors in the district where the voter lives.

Registration. Unfortunately, there are some voters who are not honest and, if nothing prevented, they would vote more than once. In towns, villages and small cities most of the voters are known to members of the board of canvassers and such practice is easily detected. In larger cities, however, many of the voters are not known. To prevent persons from voting more than once Michigan requires that each elector shall be registered. In registering, a voter must sign an affidavit that he is a qualified voter in that district and must file it with the clerk. His name is then placed on the poll list. If a voter is not registered, he may swear in his vote at any election. This he does at the polling place by taking an oath that he was unable to register because of absence or illness and that he is a qualified voter in the district. This oath must be subscribed to by two qualified voters who know him and live in the same voting district.

In the election of the U. S. Senator, state officers, and the electors of the president and vice-president, all the voters

of the state take part, but representatives in Congress, state senators and members of the House of Representatives are each elected by the voters of separate districts. 

The election of town, village and city officers occurs on the first Monday in April of each year.

The state superintendent of public instruction, the county commissioner of schools and all judicial officers, except the justices of the peace, are elected on the first Monday in April.

The election of the school district officers takes place at the time of the annual school meeting on the second Monday in July. However, school board members in cities which are not common school districts are elected at a spring election.

Constitutional amendments are submitted to the voters at either the general election in November or the spring election in April.

In towns, villages and cities questions of the issuance of bonds may be submitted at a time decided upon by the town board, village board or city council. Special elections for the purpose of filling a vacancy or voting upon questions such as bond issues may be called at any time provided the proper notice is given. 

Notice of any election, regular, special, or primary, must be given. The length of the time such notice is given is prescribed by law. 

Nomination of candidates. Voters do not always agree as to the kind of service which the state should render, or as to the manner in which that service should be performed. 

Those who believe alike form into one group while others who have different opinions form another. Such groups are political parties. There may be many such groups but usu-

That they may carry out their ideas, they must elect representatives of their party to the offices in the state. But how is it decided what party member shall oppose the candidate of the other parties?

In order that all members of the party shall have an opportunity to help select the candidates for various offices, an election is held. This is called the primary election and is conducted in the same manner as the regular election. Each party has a ballot on which are the names of all the candidates who are seeking the nomination for each particular office.

On entering the voting booth an elector is given a ballot according to his party affiliation. If he is a Democrat he asks for a Democratic ballot. On this ballot appear all the Democratic candidates running for each office. The elector then declares his preference for each office. After the voting is finished the votes are counted. The person having the largest number of votes for each office on each of the party tickets then becomes the candidate to represent his party in the general election. 

Any member of the party may have his name placed on his party ticket for nomination to an office by presenting a petition signed by a certain percentage of the electors. If the office is a city office, the petition is presented to the city clerk; if it is a county office, to the county clerk; if it is a state office, U. S. Senator or Representative in Congress, the petition is presented to the secretary of the state. The clerk of the proper political unit then has all names placed on the primary ballot. 

The primary elections are subject to the same laws and

regulations which govern the regular elections and the voter has the same guarantees.

The caucus. In towns and villages the members of each political party meet about two weeks before the election and select candidates for the various offices. The voting in such meetings is very informal. Such a meeting is called a caucus. The caucus is not governed by the primary election laws, but the names of nominees must be given to the town or village clerk who has them placed on the ballot for the regular election.

The campaign. After the nominations have been made at the September primary, each party usually begins a campaign for the purpose of securing support for its candidates. 

Candidates for state office usually travel to all sections of the state on a speaking tour. They try to speak in at least one and sometimes two or three places in each county of the state. In each large city several meetings are held. Besides the candidates, other leading members of the party speak in the interest of the ticket.

The candidates for county offices do not usually go on a speaking tour but do make it a point to visit the voters of the county and make a personal campaign. They are found at all public gatherings, such as picnics, fairs, etc.

Corrupt practice. It is the intention of the state that each voter shall be free to express his choice of the candidates.

To prevent any candidate for office from using fraudulent methods in securing votes for himself, each one is limited in the amount of money he may spend for campaign purposes. 

He is not only limited in the amount which he may spend, but it must be used only for legitimate campaign purposes such as traveling expenses, printing, etc. Each candidate for state office must file with the secretary of state an item-

How votes are cast. The place where the election is held is called the polling place or the polls. It is located in a con- 

A Voting Machine venient center so that all voters may reach it easily. Elections must be held in all towns, villages, wards and precincts of the state. The selection of the polling places in each town is made by the town meeting or by the supervisors; in the villages, by the trustees; and in the wards, by the city council. In most townships there is only one polling place, but the supervisors may designate two or more to meet the convenience of voters. In wards, villages or townships additional voting places must be provided when necessary. The place of voting shall have a number of booths. 

The election is conducted by three inspectors, two ballot clerks and two clerks of election. Upon entering a polling place the voter's name and address are checked on the poll

list by the election clerks. If the voter's name does not appear, he must take an oath that he is a qualified voter in the voting district. A person's vote may be challenged by an inspector or by any qualified voter. In such a case the voter must make an affidavit that he is a voter. He then receives a ballot from the ballot clerk, on the back of which must appear the names or initials of the clerk. The voter then retires to one of the booths where he may mark his ballot secretly. The voter must then hand his ballot to one inspector. The ballot must be folded in such a manner that the signature of the ballot clerk appears on the outside. 

Counting the votes. The polls are closed at a fixed hour; that is, no more voters are allowed to enter the voting place. 

However, all those who are there at the time of closing must be allowed to cast their votes.

The number of ballots is then counted to see if it is the same as the number of names shown by the record of the election clerks. Then the number of votes for each separate candidate is counted. When all votes have been counted, the result is publicly announced. A report must be made to the town, village or city clerk.

A copy of the tally sheet and one of the poll list must be sent to the county clerk. The county clerk must make a report of the vote for all state officers to the secretary of state. When reports of all counties have been received by him, the results are tabulated by the state board of canvassers consisting of the secretary of state, the state treasurer, and the superintendent of public instruction. 

Initiative and referendum. Most of the laws by which we are governed are passed by the legislative body of the town, village, city, county or state. They are proposed by the members of these bodies and not by individual voters.

MY GOVERNMENT 

However, laws may be proposed by the citizens of the state by what is called the initiative. Such laws may be proposed by petitions signed by qualified electors of the state, equal in number to eight percent of the total vote cast for all candidates for governor at the preceding election. The secretary of state must present such petitions to the legislature as soon as it meets. The proposed law may be either passed or rejected by the legislature without change within forty days after it is received. If it is rejected by the legislature, the secretary of state shall submit the proposed law to a vote of the people at the next general election. 

The citizens may also review laws which have been passed by the legislature by what is called the referendum. Within ninety days after final adjournment of the legislature, a petition signed by qualified electors equal to five percent of the total number of votes cast for governor at the last election may require an act which has been passed by the legislature to be submitted to the people. Such an act shall not go into effect unless approved by a majority of the qualified electors voting thereon.

In these two ways the people are able to check up on their legislature and pass laws over their heads or disapprove of laws already passed if they feel that their representatives are not truly representing them.

Recall. When an officer is elected to serve the state, he is not in a position to act as he pleases. The powers and duties of his office are defined by law and, if he exceeds these or is guilty of corrupt practices in the exercise of his duties, he may be removed in two ways. First, he may be impeached.

All cases of impeachment are in the hands of the state legislature. Charges are preferred in the House of Representatives which may vote to impeach him. In such a case he

must be tried by the state Senate. To convict him requires a two-thirds vote of the Senate.

In the other case he may be recalled by the voters. The qualified electors of any state, county, congressional, judicial or legislative district may present a petition for the recall of any elective officer. The petition must be signed by a specific number of persons in the district from which such officer is to be recalled. A special election must then be called and the officer against whom the petition has been filed must have his name on the ballot with others who may be nominated.

What are taxes? Taxes are moneys paid by people to their government in order that certain services may be performed for them. The Indians in America had no taxes to pay because they had no government to do things for them. 

As people become more civilized, they ask their government to do more and more for them. They pay for these services by means of taxation. Farmers often join together to buy certain things which they want. Farmers' cooperative stores, farmers' elevators, farmers' creameries are illustrations of organizations of people to buy or to sell as a group. People in cities do the same thing. A group of people may buy a carload of potatoes and divide them up according to the amount paid in by each individual. In the same way people buy services by means of their government and pay for them in taxes.

General tax law. The General Tax Law of the state of 

Michigan says "For the purpose of taxation real property shall include all lands within the state, and all buildings and fixtures thereon, and appurtenances thereto, except such as are expressly exempted by law." There are various kinds of real property therefore, that are exempt from taxation, such as the following: all public land and buildings belonging to the United States, the state, the county, township, village, city or school district; land used for parks, agricultural societies or burial grounds; buildings and lands used for libraries, charitable homes, churches; the property of persons whom the Board of Review recognizes as being too poor to help contribute to the general expense of the community and state.

The confederation of states. In your study of the history of the United States you learned how the thirteen colonies in America declared their independence and separated from Great Britain. It became necessary immediately to unite in some general form of government. A convention made up of one representative from each colony prepared a plan of government which was called the Articles of Confederation. This plan left all of the real power in the hands of the various states and many troubles arose. The central government was not powerful enough to protect the states from invasion. Each state settled its own problems and many quarrels arose between the states. All power of taxation rested with the states and competition for foreign trade caused many difficulties. Each state tried to gain advantages over the other states. Sometimes one state would tax goods coming from a neighboring one. For example, New York taxed shipments of chickens, vegetables and butter from New Jersey and firewood coming from Connecticut. New York had built a lighthouse on the New 

Jersey shore, and New Jersey placed a tax of $1,800 a year upon this lighthouse.

Each state issued its own paper money and great confusion resulted from this. The states quarreled among themselves about the ownership of the land between the Ohio and the Mississippi rivers and about the control of navigation on rivers between states, such as the Potomac river between Maryland and Virginia. How the states formed the nation. Recognizing that this country could not continue to exist as an independent nation without a strong central government, a convention was held to draw up a better plan of union. Each state sent delegates to this convention just as each school club did when the Jackson School Student Council was formed. 

After a great deal of discussion a constitution was drawn up providing for a federal government. The proposed constitution was submitted to the thirteen states for ratification in September 1787 and was finally accepted by nine of the states in June 1788. As a result of this a new federal government for our country was established.

All of the powers given to Congress had before this time belonged to the states. Their own affairs they could still regulate. All matters which concerned the states in common were regulated by the federal government.

AN'S wants. Food, clothing, shelter! From prehistoric times to today these have been the most important wants of man. Bread has been called the staff of life. Food and clothing are often exchanged for labor. "Give us this day our daily bread" is a prayer. The first cry of a child is for food. Without food for very long, we starve. Without variety in food, such as fruit, cereals, vegetables and meat, we grow ill.

It is not to be wondered at, then, that the need for food has spurred on the development of man from savagery and has brought about cooperation among people. For, in order to secure a variety of foods man has learned to exchange what he has produced for foods produced by other people. 

The intense cold of winter is sufficient proof that man needs clothing as well as food. The hides of animals killed for food furnished man with his first covering. Social demands have added to the clothing needs so that all the countries in the world are called upon to furnish us with silks, linens, woollen cloth and other fabrics. Thousands of people are required to weave these cloths, transport them to us and make them into suitable clothing.

Ways of making a living. In making a living, man has passed through several different stages. At first the method was very simple. He went out to the streams and caught fish with his hands or with a sharpened stick, or picked berries from the bushes. He ate leaves and roots of trees and killed animals with a club. This was the hunting and fishing stage in his progress toward civilization. In time he found that some of the animals could be tamed and gathered into herds and flocks and these could be driven from place to place for pasture. This was the pastoral stage of man's progress. As the population increased and as he found that he could raise a larger supply of food by cultivating the soil, he began to clear pieces of land for farming and to settle down in one place. This was the agricultural stage of his development. During this time man found that he could make several instruments by hand which would help him to secure his food, clothing and shelter. Crude plows and hoes and other implements were constructed. 

Population continued to increase. Some people made more implements than they themselves were able to use and began to trade them to other people. This was the handicraft stage. As the mind of man continued to develop, he discovered that machines could do much of the work which he formerly did by hand so laboriously. These machines could do the work faster than human hands could, and more products than before for more people were made possible. 

This was the industrial stage. 

In the hunting and the fishing stages, the area which furnished people the means to satisfy their wants was very great in comparison with the number of people who were

seeking food, clothing and shelter. These people could fish, hunt and gather fruit without coming into contact with each other. There were no cities; no means of transportation except perhaps a rude canoe or boat; no highways, no schools. Their homes were in caves, rude tents or under the shelter of some tree. There was no property to protect and each individual defended his own life and that of his family. There was little if any government and no law except a rude custom. There was no particular need for government and law.

Man comes into contact with his fellow man. In the pastoral stage men came more and more into contact with each other. Each individual or family owned herds of cattle and sheep and, therefore, a method of marking one's property so that it could be identified and protected was needed. 

It was necessary, also, to have an agreement regarding the use of the pasture land for grazing. Perhaps the clan or tribe lived in a small space and the many tents or rude huts were close together and they needed some rules to determine their location. Each individual of the clan or tribe was interested in the whole; therefore, they must work together to protect their rude homes and their herds.

When men finally began to cultivate a piece of land to raise crops of grain, there was need for a method of determining what land each should have. In passing to and from the fields, there may have been several people moving in the same direction and they needed highways. These were perhaps mere trails, but there must be certain rules governing their location and use. No doubt, by this time the clan or tribe had broken up and there was further need for the protection of the individual home. Each person had some implements and he must be protected in his ownership of

In the handicraft period the population had increased and man had begun to live in towns. With the coming of the town, people were brought into closer contact than ever before and there was great need for setting up rules or laws to govern their relations. Not all were engaged in doing the same thing and regulations dealing with the exchange of goods were necessary.

These towns with homes and other buildings crowded closely together brought need for protection from fire, thieves and from the spread of disease. As the towns grew, some persons devoted their time to the performance of these services. Such persons must be paid; all the inhabitants must contribute; taxes must be levied. 

In the towns, also, men began to specialize in their work. 

There were some who made clothing, some who made rude furniture, some who did carpenter work and some one was a blacksmith. There were many ways of making a living.

During all of this time, the population kept increasing; the towns and cities grew larger and more numerous; trade was developing; men's minds were discovering new ways of doing things; new inventions were making it possible to use the power of machinery for work which had formerly been done by hand. The industrial stage of making a living was upon us. As man used machinery, it became necessary for each one to do fewer things and devote all of his time to one special kind of work. The number of things which man. needed was greatly increased and these in turn created new wants.

A study of these five stages shows us that man's wants have increased; that in his efforts to satisfy these wants he has passed from a very simple life, where he alone secured everything which he needed, to a very complex life in which he can produce only one thing. He secures the means of satisfying other wants by exchanging goods.

243 We must also understand that, as the population increased, he was brought into closer contact with his fellow man and could no longer live as he pleased but must recognize the rights of others. He learned also that there were many services which could be performed better if he would join with others. As he began to live closer to other people, he found that they needed many things in common; a good highway, for example.

In all of these stages, man has been anxious to make a living for himself. As he has progressed, his standard of living has increased. But he has learned that, in order to have a high standard of living, he must cooperate with his neighbor; he must respect other peoples' rights; all must work together in peace and harmony. 

Man has learned that there are many things for the common welfare that can be accomplished only by cooperation: 

1. He must be able to go about his work without fear of destruction of his life or the lives of members of his family. 

2. He must be certain that the property he has secured will be safe from theft or destruction. 

3. He must respect the personal and property rights of other people. 

4. He must join with other people to secure certain services which he can no longer perform as an individual.

To secure this protection and certain common services people have joined together to form a government. This government is formed to perform services for the common welfare.

As soon as man gained possession of property and began to meet other men, government became necessary in order to furnish him protection of property and life and to regulate his relations with his fellows.

This same need for government is felt by the young child. His property consists of toys. His fellow man is his brother or sister, playfellow or parent. Some one must protect his property and govern his relations with other people. He is in need of many services from the day he opens his eyes in this world to the time when he becomes independent of his home protection.

Have you ever thought how you are constantly in the company of other human beings. At home you are with brother or sister, mother and father. At school you are with other pupils and with your teachers. When you leave school, you go out into a world of people.

There was a time when government was thought to be placed over people by some outside force. Those who were in authority thought their power to control people came from God and that the rulers could do as they pleased. If what they did was for the good of all, the people accepted it and were happy; if the government was cruel and tyrannical, the people endured it, hoping for a better government when rulers changed.

In some countries there were men who had control of the government because they were stronger and more powerful than any one else, and people were forced to accept their authority. Other men have believed that they should rule large groups of people because of their birth or intelligence. 

In our country we do not think of government as something placed over us by others without our will but as a way of living together established by common consent. The constitution of Michigan acknowledges that government derives its powers from the consent of the people who are governed. The first duty, then, which you owe to your government is to think of it as yours and to realize that it can be no better than you make it. You are a part of it. 

It gets its authority from you and you may, either directly or by your representatives, have that government perform those services which you wish it to perform. 

The citizen should respect the authority of government. 

If, then, the government is given its authority by your consent, you owe it to your government to respect its authority. That authority exists in the laws that are passed and the officers who are chosen to enforce them. The success of the service rendered depends upon the respect you have for those laws and officers who carry out your wishes.
