FOREWORD 

This bulletin has been prepared to present comparative information on the fire losses and the fire protection classification of Oregon cities and also to explain the factors involved in determining the fire protection classification of cities and the effect of that classification on basic fire insurance rates. 

Increasing fire losses and the rapid growth of many Oregon cities have combined to create a demand for the expansion and improvement of fire prevention and suppression measures and facilities, and it is hoped that the discussion of grading procedures will be helpful to city officials and others in evaluating the fire defenses of their city and in developing programs which will reduce fire losses as well as fire insurance rates.

Most of the data presented in this bulletin was compiled from reports of the State Fire Marshal's office or provided by 

W. F. White, Oregon Insurance Rating Bureau engineer. The generous assistance of P. C. Shepherd, a private insurance engineer in Eugene, in advising on technical problems also merits special acknowledgment. Virgil H. Wintrode, Bureau staff member, assumed principal responsibility for compiling the data presented in this bulletin. 

Respectfully submitted,

Herman Kehrli Director

Part I AN ANALYSIS OF FIRE LOSSES AND FIRE INSURANCE CLAIMS IN 1945 AND 1946 

Insured fire losses in the incorporated cities of Oregon and in suburban areas receiving recognized fire protection mounted rapidly in the war period and reached an all-time high in 1944. Total losses decreased in 

1945 and again in 1946, but were still much higher than those for any previous year except 1944. This same trend existed also for the state as a whole.

The largest number of claims against fire insurance in Oregon cities and other protected areas and also in the state as a whole were made in 1946, however. There were 6,874 claims in protected areas that year and 10,338 in the state. There were 61 per cent more claims in cities and other protected areas in 1946 than in 1936, but only 28.9 per cent more than the average number of claims during the previous ten years. 

Actual losses in 1946 were 56.2 per cent higher and the average loss per claim 21.3 per cent higher than the ten-year average, while in 1944 actual losses were 142 per cent higher than the average loss per claim and 117.1 per cent higher than the ten-year average. The trend of claims and losses as reported by the state fire marshal is summarized for cities and other protected areas in Table I and for the state as a whole in Table II.

Part II GRADING OF FIRE PROTECTION FACILITIES AND CLASSIFICATION OF CITIES 

Fire insurance rates on buildings and their contents are based primarily on the following factors: 

1. The Class of Property to be Insured. 

2. The Fire Protection Class of the Municipality. 

City officials will be primarily interested in the methods used in determining the fire protection class of cities and in the influence of this classification on fire insurance rates in their city. Other factors that determine the insurance rates on a particular building or property are discussed in this bulletin only to the extent necessary to indicate their importance and to provide background for evaluating the effect of a city's fire protection class on insurance rates in that city. 

1. The Class of Property to be Insured 

The most important factor in determining the basic fire insurance rate to be applied to a building or its contents is the class of property involved. 

The risk in the case of any building is affected by its structural features, the height of the building, private protection provided by extinguishers and by automatic sprinklers, type of heating unit, the kind of occupancy, the exposure to possible fires in adjoining buildings; and the insurance rate for each building roflects the classification of the building with respect to these features. 

The following classification is used by the Oregon Insurance Rating

Bureau in appraising the structural features of buildings: 

Class A: A building with walls, floors and roof of masonry (including concrete), with all loads supported by an independent steel or reinforced concrete frame or by masonry walls, and with all interior structural elements of fireresistive or incombustible materials. 

Class B: A building with all exterior walls constructed of brick, stone, concrete, or solid cement blocks, with an approved roof. (An approved roof is a roof having an exposed surface entirely of metal, slate, tile, tar and gravel, or composition roofing materials, except tar paper, and with exterior masonry walls extending above the roof). 

A building with exterior walls not extending above the roof but in all other respects conforming to the Class B requirements, may be treated as such if it is detached 50 feet or more from all Class B, C, or D buildings. 

Class C:A building generally conforming to the requirements of 

Class B, but which varies therefrom in any one or more of the -23-

following particulars: 

(1) In having a front or rear wall of non-masonry construction. (2) In having one or more exterior walls of hollow tile, hollow coment block or plastor block. (Hollow pumice block is generally considered satisfactory, but has not been officially recognized by the 

Rating Bureau.) 

(3) In having exterior walls not rising above the roof unless the building is detached 50 feet or more from all Class B, C, or D buildings. (4) In having a mansard or unapproved roof. Unless the entire roof (other than the roofs awnings, bay windows, cornicos, open porches, and outside stairs) is of approved material, the roof shall be classed as unapproved. (An unapproved roof is one having a surfacing of wooden shingles, shakes, boards, tar paper, or materials other than those approved.) (5) In having a frame structure on the roof, except skylights, cornices or balustrades.

Class D: A building with oxterior walls of wood, metal on wood or steel frame, stucco or masonry vencer over wood, or any other type of nonmasonry construction, or a building having part masonry walls but not up to tho requirements for Class B or C buildings. (Walls of masonry not over 4 inches in thickness shall bo considered in the same manner as vencer whothor constructed over wood or not.) 

The fire protection facilities within the building and in the area in which it is located, and the condition of surrounding buildings in the arca are among the factors which offect the insurance rates for buildings in any structural classification. These factors do not stand alone but are interrelated, making it impossible to dotermine insurance ratos on any particular property without having the evaluation of all of the factors. For example, a fire in a one-story building is more easily extinguishod than a fire in a building having two or more stories, but the rate variation for buildings of different height is less in citios having lowor fire protection classification because such cities havo greator ability to cope with fires in multi-story buildings.

In determining the grade of a city's water facilities the National Board Grading Schedule considers many factors; including supply, sources, reliability of pumps if they are used, and reliability of plant equipment, fuel and accessories for transmission of power, and condition of hydrants. 

The fire department is rated according to size and condition of apparatus (engines, hose, etc), number of men, engine capacity, response to alarms, and conditions affecting fire department operations. Some of the factors determining the efficiency of the fire alarm systems are apparatus, current supply, condition and materials of circuits, distribution and accessibility of boxes, speed of alarms, and telephone servico. 

The police department is judged by its cooperation with the fire department, number of cars, cooperation with building department, and its communications system. Building laws to be efficient must contain proper restrictions and must be adequately enforced. The National Board recommends the adoption of the National Electric Code by ordinance as a standard for electric wiring. Oil and gas lighting and heating and explosives and inflammables should also be carefully inspected and regulated by ordinance.

The schedule of structural conditions is applied largely to mercantile and manufacturing districts. The area of the district, street widths, accessibility of blocks, height of buildings, walls, floor openings, etc., determine the rating given in this division. 

This discussion, of course, only outlines briefly the factors considered by the National Board schedule which materially affect firo insurance rates. It does indicate where increased efficiency can make possible a considerable saving to the city, as well as to property owners. -26-

Credits: It is also possible for a city to gain credits which will reduce the total number of deficiency points and help to lowor the classification. These credits may be obtained for superior construction and protection and for a high pressure water supply. Points gained for superior construction and protection vary with the percentage of fireproof construction. To be considered fireproof for this purpose it is necessary that the structure comply with the building code recommended by the National Board of Fire Underwriters, except for protection to vertical and horizontal openings.

Additional Deficiency Points: The schedule places the greatest weight on the water supply and fire department. If, however, there is a wide divergence in the grading of water supply and the fire department the insurance class is effected by what is called a "divergence chargo". 

It is obvious that a good water supply in connection with a poor fire department, or vice versa, is of loss value than if both are good. recognition of this fact, a modification of the better one of the two features is made in accordance with schedule D, provided the divergence exceeds the equivalent of two classes.

Effect of the City's Fire Protection Class on Insurance Pates 

As indicated above, the fire insurance rate applicable to each individual property is determined in part by factors beyond the scope of the city administration. To a lesser extent, however, the insurance rates on property are influenced by the fire protection class of the city; which -27-

roflects the adequacy of the city's water system, its fire-fighting equipment and manpower, its building code, etc. 

It would be impossible to indicate exactly the effect of the fire protection facilitios of a city on insurance rates for all types of property within a city without publishing the complete rate book which is prepared by the Oregon Insurance Rating Bureau. The Rating Bureau is, however, prepared to advise with city officials with reference to improvements which will reduce the fire protection class of the city and the offoct this would have generally on insurance rates in the city, and will also provide information to property owners with reference to the best way to purchase insurance for any particular property.
