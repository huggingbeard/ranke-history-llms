The Commonwealth of Massachusetts SUMMARY OF REPORT ON SCHOOL CONSTRUCTION METHODS AND COSTS AND UNIFORM ARCHITEC- TURAL PLANS. 

In conformance with the directive of the General Court (Senate, No. 581 of 1957), this extended report presents a study of the methods and costs of school building construction in the Commonwealth, and discusses the use of uniform architectural plans in school construction.

The following summary of the report is primarily limited to the highlights of school building problems and omits much of the historical, statistical and legal material discussed in the main body of the document.

STATE-AIDED SCHOOL CONSTRUCTION AND FUTURE NEEDS. 

Since the passage of the School Building Assistance Act in 1948, which authorized state grants-in-aid, local governments have undertaken over 769 public school building projects, of which 436 have been free-standing (separate) schoolhouses, and 333 have been additions to existing school buildings. The cumulative cost of all 

769 projects has been $447.2 million, of which $159.3 million, or over a third, will be paid by the State by the time all local school bonds have been retired. The annual expenditure by the State for school grants-in-aid now exceeds $10 million. 

The report reviews the findings as to existing and needed school plant throughout Massachusetts which were brought together in the lengthy Public School Survey of 1955, and brings the material up to date wherever possible. That survey estimated a total expenditure of about $215 million would be necessary to provide the classrooms and other facilities to house the probable increase in school population over the next five years. 

The report indicates the limited progress thus far made in replacing the 572 schoolhouses which were condemned as unsafe or obsolete by the 1955 survey; it is estimated that another $140 million will be required to finish that task.

[Mar. 

Hence, no early relief may be anticipated from the financial burden of new school construction. This prospect adds emphasis to both public and private concern as to ways and means of reducing school building costs, and prompts this extended report dealing, among other matters, with educational programming, school building planning and design, uniform architectural plans, modular construction techniques, and building code requirements. 

PURPOSE AND FEATURES OF SCHOOL Buildings. 

Schools fulfill various functions, the first and most important of which, of course, is that of providing an educational program. The most economical schools are therefore those for which the planners have studied all the practical details connected with the desired educational program, and have then designed a structure to accommodate those details at the least cost. 

Educational needs vary from one community to another and will require varying school curriculums and school buildings as well. Thus a "white collar" suburban town may establish a strong college preparatory curriculum because many of its boys and girls are going to college. On the other hand, a somewhat different type of high school building will be required in communities whose children will mostly be seeking industrial or other employment, many of them locally, immediately after leaving the public schools. 

The latter kind of community may sensibly provide more vocational shops, for example, than the former.

School building costs will also be influenced importantly by the extent to which community facilities are incorporated. Thus, school buildings may provide extra large auditoriums to accommodate large town meetings; or health facilities may be designed to meet health needs of more than school children alone. Some 

States, such as Washington, exclude such "excess" provision in determining the amount of school construction expenditures to be the basis of state grants-in-aid. 

PLANNING THE SCHOOL BUILDING. 

Careful planning, which involves the co-operation of various individuals and agencies of government, is of primary importance if the waste of public funds is to be avoided. Such co-operation is

1958.] SENATE - 

No. 675. especially difficult to achieve in Massachusetts and the other New England States because of the extensive decentralization of responsibility entering into the planning and construction of schools in this region, as indicated below. 

School Committee. The school committee, with the help of its superintendent, establishes the local educational program. That program is incorporated in the detailed educational specifications developed for the proposed new school, which then become the basis of the architectural plan of the building. 

To avoid later costly difficulties, the school authorities should obtain advance clearance and guidance from various water, zoning, sewerage, traffic and other local agencies. Of real importance on this score is the local planning board which can make a particular contribution of two major types toward economy in school building construction, namely, the projection of population growths for different areas of the city or town, and selection of a building site which will best serve population trends, topography of the land and municipal utilities. Failure to utilize properly the services of the planning board has been a source of waste in school construction in Massachusetts.

Further effort to transform a set of educational specifications into a completed structure rests with the local school building agency, assisted by an architect and sometimes by an educational consultant as well.

School Architect. The employment of a well-qualified architect by the local school building agency is an important factor in school construction. That professional expert must design a structure which will meet the required educational specifications at an economical cost, both in terms of immediate construction and long-run building operation and maintenance.

With some variations, it is common practice for Massachusetts communities to hire the architect under two separate contracts: first, to prepare preliminary plans and drawings for initial decision; and secondly, to prepare final plans and then to give general supervision to the building project once construction has been authorized. 

The fees of architects who must be registered by recent statutory requirement tend to follow the schedule recommended by the Massachusetts State Association of Architects, which provides for fees based on declining percentages as construction costs increase;

[Mar. 

thus, the fee for a $0.1 million building is figured at 8½ per cent, while the fee for a $2.5 million school is figured at 6½ per cent. 

Assistance by State Authorities. - The Division of Elementary and Secondary Education, in the State Department of Education, furnishes technical advice to local authorities, upon request, relative to curriculum, school organization, and school building planning problems. This advice helps in the development of economical educational specifications for schoolhouses, and in planning longrange school needs.

In addition a temporary School Building Assistance Commission was established in 1948 in the State Department of Education, but not under its control, to administer the state grants-in-aid for school construction. The Commission is charged by statute with the four following duties: (1) promotion of regional school districts and con solidated schools; (2) promotion of safe and adequate public schools; (3) furnishing of technical assistance for local surveys of school building needs; and (4) administration of a state grant-in-aid program under the School Building Assistance Act of 1948. 

To these ends, the Commission has been given extensive powers over the planning and construction of those local school buildings which are erected with state aid. Thus, it must determine whether proposed projects are in the best interests of the county, regional school district or municipality, and whether the specific school plan will be "adequate" and its accommodations "sufficient." These quoted statutory requirements are not defined, and their interpretation is therefore left to the Commission whose decision is conclusive. The Commission has established no uniform minimum standards of such "adequacy" and "sufficiency" on a comprehensive basis. Nor has the Commission met the statutory requirement that it shall "from time to time, by regulation, establish maximum unit costs of construction." The report reprints in full a letter from the Commission contending that "under the present law it is unnecessary to do so."

Greatly affecting the detailed architectural and engineering plans of a new school building, is the maze of regulations relative to plumbing, fire prevention, fire and structural safety, health, zoning and other matters which have either been promulgated by state and local authorities, or have taken statutory form. 

Architects, educators and others complain about the obsolete nature of many of these building code provisions, and the enormous

1958.] 

jurisdictional confusion which exists in their formulation and enforcement. They invite disputes, delays and avoidable late orders to change features of design, all of which boost the cost of the school building.

To help correct the latter situation, the General Court established a temporary Board of Schoolhouse Structural Standards in 1955, with authority to modify most of the building code requirements applying to public school buildings. This new Board has done the useful work described in the report. 

BUILDING CONTRACTS AND CONSTRUCTION SUPERVISION. 

State Bidding Law. The report discusses the procedures which must be followed in letting school construction contracts and subcontracts and the widely held misconception that the State Building Law requires an awarding authority to let the contract to the lowest bidder, even though the awarding authority believes the bidder to be incompetent. Consideration is also given to the additional school costs from legally defective bids.

Architect and Clerk-of-the-Works. During the construction of a new school building, intermittent supervision is given to the project by the architect. Continuous on-site supervision is provided by a clerk-of-the-works who is retained on a fee basis either by the architect on behalf of the municipality, or directly by the local school building agency; his primary function is to protect the interests of the governmental unit which employs him, and to compel compliance with building plans and specifications. 

It is important that the clerk-of-the-works be a qualified person, professionally trained, so that contractors will not be tempted to "cut corners" with subsequent serious losses to the community. 

At the present time, the state law is limited to the prescription of minimum professional standards for clerks-of-the-works on projects for state-owned buildings, and does not apply in connection with local building construction.

While heavy school building expenditures have attracted much public attention, less note has been taken of the fact that school construction costs have been rising much more slowly than the costs of erecting other types of buildings. A recent survey of nation-wide building cost trends indicates that over the past two decades, school building costs have gone up only 150 per cent, as contrasted with increases of 210 per cent for all buildings, of 220 per cent for skilled labor, and of 330 per cent for unskilled labor. 

Presumably this contrast indicates that considerable progress has been made in improving the design of school buildings so as to make improved use of the materials entering into their construction. 

UNIFORM ARCHITECTURAL PLANS. 

At the present time the six following States are utilizing uniform architectural plans for public schools: Arkansas, California, Maine, Mississippi, Oklahoma and West Virginia.

Twenty States, as follows, made use of such plans in the past, but have abandoned them: Alabama, Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New York, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Vermont, Virginia and Wisconsin.

Basically, uniform plans call for preparation of architectural plans, drawings and specifications for school buildings of the most frequent sizes, to help obtain minimum unit costs. The plans are specially designed by architects retained for the purpose, and are to be made available on request to local school districts. In some cases a very large school district may develop its own uniform plans. 

The declining use of uniform architectural plans for schools may be related to the decreasing number of small districts as the national trend toward larger consolidated school buildings continues. It is unknown how big a part the opposition of educators and architectural organizations has played in this decline. 

Little reliable data have been found regarding the costs of schools constructed on the basis of uniform architectural plans. A study in 

California in 1955 showed that the cost of such schools ranged from $6.85 to $10.46 per square foot, as against $10.75 per square foot for a custom-built schoolhouse of comparable size. 

THE CASE FOR AND AGAINST UNIFORM PLANS. 

Arguments for Uniform Architectural Plans. Proponents of uniform architectural plans for school buildings urge that such plans should be prepared and made available for optional use on new school construction by the cities, towns and regional school districts of Massachusetts.

These proponents stress that 144, or about half, of the 312 elementary school buildings constructed during the past dozen years have had from eight to fourteen classrooms, and should have been built from a stock twelve-room elementary school building plan, with any necessary modifications being made to meet special conditions.

The advocates of uniform plans emphasize that once uniform plans are prepared, much time and money can be saved on the design of future school projects. The work of the architect is then limited to the comparatively small activity of designing changes needed due to special site conditions and other factors. 

Standard plans would enable the project to move forward more promptly. They would gradually assemble corrections dictated by experience and could quickly incorporate new improvements in

1958.] 

building construction.

Uniform plans would make cost-estimating easier. 

Uniform plans are also urged on the grounds that they would facilitate mass production economies in the manufacture of building parts. Producers of such parts could then do more to standardize their products. Greater use of assembly-line techniques would also be feasible to speed up delivery.

Proponents further suggest that uniform plan schools would soon become good yardsticks against which to measure the costs of "custom-built" schools of comparable size which they feel certain will be higher. Finally, proponents contend that there would be no damage to educational programming, since the arrangement of rooms are not believed to materially affect the educational program of the building.

Arguments Against Uniform Plans. - Opposition to uniform plans for school buildings centers primarily on the educational aspects of such plans. The principal fear of educators and others is that stock plan schools would adversely affect educational programming in the various school systems by pressures toward local school programs based on a state-determined average. Such regimentation is deemed likely because it is next to impossible to devise sufficiently broad standard plans to accommodate the varying educational programs of different categories of communities: e.g., industrial, "bed-room suburban," mill, agricultural and fishing. Educational improvements requiring building design adjustments would be discouraged.

These adverse results would occur, say opponents, even though the use of the uniform plan was optional under the laws. Local officials would be very hesitant to stray too far from uniform plans, thus arousing criticism of economy groups. As a result of this system, important educational decisions would be made at the state capital, far from the reach of local authority.

Opponents further contend that uniform plans, because of inflexibility, would at least discourage and possibly prevent the incorporation of community use features which local communities might desire in their school buildings. Thus, communities could not incorporate a larger auditorium, for example, into the new school in order to avoid the wasteful and unnecessary construction of additional municipal buildings which may be under consideration.

[Mar. 

The opponents also assert that no substantial economies would be realized from the use of uniform plans. The services of an architect would be needed in any event to adapt the plan to the site, to make other changes necessitated by local conditions, and to provide general supervision of construction. The advantages of the contour planning of the custom-built school would be lost. And the uniform plan would tend to hamper or discourage the use of new building materials and techniques which have been so important in holding down the increase of school building costs. Competition, invaluable to any type of construction, would also be stifled. 

Finally, opponents complain that standard architectural plans would be aesthetically poor because they might well produce an oppressively monotonous uniformity in school buildings about the countryside.

ALTERNATIVE MEASURES. 

Modular Design and Construction. In Europe and in various parts of the United States efforts are being made to achieve in individually "custom-built" schools at least some of the economies sought by advocates of the uniform plan school, while retaining individuality of educational program and of building design. 

This objective is being sought through individual designs of buildings which employ "modules" or uniform small sections thereof. These sections of framing members, wall panels, and the like can then be mass-produced in uniform dimensions and style, to be utilized to suit the tastes of the architect and local educational authorities.

Regional Schools. One of the great areas of waste in school construction arises from the construction of schools which are too small to provide adequate yet economical educational services. 

One medium-sized school plant to house a given number of pupils is almost certain to be less costly to build than a number of small schools with the same total capacity. Moreover, the larger school is less costly to staff, operate and maintain. 

Massachusetts is attempting to deal with the small school question through the extra state aid which has been made available when regional school districts are formed among the small towns on a voluntary basis. Over the past decade approximately 32 such districts, with a total membership of 88 towns, have been formed on this basis.

Statutory Controls over Design of State-aided Schools. - Under the present Massachusetts School Building Assistance Act, state grants are forthcoming on a total project cost percentage basis to help pay for expenditures to build acceptable new local public schools. No statutory ceiling is imposed on any grant in terms of the adequacy or extravagance of the building. The statute only directs the School Building Assistance Commission to establish maximum unit costs of school construction, but this the Commission has failed to do.

As indicated in some detail in the report, various States have undertaken to prevent wasteful or extravagant uses of state school building grants by prescribing maximum unit costs or other types of financial control beyond which state aid will not be extended. 

Thus, the Education Code of California applies both space and cost controls on a square foot basis for different types of school buildings. 

Variations from these standards are permitted only with the approval of the State Department of Education. 

Connecticut only makes grants on a per pupil basis up to a maximum of half the project cost, -$500 per elementary pupil to be housed; $700 per secondary pupil; and an additional $100 per pupil, if the building is a regional school. Costs above these per pupil grants must be borne entirely by the local government, which can then be as generous as it pleases. (See Appendix E for full text of Connecticut law.)

Delaware prohibits any payment of state aid to a project if the cost per classroom exceeds $30,000, or if the school is to contain a swimming pool, or if the architect's fee is more than 5 per cent. 

Finally, Washington law directs that the state grant per square foot may not exceed the prevailing per square foot cost of school construction in the State; and no state contribution may be made toward the cost of auditoriums, garages and certain other facilities.

LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND. 

Origin of Study. 

Senate, No. 581 of 1957, reprinted on the inside of the front cover of this report, directed the Legislative Research Council to study local school building construction costs and methods, and the advisability of uniform architectural plans for such buildings where state aid is extended. Council was directed to provide "the staff and other services that are needed." 

3. House, No. 2256 of 1957, filed by Rep. James R. Lawton of

Scope of Study. 

In construing the directive in Senate, No. 581 to study the "costs and methods" of school building construction, the Research Bureau has applied a broad interpretation. "Methods" of school construction has been expanded to include the entire school planning process, from preliminary surveys of the need for and location of a school, to the design of the project, to inspection work during the construction of the building.

Because of staff limitations, the Research Bureau could not analyze engineering techniques and details of school construction or comparisons of construction materials. The Research Bureau study of "uniform architectural plans" did include related modular construction and other practices involving the repetition of units in school buildings.

HISTORICAL ASPECTS OF SCHOOL CONSTRUCTION. 

Concept of Free Public Schools. 

Since 1642 Massachusetts municipalities have been required by law to provide their children with a free public elementary school education. The statutory requirement is as follows: 

Every town shall maintain, for at least one hundred and sixty days in each school year unless specifically exempted as to any one year by the department of education . . a sufficient number of schools for the instruction of all children who may legally attend a public school therein. Such schools shall be taught by teachers of competent ability and good morals, and shall give instruction and training in orthography, reading, writing, the English language and grammar, geography, arithmetic, drawing, the history and constitution of the United States, the duties of citizenship, physiology and hygiene, good behavior, indoor and outdoor games and athletic exercise. In connection with physiology and hygiene, instruction as to the effects of alcoholic drinks and stimulants and narcotics on the human system, and as to tuberculosis and its prevention, shall be given to all pupils in all schools under public control, except schools maintained solely for instruction in particular branches. Such other subjects as the school committee considers expedient may be taught in the public schools. (G. L. c. 71, § 1.)

In 1789 the General Court enlarged the law relative to municipal educational obligation by requiring towns having 200 (now 500) or more families or householders to maintain a secondary school for persons desiring a post-elementary school training, as follows:¹

One or more courses of study, at least four years in length, shall be maintained in such high school, and it shall be kept open for the benefit of all the inhabitants 

1 Acts of 1789, c. 19, § 1.

1958.] 

 of the town for at least one hundred and eighty days, exclusive of vacations, in each school year, unless specifically exempted as to any one school year by the department (e.g., of education) because of epidemic, extraordinary weather conditions deemed by school officials to be dangerous to the safety and health of pupils, or other emergency. (G. L. c. 71, § 4.) In 1891, towns of less than 500 families not maintaining a high school as above were required to send their high school students to a public high school in another city or town, and to pay the tuition and certain other expenses. Later amendments to this statute permitted sending high school students to approved public high schools in adjoining states. (G. L. c. 71, § 6.)

In 1870, municipalities were required, under certain circumstances, to maintain evening schools for persons over 14 years of age.2 An 

1886 statute, as amended, stipulated that cities of 50,000 or more inhabitants must provide an evening high school if 50 or more persons 16 years of age or older petition the city school committee therefor.3 

Massachusetts was the first State to enact a compulsory school attendance law (1852). It requires every child between the ages of seven and sixteen, with certain exceptions, to attend either the public schools or private schools which conform to statutory and local requirements. (G. L. c. 76, § 1.)

Responsibility for Erecting Public Schools. 

From the early colonial days to the present time the responsibility for providing suitable facilities to house public elementary and secondary school activities has rested with the municipal governments. The history-making statute of 1827 (chapter 143) provided for "the instruction of youth" on a school district basis within towns. Later educational legislation made the city or town the basic school organization unit:

Every town shall provide and maintain a sufficient number of schoolhouses, properly furnished and conveniently situated for the accommodation of all children therein entitled to attend the public schools ... The school committee, unless the town otherwise directs, shall have general charge and superintendence of the schoolhouses, shall keep them in good order, and shall, at the expense of the town, procure a suitable place for the schools, if there is no schoolhouse, and provide fuel and all other things necessary for the comfort of the pupils. (G. L. c. 71, § 68.)

The formation of union or regional school districts of two or more municipalities to obtain jointly operated public schools has been authorized since 1848.¹ No use was made of this school device for nearly a century, due firstly to financial and educational policy differences between the wealthier and the poorer towns; secondly, to the almost total reliance of local school financing on the property tax; and thirdly, to fear that a union school district would diminish town control over education.2 Thus a survey in 1948 and 1949 found that there were 176 one-teacher schools in Massachusetts where the teacher taught pupils in eight different grades, and that there were 60 small towns maintaining high schools (representing about one-fourth of all high schools in the State) with a student membership under 100.3

To encourage economical school organization and construction in the smaller communities, the General Court passed the School Building Assistance Act of 1948, providing state aid to municipalities and regional school districts for schoolhouse construction, with payments to regional school districts being authorized on a more liberal basis than payments to cities and towns. That act, which will be discussed at greater length elsewhere in this report, further established a temporary five-member School Building Assistance Commission for the purpose of promoting the planning and construction of school buildings and the establishment of consolidated and regional schools, in order to insure safe and adequate plant facilities for the public schools, and to assist towns in meeting the cost thereof. . . .4 

This statute was substantially liberalized in 1949 on recommendation of a special commission. Renovation and Enlargement of Present Structures. 

The question as to whether an existing old school building should be renovated and enlarged, or replaced by a new structure, is frequently one of the first issues which must be resolved locally. On the one hand, the school staff will probably desire a completely new structure, much as a man will prefer a new to a second-hand suit. 

On the other hand, the local finance committee, taxpayers' association, and other groups will prefer the latter if they are satisfied that renovation will cost less than new construction and yet meet existing minimum educational standards of the community.

Rehabilitation and remodeling involves major structural changes, such as new roofs, new stairways, utilizing idle space for classrooms, converting two rooms into one large one, and elimination of facilities which do not comply with safety requirements. One important element which deserves particular attention in any consideration of the school building situation in the Commonwealth is that of the unique character of the existing school plant in Massachusetts. The school buildings in use throughout the State today range in age from very old to new. Since 1950 many wellplanned, functional schools have been constructed in all sections of the State. In contrast to these fine educational structures used for school purposes, the average age of school buildings in some Massachusetts communities, including the larger cities, is well beyond the fifty-year mark, as indicated above.

Most of the old school buildings in the Commonwealth are decidedly uneconomical to operate. They often have twelve to fourteen foot ceilings, huge unusable corridor and basement spaces, open stairwells, ill-fitting windows and doors, and other features

They are often difficult to clean and to keep in sanitary condition. Because they are frequently multistory structures, usually with wooden staircases, they tend to be unsafe in case of emergency.

Since there is general agreement among authorities that the optimal lifetime of a school building is approximately fifty years, it would seem likely that expenditures for major rehabilitation of school plants up to that age level are usually warranted, provided that there are then obtained more functional, attractive and safe school buildings.

Similar questions arise as to when it is advisable to construct an addition to an existing school building. Beyond need of classroom space, other factors which are determinate in this matter are the age of the existing school, its basic design, and the site area which is available. School construction experts doubt the advisability of adding to any school building which is thirty-five or forty years old.

Among older school buildings there are often found structures with a basic design which militates against any economical extension. They may be ornate structures devoid of any functional planning. They may lack such essential features as adequate window area. In order to add to some of the older school buildings it is sometimes necessary to move staircases, or to relocate plumbing, or to renovate at a scale and cost incompatible with the total current valuation of the building.

The question of school additions also involves the question of optimal enrollment size in given school buildings. If the community works out a policy, for example, that the enrollment in single elementary schools should not exceed approximately 600 pupils, and in single secondary schools should range between 750 and 1,500 pupils, these factors will prevent gradually increasing the building enrollment to too great a size. Because a school building is perhaps the largest and most visible of the more expensive public works undertaken by a community, it is bound to undergo close scrutiny by persons in the town who are heavily burdened with property taxes, particularly if they are persons whose families have grown up, or if their children do not attend the public schools.

Chapter III. Purpose and Features of School Buildings. 

BUILDING AS EDUCATIONAL TOOL. 

General. Any discussion of school buildings and the costs of school buildings is incomplete unless consideration is given to the purposes of the school plant. There is need for an awareness of school building elements and features which are essential to a modern educational program. Indeed, much current controversy is fed by lack of consideration and understanding of the whys and whats of today's school.

A school building may fulfill many functions, but first is that of housing an educational program. Thus, the most efficient and worthwhile schools are those in which the planners have started with a careful consideration of all the details of the desired educational program and then designed a school plant meticulously calculated to achieve that end.

Differing Community Educational Needs. - Educational needs differ from community to community. Children in the metropolitan core communities need a different kind of emphasis in their education than do children from the agricultural parts of the State, or from the mill towns. The greatest problem is that of providing a useful, sound education for the larger proportion of boys and girls for whom public education represents the terminal point in their formal education. For example, the people of Gloucester could justify, in addition to a high quality college preparatory course, public school instruction in marine engines, boat building, or other aspects intimately tied to life in a coastal community which is so dependent upon the fishing industry. 

Where widely diversified industry characterizes a community, it would obviously be impossible to try to meet all of its special educational needs. In such a community, in addition to providing a strong college preparatory curriculum, emphasis might also be placed on broadly specialized training for those boys and girls who will be seeking employment immediately after leaving public school. When comparison is made between urban communities and the smaller towns, such as Lenox, Williamsburg, Lunenburg and Westborough, the variation in educational needs comes more clearly into focus.

These desirable variations in the educational programs also greatly determine the school building components which must be provided to teach effectively the areas of knowledge involved. While in one community, shop facilities offering instruction in such areas as woodworking, electronics, metals, home mechanics and the like, would be best, the neighboring community might well decide that a series of separate, specialized shops for the teaching of automobile mechanics, masonry or graphic arts, would provide the best answer to its special situation.

Similarly, another community may wish to emphasize health and physical education. The space required for such a program would be quite different from that of the more traditional health and physical education programs. There are standards which have been evolved to indicate the usual instructional and operational spaces to be provided in a school of a given size or on a per pupil basis. NON-EDUCATIONAL FACILITIES IN SCHOOLS. 

Beyond the strictly educational features, school buildings often contain facilities which are mostly for community use. Sometimes an auditorium large enough to house the entire town meeting is built into the school. Otherwise a much smaller auditorium, or an all-purpose room in the case of an elementary school, might have served adequately. Occasionally, a town library will be incorporated into a school building. Frequently a large health or dental clinic may be installed for community service, far beyond school requirements. Indoor and outdoor recreational spaces and parking areas may also be increased in number or size to provide for community needs far beyond those of the school.

In certain instances these facilities serve a dual purpose and so may be defended on an economy basis. Indeed, more widespread community planning of this character may be advisable in order to prevent duplication of facilities. In such cases, however, the extra cost of facilities provided for non-educational purposes ought to be attributed properly to the recreational or other programs involved. 

Some States, such as Washington, successfully restrict the areas of a school building upon which state aid for school construction may be sought. They exclude from eligibility for state aid the elements built for community use, along with areas in excess of allowable minimal ratios. Should a city or town wish to build a library facility larger than that warranted by the school's enrollment, it may do so, but state aid is limited to the costs of constructing a

1958.] 

normal square footage of library space per pupil in actual enrollment. Or should the community build an oversized auditorium to accommodate its town meeting, or build an additional gymnasium facility, state aid is extended only to the limit specified in a formula based upon the number of pupils and the allowable space therefor. 

Possible misuse of such restrictions, however, cause educators to urge that any limit placed upon the degree of subsidized facilities within a school building should conform with such objective standards as those developed for school building spaces by the National 

Council on Schoolhouse Construction. 

Chapter IV. Planning the School Building. 

LOCAL RESPONSIBILITIES. 

Decentralization of Local Planning and Construction. 

In the 312 towns of Massachusetts the local government organization and procedures for school construction are more decentralized than in the cities and regional school districts of the State, or in the communities outside of New England. From one city or town to another, however, this organization may vary, depending on local political considerations, traditions and special statutory provisions applicable to the particular municipality. Only in regional school districts is there much uniformity, mostly because they are governed by a single specific general statute.

Practice in Massachusetts Towns. In the towns of this State the school committee is rarely called upon to take full responsibility for the planning and construction of a public school, although it may exercise this responsibility with regard to small additions, and generally is required to handle all aspects of major repairs to school buildings. Ordinarily, when a new school or a school building addition is to be erected, the school committee finds itself just one of several interested parties in the project so far as the law is concerned; and the actual planning of the project as to design and utilities, and the supervision of construction, is vested in an ad hoc building committee.

In towns, a school project may be planned by one temporary committee and built by another; but the usual practice is to appoint a temporary study committee to obtain plans, drawings and specifications, and then reconstitute the study committee as a temporary

[Mar. 

building construction agency to carry out the proposed project if the plan is approved at a town meeting. A few towns have permanent public building committees or permanent school building committees which plan and carry out the construction. Options on needed school sites may be obtained by the school committee, or by a long-range "future school needs" committee of the town meeting, or by the building study committee, as the town meeting determines. The local building committee may include one or more persons appointed by the school committee, but it is not subordinate to the latter body.

This decentralization of school planning and construction in towns is attributable in part to their traditionally decentralized character and decided informality of New England town government with its maze of independent elected officers and boards, its town meeting institution, and its penchant for "representative" ad hoc committees set up on temporary bases to do given jobs. The decentralization of school planning and construction is paralleled in other areas of town administration, such as public works, public safety, parks and finance. It is reinforced by the fact that the function of constructing schoolhouses is vested by law in the town and not in the school committee, as indicated below:

Every town shall provide and maintain a sufficient number of schoolhouses, properly furnished and conveniently situated for the accommodation of all children therein entitled to attend the public schools. (G. L., c. 71, § 68.) Likewise, the town meeting, and not the school committee, has the final determination of the "situation" of its schoolhouses. (G. L., c. 71, § 70.) On the other hand, the completed school building is placed by law under the general superintendence of the school committee "unless the town otherwise directs." The town meeting thus bas authority to assign to some other town agency than the school committee the responsibility for keeping the schoolhouses in good order, providing fuel and furnishing "all other things necessary for the comfort of the pupils." (Day vs. Greenfield, 234 

Mass. 31, 34, 1919.) Normally, once a building is accepted for school purposes by the town school committee, the structure remains under the committee's administration until taken completely out of school use.

Criticisms of the Decentralized System. -Educators and school officers outside of Massachusetts expressed criticism to Research Bureau staff members of the Massachusetts procedure of separate school planning and school building committees.

They argue that this procedure weakens the rôle of the school committee, and creates opportunities for "passing the buck" when difficult or unpopular decisions must be made. They further hold that the more this authority is divided, the more likelihood there is for costly technical planning errors. Delays may also ensue, during which the project cost estimates will be thrown off by rising labor charges and material prices. There is some indication that administrative expenses go up as more governmental agencies become involved in the planning of the project and in its execution; a 1953 study of public building construction in Massachusetts was largely concerned with increased project costs resulting from "red tape." (House, No. 2575.) A 1956 California legislative report similarly suggested that excessive "red tape" can cause unnecessary hidden costs.

Defence of the Decentralized System. - Proponents of the Massachusetts system contend that the foregoing criticisms are exaggerated, and that they fail to take into consideration the special organic and administrative problems of school committees in this State.

They emphasize that town government is a very basic type of democracy which is largely dependent on the willingness of citizens to give unpaid service to the town. School committee members are primarily occupied with many policy and administrative questions presented by the superintendent, with reference to the budget, personnel, public relations and existing school buildings. 

If school committeemen must also function as a school building study group and school building construction committee, an impossible burden would fall on them should they not have a school construction staff specialist, such as an architect or engineer, to do the work. The Local School Committee and Superintendent. 

Notwithstanding the great decentralization of local school planning in Massachusetts, the primary responsibility for the educational planning of a new school or addition rests with the school committee and its superintendent.

The costs of the desired project will be greatly influenced by the community's educational program, organization and zoning. 

Whether the educational consultant retained for school planning is hired by the school committee, the building study committee or some other town committee, his specifications must be consistent with the policy laid down by the school committee. An overly elaborate program, or miscalculations in school organization and zoning can add greatly to building costs, no matter how economical the design of the architect and his choice of materials and con-

The school committee must assure the teaching of the courses required by statute for all schools, of certain other courses if the appropriate number of persons so petition, and of such other subjects as it considers expedient. Within this framework the committee must approve an educational program suited to the needs of the locality, and a suitable school organization to implement that program.

RESPONSIBILITIES OF PROFESSIONALS. 

Educational Consultants. 

The professional educational consultant should bring several important assets to bear on the problem of planning the school plant. 

He should have a broad background in the field of education and a working knowledge of contemporary teaching practices, curriculum content, and the range of services which the school should provide. 

He may have had teaching and administrative experience in the public schools, sometimes at all grade levels. 

Appointment. -The educational consultant is usually employed by some agent, committee or individual, of the city or town which is contemplating the construction of a new school. In Massachusetts, action is often taken by a special school building committee set up to act as the agent of the town in the planning and construction of the new school. Mayors or city managers may be local individual agents of cities.

Elsewhere in the country the school board frequently has the authority and responsibility, thus avoiding harmful dispersion of authority. Occasionally the architect retains the educational consultant because of his need for educational guidance, or because the retention of a consultant was provided in the architect's contract. 

Services. The scope of services provided by the educational consultant varies. Most often, he gathers data and makes projections which relate to future school needs, including such phases as population studies, pupil enrollment, land usage, and existing school plant. He views available school sites and makes recommendations in an order of priority. On the basis of the above projections he works out a written schedule of instructional and ancillary spaces, using nationally accepted standards. He also describes the purpose and scope of the activities proposed for each of the spaces, for the better understanding of architect, committee and community. In part, this information will stem from conferences with parents, teachers, pupils and others concerned with the new school.

1958.] 

SENATE 

No. 675.

The above material is usually incorporated into a set of educational specifications. This document becomes the written blueprint for planning the new school, and guides joint efforts of the educational consultant and the architect in the development of school building plans tailored in general to the unique needs of the town and specifically to the site selected. 

Good consultants will encourage provisions which develop a wellrounded building as a real educational tool and which insure a building suited to the educational program for many years to come. 

While some persons may criticize individually certain proposed features of the building, all will applaud efforts to seek out and eliminate waste space. Such waste may be found in unusable basement spaces, oversized corridors, foyers or stairways, and in larger than standard spaces which make no contribution to the operation of the program. 

The consultant's knowledge of good scheduling practices should bring about the correct number and size of instructional spaces, which are needed to house the program. Through his and the architect's efforts, plans can be devised to fit the building to the contours of the site and to take advantage of its topographical condition without excessive grading costs. Finally, the consultant's knowledge and experience is valuable to advise on tightly drawn specifications for school equipment and to aid in the actual selection of those furnishings. Throughout these many steps various local officials have stressed that in their experience consultants have saved towns considerable amounts. 

Consultant's Fees. In the Commonwealth the consultant's fee is usually stated as a percentage of the total cost of the construction of the building, with or without the cost of equipment of the school. 

The usual range of fee in Massachusetts is from one third to one half of 1 per cent of the total construction cost, although occasionally this may extend to twice that range of percentages where a wider range of services is required by the community. Less frequently, a flat sum may be agreed upon in advance as the fee. 

This fee range usually provides competent services.

Since these maximum costs have not yet been established, an inquiry was made by the Legislative Research Bureau which brought a confused reply from the Administrator of the Commission, as follows:

The School Building Assistance Commission has not established any maximum unit costs of construction because under the present law it is unnecessary to do so. 

In order to understand the reason for this, an examination of the history of chapter 645 of the Acts of 1948 is in order. When chapter 645 was originally enacted, the first sentence of the first paragraph of section 8 required the city or town to apply to this Commission for a school construction grant, and this Commission, under the second paragraph of section 8, was compelled to approve or disapprove the project within ninety days. Furthermore, under the second sentence of the first paragraph of section 8 this Commission was required to determine the approved cost of the project almost immediately. 

It was therefore essential to determine the approved cost of the project long before construction was even commenced. Under the second and third sentences of the first paragraph of section 8, the Commission was directed to base the cost upon the estimate submitted by the city or town itself or by the establishment of maximum unit costs. Thus, it was necessary to determine almost immediately the amount of the state construction grant upon the lower of the two following criteria: (1) the amount which the municipality estimated as the cost of the project, or (2) the cost of the project as determined by maximum (not minimum) unit costs of construction. 

The officials in this office and the Comptroller of the Commonwealth very quickly realized that such a procedure was contrary to sound, businesslike practices. Every one agreed that every city or town would put in an exaggerated estimate of the cost of the building if it knew that the grant might be based on such an estimate. The only check in the law was that this Commission should set up maximum unit costs. This was also undesirable because the maximum unit cost might be in excess of what a particular community might actually expend. 

Before any grants were actually made to the cities and towns, the Legislature radically revised section 8 by enacting section 1 of chapter 528 of the Acts of 1950. . . . The entire tenor of section 8, as so revised, evinces a clear intention on the part of the Legislature that the School Building Assistance Commission should not determine at an early stage the final approved cost. In fact, the third paragraph of section 8 states that the final approved cost of the project shall be determined within a reasonable time after the acceptance of the completed project by the local school committee.

Therefore, when the Legislature amended section 8, it realized that it would take a considerable length of time before the final cost could be properly determined.

Chapter V. Contracts and Supervision of Construction. 

STATE BIDDING LAW REQUIREMENTS. 

General Background. 

In the course of this study, the repeated belief was expressed to Research Bureau staff, by local officials and others, that added costs are being incurred in school construction because of alleged defects in the so-called "State Bidding Law," and the practices under that law. (G. L., c. 149, §§ 44A-44L.) 

This statute regulates the awarding of contracts for the construction of (a) state building projects, with an estimated cost of more than $5,000, and (b) local building projects, estimated to cost over $2,000. The regulations cover the construction, reconstruction, alteration, remodeling, repair or demolition of public buildings and structures. They do not cover projects other than the construction of a building, for example, highways, bridges and other public works which are regulated under other provisions of law. These 

State Bidding Law provisions are enforced by the State Department of Labor and Industries through proceedings in the State Superior 

Court. (G. L., c. 149, § 44K.)

[Mar. 

State Bidding Law requires that public building contracts subject to its provisions must be awarded to the "lowest responsible and eligible general bidder" on the basis of competitive bids. The phrase "lowest responsible and eligible bidder" is defined to mean- the bidder whose bid is the lowest of those bidders possessing the skill, ability and integrity necessary to the faithful performance of the work and who shall certify that he is able to furnish labor that can work in harmony with all other elements of labor employed or to be employed on the work. 

Subcontracts are similarly controlled by the statutory requirement that each subcontract for work estimated to cost more than $1,000, must be awarded competitively to the "lowest responsible and eligible sub-bidder against whose standing and ability the general contractor makes no objection." (G. L., c. 149, § 441.)

The law provides for the rejection of general bids and sub-bids by the "awarding authority," which is the regional, cown or city official or committee in charge of school projects. This source of local control is called the "school building agency" in the following text. 

Procedure for Awarding Contracts. 

To solicit bids of general contractors and sub-bids of sub-contractors for construction of a school project, the school building agency must advertise for such bids and sub-bids. For this purpose it must provide every person applying therefor with a copy of the plans and specifications of the project, and furnish him on request with a copy of a general bid form or sub-bid form which is in accord with the format and content also prescribed by the State Bidding 

Law. (G. L., c. 149, §§ 44E, 44F and 44G.) 

The proposals submitted by each general bidder must include specifications and if deemed necessary by the school building agency plans detailing all labor and materials to be furnished. 

The specifications must list separately the following 15 sub-trade items, if they are estimated to cost over $1,000: roofing and flashing; metal windows; waterproofing, damp-proofing and calking; miscellaneous and ornamental iron; lathing and plastering; accoustical tile; marble; tile and terrazzo; resilient floors; glass and glazing; painting; plumbing; heating; ventilating and air-conditioning; electrical work; and elevators. Listing may also be required of other classes of work if "the awarding authority deems it necessary or convenient to receive sub-bids." (G. L., c. 149, § 44C.)

1958.] 

The general contractor's bid must present the proposed work according to "Item 1" and "Item 2." The former Item 1 must cover the work of the general contractor, including the amount necessary to pay for each sub-trade service of $1,000 or less. Item 2 must list for each of the 15 sub-trades to cost over $1,000, the sub-bids proposed by the general bidder, giving the name of each sub-bidder and the amount of the sub-bid. A general bidder who customarily does any Item 2 work may also bid on such work; but his sub-bid may be rejected if he cannot prove that he is qualified to do the work called for by the specifications of the sub-trade. (G. L., c. 149, 

§§ 44F and 44J.) 

Sealed bids of sub-bidders must be filed with the school building agency four days before the opening of general bids, and opened two days before the general bids are opened. After scrutiny at that time a list of the sub-bids which were not rejected by the school building agency must then be mailed to every person on record who received a set of plans and specifications. To this list there must be attached a memorandum of the general contractors expressly precluded from using certain sub-bids.

From this list of sub-bids, the general bidder must select for each of the 15 sub-trades the lowest responsible and eligible sub-bidder against whose standing and ability he makes no objection; and his selections must be indicated in the general bid, under Item 2.

With the agreement of the school building agency, the general contractor may substitute for any sub-bidder another sub-bidder acceptable to the general contractor as to standing and ability. 

Every general bid, and every sub-bid under Item 2, must be accompanied by a bid deposit of 5 per cent of the value of the work to be performed, but not less than $100 nor more than $50,000. 

These deposits are returned immediately to bidders whose bids have been rejected; and successful bidders receive back their bid deposits after they have executed the required contracts and posted the prescribed bonds.

The school building agency must award the general contract for the project within thirty days. It may reject any or all general bids in the public interest; and any sub-bid if the sub-bidder is not deemed competent. Similarly, the agency may reject a sub-bid if only one such sub-bid has been made and it is deemed to be unreasonable. (G. L., c. 149, § 44D.) However, the courts have

[Mar. 

ruled that this right to reject bids is not the source of any option to disobey the statutory mandate in sections 44A to 441 to award contracts to the lowest responsible and eligible bidders.¹

Cost of Defective Bids. 

Architects, attorneys and state officials familiar with practices under the State Bidding Law express the opinion that the submission of legally defective sub-bids has cost the state and local government much money in public building projects. However, they do not estimate the amount of this loss. 

In a school project, the school building agency may solicit subbids for between 10 and 15 of the sub-trades classified under Item 2 of the general contract form. A total of 100 or more sub-bids may then be filed by sub-bidders covering these categories of work. A good sub-contractor may, in submitting his sub-bid, omit some alternative item required, thereby invalidating his sub-bid. The awarding authority, under the law, may not permit correction of a legal defect or of an omission in a sub-bid after the mandatory date for opening sub-bids, and must reject any bid which is "incomplete, "obscure" or "conditional." (G. L., c. 149, §§ 44F and 44H.)

Hence it must go to the next highest sub-bid, which may be substantially greater. On this basis it is stated that a sub-contract may be awarded to the next highest sub-bidder, at an added cost of $10,000, because the lowest sub-bidder may have omitted an alternative item costing considerably less than $1,000. 

Defective sub-bids can result from faculty practices on the part of the school building agency and its architect, or from carelessness on the part of the sub-bidder or his legal counsel. The agency may confuse or mislead sub-bidders by using specifications which are not concise or clear, with the consequence that the sub-bids received fail to meet what the agency and its architect had in mind. Or the sub-bidder and his attorney may be at fault because they did not read the State Bidding Law or the specifications of the work carefully.

In any event, the remedy for the situation is not clear. If the 

State Bidding Law were amended to permit sub-bids to be corrected after they are filed, a host of new troubles may arise; not the least of which would be preventing use of the amendment to defeat the "fair play" policy of the present law.

The Choice of the Lowest Responsible Bidder. 

There is evidence indicating a considerable misunderstanding on the part of local officials of their legal rights under the State Bidding Law with respect to the selection of the "lowest responsible and eligible bidder." A common complaint is that the statute is too "rigid" and that as a consequence the school building agency must give the contract to the lowest bidder unless it can prove him a criminal or a charlatan.

A less extreme view is reflected in the following except from the report of a town meeting committee in one of the communities of metropolitan Boston, emphasizing a desire for more elbow room for building committees in awarding contracts:

While we heartily approve of the basic concept of the State Bidding Law and the bulk of the procedural safeguards incorporated into it, we share the opinion of many building committee members . . . that the statutory proviso limiting the consideration of bidders to one bidder operates from time to time to the disadvantage of the town. This requirement that the town select the lowest "respon-

[Mar. 

of which there may be only one sible" bidder contrasts with the more liberal State Civil Service Rule 14 which permits a department head a choice of appointing any one of three top men on a civil service eligible list. The State Bidding Law ties the town down to an "eligible list" of one.

Problems arise for the building committee when it is confronted by a number of low bids, perhaps not too far apart, all of them from "responsible and eligible" bidders possessing the "skill, ability and integrity" necessary to the project. Yet the lowest bidder may not be as good a workman as one or both of the next two lowest bidders. Or he may possess a fine record in the construction of building projects other than of the type which is to be erected for the town. The lowest bidder may thus actually be the more costly choice to the town in the long run. 

Further, building committees tend to feel that they must accept certain lowest bidders, even when they have serious reservations about their choice, because they are uncertain of their as yet vaguely defined legal rights, and because of public pressures for immediate "economies" in the project. 

In reply to these criticisms, it is stated that the "lowest responsible bidder" clauses of the present State Bidding Law are nowhere near as rigid as alleged, and that the school building agency is free to reject any bid and to substitute one sub-bidder for another. These proponents concede some rigidity in the law, but argue that the limitations are minor inconveniences compared to protection obtained from competitive bidding under the "lowest responsible bidder" requirement.

Proponents also fear that any departure from "the lowest responsible and eligible bidder" rule to give the school building agency a choice between the two or three lowest responsible bidders would be impracticable. The bid of the second lowest bidder might be substantially higher than that of the lowest man, with the result that the choice of the former would increase the costs of the school building unnecessarily. The modification might well create uncertainties for bidders which would be subject to costly political manipulation. Moreover, proponents of the present rule believe that such a modification would reduce the pressure on bidders to cut their bids to the minimum.

SUPERVISION OF CONSTRUCTION. 

Duties of the Architect.

Aside from designing a school building which is acceptable to the school building and other agencies, the architect has three major functions, as follows: (1) To assist in the preparation of the required bidding forms, plans and specifications, and to advise the agency in evaluating bids received. (2) To provide continuing architectural services to the school building agency after construction contracts have been awarded. 

In the main, these services are concerned with the evaluation of change-order requests by contractors. Such requests may stem from planning mistakes, unanticipated difficulties with the site, or shortages of materials. The architect's fee normally includes the services of all engineering designers of the project who assist him as required. (3) To provide general supervision to protect the locality against defective work by contractors and sub-contractors. As a rule, the architect shares his full time among various buildings in different stages of design or construction. According to the practice of his profession and established legal and governmental procedure, he

[Mar. 

does not guarantee the performance of contractors and sub-contractors. The continuous on-site full-time supervision in detail of construction activity on the project is an obligation of the clerk-ofthe-works, discussed below. 

Duties of the Clerk-of-the-Works. 

The clerk-of-the-works, variously known as "construction supervisor," "resident inspector," "resident engineer" and the like, plays a most important role in the protection of the public interest in the construction of a schoolhouse or other public building. His work was discussed as follows in the 1953 report of the "Little Hoover Commission" relative to public building construction:

On private projects he is normally appointed by the architect, in consultation with the owner of the building to be erected. His salary is paid by the owner, or by the owner through the architect under a special account. 

The clerk-of-the-works himself is usually an architect or engineer and must be thoroughly conversant with the architect's work. It is essential to the success of the project that the relationship between the architect and the clerk-of-the-works be close. His

Basically, the job of the clerk-of-the-works is to hold the contractor to the plans and specifications developed by the architect and approved by the owner. reputation depends on his competence and his capacity to protect the person for whom the project is being built. . . (House, No. 2575 of 1953, p. 25.) 

The clerk-of-the-works must possess technical knowledge of and experience with building materials and processes, and should be thoroughly cognizant of the practices of the various trades involved in the project. He should be able to interpret drawings and specifications correctly. While he must not interfere with a contractor's work program, he should establish in his mind the sequence and proper progress of operations under the different trades. 

He must examine materials and workmanship, verify lines and levels, measure work departures from the contract, and promptly report all irregularities in the performance of the contract to the architect.

Variable Cost Factors. 

Educational Programs. -Unit costs quoted for different school buildings may not reflect accurately the differences in the educational programs involved. The educational programs of elementary school buildings will show relatively small variations, due to the relative uniformity of school work of children from the kindergarten to sixth grade level. However, there are likely to be significant and major educational differences between secondary schools because of the flexibility and complexity of their work. 

Types of Construction. - Cost discrepancies may be due to differences in the types of construction used in different buildings which are apparently equal in other respects. One structure may be built of reinforced concrete, another of structural steel, and a third may be a wood frame building. One building may have a complete basement, while another may have basement facilities which are only complete under part of its structure. The cost variation on account of the foregoing factors alone may be as much as five dollars a square foot.

Non-School Facilities. Often cost data do not show readily the impact of "non-school" facilities which are included to serve community non-school uses. The most common example of this is the oversized auditorium intended to serve also as a town meeting hall. 

Multi-Purpose Combinations. Cost data may give no clue as to multi-purpose school building combinations of auditoriums, cafeteria and gymnasiums. Two or three of these features may be consolidated in a single "multi-purpose" space, or they may be provided separately. Some schools may omit a cafeteria, since the children bring their own lunches or go home to eat.

Site Difficulties. Generally, cost data do not indicate added

Variable Area and Volume Measurement. The measurement of square footage and cubic footage of classrooms and certain other rooms does not vary greatly. Chapter VII. Uniform Architectural Plans. 

Types of Uniform Plans. 

GENERAL ASPECTS. 

Efforts have been made in 26 States to reduce school building construction costs by the use of uniform building plans which are variously referred to as "uniform architectural plans," "uniform plans," "standard plans" and "stock plans." In all but the six

States listed below, the use of uniform plans has been abandoned. 

Essentially this practice involves the preparation of one or more sets of architectural plans, drawings and specifications for a number of school buildings of fairly common sizes, generally with a view toward obtaining minimum unit costs. The plans are designed by architects who have been retained by the state departments of education or some other suitable agency. These plans are then made available to local school districts requesting them. In some cases, a very large school district may develop its own uniform plans.

A uniform plan may be just a set of plans, without advance arrangements to produce a large number of the structures. Or the

[Mar. SENATE - - 

No. 675. uniform plan may be geared to a system of prefabrication, with units being mass-produced and shipped to the point of assembly. 

In five of the six States currently using uniform plans, the buildings concerned are all small structures of one to four classrooms; in the sixth State, uniform plans for schools of up to nine classrooms were authorized by the Legislature in 1955, but this directive was never implemented beyond the standard rural school structure containing one and two classrooms.

In general, the uniform plan for rural one to four classroom schools has tended to survive because the needs of such projects are very simple, due to their small size. Ordinarily, architectural firms would not be particularly interested in so small a building project. 

Moreover, the relatively high percentage charged by architects as fees on buildings with contract costs of less than $100,000 spurs school authorities to utilize some kind of uniform plan for small structures. Thus, a simple architectural plan becomes available which can be given to any reasonably competent local builder to follow, possibly under the guidance of a staff architect or an architectural consultant working for the school district or for the State 

Department of Education. 

Costs of Uniform Plan Schools. 

Information gathered by the Research Bureau regarding the costs of construction of uniform plan schools has been scant and inconclusive, largely because of the small number and size of such schools in this country. A 1955 California legislative study of school construction costs in that State cited costs of from $6.85 to $10.46 per square foot for stock plan schools of from one to five classrooms in five California counties. A comparatively small permanent structure was reported to have cost $10.75 per square foot. 

No data were available to suggest the probable comparative costs of larger structures. Thus, the cost aspects of the latter are likely to remain in the area of conjecture and debate.

Analysis of New Schools in Massachusetts. Proponents of uniform plans note that 436 school buildings have been approved by the Massachusetts School Building Assistance Commission for State Aid, not including additions, in the past ten years (to December 31, 1957), and there are indications that school building construction will continue at the same rate.

Of the 436 plans made for the buildings constructed, less than six repeated the same plan. About 121 of the buildings were large, complicated, secondary school buildings, or were regional buildings, and approximately 70 of the elementary school buildings either had less than 6 to over 14 classrooms. But there remained about 250 smaller structures where uniform plans might well have been used. 

For the sake of argument, say the proponents of uniform plans, eliminate those buildings with less than 8 regular classrooms, leaving 144 elementary school buildings with from 8 to 14 classrooms. Such structures might have been built from one or two stock, 12-classroom elementary school building plans with some modifications, if necessary, for special conditions. 

Proponents note that all but three of the above buildings were built from individually prepared "custom" plans. Only three were constructed from plans that were re-used. Twenty-eight of the listed buildings are two-story buildings.

[Mar. 

The individual plans of 34 of the above 144 buildings with only a single story are reproduced diagrammatically in Appendix C. They show the marked variations in the "custom" plans in use. It develops that these 34 floor plans resemble the letters of the alphabet, as follows: 11 conform generally to the letter "L"; seven to the letter "T"; five to "U"; four to "H"; three to "I"; and two each to "F" and "Z." These variations probably reflect mostly the different local policies followed with respect to multi-purpose rooms, but also adaptations of the school buildings to site contours, and to odd-shaped lots. 

Proponents of uniform plans inquire whether these great variations in floor plans are really necessary. They ask why the “L," "I," "F" and "Z" buildings could not, for example, have been built from a standard "L" plan, and the "T," "H" and "U" structures from a stock "H" plan. They insist that an examination of these floor plan sketches will demonstrate that the latter two types of plans would have been adequate for all purposes with but little modifications of the individual floor plans actually used by the designers.

Major Points of Proponents. -The proponents present the following arguments in favor of standard plans for school buildings: 

1. Time would be saved in the preparation of the plans for such structures. Once a set of standard plans had been developed by an architectural and engineering staff for a category of school buildings, such as a 14-room elementary school, the amount of time which would have to be spent for such work on future school projects would be reduced considerably. The architect using the standard plan in a school project under his supervision would largely be concerned with adapting the plan to the particular site. Thus, the time difference between drawing a whole plan "from scratch" and adapting or modifying an existing plan is comparable to the time difference between writing a book and editing it. In any building project, time saved is money saved. 

2. Savings would be made in the cost of preparing plans. For reasons previously cited, once a set of uniform plans had been brought into being, the architect's job on a project would be lightened. His office staff would not have to produce a new set of plans, but only "edit" the existing plan in terms of the project site. 

The existence of this savings possibility is revealed in the discount

1958.] 117 

1 currently given by an architect when he is asked to build another structure, using a set of plans previously devised by him. This discount varies, a 2 per cent reduction in the percentage of the architect's fee being common. 

3. It would be easier to estimate the cost of a building following a uniform plan. Material and labor quantities would be available for quickly estimating the cost of construction. These estimates would tend to become more exact as experience was acquired with the use of the plan. 

4. Economies through mass production of building components would be possible. The existence of standard component specifications would enable manufacturers to stock-pile items in advance, with resulting time savings when these parts were ordered by the contractors. Lower unit prices for these parts would also be possible. With uniform plans in use, manufacturers of building materials would be encouraged to standardize their products for inclusion in a standard building. With every building now different, there is now no inducement to seek such economies. 

5. Voters would know what the building they are asked to buy will look like after the first one is erected. 

6. The introduction of "frills" and "special ideas" would be discouraged because public attention would be focused on additions, changes or deviations in the uniform school building plan. The uniform plan would thus become a "measuring stick" with which to evaluate different projects built from the same plan; and it would also function as a standard against which to measure custom" plans of schools of comparable pupil capacities. 

7. The construction of a building of a lower grade than the standard building would be discouraged in communities where there is a tendency to cut costs beyond reason. Again, the uniform plan would function as a measuring stick. 

8. Local architects familiar with local conditions could be employed, even though they may not be experienced in school building design. Thus the opportunity for architects in general would be increased, where at present there is a tendency for school design work to be limited more and more to a segment of the architectural profession. 

9. Use of the plans could be optional with the community. 

However, they could serve as a basis for a fair system of unit cost

[Mar. 

controls in the state grant-in-aid system.

Arguments Against Uniform Plans. 

Undeniably, strong arguments exist against the use of uniform plans for school buildings. These contentions are vigorously advanced by those who believe that there is considerable value in the very process of planning for schools. It is stressed that not only is it the distinct right and obligation of each school district to be intimately involved in the planning of each new construction project, but that each such construction project should become an opportunity to improve school design and to enhance the community's knowledge of what today's schools are all about. A group of distinguished school men, administrators, consultants, architects and engineers met recently by invitation of the editor of Architectural Forum, a large, well-known architectural publication, to explore every possible avenue of schoolhouse economy. SAVINGS THROUGH REGIONAL SCHOOLS. 

General Aspects. 

Reorganization of school districts into efficient units is perhaps the least controversial of the methods to obtain savings in school costs. Not only does efficient reorganization eliminate expenditures to build unnecessary school buildings, but it also effects savings in operational costs. For the past decade and more there has therefore occurred an intensified drive throughout the Nation to reshape thousands of small school districts into a smaller and logical number of more efficient consolidated school districts. 

Massachusetts has participated actively in this development since enactment of the School Building Assistance Act, which provided the stimulus of more liberal state grants to those school districts which pool their resources and build consolidated or regional schools. There have been saved large sums of money through avoidance of expenditures to construct small new school buildings in areas where there were not enough pupils to permit the efficient operation of a sound and broad educational program. Not only have fewer school buildings been erected as a result, but the regional

1958.] 133 

 schools have also served to raise the total educational quality of the State.

Pressures for Further Regionalization. 

Educators believe that while sizable advances have already been made in this direction, there remain two important steps which would strengthen the regional concept and bring about further economies. The first of these is the encouragement of a broader regional concept to embrace both elementary and secondary schools. 

Most of the regional school districts set up today throughout the State are for the purpose of providing secondary schools alone for the regional district, despite the fact that the enabling legislation makes equal provision for the consolidation of elementary schools. 

Important financial advantages may be expected from this broader concept. Unfortunately most school districts cling desperately to local autonomy for their elementary schools, despite the fact that the advantages of regionalization are being demonstrated daily at the secondary school level. 

The second aspect of school district reorganization in Massachusetts which might well produce further economies in school construction is that of the creation of more logical regional districts. 

Many of the regional high school districts which have evolved thus far are sound consolidated districts. However, certain others are comprised of towns which could both be affiliated with other neighbors to achieve even better regional districts. In certain other cases, through the unordered growth and development of nearby regional districts with which a given town is unwilling to affiliate itself, it has now become isolated and cut off from any future prospect of regionalization.

An example of this may be seen in the town of Boylston. At the time of the formation of the Wachusett Regional High School

District, some discussions were held concerning the desirability of combining the three towns of Sterling, West Boylston and Boylston into a regional school district. But agreement could not be reached on this matter, so that the town of Sterling became an affiliate in the Wachusett district, even though, on the one hand, it is not contiguous to the town of Holden where the regional high school is constructed, and, on the other hand, it is adjacent to the town of West Boylston where another regional high school could have been

[Mar. 

built. Later the towns of West Boylston and Boylston attempted to get together in a regional school district, but again no agreement could be reached. Meanwhile, the town of West Boylston grew so rapidly that it was felt that it could afford a junior-senior high school of its own, and such a building has recently been constructed. 

Within the past few months the town of Boylston sought to affiliate itself with Berlin, the only other small town contiguous to it, Shrewsbury being large enough to be self-sufficient and have its own school. The formation of the regional district with Berlin was voted down, however, and now the town of Boylston finds itself forced to build its own high school alone under unfavorable circumstances. Much of the needless expense involved in this building could have been avoided by a more logical grouping of the towns in this region and a realization by the towns involved of what is likely to happen when logical regionalization is not carried through. In such a situation the state finds itself torn between the deeply rooted Massachusetts tradition of home rule and the avoidance of wasteful expenditures for school construction.

Like the rest of the nation, Massachusetts has far to go to improve all of its small, inadequate school districts. The economies. to be had through this means are somewhat proportional to the extent to which effective school district reorganization is brought about throughout the Commonwealth.

Project Cost Controls. 

Under the present Massachusetts statute, state grants are forthcoming on a total project cost percentage basis to help pay for expenditures to build acceptable new local public schools. No effort is made to differentiate between those communities which provide reasonable school facilities and those which clearly have been extravagant. The problem here, of course, is deciding when extra facilities may be justified as providing considerably greater and useful arrangements without arousing too much criticism that they are too large for the resources of the community when such a variety of governmental needs must be met. 

To meet this situation it has been proposed that the present statutes of Massachusetts might well be amended in various ways to the general end that state grants should only be made in any given case up to a reasonable amount of educational facilities. 

Several States have experimented in this direction as indicated by the following text relative to California, Connecticut, Delaware and Washington.

These States have established maximum unit cost controls or other restrictions designed to prevent the wasteful use of state grants-in-aid to local school districts for school construction. 

California. -The Education Code of the State of California seeks to prevent extravagance in new school construction by applying both space controls on a square footage basis, and maximum cost controls, also on the square foot basis.

The purpose of the space limitation is to prevent like school districts from trying to outbuild each other. Thus, elementary schools (grades, kindergarten through six) may not exceed 55 square feet per pupil, including classroom, administration, corridors, multipurpose rooms and the like. Space in elementary schools for seventh and eighth grade pupils may not exceed 75 square feet per pupil. And the limitation on high school space is 92 square feet

A square foot cost limitation is established by the California Department of Finance in accordance with the type of facility to be built. The square foot unit cost does not include either off-site or on-site development, or the purchase of sites: sites are purchased separately, and an allowance is made for site development. The square foot costs are prescribed on a geographical basis, and must not exceed the cost of comparable new construction by non-stateaided school districts in the same geographical area. The school building's budget must come within the maximum allowable. 

The square foot maximum unit cost of construction promulgated by the State Department of Finance is not a flat figure for the entire building. The spaces in the building have been divided into 21 types, each with its distinctive per square foot cost. And the maximum allowable cost for each of the 21 types of spaces varies from one of the seven geographical areas of the State to the next. 

Connecticut. -- The state aid formula in Connecticut restricts the state grant for the erection of an elementary school to $500 per pupil, or one half the cost, whichever is less. In the instance of a secondary school, the state contribution is limited to $700 per pupil, or one half the construction cost, whichever is less. Regional school districts are given an additional $100 per pupil.¹ This system rewards economy by giving the community the benefit of each construction dollar saved between the total per pupil cost and the amount of the state aid. On the other hand, if the community wishes to be liberal, it does so at its own expense: the State's contribution remains the same, but the balance of the cost must be met fully by the community.

Delaware. -The statutes governing state aid to local school construction in Delaware prohibit the payment of such aid toward any classroom which costs more than $30,000. The law also bars state aid if the school project includes a swimming pool, or if the architect's fee exceeds 5 per cent of the construction cost.2

CONNECTICUT SCHOOL BUILDING ASSISTANCE ACT. 

[Public Act No. 593 of 1957.] SECTION 1. As used in this act, the term "elementary school building" means any public school building designed to house an elementary school consisting of any combination of grades below grade nine; "secondary school building" means any public school building designed to house a secondary school consisting of any combination of grades seven through twelve; "school building project," except as used in section 8, means the construction, extension, furnishing, equipping or major alteration of a building to be used for public school purposes, including the improvement of land therefor, with the improvements thereon, if any, but shall not include the cost of a site; "completed school building project" means a school building project declared complete by the state board of education as of the date shown on the final application for grant payment purposes as designated by the secretary of the state board of education or his agent; "legislative body" has the meaning ascribed to it in section 1 of number 13 of the public acts of 1957. 

SEC. 2. Each town or regional school district which has completed or shall complete a school building project on or after January 1, 1957, shall be eligible to receive grants as provided in this act. 

Any town desiring a grant for a public school building project may by vote of its legislative body direct the selectmen or the board of education of such town to apply to the state board of education and may authorize the selectmen or the town board of education, whichever is designated, to accept or reject such grant for the town. 

Any regional school board may vote to apply to the state board of education for such a grant.

SEC. 3. The state board of education shall have authority to receive, review and approve applications for state grants under this act, or to disapprove any such application but only so far as such. application does not comply with the requirements of the state

[ 1958.] 153 

fire marshal or the state department of health. When any such application is approved, said board shall certify to the comptroller the amount of the grant for which the town or regional school district is eligible under this act and the number, amounts and times of annual payments thereunder. Upon receipt of such certification, the comptroller is authorized and directed to draw his order on the treasurer in such amounts and at such times as certified by the board.

SEC. 4. When any such application is approved, said board shall notify the town or the regional board of education that an allotment has been made to it for the purpose, and such allotment shall be accepted or rejected by a vote of the selectmen, the town board of education or the regional board of education, as the case may be. Within five days after the taking of such vote, the town clerk, the secretary of the town board of education or the secretary of the regional school board, as the case may be, shall notify the state board of education of the action taken. 

SEC. 5. The amount of the grant approved by the state board of education on or after January 1, 1957, for any completed school building project shall be computed as follows: (a) In the case of a new elementary school or an extension of an existing elementary school, $500 for each pupil which the school is designed to accommodate or one half the cost of such project, whichever is less; (b) in the case of a new secondary school, or an extension of an existing secondary school, $700 for each pupil which the school is designed to accommodate or one half the cost of such project, whichever is less; (c) in the case of a new school or an extension of an existing school designed to accommodate a combination of elementary and high school pupils, $500 for each elementary and $700 for each secondary pupil to be accommodated therein, or one half the cost of such project, whichever is less; (d) in the case of any such project included in sub-sections (a), (b) or (c) above which involves a regional school district, an additional grant of $100 for each pupil which the school is designed to accommodate; (e) in the case of projects involving the major alteration of any existing building to be used for public school purposes, one third of the necessary cost as determined by the state board of education. 

SEC. 6. Grants under this act shall be paid in equal annual instalments, the number of which shall be twenty except in cases

[Mar. 

where the project has been fully paid for, in which cases the number of instalments shall be five. Grants under $10,000 shall be paid in five annual instalments. All orders and contracts for school building construction receiving state assistance under this act shall be awarded to the lowest responsible qualified bidder only after a public invitation to bid which shall be advertised in a newspaper having circulation in the town in which the construction is to take place. 

SEC. 7. Any town or regional school district having a school building project which it is unable to finance, after estimating any grant available to it under section 5 of this act, may by vote of its legislative body or by vote of the regional board of education direct the selectmen or the chairman of the board of education of such town or regional school district to apply to the state board of education for a hardship grant for such purpose. The board shall, in determining the town's or district's ability to finance such a school building project, consider among other factors for such town or for the towns comprising such district the valuation of real property within such town or district as reflected in a grand list adjusted on the basis of true market value, tax-supported bonded indebtedness, the tax rate, expenditures for school building projects since July 1, 1945, school building needs as determined by the local board or boards of education for the present biennium and for such future period as the state board deems appropriate, and planned and urgently needed capital improvements which will affect the debt burden or tax rate of the town or towns. If the state board finds that (1) the town or district is financially unable to complete such projects, and (2) the standard of education in such town or district will deteriorate unless a hardship grant is received for such project, the state board may, with the approval of a committee consisting of the governor, the attorney general, the comptroller and the commissioner of finance and control, make a hardship grant to such town or district in such amount as it considers necessary and proper, and may in its discretion pay such grant in one sum or in instalments. In case of a hardship grant to a regional school district, said state board may allocate the amount thereof which shall be credited to each town's proportionate share of the project or of the district's indebtedness and current expenditures as determined under the provisions of section 906d.

1958.] 155 

SEC. 8. As used in this section, the term "school building project" means the construction, extension, furnishing, equipping or major alteration of a building to be used for public school purposes, including the acquisition and improvement of land therefor, with the improvements thereon, if any. Any town, upon approval by a vote of the members present at a regular or special meeting of its legislative body, may, without further authority from the general assembly, issue its bonds which shall be obligatory upon the inhabitants thereof, for the purpose of financing in whole or in part any school building project.

SEC. 9. The state board of education shall maintain an advisory school planning service. Such service shall be available on the request of any town or regional board of education or town school building committee to advise in the preparation of and to review the preliminary plans for any school building project and the educational program which it is designed to house. 

SEC. 10. No school building project shall be undertaken except according to a plan approved by the town board of education and by the building committee of such town nor at an expense exceeding the sum which the town may appropriate therefor. A copy of final plans and specifications of all school building projects including those for the site shall be filed with the state board of education before construction shall be begun.

SEC. 11. Upon receipt by the state board of education of the final plans for a school building project as provided in section 10 of this act, said board shall promptly review such plans and check them for conformity with the requirements of the state fire marshal and the state department of health, and a written report of such review, together with the recommendations of said board, shall be made to the school building committee and to the town board of education.

SEC. 12. Chapter 77 of the general statutes is repealed. 

SEC. 13. This act shall take effect July 1, 1957. 

SEC. 14. The sum of $1,500,000 is appropriated for the biennium ending June 30, 1959, to carry out the purposes of this act.
