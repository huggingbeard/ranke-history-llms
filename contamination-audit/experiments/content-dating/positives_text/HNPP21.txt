PREFACE 

There are several purposes for this study of state-chartered savings, building, and loan associations. Inadequate as it is, it presents a picture of the condition and trends of these associations during recent years. 

It tells the direction in which they have been moving, and how rapidly they have been progressing. 

Too few people realize the importance of building and loan associations in our economic and social life. Although competing institutions, such as insurance companies, banks, and government agencies, have taken some of their business, they still constitute the most important agency in home financing and thrift. They hold an important place in the savings and welfare of the thrifty working and middle classes. Building and loan associations play a prominent role in the investment and wealth accumulation plans of these classes. 

During the severe depression years these associations took in and repaid millions of investors' dollars. This was done, for the most part, in an orderly and safe manner. Although these institutions were faced with serious problems, they discharged their obligations in a very satisfactory manner. Not all of their problems have been dispelled, but most of them are being solved. The data presented in this bulletin show some of the changes in these institutions during their years of very rapid growth, through the depression, and well into recovery, but provide only a general survey picture of the events of these periods. 

Without the aid of the Bureau of Business Research the study would not have been possible. Acknowledgment is made of the work of the Bureau staff in assembling and compiling the tabular data employed in this bulletin and for assistance with the manuscript.

I. INTRODUCTION 

The tables and other data presented in these pages should not be regarded as a complete picture of all building and loan associations in Illinois. Prior to 1933 all associations were state-chartered; since then it has been possible to incorporate under Federal charters in a partly competing system. Because these Federal institutions operate somewhat differently and because of the desire to study only state associations, data shown here for the Federal associations are merely incidental. 

Most of the material presented in this bulletin was taken from the 

Annual Reports of Mutual Building, Loan and Homestead Associations compiled by the Auditor of Public Accounts of the State of Illinois. 

The study is subject to a number of limitations, a few of which should be mentioned here. Not all state associations end their fiscal years at the same time. Hence the data are for the fiscal years ending in a given calendar year. Besides, there are differences among various associations as to account classifications and modes of operation. When a comparison of data is made over a period of years, the lack of uniformity of account classifications immediately complicates the portrayal. 

In recent years state associations, largely at the insistence of state supervisory officials, have greatly improved their accounting technique and statements. Partly for these reasons the accounts are not always set up in exactly the same way. The main items, however, are sufficiently alike to show clearly what has been taking place. 

Within the last few years a new plan of operation has been adopted by many state associations. This is of interest largely, but not exclusively, to borrowers. It is the so-called "direct-reduction plan” of lending money. Instead of the borrower's purchasing shares on which the accumulated dividends and installments finally extinguish the debt, he amortizes his loan in a definite number of years irrespective of the association earnings. The borrower's payment thus goes for payment of interest on his loan and the reduction of the net loan, each month. This plan of operation was not common prior to 1936. Although the data presented in Sections III and IV segregate the associations by type of operation, no great emphasis is given to these divisions. 

There were two reasons for this treatment. One of these was to keep the study simple. The other was that within the period included in the study the direct-reduction plan was not in general use.

II. OPERATIONS OF STATE ASSOCIATIONS, 1920-1936 

Following the limitation of private construction during the war period of 1917 to 1918, there was a boom in residential construction. 

Resources of the Illinois building and loan associations jumped from $148,779,000 in 1920 to $317,404,000 in 1925. This growth in resources was even greater than that in numbers. As shown in Table I, the number of associations rose from 714 in 1920 to 891 in 1925. Although the number of institutions was even larger at the beginning of 1930, namely, 938, thereafter it began to decline. In 1920 there were 27 new chartered associations and a loss of 6 due to consolidations. New associations numbered 39 in 1925 as compared with 9 losses. In 1930, and in each year thereafter, losses have exceeded gains. Only 5 new charters have been granted since 1930. In the same period, from 1931 to 1936 inclusive, 194 associations have retired either voluntarily or involuntarily. In 1935 and 1936, changes to Federal charters accounted for a large proportion of the losses. Liquidations, however, were also numerous.

At the end of 1936 there were 744 state associations in Illinois. 

This was moderately larger than the 714 operating at the end of 1920. 

The actual number of all associations in Illinois, including the 93 Federal-chartered, was 837, or more than the 1920 number.

Table II presents a summary of the changes in the main balance sheet items of state associations. Not all items are presented. The total assets constitute 100 per cent, and each item is expressed as a percentage of the total.

The main productive asset of savings and loan associations has been and still is loans on real estate. These formerly accounted for about $90 out of each $100 owned by the association. Together with loans on shares, they amounted to 94.9 per cent of total assets in 1925. From 

1930 to 1936 the decline of loans on real estate was more rapid than the decline in total assets. By 1936, real estate loans accounted for approximately $61 out of each $100 owned by the associations. Loans to stockholders on their own shares accounted for about $2 in each $100 of total assets. 

Two important explanations may be advanced for these changes in loans. One is the conversion of some loans into real estate through foreclosure and similar procedures. The second is the change-over of loans from the so-called share-purchase plan to the direct-reduction plan. Under the older share-purchase plan the mortgage loan account remains unchanged until enough has been saved and earned on the shares to cancel the loan. The direct-reduction plan reduces the loan balance each month. Furthermore, when some associations offered their shareholders the opportunity to use direct-reduction loans, many of their shareholders changed from the old to the new basis. This meant that they cancelled their share balances plus profits against the loan in order to start on the direct-reduction plan. Thus a borrower on the share-purchase plan with a $5,000 loan, installments on his shares of $2,100, and earnings of $300 would cancel his stock balances against the $5,000 and owe only $2,600 on his new direct-reduction loan. The result would be a reduction of $2,400 in the assets and liabilities of the association.

Particularly significant have been the decline in loans and the rise in real estate, the former the source of most normal earnings, the latter generally an unhappy and unprofitable investment.

Installments that were delinquent reached their peak on a percentage basis in 1934 at 4.1 per cent. By 1936 they had receded to 3.3 per cent. Although not so large in themselves, if compared with the amount of installments paid in the delinquent installments are found to exceed 10 per cent thereof in both 1934 and 1936. 

Changes in the loan accounts and in real estate tell only part of the story of what has taken place. A closely related item is the real estate sold on contract. This represents properties which have been taken over by the associations for non-payment of loans, and which have been resold.

The sale of such properties on contract has certain characteristics which differ from those of regular loans. Generally deed is not given to the buyer until after several years of satisfactory payments and reduction in the debt. One reason for this procedure is that such sales are generally made with small equity or down payments. In order to protect itself against future trouble and loss in case of non-payment, the association retains title.

Such real estate sold on contract increased sharply from 0.3 per cent of total assets in 1925 to 4.6 per cent by 1936. The rapid increases representing sales of properties in 1934, 1935, and 1936 did not, however, effect a reduction in real estate owned. It is only since 1936 that building and loan associations have sold their real estate with sufficient rapidity to reduce the balance of this account. 

Another item indicating a trend toward unhealthy conditions was the rise of taxes and insurance advances from 0.1 per cent in 1920 to 

0.4 per cent in 1936. This represented moneys paid by the association for taxes and insurance on loans on which the buyer had not made these payments. Such action by the association was necessary in order to protect the properties on which it had loaned money. These advances represent a lien upon the properties protected. 

One of the significant changes in building and loan policy was found in their cash holdings. Formerly it was considered adequate for associations to hold very small balances. The sound philosophy underlying this practice was that an association dealt with long-time loans and long-time capital. But savers not infrequently desire to withdraw large sums. A request for formal advance notice of intention to withdraw is always provoking. Good will and confidence are created by prompt repurchase of shares. These facts, plus the uncertainties of the

last few years, have tended to make management and savers more "liquidity" conscious. As a result, the normal cash balances of the last few years have increased greatly. By 1936 they were 3.2 per cent of total assets, or higher than in any other year covered by the study. 

The capital of savings and loan associations is generally divided among several classes of stock. The basic stock is the installment shares, which are sold on an installment basis. These may be purchased for savings, or for the future extinction of a loan from the association. 

Concomitant with the shrinkage of loans during recent years, because of conversion to the direct-reduction plan, and because of withdrawals of many persons to consume their savings, installment shares have declined in relative importance. Whereas installments paid in provided 64.3 per cent of total assets in 1920, by 1936 they had fallen to almost half as large a percentage, or 33.2 per cent.

Even before the real estate difficulties of the last decade, installment shares had begun to decline in importance. To an increasing extent other classes of investments were taking their place in providing money for the associations. One class of stock that increased for a while and then declined was "prepaid shares." An investor may place a lump sum of $60 or $70 with the association, and leave it undisturbed until the dividends accrued to it mature the stock at $100. This kind of stock was most popular in 1930 and has since declined slowly, so that by 1936 it provided 15.6 per cent of total assets. 

Paid-up shares represent investments wherein a full share, generally of $100, is purchased from the association. The owner receives a semiannual dividend which he may withdraw in the same way as dividends from other corporations. This type of stock grew rapidly from 2.9 per cent in 1925 to 18.1 per cent in 1936. It represented a convenient medium of investment for the middle and poorer classes. 

Building and loan associations also obtained a small share of their funds from matured stock. Ordinarily this type represents prepaid or installment stock that has matured. In some cases the shareholder has not been paid for his matured stock because the association lacked ready cash. In other cases the shareholder did not bring his certificate in for payment or did not transfer his matured stock to paid-up stock as is frequently done. At their peak in 1934, such unpaid maturities amounted to only 5.3 per cent of resources. By 1936, they had been reduced to 4.5 per cent.

Although it is one of the smaller items, "Bills Payable and Advances" is significant in its indications of condition and policy of savings and loan associations. Generally the choicest assets in the

nature of mortgage loans are pledged by the association as security for money borrowed by it. Since the establishment of the Federal Home 

Loan Banks, which act as bankers to the building and loan associations, the latter have been able to borrow on much cheaper and more flexible terms. Bills payable and advances declined from a peak of 3.6 per cent in 1920 to 1.9 per cent by 1932. They increased again and by 

1934 were 3.3 per cent of assets. This was followed by a decline, so that in 1936 borrowed money was 2.9 per cent of total assets. By 1936 the urgent demands on associations had begun to recede. In fact, some of the borrowing was continued because of the desire of associations to avail themselves of a source of economical funds, which in turn were reloaned to home buyers and builders. Thus the slow decline of this item should not necessarily be construed as unfavorable. 

Among the assets owned by associations in which the values were highly debatable and uncertain were their holdings of real estate. 

Properties repossessed are generally run-down and neglected. Thus the burden is placed on the association for rehabilitation and for setting. up reserves to care for probable losses on the sale of such real estate, both of which draw heavily on current income. The real estate problem did not become acute until after 1930, and the expansion of real estate owned was most in evidence in 1933, 1934, and later years. 

The building and loan associations in Illinois have not recognized physical depreciation of real estate owned by creating a periodical charge for such depreciation. Furthermore, with the increased holdings and hazards involved, it was not readily recognized that large losses might accrue in the future. Consequently, it was not until substantial amounts of real estate had been accumulated that a general reserve was commonly set up by associations to bring book values into line with market or realizable values. These several factors partly explain the smallness of "Reserve for Real Estate," and its rapid rise to 3.1 per cent by 1936. This real estate reserve proved a heavy drain on the earnings of many associations, resulting in temporary reductions in dividends.

Contingent reserves did not grow rapidly until the past ten years. 

In both 1920 and 1925 they stood at 1.2 per cent of total resources. 

This was indeed a very small reserve. By 1930, the reserve had risen to 1.9 per cent of resources, and has since experienced rapid increases to 4.0 per cent in 1936. There is still considerable opportunity for the building up of larger contingent reserves. Changes in Earnings 

Unfortunately, complete and dependable profit and loss statements of state associations are not published over a period of years. A summary is presented each year, however, for the main sources of the gross income of the building and loan associations. Although changes in account classification and procedure may have weakened the comparability of the data presented in Table III, the general picture is sharply enough defined to show that the trends are clearly portrayed. 

Total earnings for these selected items amounted to $9,363,000 in 1920, at which time they were the equivalent of 6.3 per cent of total resources. By 1925 they had more than doubled, in keeping with the growth of resources. The peak for the years shown was 1930, when the earnings of these selected items totalled almost $30,000,000 and were equal to 6.4 per cent of total resources. Moderate declines were in evidence in 1931 and 1932, followed by very rapid decreases. By 

1936 earnings were $17,086,000, or much less than twice those of 1920, whereas resources were still almost twice the 1920 figure. The rate of earnings in 1936 was 5.9 per cent.

These earnings, however, tell but a small part of the story. Quite as important is a knowledge of the sources of the earnings and the outlays which they entail. The lower rows of Table III divide the total earnings of these selected items among interest, premiums, fees,

fines, and rents. For this purpose the total earnings of each year are taken as the equal of 100 per cent.

The main source of revenues has been and still is the interest paid by borrowers on loans. Interest increased both actually and relatively from 1920 to 1930. In 1930, interest accounted for 93.4 cents out of each dollar of revenues for the five items shown. In the following years the total earnings declined, but interest declined even more rapidly. By 

1934 it was back to the relative size it attained in 1920. By 1936 further declines reduced interest to 76.3 cents per dollar of revenue.

The main source of income has thus tended to decline to a marked degree. In this period changes in accounting procedure occurred. 

Formerly associations had accrued unpaid interest and credited it to earnings. In recent years, partly because of the large sums delinquent, this practice was found unsound, so that only interest realized has been credited to earnings.

Premiums paid by borrowers in addition to interest declined. steadily from 7.1 per cent in 1920 to 2.6 per cent in 1936. This change reflects the decreasing competition for funds among borrowers, which made it unnecessary for them to pay this extra cost. This source of income has continued to decline. With the continuation of severe competition from governmental and other agencies, it bids fair largely to disappear.

Fees of all kinds have declined sharply from 3.0 per cent in 1920 to 0.5 per cent in 1934 and 0.7 per cent in 1936. Included in this item are such charges as membership fees, passbook fees, stock transfer fees, and the like. Several factors have contributed to their decline. 

During recent years the associations have not recruited new members and borrowers in the same numbers as during the years of their growth. 

Then, too, there has been a tendency to eliminate certain trivial fees. 

Fines were formerly rather generally assessed against all delinquent savers, the assumption being that the fine compensated the association for the income loss it had suffered. Although delinquencies increased during the depression, the collection of fines became difficult. In many cases they are no longer regularly assessed. In their stead one occasionally finds that a small sum is charged at the time the stock matures. 

Income from this source has generally ranged between 1 and 2 per cent. 

One of the most significant changes is to be found in rentals on real estate owned by the association. Rentals increased from almost nothing (0.2 per cent) in 1925 to 18.5 per cent, or nearly one dollar in five, in 1936. Almost as significant as the changes in this item as a contributor to revenues are the responsibilities and outlays which it entails.

Changes in Capital Stock 

The capital stock of building and loan associations differs from that of most corporations in that it changes from day to day in accordance with the issuance of new shares to savers, payments on shares by savers, and the cancellation of shares when repurchased from members. Building and loan associations freely sell and repurchase their own stock.

Changes in the number of shares of building and loan associations are indicated in Table IV. These numbers of shares are not necessarily full paid, since many of them are installment shares being purchased, or prepaid stock which must remain invested for some years before reaching maturity.

The number of shares that was issued approached 3,000,000 in 1930. In subsequent years the number of new shares issued fell off by more than two thirds to the low level of 850,612 in 1935. By 1936 mild increases had set in. During the years 1925, 1930, and 1931 the withdrawals were less than new shares issued, but by 1932 just the

opposite situation prevailed. Withdrawals were exceptionally heavy from 1930 through 1933. Even though new shares issued began to increase in 1936, the withdrawals still far outnumbered them.

There are, however, several factors explaining these changes. No doubt economic conditions and the need of some borrowers to use their savings accounted for the major change. Many others recast or refinanced their loan accounts, particularly by changing to the directreduction plan. By so doing they cancelled their former stock and profits against their loan, thereby increasing the withdrawals and reducing the stock in force. Many people scaled down their loans by the cancel-and-endorse method, thereby reducing their stock and loan account but still remaining on the share-purchase plan. In this way they protected themselves against some of the disadvantages of the old partnership plan of loan repayment. 

Shares that were permitted to remain with the association until they matured through the normal accumulation of installments and/or a single payment plus profits, are listed in Column 4 of Table IV. Maturities were at their peak in 1931 with 595,512 shares. These maturities represented shares invested from five to ten years earlier. 

Maturities remained at a high level for several years, reflecting the completion of the investment cycles started some years prior. They dropped to a very low point in 1936, but this fact was attributable in large measure to the sharp shrinkage in stock outstanding, which resulted from withdrawals in the depression years. With the marked reduction of shares in force, there would naturally be fewer shares to mature.

The last column shows that shares in force in 1936 were fewer than in any year surveyed, and less than half the number of the peak of 10,000,000 shares of 1930 and 1931.

A glance at the data in this table, as well as other tables presented, gives the impression that these savings and loan associations have been dynamic and flexible, even though they deal with long-term and comparatively fixed mortgage investments. Although some associations were highly unliquid, most of them repurchased and matured a very large sum of capital stock during a period of unsettled economic conditions.

III. DISTRIBUTION OF STATE ASSOCIATIONS ACCORDING TO SIZE OF COMMUNITY 

Savings, building, and loan associations are found operating in all kinds and sizes of communities in the State. Contrary to the situation. found for many other institutions, the size of community is not a criterion of the size of the association. As Table V shows, the 744 associations operating in 1936 had average resources of $388,000, or approximately a third of a million dollars. The average varied from $92,000 for the 152 associations in communities of less than 2,000 population to $1,256,000 for the 48 associations in cities of 25,000 to 50,000 population. In cities above this size, the average association was smaller.

Chicago had the smallest and the largest association in the State; the resources of the former were only $14,148 and those of the latter, $9,149,676. In general, Chicago had many small institutions, the average for 289 being $239,000, which was much less than the average for the State as a whole. Excluding Chicago, the smallest association. had resources of $4,659; it was in a community with a population of 

467. The largest downstate association was found in a city with 

46,589 population, and its resources were $6,442,026. 

Many of the small associations are unable to support a full-time managing secretary-treasurer. Many of them are operated as adjuncts or side lines of other businesses. They are usually operated in conjunction with real estate offices. This is true both in the larger communities and among the associations of substantial size. If careful business standards are observed, this dual interest may not be harmful to the association. If, however, the resources of the association are parcelled out to finance real estate sales and activities of the officers and their associates, a serious and unhealthy condition is created. 

In examining the percentage figures in Table V and making comparisons among associations in different sizes of communities, it is well to remember that some of the differences at first evident are attributable in part to the extent to which associations had changed to the direct-reduction plan of operation. Because of the keener competition in larger cities, there was a tendency for associations in such places to adopt the direct-reduction plan sooner than in the small towns. 

Loans on real estate were 66 per cent of resources for the 152 associations operating in communities of less than 2,000 population. In cities of more than 50,000 population real estate loans were 58 to 60 18

per cent of resources. In cities of intermediate size the loans on real estate averaged between 60 and 62 per cent. Loans on shares were uniformly small, averaging less than 2 per cent. The largest percentages for this item were found in the smallest villages and in Chicago. 

Delinquent interest on loans was less in the larger communities. 

Unpaid interest was 2.7 per cent of total assets in towns of less than 

2,000 population. It declined steadily to 1.4 per cent for cities of 

25,000 to 50,000 population. In the larger cities unpaid interest again increased moderately.

Delinquent installments on shares disclosed a similar trend, declining from 3.8 per cent in the smallest towns to 1.7 per cent in the 

10,000 to 25,000 population groups. In larger cities delinquent installments were much larger; the percentage for Chicago was 5.8. 

Although associations in small towns had more delinquent interest and installments than those in most larger cities, the real estate owned or in process of acquisition was smaller comparatively. Real estate ranged between 15.2 per cent and 24.1 per cent for the small associations in small towns and those in cities of 10,000 to 25,000 population, respectively. Approximately 22 per cent of the resources of associations in towns of more than 25,000 population was represented by real estate and judgments. Perhaps the smaller real estate item on the one hand, and larger delinquent interest and installments on the other hand, of associations in small as compared with large communities. indicate a greater willingness of the associations in small towns to carry slow accounts for a longer time before taking over properties. 

No well-defined trends are evident for real estate sold on contract. 

Chicago, which had among the highest percentages of real estate owned, had the lowest percentage of real estate sold on contract, suggesting that little progress was being made in real estate liquidation. 

Associations in cities of 50,000 or more population (excluding Chicago) made the best showing on this score; balances due on real estate sold on contract equalled 6.8 per cent of total assets. 

Cash on hand averaged 3.2 per cent of total assets for all associations. A marked contrast is to be found between associations in Chicago and those in all other cities with more than 50,000 population. 

Chicago associations had 2.2 per cent of assets in the form of cash; associations in all other cities of 50,000 population or more had a cash to total assets percentage of 4.1. Certain limitations must be attached to these percentages for cash. Many associations declare dividends at about the same time, or shortly after their fiscal year ends. Bills payable are one of the small but highly significant liability items. For associations in most cities the average percentage for these was small. Only in Chicago was the amount owing on notes for borrowed money relatively large, averaging 5.9 per cent. When the large amount of bills payable for Chicago associations is considered in conjunction with the smallness of their cash funds, and their large amount of real estate owned is compared with the small percentage of real estate sold on contract, it is evident that the Chicago associations were in general in a less satisfactory condition than most downstate associations in 1936.

There was an irregular but clearly defined trend for installments paid on shares to decline as size of community increased. The percentage of installments paid in to total assets ranged from the maximum of 45.6 per cent in the smallest communities to 27.3 per cent in 

Chicago. As previously mentioned, part of this showing may be attributed to conversion of loans from the share-purchase plan to the direct-reduction plan. The trends evident in the installment stock are partly counter-balanced by those in other classes of stock. Although the trend was irregular, the percentage of prepaid shares to total assets increased from the small to the large communities. A marked exception was Chicago, which obtained less than 5 per cent of its total assets from prepaid stock. On the other hand Chicago had a much larger proportion of its resources provided by paid-up shares than associations in other parts of the State. The percentage of paid-up stock to total assets was 27.4 for Chicago, followed by 22.3 per cent for cities in the 5,000 to 10,000 population group. The towns with population below 5,000 obtained less than the average resources from paid-up stock, whereas cities of 5,000 population or more obtained more than the average percentage of resources from this type of stock. 

The amount of funds provided by matured shares was fairly small, ranging between an average of 2.0 per cent of total assets in cities of 

25,000 to 50,000 population to 6.6 per cent in cities of 10,000 to 25,000 population. Cities of less than 10,000 population obtained a smaller portion of funds from matured shares than did the cities of larger size; the 25,000 to 50,000 group was the only exception. 

The item of profits represents a comparatively large amount for most associations. The balances in this account represent earnings accumulated in the past years, depending on the rate of earnings and

the amount of certain classes of stock. If an association has large amounts of prepaid and installment shares, then it is likely to have correspondingly large amounts of profits. The older these stocks, and the closer to maturity, then the larger will the profits account be, since it will represent the sums retained to mature these stocks. As previously mentioned, those associations that have entered more vigorously into the use of the direct-reduction plan will have less installment stock and also less profits. Although the profits account may contain sums to be paid as dividends on paid-up and matured stock, these profits are generally paid out currently and not accumulated over a period of years.

Although the percentage of profits to total assets and to various classes of stock varies considerably, there are only two groups in Table V which stand out pronouncedly. These are the associations in cities of 10,000 to 25,000 population and those in Chicago. Associations in these communities had distinctly less profits accumulated than the average in other towns. Chicago in particular had an exceedingly small amount of profits, namely, 3.9 per cent of total assets. In the case of Chicago this may be partially explained by the smallness of the installments paid in on stock and the very small amount of prepaid shares. Even after making allowance for the small installment and prepaid stock, however, the profits of Chicago associations were very small. Nor could this be explained entirely on the basis that the directreduction plan was an important factor. 

Contingent reserves averaged small in all communities of the State. 

There was a slight tendency for associations in cities of less than 10,000 population to have relatively larger percentages of contingent reserves to total assets than associations in larger cities. Chicago, however, was somewhat of an exception, since contingent reserves in Chicago associations averaged higher than for the State as a whole. 

Real estate reserves varied widely among the cities of the State. 

The smallest reserves of 2.3 per cent were found in cities of 10,000 to 25,000 population, which had the largest real estate holdings and should presumably have had the largest reserves. Associations in these same cities also had the smallest profits account (with the exception of Chicago) and the smallest contingent reserves. Taken together, the small real estate reserves seem to have been attributable to small earning power rather than to the fact that high quality properties did not require larger reserves. 

Associations in cities of 50,000 or more population (except Chicago) also had small real estate reserves in view of their large

real estate holdings. The average reserve for these associations was 2.4 per cent of total assets. Chicago associations had larger reserves than others in the State, with 4.4 per cent of their assets represented by this source.

Although the direct-reduction loan is today widely employed among building and loan associations, in 1936 the movement was still in its infancy for most associations. The financial statements for the year 

1936 showed that only 129 out of 744 had made direct-reduction loans; furthermore, they still retained most of their old share-purchase loans on their books. The extent of acceptance of this loan plan varied materially among the associations, depending upon the size of the communities in which they were located. Of the 303 associations in towns of less than 10,000 population, only 11.9 per cent had direct-reduction loans as contrasted with 21.1 per cent of the 441 associations in larger cities. Chicago associations reported the new plan of loans in 58 out of 289 cases, or 20.1 per cent.

Although a change to the direct-reduction loan would tend to reduce total assets, comparison of the data in Appendix Tables 1 and 2 discloses that the associations with the new plan were generally larger than those that were less progressive. The average total assets of all the associations offering direct-reduction loans were $678,000, as compared with $327,000 for those offering only share-purchase loans. As among the several groupings by size of city, the only exception was found in cities of 50,000 or more population; Chicago, however, had larger direct-reduction associations.

There were a number of differences between associations employing and not employing direct-reduction loans. Some of these have significance, others do not. For example, loans on real estate were comparatively larger for the direct-reduction associations, but part of this could be ascribed to the elimination of share balances and profits of borrowers on the direct-reduction plan. The larger real estate loans, together with smaller percentages of delinquent interest and installments, suggest that the direct-reduction associations were in better condition than their competitors who were employing only the share-purchase loan plan. Even this statement, however, is subject to qualification, because some associations converted loans from the sharepurchase plan to the direct-reduction plan and thereby eliminated the delinquent interest and installments of some loans.

Other factors point to a somewhat better condition for the associations offering direct-reduction loans than for their competitor associations. The former had a relatively smaller percentage of real

estate, namely, 18.7 per cent of total resources as compared with 23.3 per cent, and more real estate sold on contract, namely, 5.0 per cent as compared with 4.5.

Direct-reduction loan associations had larger amounts of borrowed money owing to creditors than the share-purchase associations. They also had smaller installment stock and profits, which in turn were compensated by relatively larger prepaid and paid-up stock. These variations in stock and profits accounts largely reflect the cancellation of installments and profits against loan balances converted to directreduction loans.

In Chicago there were enough associations employing the directreduction plan to show differences. In general, the same characteristics pointed out in the preceding paragraphs for all associations apply to those in Chicago. The differences between direct-reduction and sharepurchase associations in Chicago, however, are more marked than in the smaller communities. Judging from the balance sheet figures alone, the Chicago associations on the direct-reduction plan were probably better situated than most other direct-reduction associations in smaller communities. Contrariwise, the share-purchase associations in Chicago appeared in less satisfactory condition than similar associations in smaller cities. There were sharp differences in the two groups of associations in Chicago on the basis of their loans, delinquent interest and installments, real estate owned, real estate sold on contract, and also their cash position. For each of these items, the direct-reduction loan associations appeared in the better light.

With respect to borrowed money the direct-reduction associations owed the equivalent of 7.5 per cent of their resources as compared with 5.2 per cent for the share-purchase associations. The former apparently needed and also had somewhat larger reserves for real estate and contingencies than the latter. In these respects the share-purchase associations appeared in slightly better position.

During the last few years, however, many associations have been encouraged to borrow money for new loan and construction activity. The 340 associations using the share-purchase plan in downstate counties averaged somewhat larger in resources than those of the entire State. IV. DISTRIBUTION OF STATE ASSOCIATIONS ACCORDING TO AMOUNT OF TOTAL ASSETS 

Although the associations with larger total assets are found in large cities such as Peoria, Springfield, Quincy, Danville, and Chicago, it does not follow that the small associations are to be found in small cities. Many of the small associations are found in small communities. which can support only a small association; on the other hand, a large plurality of the small savings, building, and loan associations are located in Chicago, the largest city in the State. As shown in Table VI, the state associations are widely distributed according to total assets. 

Of the 744 associations, 138 had total assets averaging $30,000, and at the opposite extreme 15 associations had average total assets of $5,236,000.

In some respects these various associations disclose distinct differences which may, in part, be attributed to the problems of size. With respect to loans on real estate and also loans on shares, the differences among associations do not reveal definite trends. Significance may be attached, however, to the larger-than-average percentage of loans on real estate and the smaller-than-average percentage of loans on shares of associations with total assets of more than $3,000,000. The first of these indicates an investment in productive assets higher than the average; the latter suggests that investors have been paid for their stock rather than given share loans, as is too frequently the case. 

Interest accrued and unpaid shows an irregular but definite tendency to be larger for the small than for the large associations. The largest associations had the least delinquent interest. An even clearer case is found in the delinquent installments, which ranged from 7.0 per cent of total assets for the group of smallest associations to 1.5 per cent for the group of largest associations. Both these items indicate that the larger associations have been better able to collect their interest and installments.

All size groups reported more than 20 per cent of total assets tied up in real estate owned. Associations with total assets between $250,000 and $500,000 and between $1,000,000 and $3,000,000 had somewhat larger real estate holdings than the others. A limited superiority of large over small associations is suggested by their ability to sell real estate. The account "Real Estate Sold on Contract" indicates that the larger associations have sold a greater portion of their real estate in this manner. If real estate owned and real estate sold on con- 26

tract are combined, the apparent advantage of the larger associations disappears. The larger associations can be in a more favorable position only to the extent that their real estate sold on contract proves to be a sound producer of interest and principal payments. 

The groups of smallest and of largest associations maintained the largest cash balances with 4.7 and 3.8 per cent of total assets thus invested, respectively. The remaining groups approximated, or were well below, the general average of 3.2 per cent.

Not only did these two groups at the extremes in size have the most cash, but they also had the least borrowed money, another favorable indicator. Associations with less than $50,000 of resources had bills payable equal to 1.6 per cent of total assets; those with more than $3,000,000 had borrowed the equivalent of only 1.4 per cent of total assets. The $250,000 to $500,000 group, which was found to have the largest amount of real estate owned, also had the largest percentage of borrowed money, namely, 5.5 per cent of total assets. 

Installments paid in on shares being acquired on the monthly or other periodical payment plan averaged 32.9 per cent of total assets for all the associations. There was a tendency for this percentage to decline as size increased until total assets amounted to more than half a million dollars. Associations with less than $50,000 of total assets obtained 36.6 per cent of their resources from installments paid in. 

This percentage declined steadily until it fell to 28.8 per cent for institutions of one-quarter to one-half million resources. Among the larger institutions, installments paid in provided between 30 and 35 per cent of total assets.

A wide variation was found in the use of money provided by shares which were permitted to accumulate to maturity from a single lumpsum payment—the prepaid shares. Such prepaid shares were far more important for the larger than for the small associations. Approximately one dollar in twenty was obtained from this source by small associations in contrast with one dollar in four for the very large associations. 

Paid-up shares were relatively more important in the medium-sized associations than in either the smaller or larger ones. Matured shares provided less than one dollar in twenty for all associations, and were small for all groups. When all classes of stock are combined, it is found that the percentage of capital paid in to total assets increases quite regularly with size of association from 60.5 per cent for the smallest to 76.9 per cent for the largest. The 744 state associations that were presented in Table V of the text are further analyzed in Appendix Tables 5 and 6. These two tables divide the associations as between those operating entirely under the older share-purchase mortgage plan, and those operating under a combination of this older plan with the more progressive plan of making direct-reduction mortgage loans.

Of the 744 associations, 615 were operating entirely on the sharepurchase plan in 1936 and the rest were employing the new plan in addition to or in exclusion of the older plan. There was a very clear indication that the larger associations were using the newer methods more generally than the small associations. Among the 138 associations with total resources of less than $50,000, only 12, or 8.7 per cent, employed direct-reduction loans. At the opposite extreme, among the 15 associations whose resources exceeded $3,000,000, 6, or 40.0 per cent, were employing direct-reduction loans. Between these extremes there were definite trends toward the more frequent employment of direct-reduction loans as association size increased. 

Because of the small number of cases found among some of the size groups for the associations employing direct-reduction loans, variations among size are less clearly indicated. By comparing the total columns of Appendix Tables 5 and 6, however, important differences among these associations may be noted. 

The 129 associations employing direct-reduction loans had mortgage loans equivalent to 64.3 per cent of total assets as compared with 58.8 per cent for associations employing only share-purchase loans.

Normally, the cancellation of the share and profits balances against the loan in conversion to the direct-reduction plan reduces the mortgage loans relatively more than the total assets, so that the percentage of loans to total assets of these associations would, therefore, become smaller. The contrary showing must be explained on the basis of the relatively greater proportion of large associations among those employing direct-reduction loans than of those on the older plan. Possibly somewhat better operations were resulting in the higher percentage of loans to total assets.

In a number of other respects the associations employing directreduction loans seemed to be in better position. They had smaller proportions of their assets tied up in loans on shares, less in delinquent interest on loans, less in unpaid installments on shares, and smaller percentages of real estate and the like owned. The associations employing direct-reduction loans had only 3.1 per cent of total assets tied up in cash as compared with 3.3 per cent for the corporations employing only share-purchase loans. In the opposite direction, the former associations had bills payable equal to 3.6 per cent of total assets as compared with 2.5 per cent for the latter group. In both respects the 129 associations offering direct-reduction loans appeared less favorably situated than the 615 not using directreduction loans.

Variations in the capital stock accounts are to be explained partly in terms of the types of operations. The smaller percentage of installments paid in for associations with direct-reduction loans is partly attributable to the cancellation of loan stock against mortgage debts. 

Not only did the group employing direct-reduction loans have smaller installments paid in, but they also had smaller profits, largely because of the cancellation of installments and profits of borrowers against loans. The associations with larger equities were essentially in sounder condition. 

The marked differences in the profits accounts are attributable to a number of factors. All these associations employed only share-purchase loans. Some associations on the share-purchase plan may also use the so-called "cancel and endorse" method, by which installments. and profits are cancelled against part of the loan when full shares are accumulated. This practice tends to reduce the profits. Furthermore, the profits account represents the accumulation on several years' savings, the older savings generally being accompanied by larger profits. 

Relatively larger use of prepaid stock also increases the profits account, since more profits must accumulate for the stock maturity. 

Heavy withdrawals of share-purchase stock or prepaid stock would thus tend to reduce materially the profits accounts. The same result would follow the taking over of real estate at foreclosure or by similar process, in which the stock and profits are cancelled against part of the unpaid loan and the real estate accepted as full or part payment for the balance of the loan. Then, too, the profits accounts contain an apportioned profit sufficient to care for all stock payments that should have been paid in. If there are large delinquent installments, the profits account may be somewhat larger than it would otherwise be when compared with paid-in capital.

V. SUMMARY 

Savings, building, and loan associations are incorporated mutual thrift institutions serving the middle class and thrifty working class of the population. To an unusual degree they contribute to the social and economic welfare of the communities in which they operate. In many cities of the State their resources exceed those of the commercial banks. They have encountered and largely solved many serious problems of operation, finance, and competition during the past fifteen to twenty years.

In the last few years there have been important changes in the plans of operation and methods of incorporating associations. Prior to 1933 only state-chartered associations operated. Since then the 

Federal government has fostered a partially competing system of associations, which by the end of 1936 included 93 Illinois associations. 

These were in addition to the 744 state-chartered associations at the end of 1936. This study is concerned only with the state-chartered associations. Many of the state associations have been modifying their modes of operation to include the so-called direct-reduction mortgage loan, in which the monthly payments of the borrower are applied first to interest and the balance to debt reduction. Formerly monthly payments of borrowers went to interest payment and the balance to share purchase, which ultimately extinguished the debt. This change has tended to weaken their orthodox mutual plans of operation and to increase the fluctuations in profits for those savers who are not borrowers. 

There was a rapid growth in number and resources of these savings and loan associations following the War period. The number operating increased from 714 in 1920 to 933 in 1930 and then began to decline, falling to 744 in 1936. Their resources fluctuated even more widely from $148,800,000 in 1920 to $470,100,000 in 1930 and down to $287,- 800,000 by 1936. At the end of 1936 the average association was much larger than in 1920.

Throughout these years significant changes were taking place in the manner in which associations obtained and invested their funds. 

Their mortgage loans, which provided the bulk of their income, declined in importance from more than 90 per cent of the total assets to approximately 60 per cent and were largely replaced by real estate owned, an unwanted and not entirely profitable asset. During the depression years delinquent interest and installments increased rapidly, but by 1936 these were declining, reflecting definite and widespread 33

improvement in the condition of these thrift associations. During these years, the building and loan associations also became "liquidity" conscious and began to maintain larger cash balances. By 1936 more than 

3 per cent of their resources was in the form of cash. 

Equally significant changes occurred in the sources from which associations obtained their funds. In 1920 installments paid in on monthly savings shares accounted for almost two thirds of all assets, whereas by 1936 less than a third of the funds came from this type of stock. Two other classes of stock increased in importance during these years, namely, the prepaid shares and the paid-up shares, which increased from 11.4 per cent to 33.7 per cent. Most of this increase, however, occurred in the paid-up shares. Although small in size, reserves and contingent funds increased rather steadily throughout these years and equalled 7.1 per cent of total assets by 1936. Profits accounted for about 13 per cent of total assets from 1920 to 1932, but by 1936 had declined to 10.5 per cent. Since direct-reduction loans did not come into common use until 1935, it is evident that these changes must be attributed to economic conditions rather than to the new mode of lending.

Changes in gross earnings of state-chartered associations were equally marked. From $9,363,000 they increased to $29,856,000 in 

1930, and then receded to $17,086,000 by 1936. Interest income had formerly contributed 90 per cent of the total earnings, but by 1936 had fallen to three fourths of the total. Although small, premium income declined markedly. Rents from real estate owned increased from less than 2 per cent of total earnings prior to 1933 to 18.5 per cent by 1936.

But these rentals entailed large expenses in operation and maintenance of real estate so that net earnings were substantially reduced. 

When the 744 state associations operating in 1936 were classified by the size of city in which they were located, it was found that their resources increased from an average of $92,000 for those in the smallest communities to a maximum average of $1,256,000 for those in cities of 25,000 to 50,000 population. The 289 Chicago associations averaged only $239,000 in resources. A few of the associations in 

Illinois are large but most of them are small, with much less than half a million dollars in resources. 

Although there are some irregularities, certain definite differences may be found among associations in different sizes of cities. Delinquent interest and installments declined in importance as size of city increased except in the large cities, where the trends were reversed. 

On the contrary, real estate owned and in judgment, and the like,

was comparatively smaller in the small cities and increased as a percentage of total assets to a maximum of 24.1 per cent in cities of 

10,000 to 25,000 population; it stabilized at about 22 per cent for larger cities.

Clearly defined trends were noted for the use of various classes of stock. The use of installment shares declined as size of city increased, ranging from 45.6 per cent in the smallest communities to 27.3 per cent in Chicago. Although the trend was irregular, prepaid shares increased in percentage with size of city; Chicago was an exception, obtaining only 4.4 per cent of total assets from this source. If all stocks are added together, it is found that the funds contributed by stockholders, exclusive of profits, increased from 67.0 per cent of the total in very small towns to a peak of 76.3 per cent for associations in cities of 10,000 to 25,000 population, and then declined to 64.4 per cent for Chicago associations. Profits varied considerably but were larger in small than in large cities. The extent to which direct-reduction loans were employed may have been a factor explaining the smaller profits accounts of associations in large cities. 

A regrouping of the 744 state-chartered associations operating in 1936 brought out certain characteristics that appeared to be related to the size of the associations. The 507 associations with total resources of less than one quarter million dollars constituted 68 per cent of the 

744 associations but controlled only 18 per cent of the $288,615,000 of resources. The 59 associations with resources of more than one million dollars constituted 8 per cent of the total associations, but controlled 53 per cent of the resources.

Both delinquent interest and installments tended to decline with increase in size of the association. Real estate sold on contract was relatively larger among the large than the small associations. Cash declined as a percentage of total assets from 4.7 per cent in the smallest ones to the minimum of 2.3 per cent for those with a quarter to a half million dollars of resources, and then increased to 3.8 per cent for associations with more than $3,000,000 of resources. Bills payable increased with size to the peak of 5.5 per cent in the quarter to half million size and then declined to the minimum of 1.4 per cent for the group of largest associations.

Similar trends were found for installments paid in, which decreased from 36.6 per cent of total assets for the smallest associations to the minimum of 28.8 per cent for those in the quarter to half million size and then increased again to 35.0 per cent for the large associations. 

Prepaid shares increased with size of association from 5.3 per cent in

the smallest sizes to 23.3 per cent for the largest group. Paid-up shares increased from 11.9 per cent for the small associations to the peak of 

22.2 per cent for the half million to one million group, and then dropped to 15.4 and 16.3 per cent for the two large-sized groups of associations. 

If all classes of stock are added together, there is a very regular increase in the percentage of funds thus obtained from 60.5 per cent for the smallest to 76.9 per cent for the largest associations. Profits likewise increased with size, reflecting the larger capital accounts as well as the variations in installments and prepaid stock with their accumulated profits from previous years. Both contingent fund and real estate reserves tended to decline as size of association increased. 

Although based on data that were not entirely adequate nor refined so well as might be desired, the tables show that there are certain patterns to which state-chartered associations tend to conform. There are patterns of both time-wise and present conditions. The savings, building, and loan associations have changed and are still changing their modes of operation, their resources and equities in accordance with changing economic conditions. There are basic differences among them attributable to their size and to the problems inherent in the efficiencies of specialization versus delegated management. Only within the small communities does it seem that the size of city is likely to be a severely limiting factor in the achievement of the association.

Perhaps the clearest indications were found in regard to the sharp manner in which associations of all sizes and types received the imprint of unfavorable economic conditions. Although they were influenced in varying degrees, the same basic problem appeared among all associations. Although there may be many special exceptions, most associations are still faced with basically the same problems. Outstanding among these are reduction of delinquent interest and installments and the sale of real estate acquired during and immediately following the depression. By 1936 definite progress was clearly evident, but the problem is present even today.

Perhaps one of the outstanding results of the study is the disclosure of the extent to which state associations were flexible and responsive to economic conditions. During the Twenties they accepted millions of dollars from investors and loaned them to borrowers. 

During the early Thirties they were able to collect millions of dollars from loans they had made, and from savers, and return them to investors who wished to withdraw their funds.
