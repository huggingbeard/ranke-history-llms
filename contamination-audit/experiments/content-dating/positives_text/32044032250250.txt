Equity 16 the system of law that was administered by the English court of chancery and was adopted and 18 administered by courts of the same name or possessing the same functions in the United States. In England the body of rules and doctrines of equity were incorporated into the law of the land by the Judicą. ture Act of 1873. In the United States the same merging of law and equity has taken place more or less completely in most of the states. The American colonios generally adopted the principles and practice of the chancery court of England but in most states both common law and equity are administered by the same body sitting according to circunstances either as a court of law or of equity. This is true in the District of Columbia. 

Equity 18 the modern name for chancery.

Chancery courts were radically different from common law courts both in substantive and adjective law. The most important difference was that issues of fact at law were tried by a jury whereas in equity they were tried by the chancellor. 

The jury is no part of equity procedure. The characteristic feature of equity 

Jurisprudence 18 the infinite variety and efficiency of its remedies as comparsa with the three remedies of modorn common law, namely the recovery of the possession of chattels or real estate or a judgment for a sum of money.

Almost as great a contrast was presented until only recently by the progressive and enlightened character of equitable principles compared with the arbitrariness and harshness of a large part of the early common law. This latter contrast 18 gradually disappearing. The greatest progress made in the law in the 

19th century in the United States consisted in the adoption by courts of law of equitable doctrines. It is only a matter of time until equitable principles will completely swallow up the rules of courts of law. 

For centuries Anglo-American Jurisprudence has presented the curisoity of two sets of courts and of two systems of Jurisprudence dealing largely with the same subjects but administering systems of law and justice altogether different in character and atmosphere. For example, one may not come into a court of equity with unclean hands, but courts of law cared not whether a party washed his hands or not. The common law courts built up the cormon law through its case history and by reducing it to the form of written precedents through recorded decisions. 

A characteristic feature of the common law courts was the tremendous homage paid to procedent and the unwillingness of the judges to change or adapt a rule once laid down by procedent (stare decisis) to meet the needs of a changing and advancing society. The common law system was marked by an inexorable and exaggerated formalism, Under the method of common law procedure by writs a plaintiff's cause of action had to fall within one of a limited number of settled forms or precedonts or under one of the forms of common law actions or else he could have no relief. In other words under the old notion where thore was no remedy there was no right. This is exemplified (before the action of assumpsit had been invented in 1286) by the case of attempting to enforce a contract not under seal.

After 1286, the remedy of assumpsit having come into existence, a contract not under seal became enforceable which gave a plaintiff the substantive right to do so for: the first time, This necessitated the doctrine of consideration.

A plaintiff who could find no remedy in the common law courts soon discovered that he had no substantive right in such courts, but as a last resort did have the right to potition the king or his privy council for relief based on grounds of justice, good conscience and common sense. At some undetermined date, certainly before the year 1300, it became common to refer such petitions to the chancellor. The chancellor was the king's right-hand man and the keeper of the king's conscience. He was the most important officer of the crown and his duties wore numerous. He also exercised a certain amount of jurisdiction as an ordinary cormon law judge. The petitions referred to him by the crown came to be known as his extraordinary jurisdiction and such cases were decided by him based on principles of honesty, equity and good conscience. The chancellor was usually an ecclesiastic who was trained only in the Roman law but not in the common law of England. Before 1400 A. D. the court of chancery was operating fully although its jurisdiction and rules of decision for a very long time continued to be somewhat undefined and vague. Long before the English Reformation (1532), however, there were already in existence and use a great many of the rights and remedies of equity which we know today such as the injunction and uses (trusts). A great proportion of our present doctrines of equity originated before 1700. The Judicature Acts of 1873 in England abolished the court of chancery as a distinct tribunal with separate judges. By this great roform equitable and legal rights and remedies are administered in the game case. The rules or byuity, however, are given preference where they conflict with rules of law. Practically the same reform was inaugurated in this country beginning in the state of New York in 1848, 25 years before England took the samo step. More than half the states today have followed the example set by New York and England. These states are known as code states from the fact that the main principles of their procedure are embodied in a code modeled upon that of New York. The District of Columbia by virtue of the new Federal Rules of Civil Procedure is considered a code jurisdiction. The procedure in the District of Columbia today is the same for suits in law and in equity, but the same court hears both classes of suits sitting part of the time as a law court and part of the time as an equity court. In spite of the new Federal Rules of Civil Procedure in the District of Columbia the principles and remedies of equity remain in theory at least undisturbed by the consolidation of courts or by changes in procedure. In other words while the law and equity adjoctive law has been merged in the District of Columbia nevertheless their respective substantive principles have not boon merged and cannot be merged without a Constitutional amendment because the federal Constitution recognizes the distinction between equity and law as separate and independent systoms of jurisprudence.

This nexin is partly true and partly untrue. Equity will follow the law as to the law of descent and distribution of property but it will not necessarily follow the law where it enforces a contract to convey real estate or grants an injunction to restrain a threatened tort. To do so in the latter two instances would be to deny that there is any such thing as equity.

In such a usual case equity will give him no remedy although he has the right. This maxim originated during the days of the early growth of equity when there were many rights which could not be enforced due to the formalities of the common law courts. In such cases equity would grant specific performance, an injunction and the like and state in giving its decree that where there is a right there is a ramedy." An example of this maxim is found in the doctrine of equitable conversion whereby real property which has been directed to be turned into personalty and personalty which has been directed to be turned into realty are treated for certain purposes as if the dirocted conversion had already taken place. This maxim is therefore the basis of implied trusts. Where a trustee commingles trust funds with his own private bank account and later checks out money for his private use, equity prosumes he intended to draw upon his own money and not upon the trust money

EQUITY DELIGHTS TO DO JUSTICE AND NOT BY HALVES. This means that equitable jurisdiction once having attached it will be continued for the final adjudication of all rights involved and thus prevent a multiplicity of suits and avoid further future litigation even though this involves granting relief usually classified as legal. This maxim 16 often recited as "Equity delights in doing full justice." 

A 18 injuring Y by a continuing tort. Y may rightfully enjoin A in equity and at the same time enforce his legal right to recover damages from the tortfeasor. Of course if Y wanted nothing but money damages he would have no standing in equity. 

EQUITY PREVENTS A MULTIPLICITY OF SUITS. Partly due to the jury system, a common law court could only deal with a bilateral case; equity however not having à jury system could deal with a multilateral case thus preventing unnocessary litigation. An example of this is found in the relief afforded by an accounting between many members of a firm and the use of a bill of interpleader. 

EQUITY IS EQUALITY. This means that unless there is a specific requiremont to the contrary, equity will treat all members of a class equally and on an equal basis and will distribute benefits or impose burdens either equally or in proportion to the several interests. An exemplo of this may be found in the distribution of insolvent estates, abatement and ademption of legacies and the marshalling of assets. This maxim is the basis for distribution of assets in bankruptcy. The maxim 18 often applied in cases where a wife seeks alimony. An actual

The wife having convincod the judge of her right to alimony was given $1,000 a year, the judge remarking that now each spouse would have $3,000 with which to enjoy the more abundant life. The most famous example of the application of the maxim that equity is equality (or equality 18 equity) is found in the old Testament where both the true mother and a pseudomother claimed custody of a baby and King Solomon applying the doctrine that equity is equality ordered that the baby be divided in two equal parts and each woman to receive one half in the absence of evidence as to the true mother. It is doubtful whether any court in the District of Columbia would go that far today.

HE WHO SEEKS EQUITY MUST DO EQUITY. The common law emphasized remedies wheroas equity emphasized duties and applied this rule to plaintiffs as well as to defendants. Equity being a court of conscience and able to render a conditional decree insists that if a party either plaintiff or defendant wants the aid of the court of equity he himself must do what justice, good conscience and equity demand in the particular case. If equity is asked to rescind a contract it will require as a condition to the granting of relief that the plaintiff restore to the defendant any benefit he may have received from the transaction. EQUITY ADDS THE VIGILANT. This means that equity will not aid one who sleeps on his rights or lets grass grow under his feet. There is no hard and fast rule as to what constitutes laches but each case must be determined individually. In some cases it has been held that a: shorter time than the analogous statutory period may bar the plaintiff in equity while other cases have given him a longer time within which to come into equity for relief especially where there has been a concealment of the cause of action on the part of the defendent or where the latter has actively lulled a plaintiff into repose with the intention of permitting the statuory period to elapse.

LEADING EQUITABLE REMEDIES Specific Performance of Contracts 

Specific performance 18 the remedy by which a court of equity compels the parties to a contract to do the very things which the contract calls for. 

If X contracts that he will lease certain premises to Y and refuses to do so, equity will compel him to execute and deliver the lease as he promised. Equity may also award the injured party money damages at the same time as equity will not do justice by halves. On the other hand the injured party may recover at law in which event he could get nothing but money damages. 

The history of equity shows that it came into being not to displace but to supplement the law. It is a basic principle of equity jurisdiction, therefore, that a court of equity will not as a general rule afford a party its peculiar relief where he has a plain, complete and adequate remedy at law. 

The exception is in cases where equity has and exerciso exclusive jurisdiction over matters not recognized by courts of law as in the case with trusts. 

Supposo a contract for the sale of 100 bottles of wine at $1. a bottle. When the time comos for delivery the market price of the wine 18 $1.20 a bottle and tho vendor refuses

to go on with the contract. The damages recoverable in a court of law are measured by the difference between the contract price and the market price at the time and place of delivery. Adding these damages, which amount to $20, to the contract price of $100, the buyer can obtain the same wine in the open market as that which he was supposed to get from the vendor. Equity could do no better by ordering the seller to deliver the 100 bottles. The vendees remedy therefore is adequate at law and for this reason he has no grounds for coming into equity. This rule applies generally to ordinary chattels and ordinary things. But it does not apply to extraordinary or unique chattels and things. If the 100 bottles of wine. in the case just mentioned were unique or extraordinary so as to not be obtainable, then on this ground of uniqueness equity would consider that the remedy at law was inadequate because money damages could not procure what the vendee had bargained for. In such case equity would decree specific performance and order the vendor to come across with the wine.

Land and any interest in land are always unique because equity considers there are no two pieces of land exactly alike. They differ invariably if only in location. Therefore if a seller refuses to perform his contract to convey or lease land equity may decree specific performance because money damages will not enable the vendee to buy another parcel of land which is the exact equivalent of what he ought to have obtained. Therefore as to land contracts it is a rule that damages aro always an inadequate remedy. 

It has been said that equity will decree specific performance for the sale of extraordinary or unique chattels, Examples of this are: shares of stock in a "close" corporation whose shares are seldom for sale on the stock market; an antique Egyptian vase; any article of such peculiar value to the individual plaintiff that no damages which a jury might assess could compensate him for not. having it; articles of sentimental value; articles one of a kind; autographs;; a well known race horse; a soup bowl used by Julius Caesar. Before the Civil War household slaves were deemed to have a special value to the master resulting from their personal traits over and above their market value as laborers or mere chattels and in sales pertaining to slaves specific performance was often granted. 

Contracts for personal services are generally not specifically enforced. This is based on roasons of public policy and the inefficiency of compulsory service particularly in contracts involving innumerable details, such as erecting an office building or building a railroad, or calling for a great many acts running over long periods of time. In the latter instances to specifically enforce such contracts would unduly take up the time and attention of the court. Besides, a court of equity has no procedure or machinery for effectively supervising such performances. Contracts for personal service, for building or construction and for continuous acts are consoquently rarely specifically enforced. In a few exceptional cases equity will wrestle with the inconvenience of enforcement in order to forestall rank injustice. The Pennsylvania Railroad buys a right of way across 

X's plantation and as a part of the consideration promises to build a plantation crossing through its embankment, without which the plaintiff would have to take a long roundabout route to reach his fields from his house and barn. The land was acquired from X as part of the contract sought to be enforced. The construction called for is on the carrier's land and not on the plaintiff's. The inconvenience resulting to X from the breach of the contract is in its nature extremely difficult to estimate in money damages. In such case the construction work is simple and it will not be difficult for the court to determine whether its decree is complied with. Contracts for personal services often contain a stipulation that one of the parties will not render services to any other person for the duration of the contract. Suppose Rudolfo, an opera singer, agrees to sing for the Metropolitan Opera Corporation during the theatrical season and not to sing for any other opera company. Rudolfo breaks his contract and begins to sing for the Alexandria 

Opera Company, a rival outfit. Because this contract involves personal services equity will not decreo specific performance by ordering Rudolfo to sing for the Metropolitan for the reason that equity could not efficiently execute such a docroe. He might be ordered to sing and instead of singing he might squawk which would result in ridicule and mockery of the chancellor's decree. But equity may give relief to the Metropolitan Opera by soizing on the negative stipulation of the contract and by injunction order Rudolfo not to sing for anyone else except the Metropolitan. This is known as a negative injunction as compared to a mandatory injunction which would compel affirmative performance. A negative injunction is not objectionable on the ground of difficulty of enforcement. The court would merely accomplish indirectly what could not be accomplished directly. Nevertheless if Rudolfo had any other talents equally as lucrative as singing he could defeat the negative injunction by playing the violin in concert for someone else becauso he only contracted that he would not sing for anyone elso during the season. On the other hand if he had contracted not to render any services whatever to anyone else the negative injunction would prevent his earning a livelihood and he could only defeat the decree by remaining idle and living on his income if he had any. 

Lumley v. Wagner. On the other hand a negative injunction will be denied even if a case like that of the opera singor just mentioned where the plaintiff has a plain, complote and adequate remedy at law. Such would be true where the singing of 

Rudolfo was in no way unique and the opera company could have found a ready substitute for him for the remaindor of the duration of the contract. In that event the opera company could only maintain a suit for money damages at law. A negative injunction will issue in cases involving personal services only where the services are shown to be extraordinary or unique. 58 Conn. 356.

Injunction against a breach of a negative contract is not confined to personal service contracts but is applicable also to other types of negative contracts. x sells his choose business or his medical practice or his butcher shop to Y and agrees not to engage in a similar business or profession within a certain area. The damages which will result from a breach of such a negative contract would be very difficult to ostimate. Equity therefore in such instances will enjoin the broach of such a promise unless to do so would be unconscionable, of no consequence to the plaintiff or amounts to an unreasonable restraint of trade. 106 N. The remedy of specific performance is not a right that can be demanded but its granting lies in the sound discretion of the chancellor. Equity will not always decree specific porformance even where land is involved or chattels or services are unique. Applying the rule of balancing equities it may rightfully refuse specific performance and leave a complainant to his remedy at law for money damages where it feels that to decree specific performance would in any particular case do moro harm than good.

Where equity does decree specific performance it sets higher ethical standards than does a court of law and demands that the conduct of the parties to the contract comply with such standards.

Before equity will decreo specific performance of a contract it must be proven that such contract is first of all enforceable at law. Equity follows the law in demanding that such a contract be based on a valuable consideration. But equity will not follow the law where a promise even though under seal was made for no consideration and will deny specific performance on the ground that equity looks to the substance of a transaction and will not be fooled by its form. There is nothing holy about a seal in equity as it is in law. The chancellor has the right to look behind it and see what is there. While equity will look for a consideration, the amount of it in itself 18 usually unimportant. The more fact that X contracts to sell y a $5,000 antique vabe for $1,000 is immaterial. Inadequacy of consideration in itself is no defense. But this fact considered with other facts may be evidence of fraud on which ground equity may deny specific performance. 

One asking for specific performance must come into court with clean hands and must have been guilty of no misrepresentations acting as an inducement to the contract. Such misrepresentations will be a defense to a suit for specific performanco whether they were made in good faith or not. 

X agrees to sell to Y a piece of land. Y knows that the land contains valuable deposits of radium but X 18 ignorant thereof. The land was sold as farm land. It has been held that the vendee must disclose his knowledge of these facts to the vendor otherwise he will lose his right to the remedy of specific performance though he is still entitled at law to recover damages for the refusal of the vendor to convey the land to him under the contract. This case illustrates the discretionary nature of the remedy of specific performance and also the principle that he who comes into law does not have to come in with clean hands. 

85 Ala. 256. It still remains true however that the mere failure, without other intervening circumstances, to disclose such secret information is not a ground for sotting aside a contract or deed executed in consummation thereof. But the concealment of the vendee in such cases as these will ordinarily preclude his right to specific performance.

Mutual mistake means a mistake as to the same thing shared in by both perties to the contract and is often a good reason for setting the contract aside in equity. Mutual mistake 18 also a good defonse to specific performance. A unilateral mistake is a mistake by one party, the other one believing the contract 

18 what it appears to be. A unilateral mistake does not invalidate a contract oither in law or in equity. But equity acting within its sound discretion will often permit a unilateral mistake on the part of the defendant to be set up as a defense against the granting of specific performance. Where a defendant walked into the middle of an auction sale of a piece of real estate and hurriedly put in a bid without realizing what land was sold or informing himself whether there were any mortgages on it or not and unwittingly purchased an entirely different lot fror what he thought he was buying, specific porformance was refused on the ground not only of unilateral mistake on the part of the defendant but also on the ground of hardship. The buyer however would have no defense to an action at law.

In order to obtain specific performance there must be a mutuality of equitable remedies. It has been incorrectly stated in many books and in dicta that the contract "from the time it is made must be mutual in obligation and in remedy." An examination of actual cases serves to prove that the decisions of all courts require only a mutuality of oquitable remedies and that is sufficient. 

A agrees to deed Blackacre to B and B agrees in the contract to pay for it, after conveyance, by remodelling A's house. A defaults. B petitions for specific performance offering to do his side of the bargain and remodel. Specific performance will be denied because the court cannot compel B to do the remodelling; and because it cannot give to both parties the remedy in equity, it prefers to give it to noither. 82 Cal. 46. This is the rule that specific performance will not be granted where there is no mutuality of equitable remedies. Suppose A and B enter into a contract in writing within the statute of frauds but that it is signed only by A. At law B may sue A but A may not sue B because the lattor did not sign. Equity may decree specific performance in such a case on the ground that while there is not mutuality of legal remedies thore is nevertheless mutuality of equitable remedies. B, the plaintiff, can be compelled by the equity court to do his part in order to obtain specific performance and it is immaterial that A could not have brought an independent suit against him. 

206 Ill. 159.

Go back to the case between A and B where B promised to remodel A's house and A promised to deed land to B. If B had already remodelled A's house then he would be entitled to specific performance against A even though it is true that before B performed a suit against him in equity by A could not have been maintained. 

179 Mags. 30.

A for a consideration of $10 gives B an option to buy a parcel of realty within three months for $5,000. Within the three months B notifies A of his intention to buy and offers the price but A refuses. The fact that there was nothing on which A could sue B before B exercised the option is immaterial because as soon as B exercised his option A ought to have performed and on the doctrine that equity regards that as done which ought to have been done there is mutuality of equitable remedies. 57 N, J, Eq. 257.

Equity will never decree specific performance of a contract to convey land where the title 18 doubtful, even though the court itself may be of the private opinion that the title is good. 42 Minn. 443. The vondor should first establish his title by a suit against adverse claimants or remove clouds on the title and not cast this burden on the vendee who did not bargain for land with a headache but only for land.

Generally, time is not of the essence and therefore a party to a contract who fails to perform his part on the agreed date does not lose all his rights in equity under the contract. A agrees to pay $5,000 and receive a deed to real estate on March 17 but is unable to procure the money and pay it over until April 17, or A is ready to pay the money on March 17 but X the vendor is not prepared to deliver a good title until April 17. The rule is that since time is not of the essence of the contract in equity the party in default does not forfeit his right to relief in equity. The provision as to the time of performance is not an essential element of the contract. This reasoning is based on the doctrine that equity abhors penalties and forfeitures and will consider that whatever loss is Buffered by the delay may be compensated in damages. Where one who is supposed to pay money is in default he may be compelled to pay interest on the purchase money as a condition of relief. This does not mean however that one may wait indefinitely and still be entitled to oquitablo relief. If he waits too long as for several years and particularly where he waits so long as to prejudice the other party, he may be adjudged guilty of laches. In ascertaining what constitutes lachos equity does not follow the law necessarily and is guided by no fixed standard like the statute of limitations; it will decide what is laches (unreasonable delay) according to the circumstancos of each case.

The general rule in equity that time is not of the escence does not govern overy contract but time may be of the essence where the parties so intended. 

By express agreement of the parties time may be made the essence of a contract; in such case the party in default will be denied equitable relief on the ground that "he missed the bus."

Sometimes the nature of a contract or of the subject-matter itself may indicate that time is of the essence as in cases involving sales of stocks on the New York Stock Exchange or in the sale of any other commodity whose price fluctuates rapidly. It has been held that contracts for options must be complied with within the time limited and thorofore in such contracts time is essential. To exercise an option even one day after the time limit is too late. 

In some cases circumstances may show that the parties intended the contract to be complied with by a certain date or not at all. If X contracts to sell a house to Y and give him title on July 14 kmowing that Y's lease of his present residence expires on July 14 and that Y will then require the house for his immediate occupancy as a residence then the inability of A to make out a good title on July 14 is not cured by his ability to do so two weeks later. Such a set of circumstances Iriply time to be of the essence, Where time was not originally an essential element of the contract, yet it may be made essential by means of notice served upon the party in default. 

Where a vendor 18 weary of waiting for his price he may serve notice upon the vendee specifying a reasonable time in which the price must be paid and if the Vendee delays beyond this period so specified he loses his rights as completely as if time had bean of the essence in the first place. X bought of Y a bulldog for $1.98 and said he would come in soon, pay the purchase price and take away the dog. In such a contract time is not of the essence. After 13 weeks, X being in default, Y notifies him that wiless he comes in within 3 days and pays and takes away the dog then the sale is off. From this point on, time is of the essence. Unless X complies within the specified time he will be guilty of laches and will receive no aid from equity even though the dog is unique.

One of the most usual doctrines found in American Jurisprudence is the doctrine of part performance of parol contracts which ought to have been in writing under the statute of frauds. It is a principle of equity that the statute of frauds, being designed to suppress perjuries and frauds, shall not itself be made the instrument of perpetrating a fraud. The statute of frauds 18 a shield and not a sword. This thought is the basis of the doctrine of part performance. The doctrine is that oral land contracts may be specifically enforced notwithstanding the requirement of the statute of frauds that there be a written memorandum, provided that after the contract has been made certain steps have been taken toward putting such oral contracts into effect. The Supreme Court of the United States has held in a case arising in the District of Columbia that where a party seeks specific performance of a parol contract for the exchange of lands he must show that the consideration has been paid or tendered and that thero has been such a part performance of the contract that its rescission would be fraud which cannot be compensated by any damages. 71 V. S. 513. In 1921 the 

District Court of Appeals held that the statute of frauds is as binding in equity as in law except that equity will protect one who has gone into pos8ession on the strength of an oral agreement and has made valuable improvements, performed services or advanced money in accordance with the terms of the contract. But an oral contract for the sale of lands will not be specifically enforced where the purchaser had gone into possession and had made only small payments in a desultory way and not the monthly payments which he agreed to make and insufficient to equal the rental value of the premises. 50 App. D. C. 194.

Where a contract for the exchange of real estate has been fully performed by ono party and partly performed by the other, equity will decree specific performance without reference to the sufficiency of the memorandum under the statute of frauds. 108 v. S. 10. Part performance of an oral contract to sell land must bo such as 18 referrable to the contract as alleged and consistent with it. 

50 App. D. C. 194. A parol agreement to convey realty may be specifically en. forcod whero the promisee, with the knowledge of the promisor, has changed his position in reliance thereon. 55 App. D. C. 330.

Suppose one party to the oral contract relying on the good faith of the other party is permitted or encouraged by that other to do acts in pursuance of the contract whereby his position is irrevocably changed, namely acts which cannot be undone. It has been held that merely paying part or all of the purchase price does not constitute part performance. Payment of money is not necessarily circumstantial evidence of a contract for the sale of land. The payment may have been for some other purpose. In itself, payment would not point to a sale. 

Supposo however that the vendee in pursuance of the oral contract goes into possession of the land with the consent of the vendor. If his taking possession includes removing his business or his residence, it certainly involves an outlay of time and money which will be a doad loss to him if he is ejected because of no written memorandum of the sale. These outlays of money and expenditures of tine are irrevocable and cannot be undone. Furthermore the fact that the vendee is in possession by consent of the vendor is a convincing and striking piece of circunstantial evidence that some contract is in oxistence between the two partios relating to the land. But the mere possession does not establish a sale of the land because possession is just as consistent with a lease as it would be with a grant. Nor will payment and possession together establish part performance so as to entitle the vendee to take the case out from under the statute and obtain a specific performance. By the great weight of American authorities some other act must be done in addition to payment and the taking of possession to point to a sale and not a lease of the land. This other act has been held to consist of making valuable and permanent improvements on the land in addition to the taking of possession. Therefore it may be stated to be the rule that the doctrine of part performance requires the taking of possession by the consent of the vendor and the making of permanent and valuable improvemonts on the land, payment being considered of not much importance. 

36 Cyc. 356. It 18 possible to reconcile all the American decisions by stating that possession with part payment, or, as in the District of Columbia, possession with permanent and valuable immrovements will suffice.

INJUNCTIONS 

Chancery has jurisdiction over torts chiefly by way of the remedy of injunction. The injunction commands the defendant not to commit the threatened tort under penalty of punishment for contempt of court. This is the ordinary preventive injunction. To a limited extent equity may order the tortfeasor to restore things to tho condition in which they were before the wrong was committed, This is called a mandatory injunction. An injunction that issues at the beginning or during the progress of the suit is called variously a temporary, preliminary or interlocutory injunction; an injunction issued upon final hearing is called a final or permanent injunction. An injunction issued temporarily pending litigation and lasting only until the final hearing is also known as an injunction pendente lite (pending litigation).

Injunctions are not granted except upon à hearing participated in by both parties. But in certain extreme circumstances where a plaintiff would suffer irreparable harm and injury in waiting for a bilateral hearing to come up equity may grant the extraordinary relief of a temporary restraining order merely upon the application of the plaintiff in a unilateral proceeding. Such an order must be within a specified time followed up by a petition for a temporary injunction at which time the defendant will have his day in court. 

Equity will never enjoin a threatened crime as such.

In its technical sense the term "waste" means that the one doing or permitting the wrongful act is rightfully in possession of the premises such as a tenant for years or for life; but where the wrongful act is done by one not in rightful possession the tort is then a trespass. Equity will enjoin nearly all acts of waste upon which an action at law may be maintained. It has enjoined cutting timber, quarrying or mining, changing, destroying or removing buildings or bridgos or other structures, changing the course of a stream, tearing down buildings for firewood and the liko. A mortgagee or other lien holder may enjoin the mortgagor or other person in possession from waste on the ground that he is thereby impairing the sufficiency of the security. A mortgages land to B to secure a $5,000 loan, A romaining in possession. B may enjoin A from waste on the ground that the value of his security is being impaired and if the wrongful act continues without abatement the property will not yield sufficient to satisfy the debt at a foreclosure sale. A to B for life, remainder to X and his heirs. X, the remaindorman, may enjoin B, the life tenant, from acts of waste. One tonant in common may enjoin another tenant in common from waste. 

A complainant has no right to sue in equity merely to obtain an accounting for waste or merely money damages for waste that has already been committed because his action at law for damages is adequate. But if he sues for and obtains an injunction against future waste equity will at the same time do complete justice and give him an accounting for waste that has already been committed.

A few hundred years ago in order to relieve a life tenant or one holding an estate less than a fee from the weighty responsibility for technical waste it became cormon to insert in family settlements the clause "without irmpeachment of waste." The common law courts construed these words as giving the tenant the right to commit any destruction upon the prem1 808 with impunity. 

Under such ruling he could denude the land of all its timber, permit the premises to degenerate into utter disrepair or tear down the mansion home without accountability whatever and even be guilty of such conduct purely out of malice toward his successor in the estate. It was hero that equity stepped in and invented its doctrine of "equitable waste." The doctrine of equitable waste applies where waste is cormitted by one "without impeachment of waste" and who 18 thorefore irresponsible at law; he will be onjoined in chancery. Equity will not permit an unconscientious use to be made of a legal right. 

It is only about a century ago that equity began to enjoin trespass to real property. Equity hesitated to afford relief in such cases because it folt that the plaintiff had a plain, complete and adequate remedy at law in the form of ejectment or trespass. Even today where trespasses to land are few or temporary or cause no serious injury equity will not enjoin but will leave the injured party to his legal remedy. X was allowed a given time to remove a pile of rocks belonging to him from Y's land but failed to do so. Equity refused to

enjoin X from trespassing on Y's land a few times after the time limit bad expired for the purpose of removing his rocke. Where however the injury to the plaintiff could be shown to be irreparable (incapable of repai:) an injunction will lie. Most cases hold that removing or dostroying the substance of the estate of the plaintiff 18 of itself an irreparable injury even though the damage to the plaintiff is not in a large amount. Irreparable injury has thus been held to include removing buildings, cutting timber, and taking ore or stone or oil or minerals out of the ground. It was held an irreparable injury where the defendant was about to cut a tunnel under the plaintiff's land way beneath the surface although no injury to the surface was threatened on the ground that although the tunneling would have a harmless effect it nevertheless involved the removal of a part of the substance of the land belonging to the plaintiff. 

64 Cal. 62.

Equity will not permit a defendant to appropriate permanently land to himself without the assent of the plaintiff who owns it even though the land itself be of little value to the plaintiff. He has no right to appropriate land belonging to another on the ground that such other person has no use for it or that it is of very little value and then offer to pay him what it is worth. 

To permit him to do so would be to ask the aid of equity to compol one to sell land that one might wish to keep. In an old English case a gas company laid pipes through the land of the plaintiff. The court enjoined the act although no harm was shown to result to the plaintiff. It was held that the true value of land to the plaintiff lay in the fact that his ownership enabled him to exact a price from the defendant for its use.

Whore a defendent is committing a continuing trespass or numerous trespasses as for example tearing down the plaintiff's fence or crossing his land and threatens to continue such trespasses indefinitely equity will enjoin such a continuing tort even though each trespass is trifling per se. Equity will give its aid here because it will thereby act to prevent a multiplicity of suits at law for damages that would otherwise necessarily be filed.

A college in the District of Columbia unlawfully granting degrees may be enjoined by the district attorney, in the name of the United States.

An injunction against negotiating a promissory note is not violated by suing thereon at law and proceeding to judgment and execution. 7 D. C. 247. (1848). 

Cancellation, Reformation and Rescission 

Equity will give relief by ordering an invalid written instrument to be delivered up to be destroyed or cancelled. As additional or alternative reliof, the holder of the writing may be enjoined from assigning or bringing suit upon it. The question in such cases is whether or not the plaintiff has a plain, complete and adequate remedy at law. Suppose X obtains a non-negotiable instrument from Y by fraud. Y will not be given relief in equity because his equitable defense is good at law whether or not the instrument be assigned. 

But if the instrument were negotiable then Y could obtain relief in equity by enjoining its negotiation and ordering it to be cancelled on the ground that he has no adequate remedy at law, because, should the instrument reach the hands of a holder in due course, Y's equitable defenses would thereby be cut off. 

Suppose an ordinary writton contract is subject to the defense of fraud, not apparent on its faco. Bobtains a policy of insurance from the 

A insurance company by material false representations, The ability of the insurance company to prove the fraud depends upon the oral testimony of witnesses who may be dead or scattered all over the world when B seos fit to bring suit upon the policy. Unless the company by a suit to cancel the instrument can litigate its validity now, it may at some future time be subjected to an unjust recovery on account of the inability to procure witnesses or to procure witnesses who remember enough to testify. There is a conflict of opinion in this country whether this is a sufficient ground for cancellation of the contract. 

126 Mass. 381. In the absence of any statutes providing for the perpetuation of the testimony of witnesses to be used on a future trial, it would seem that the relief shouid be granted in a bill for cancellation, otherwiso not. 79 N. Y. 

202. Rule 27 of the Rules of Civil Procedure for the District Courts of the 

United States provides that a person may perpetuate his own testimony or that of another person regarding "any mattor that may be cognizable in any court of the United States ***." By this mile such testimony 18 only entitled to be used in a court of the United States. 

This rule, however, does not limit the power of a court to entertain an action to perpetuate testimony. 

Reformation is an equitable remedy by which a written agreement is corrected so as to truly set forth what the parties Interded. A contracts to convey Whiteacre to B. By a mutual mistake unnoticed by ei ther party the land is erroneously described in the deed so that Blackacre is included. At law the legal title to Blackacre will pass to the grantee on delivery of the deed. 

There is a mutual mistake of fact. Neither party intended Blackacre to be included. As the matter stands, B holds the legal title to Blackacre under

the deed but it is impressed with an equity in favor of A. A mere correction of the instrument would not suffice to protect A. Equity will not only reform the deed but will order B to reconvey Blackacre to A. 

Suppose B in the above case having contracted for Whiteacre notices before he accepted the conveyance that Blackacre was included in the deed. This is a case of a mistake of one party but known to the other party. A was mistaken as to the matter of fact but B was not. In accepting the deed Bacted fraudulontly. Under such circumstances where there is a unilateral mistake of fact as to one party, known to the other, whereby the instrument does not express the agreement of the parties equity will also grant reformation and a reconveyance. 115 Ind. 312.

In granting reformation, equity cannot change the contract of the parties nor make one for them. A intends to offer Whitoacre to B. B.honestly belioves that Blueacre is included in the offer and on that assumption fixes the price.

There is no true contract here because there is no meeting of minds on the same subject matter. B draws the deed, including Blueacre, and A signs and delivors the instrument without noticing that Blueacre was included. This is really a double mistake; A was mistaken as to the contents of the instrument and B was mistaken as to the subject matter of the contract. But this is not a mutual mistake because they were not both mistaken as to the same thing. Such a case may call for rescission but not for reformation because there is no agreement to reform. 150 Mass. 27.

A sold B a piece of land located on the Potomac River. Both parties were ignorant that the land had been swept away by a flood. B delivers to A his bond for the purchase price.

A bond is an instrument under seal and is enforceable without consideration. For this reason, B would have no defense to an action at law by A on the bond. Equity will give B relief on the ground of a mutual mistake as to the existence of the subject matter of the contract. 

The contract will be rescinded, the bond cancelled and any payment to A by B ordered returned.

Where X had a claim against the Government of China and contracted to pay Y, a lawyer, a percentage of the amount to be recovered in consideration of the lawyer's services in prosecuting the claim and it turned out that both parties were ignorant that the claim had already been allowed it was held that the contract may be rescinded in equity. ll Pet. 63.

The difference between reformation and rescission is well-defined. 

The reformation of a contract involves an effort to enforce it as reformed whereas rescission involves an effort to abandon and to recede from a contract which the other party had not intended to make. 141 S. W. 941. A mistake which justifies reformation is ono that occurs not in the bargain itself, but subsequent to the bargain; it is a mistake which occurs in the reducing to writing the contract of the parties. 60 V. of Pa, Law Rev. 589. The object of the remedy of roscission 18 to have an instrument cancelled. Reformation is an affirmance of the bargain as it was actually made but rescission is a disaffirmance of the bargain itself. In order that reformation may be given there must have been a previous agreement which the court may use as a standard for the correction of the erroneous instrument. In order that there be rescission such previous agreement is not only unnecessary but its existence would ordinarily

prevent rescission. Rescission is the antithesis of reformation. A mistake which is ground for reformation will not justify rescission in any ordinary case while a mistake which is ground for rescission will not justify reformation since it strikes at the bargain which must serve as the standard for reformation. 

The effect of reformation is to rectify the transaction while rescission cuts off or removes the attacked transaction leaving the parties in the same position as they were before the transaction took place. Reformation is given only where the mistake is in the expression or in the performance of the real transaction; rescission lies where the mistake is other than in the expression or performance. Cancellation, rescission, surrender up, and the discharge of instruments are one and the same remedy. 

There can be no rescission where there is a mutual mistake as to the value of a thing sold. If the rule were otherwise there would be no security in the business world because few transactions would stand. X sold Y's promissory note to Z. Both buyer and seller were unaware at the time that Z was in bankruptcy and could not pay his debts in full. As the mutual mistake concerned meroly the value of the thing sold the buyer has no right to rescission; but it would have been otherwise if there was a mutual mistake as to the identity or existence of the article sold. 147 Mass. 335.

Generally rescission or reformation will not lie where there is a unilateral mistake. A offers to sell B for $5,000 a piece of land so described by metes and bounds as to include Lot 1 and Lot 2. A intends to sell only Lot 1 and believed that the description accurately expressed such intent. B in ignorance of A's mistake accepts tho offer in good faith. A is not entitled to any relief in equity on the ground of rescission or reformation. For equity to give A such relief would be to foist upon B a contract that he never made. On the other hand, B is entitled to specific performance, except in rare instances, however, where the hardship on A would shock the conscience of the court. 

X offers in writing to rent certain premises to B for $30 a week. 

Actually X intends his offer to be $50 and the lower figure was inadvertently written, B accepts the offer knowing it was made by mistake. X cannot have the contract reformed and compel B to take the premises at $50 because B never consented to pay $50 and equity has no jurisdiction to make a contract for the parties. On the other hand B will not be entitled to specific performance because of his knowledge of X's mistake. There is high authority on the other hand for holding that X may have the contract rescinded on account of B's fraud in knowingly accepting an offer made by the other party in mistake. 

A mistake on the part of the donor in making a gift is governed by rules different from those pertaining to mistake in contracts. 

IL х intends to make a gift of Lot i to Y and by mistake includes Lot 2 in the conveyance or if he intends to make a gift of a life estate to Y and by mistake conveys a fee it will be held that Y has no right to retain in equity what x did not intend to give him because Y is a mere volunteer and gave no consideration. The fact that Y expected to receive more than X intended to give him does not entitle him to take advantage of X's mistake. 

X is entitled to have the instrument reformed so as to have it correspond with his intention or he is entitled to a mandatory injunction compelling Y to reconvey the excess over what was intended.

Suppose however that X intends to convey Lot 1 and Lot 2 to Y and by mistake conveys to him only Lot I or X intending to convey a fee to Y by mistake conveys only a life interest. In such cases the mistake of the donor operated to the donor's profit and not to the donee's. Y cannot compel X to convey in accordance with his original intention because equity will not aid a mere volunteer any more than it would grant him specific performance. A different situation arises where the donor is dead and the donee brings an action against the donor's heirs. Both Y and the heirs are equally volunteers. It has been held that if Y can prove that the gift failed to carry out X's intention and there is no reason to suppose that X had changed his intention up to the time of his death, so that if Y had requested X during X's lifetime the mistake would have been corrected, then Y's claim to reformation would appear to be stronger than the claim of the heirs to resist it. I. R. Ir. 1 Ch. D. 435. 

It is a well established mile that a mistake of law cannot be relieved against but to this rule there are exceptions that cover as much territory as the rule itself. It is an ancient maxim of the common law that ignorance of the law is no excuse. This rule is based on public policy and the necessity of certainty as to the binding force of contracts and of the finality in litigation. If a party to a contract or litigation were permitted to avoid his contract or set aside a judgment because of his or of his attorney's ignorance of the law then the law itself would result in uncertainty and confusion. 

Accordingly it is well settled that a mistake of one or both parties as to the legal effect of a contract will not in itself be a ground for rescission. 

Nor will it be a ground for reformation because equity has no jurisdiction to make contracts for the parties. 8 Wheat, 174.

In a leading case, the parties supposed that the words "except the widow's right of dower" would reserve also the reversionary interest of the heirs. This constituted a mistake of law as to both parties but the court granted reformation and decreed a reconveyance. 13 Gray 373. So numerous are the decisions giving reformation for mistakes of law that one is not surprised to find it judicially declared that "the rule has no application to mistakes in the language of a contract or in the choice of the form of an instrument whereby it has an effect different from the intentions of the parties." 55 Ta. 

484; 24 Harv. Law Rev. 394. Where parties have made a contract and in reducing it to writing have employed language the legal significance of which does not expross their intentions, the contract may be reformed by substituting the proper phraseology. The rule today is that reformation is appropriate when by mistake eithor of law or fact the instrumont doos not express the contract the parties intended.

Where the mistake of law of one party is known to and taken advantage of by the other or if one party's mistake of law is accompanied by misrepresentations, undue influence, breach of confidence or the inequitable conduct on the part of the other then the transaction resulting from the mistake may be rescinded because of such other's inequitable behavior. 

141 N. Y. 35.

One may be perfectly aware of the legal effect of the contract which he made with another and yet his contract may be the result of a mistake as to his existing rights. X may contract to pay rent for Yellowacre whereas if he had correctly interpreted, according to law, the instruments which form the chain of title to that piece of land he would have learned that he himself was the owner thereof. By the weight of authority X is entitled to oquitable relief.

It has been held that the question of ownership is a complex matter and that a mistake as to ownership, even though based upon a mistake of law, is essentially a mistake of fact as to who is or is not the owner. Cooper v. Phibbs, L. R. 

2 H. L. 149.

Where X and Y have equal opportunities of ascertaining their rights and enter into a contract intended as a compromise of their rights, such compromise will not be set aside because of mistake. The court holds that each party deliberately takes a chance that he may be mistaken and that this element of chance entered into his calculations in determining the terms of the compromise. Thus, a compromise agreement cannot be set aside because of mistake. 

This presupposes however that the parties dealt upon an equal basis. Any compromise brought about by concealment, breach of confidence reposed, fraud or other inequitable conduct will be set aside. 37 Minn. 522.

Suppose A intends to give B a life estate in a parcel of land with a remainder to B's children. Because of a common mistake in legal phraseology the legal effect of the instrument was to convey a fee simple to B, although the same language would express the true intentions of the donor to an ordinary layman. Thus, A to B for life, remainder to B's heirs. To the average layman this would mean a life interest to B and the remainder to Big children but (at common law) B would take an absolute fee under the rule in Shelley's Case. If the mistake by which this result is reached had been one of fact, then either A or the children of B might have the deed reformed. Here A's mistake is one of law and there appears to be no good reason in equity or good conscience or in policy why B who is a mero recipient of A's bounty should be allowed to defeat A's plain intention and profit by the mistake. The sounder rule appears to be, therefore, that a mistake of law in gift cases stands on the same ground as a mistake of fact insofar as reformation is concerned. 16 Ga. 49; 136 111.363.

Where a contract or deed as drawn is not the mistake of either party but the mistake of the scrivener either party is entitled to reformation. 

13 Gray 373.

An admitted or clearly established mistake of law in the execution of a conveyance creates a basis for the interference of equity in the exercise of its sound discretion and is to be exercised only in the most unquestionable and flagrant cases. 27 App. D. C. 276. The rule that equity will not reform an instrument on the ground of mistake where the mistake is one of law applies only where the word "law" is used in the sense of general law or law of the country; it does not apply where it is used in the sense of a private right for in such case the mistake is really one of fact. ib.

Negligence in failing to read an instrument must amount to a failure of a positive logal duty to justify withholding of equitable relief. A mortgor's failure to read an agreement as to a debt covered by a deed of trust was held not such negligence as to proclude relief. Failure to read an instrument prepared by the other party, because of confidence reposed in the latter, furnishes a very narrow ground for denying reformation. 56 App.D.C.132. 

A bill filed by the heirs of the beneficiary under a deed for its reformation so as to show the grant of a fee instead of a life estate is not barred by laches although the deed is nearly 40 years old, where the land

. was in the possession of the beneficiary until her death (five years before the filing of the bill) and the complainants did not and could not have reasonably known of the mistake until the objection was raised by an impending purchaser whereupon suit was promptly commenced. 35 App. D. C. 569.

Bills of Peace, cloud on Title and Partition 

BILIS OF PEACE. The purpose of a bill of interpleader is to prevent double vexation to one who admits liability to somebody and also to prevent unnecessary litigation by determining the rightful claimant in one suit. The purpose of a bill of peace, however, is to prevent useless litigation by settling in one equity suit a question which would be common to many actions at law or in equity through repeated actions between one plaintiff and one defendant or numerous actions between several plaintiffs and one defendant or between one plaintiff and several defendants. A person seeking interpleader is practically in the position of a defendant at law requesting relief against two or more plaintiffs but a bill of peace may be sought either by plaintiff or defendant. 

One who seeks interpleader must be a stakeholder and is ordinarily not interested in the proceedings except to be discharged by the court; on the other hand, one who seeks a bill of peace is not a stakeholder and is always interested in the outcome of the litigation either to escape liability or to fix it on the other party. Suppose that several persons, T, S, K, P and Q have all recovered judgments against B. Each levies execution on A's personal property claiming that it belongs to B. A would have a remedy at law against each of the other parties for trespass to personal property. He could recover either the chattels in specie or money damages, But A's recovery against P, for example, is of no assistance to him in his suit against all the others in actions brought severally against thom. It would be a great benefit to A and no detriment to the others if the question of A's title could be settled once and for all. At common law 

A could not accomplish this; but by filing a bill of peace, equity would enjoin the proceedings under the executions temporarily and in one proceeding would determine the question whether the property levied upon belongs to A or B. 

If it were found to belong to A the injunction against the numerous parties would be made permanent. If title were found in B then equity would dissolve the temporary injunction and let the executions at law proceed. 87 Ga. 303.

Plaintiff claimed a right to overflow, by means of its dam, the land belonging to numerous defendants. Each defendant threatened to sue or was suing plaintiff for damages at law. The plaintiff had the same defense to each of the suits. To save the plaintiff the vexation of setting up the same defense to numerous suits equity will take jurisdiction upon the filing of a complaint, enjoin the various actions at law and determine once for all whether or not the plaintiff's claim is valid. It has also been held that trespasses by numerous tortfeasors may be enjoined in cases where the question between the plaintiff and each trespasser is a common one although the trespass of any one defendant per se would not warrant an injunction. 56 Fed. 352.

A party cannot have a non-negotiable instrument cancelled to which he has a legal defonse but is bound to await a suit at law upon the instrument. 

To this rule there are exceptions. Suppose a private or municipal corporation has issued bonds which have come into the hands of hundreds or thousands of persons and the corporation claims as a defense that the bonds were illegally issued. Through a bill of peace, equity will permit this defense to be established in a single suit for cancellation of the bonds. 57 Fed. 42.

In an old case which arose in North Carolina before the Civil War, a suit in equity was maintained for the recovery of certain slaves in which plaintiff claimed an interest as remainderman. The slaves were in the possession of a great many parties. In order to avoid separate actions at law against each of the parties, the single bill of peace in equity was permitted to prove the plaintiff's right. 22 N. C. 31.

Equity will also take jurisdiction where numerous persons would have a cause of action at law involving the same question against a single defendant. 

The City of Chicago passed an ordinance, allegedly invalid, imposing a license fee on all vehicles including bicycles. In order to avoid prosecution each owner had to pay the fee and then bring suit to recover it back. Such suits would be innumerable. In order to avoid such an enormous mass of litigation equity enjoined the enforcement of the ordinance and decided the question of its validity in a proceeding brought by a group of owners of vehicles who acted for themselves as well as representatives of the others. 175 Ill. 445. 

A tax 18 froquently absolutely invalid and void as to every taxpayer.

Each taxpayer has his legal remedy by paying the tax under protest and then bringing suit to recover it back. To avoid a multiplicity of suits a representative taxpayer may bring an injunction suit to test the validity of the tax. 

Tho American Smoke Company issued certificates to 100 different persons.

It was B's duty to pass upon and approve the securities deposited by the said company with B for securing the payment of the certificates. B negligently and carelessly accepted securities that were almost worthless. The company became insolvent. The only question between the 100 certificate holders and B was whether the latter had been negligent. They were permitted to file a bill of peace in equity to prove the negligence of B and recover damages in that procoeding instead of bringing 100 separate actions at law. 69 N. 8. 254.

Where a number of people are injured in a railroad accident equity will not permit a bill of peace to be brought by all of them to recover damages for the mere reason that cach plaintiff's claim does not necessarily present the common question of the railroad's negligence. Against passenger X the company may have the defense that he was guilty of contributory negligence. 

Against Y the company may defend on the ground that he was an employee and the like. Moreover each suit involves a different amount of damages which must be estimatod by a jury. The rule is that jurisdiction will not be taken to avoid a multiplicity of suits where the multiplicity cannot be avoided.

EXONERATION. When the principal debtor has not met his obligation at the agreed time and when the surety or the guarantor sees that he may be called upon to answer for the obligation, the surety or the guarantor may in some instances call upon another to save him harmless. Where it is possible for a surety to require that satisfaction be secured in some way other than by recourse to himself (the surety or guarantor) the surety or the guarantor is said to have a right of exoneration, In the case of a strict suretyship the creditor is not bound to pursue the principal debtor or even resort to property in his hands as socurity but may proceed against the surety in the first instance. In some cases, however, equity will compel the creditor to resort to the principal or to his property, held as security, before resorting to the surety. This right is only given where it appears that the surety would otherwise suffor a hardship and provided the right does not cause a hardship to the creditor. X, a creditor, who did an act that a court decided as

rendering void a mortgage that he hold as security, which was valid and ample to pay the debt when taken, was compelled, upon indemnity by the gurety against the consequences of risk, delay and expense, to attempt to enforce such mortgage before resorting to the solvent suroty. 4 Johns Ch. 123. 

When the obligation is due equity may at the surety's request order the debtor to save the surety harmless by paying the debt. This right is based upon the rule that the principal must protect the surety and such right 18 available even though the surety is not being molested by the creditor and though there appears no li kolihood of the principal's not being able to pay the obligation. It has been held that it is no defense to this remedy for the principal to prove that he is not insolvent or likely to become so. 70 N. J. Eq. 3OO. 

In some cases the right is also given to a guarantor provided the debt is not only due but that the guarantor has incurred some liability. 

Co-sureties and co-guarantors as to each other are equally liable. 

Therefore the right of exoneration may be exercised in some cases against them. 

When the creditor resorts only to one surety or guarantor the latter may go into equity to compel his co-sureties or co-guarantors to pay their share, if payment by him alone would work a hardship on him. Exoneration is sometimes given when to require the one surety to pay and then to demand contribution would be too late as where a co-surety fraudulently attempts to dispose of his property to escape liability. Equity will allow a complaint for exoneration in such case. 33 Mich. 183.

CONTRIBUTION. The principle of contribution extends not only to sureties but to all partios who are jointly or jointly and severally liable on the same obligation. It is an illustration of the maxim that equality is cquity. There is no contribution among joint tortfeasors. 

Suppose that A, B and C are sureties of X to secure his debt to Y. 

Y collects the whole amount of $1500 from A. The latter has not only the right of reimbursement against X, the principal debtor, but the right to compel B and C each to contribute hís proportionate share. A may sue B and C for $500 respectively in an action at law and ordinarily such common law remedy is adequate. 

But equity will always take jurisdiction over contribution cases concurrently with the law. There is a difference between legal and equitable contribution. 

It is true that A may recover $500 from B and $500 from C at law but should C be insolvent his share would be lost to A. But if A files suit in equity for contribution and shows to the court that C 18 insolvent then equity will compel B to share the burden equally and contribute $750 to A's reimbursement instead of $500. In other words equity will compel the solvent parties to share the entire debt equally where one or more parties are insolvent. Of course the solvent parties still have a right to contribution from the insolvent parties at a later date. 95 Wib. 540.

SUBROGATION. By right of subrogation is meant that the one making payment for another takes the place of the creditor in respect to the obligation of the principal and is entitled to all rights, securities and benefits available to the creditor. X, the surety, after paying the debt is entitled to subject any stocks or bonds or other collateral placed in the hands of the creditor as security by the debtor to satisfy his claim. 

This right, however, is not available to one who is not bound to answer for the principal's debt or obligation or to one who does not have any interest

41in the subject matter. Where A voluntarily pays B's debt to C (who holds a cow as security) A is not entitled to the benefit of the cow on the grounds of subrogation on the premise that A was not bound to answer for the debt or obligation and had no interest in the subject matter. This rule would apply even where A mistakenly believed that he was a surety.

The surety cannot ask for the right of subrogation until the claim of the creditor has been paid in full. Therefore a surety who is answerable for one instalment of a debt is not entitled to subrogation until all instalmente have been paid. 17 Ta. 131. This is true also where a surety has paid only half of the debt, the remainder being unpaid. He is entitled, however, to such securities where he pays only half of the debt and the remainder is paid by the principal. In any case, the surety is permitted to enforce his right of subrogation no further than the extent of his loss. Consequently, where the surety settles the debt for less than the amount due he can be subrogated only to the extent of his loss. 41 Md. 571.

INDEMNITY. In addition to the right of subrogation the surety or guarantor has another remedy for reimbursement from the principal ard tiat is the right of indemnity. There is an implied contract between the principal and the surety or guarantor that the principal will indemnify (pay back) the latter in the event of payment to the creditor. 

It is a general principle of equity that when one person in the position of a mere surety for another, whether he became so by express contract or by implication of law, is compelled to pay the debt which the other in equity and good conscience ought to have paid, he is entitled to relief against the other who was in fact the principal debtor. A long time ago courts of law recognized this equitable romedy and ever since the Statute of Westminster II (1286) permitted relief at law by substituting for the equitable remedy of indemity on an implied contract, the action of general assumpsit upon the common (money) counts. In certain cases this legal remedy was less expensive and more speedy than would be a bill in equity for the same relief. The common count at law would be "for money paid by plaintiff to the use of the defendant." 

The action of general assumpsit at law is purely an equitable remedy taken over by the law and is based upon equitable doctrine. 

The right of indemmity arises only upon payment by the surety or the guarantor. Consequently, the statute of limitations does not commence to run against the guarantor or surety in favor of the principal until payment is mado. This is important to remember because a surety or guarantor may proceed against the principal on an implied contract of indemnity where he would be barred from subrogation. Suppose C is the guarantor of a debt owed by T to X. 

A state statute of limitations provides a bar to actions on express simple and implied contracts not brought within five years. Two years after the maturity of the debt C pays the creditor. Four years later he petitions to be subrogated. 

Tho right of subrogation is barred because the debt between the creditor and T is six yoars overdue. He can, howover, sue on the implied contract of indemmity since only four years have run against this claim which accrued not at the maturity of the debt but at the time c actually paid money to the creditor for the benefit of T. A guarantor or surety who has become such at the request of the principal has the benefit of an impliod promise of indomity; and a new and independent cause of action arises thoreon whenever he is compelled to make a payment regardless of the time of maturity of the original debt. 103 Kans. 92.

AUXILIARY JURISDICTION. Discovery lies in the auxiliary jurisdiction of equity. The remedy of discovery is becoming increasingly of less importance due to statutory changes permitting the obtaining of similar relief in actions at law.

The common law furnished no method by which a party to a suit could be examined as a witness by his adversary or compelled to produce documents in his possession at trial on demand of the other side. From the early üays of the creation of equity courts a complainant was permitted in his 111 of complaint to propound questions to the defendant in the interrogating part which the defendant was bound to answer under oath and thereby disclose facts within his knowledge material to the complainant's case. This practice was called compelling discovery. Parties to actions at law desired to enjoy this remedy in equity and thereby was created the bill for discovery in chancery. In a bill for discovery the party to an action at law merely demands that the defendant answer the interrogatories enumerated under oath. Such a complainant seeks and needs no other relief. Having obtained the answer of the defendant under oath, his

purpose in going into equity is accomplished. He may now use the answer of the defendant in evidence in the legal action as a written admission. 

Under the present system of ploading and practice in the District of Columbia a party to an action at law or in equity may examine his adversary as a witness and methods for compelling the production of books and documents for inspection are provided. Therefore a suit for discovery in aid of an action at law in the District of Columbia is practically obsolete. It would appear that it may still be used, however, upon a showing that such remedy at law is not plain, complete and adequate. 71 N. H. 332; 57 L. R. A. 949.

The auxiliary jurisdiction of equity included also three remedies for the purpose of taking the testimony of witnesses other than parties to the action at law in emergencies where the common law afforded no remedy. Equity permitted a suit to perpetuate testimony where a person had a valid and existing right but for some reason or other no action could be brought immediately to determine that right. Equity permittod such a suit to perpetuate testimony because of the hardship that would result from the loss of important evidence by the death, absconding or disappearance of witnesses and their failing memories. 

Equity permitted a suit to take testimony de bene esse where an action at law Wes pending but not yet ready for trial. This was for the purpose of examining a witness who was sick, sore, disabled or senile or about to depart from the country. Equity would also entertain a suit for the purpose of taking testimony of witnesses in a foreign country. 

These three suits are practically entirely obsolete in the District of Columbia today being supplanted by romedies provided in the New Federal Rules of Civil Procedure for the District Courts of the United States.

MORTGAGES. A mortgage is a conveyance of a fee simple on a condition subsequent. The mortgagor at common law conveyed his legal title to the mortgages on the condition that if on a day certain the mortgagor paid the mortgagee a sim of money the legal title would immediately revest in the mortgagor. By the old common law if the mortgagor permitted the pay day to pass without paying the

amount due then the mortgagee's estate became absolute and the mortgagor's right to get his land back was forfeited forever. To relieve against such a severe rule, equity invented the equity of redemption. This gave the mortgagor the right to pay the amount due within a reasonable time after the agreed pay day and on such payment to compel B (mortgagee) by a suit in equity to reconvey the property to A (mortgagor). This right was regarded as creating in A an equitable estate in the land which was called the equity of redemption and which upon the death of A would pass to his heirs as an incorporeal hereditament, Equity, however, respected B's rights to a certain extent and permitted him to take possession of the land and to receive the rents and profits upon the execution of the mortgage unless the right to possession was reserved in the deed to A, or at any other time before redemption by payment of the amount due. B, as the party entitled to possession, was the only one who could recover the land from a third person. Equity, however, considered A's right as the substantial interest in the property and looked upon B merely as holding his legal title as security for the debt. Equity therefore compelled B when he had possession of the property to account to A for the rents and profits and to apply them toward payment of the principal and interest of the mortgage debt. This gave B the right to gradually liquidate the debt by going into possession whenever he pleased. B was entitled also after the due date of the debt to bring a bill for foreclosure of the equity of redemption. In such a suit the court would ascertain what was due B and permit the mortgagor to make payment within a reasonable time. The decree recited that in default of such payment within the time limited A's equity of redemption should be cut off. The practical effect of this old method of foreclosure was to remove the oquity of redemption as a cloud from the legal estate of the mortgagee. 

We have just discussed the English common law and equity theories of mortgages. There are two other theories in the United States known as the title theory and the lien theory. The District of Columbia Code adopts the lien theory.

The ingredients which make up the false representations which will be a ground for rescission or cancellation in equity or a defense to specific performance are practically the same as at common law. Thore is, however, one exception. At common law to constitute an action of fraud or deceit the false representation must be a statement of a past or present material fact; not merely the expression of an opinion; made with the expectation or intention that it be acted upon by the one to whom it is made; innocently and reasonably relied upon by the other party and not upon his own information; and there must be damage to the plaintiff. PENALTIES AND FORFEITURES. Based on the maxim that equity looks at the substance and not at the form, equity will relieve against penalties and forfeitures. If X contracts with Y to do an act and promises that upon his default he will pay a sum of money as a penalty for his nonperformance or will forfeit an interest in land, equity considers that the doing of the act was the real purpose of the contract and that the payment of the penalty or forfeiture was merely a means of securing performance. Therefore if damages for the nonperformance can be readily ascertained, exaction of the penalty or forfeiture will not be allowed. In many cases equity will relieve against forfeitures and penalties and set them aside.

For the most part the common law courts will enforce the doctrine in equity as to penalties but not as to forfeitures. If A sues B today in a court of law to recover a sum of money as a true penalty he will be permitted to recover only damages actually suffered. 

Whether a contract calls for the payment of "liquidated damages" or a "penalty" it matters little as the court will go behind the form of the language and determine as a matter of fact whether it is truly a penalty or liquidated damages. A larger sum of money agreed to be paid as security for the payment of a smaller sum is always a penalty. 65 Fod. 794. But suppose that A, a contractor, agrees to complete an office building for B by July 1 and in default will pay $25 for each day's delay. If it is difficult to estimate B's loss due to the delay and the $25 a day 18 not unreasonable in proportion to the damages actually suffered, then equity will call this a provision for liquidated demages even though the parties may have called it a penalty. Provisions for liquidated damages will be enforced. On the other hand if X promised to make Y a suit of clothes and bound himself to pay $250 as "liquidated damages" for each day's delay after the day the suit was to be delivered then such a provision will be deemed a penalty and unenforceable in spite of the fact that it was termed "liquidated damages." 

Equity will not make a decree which will result in enforcing a forfeiture. 53 N. J. Eq. 46. Equity will recognize a provision for a forfeiture where it in fact provides for liquidated damages. Equity often takes jurisdiction to relieve a party against a forfeiture not wilfully incurred. 

Where a forfeiture 18 intended merely to enforce the payment of a sum of money as where a condition in a lease renders it void on account of non-payment of rent, the forfeiture in such instance will be uphold on the ground that it is not inequitable; but the forfeiture will be set aside on payment of the rent due. 

124 N. Y. 175. Equity will relieve one against a forfeituro occasioned by fraud, accident and the like. As a rule it cannot be said that a forfeiture will be set aside in every case in which a penalty would be relieved against. It depends on the merits of each case as to whether or not equity will exercise its discretion in relieving against a forfeiture. 83 Md. 203.

THE DOCTRINE OF ELECTION. The doctrine of election is a choice which a party is compelled to make between the acceptance of a benefit under a written instrument and the retention of some property already his own, which is attempted to be disposed of, in favor of a third party, by virtue of the same paper. The doctrine of election presupposes a plurality of gifts or rights, with an express or implied intention of the party who has a right to control one or both that one should be a substitute for the other. The doctrine

rests upon the principle that he who seeks equity must do equity. It means that where two inconsistent or alternative rights or claims are presented to the choice of a party by a person who intends that he should not enjoy both that he must accept or reject one or the other and that he cannot accept a benefit under an instrument and then repudiate it. A man cannot have his cake and eat it too.

Priorities are governed by two equitable maxims, namely, "Where there are equal equities the one prior in time shall prevail" and "Where there are equal equities the one with the legal title shall prevail." Suppose A and B both have an equitable lien on Blackacre and proceeds realized from its sale aro insufficient to pay both A and B. Who gets paid first? It the equities of A and B are intrinsically equal, of the same rank and A and B are equally without fault then their equities are equal and equity will give them equal consideration. Assuming their equities are rospectively equal than it is important to ascertain whether one or the other has obtained the legal title to the property in addition to his equitable interest. The one who has the legal title in addition to his equitable title will prevail in such case over the one who has merely the equitable title. But where they have merely equitable titles, then at between equal equities the one first created will prevail.
