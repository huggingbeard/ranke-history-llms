FOREWORD TO THE TEACHER 

OUR civilization is changing more rapidly to-day than any civilization has changed before. 

Machinery is taking the place once held by the individual. This is resulting in a constantly increasing interdependence of people. With this there is to be found a division of labor which has resulted in a remarkable increase in the number of occupations from which youth may choose. 

The result of all these changes places a new demand on education. No longer is it sufficient to hand down to youth his elder's manner of behaving. In preparing the youth in this new situation, we cannot foretell what he will need to know. However, we do know that he will face the serious problem of choosing a life work. 

It is to help meet this problem that courses in Occupations and Guidance are now being given in our public schools. The threefold purpose of such a course should be: 

1. ECONOMIC: To enable the student to make a better living. 

2. PERSONAL: To enable the student to receive a greater enjoyment in life. 

3. SOCIAL: To enable the student to render a greater service to mankind. 

In order to help carry out more successfully the above purposes, this book was prepared. 

It contains a series of lessons designed for use by high school students. It may be used with any standard text. It may be used by inexperienced as well as experienced teachers. One of the central aims in preparing this book was to make it thoroughly practical for as many types of schools as possible; for it is recognized that conditions vary greatly in different classes and in different schools, and the details of the plan to be followed must be determined by the ability and previous training of the student, the time at his disposal, the library facilities, and many other diverse circumstances. 

It is desirable in this course that the student should get some practice in: 

1. Using the library card index. 

2. Using the Readers' Guide, World Almanac, and United States Catalogue. 

3. Using the table of contents and index of a book. 

4. Getting information from persons who are well informed. 

5. Getting information from newspapers, magazines, bulletins, etc. 

6. Giving oral reports. 

7. Participating in debates. 

8. Taking notes. 

9. Writing themes. 

10. Collecting appropriate clippings, pictures, etc. 

Some of the general and specific aims of the course should be: 

1. To acquaint students with the purpose and value of school and help them to acquire correct habits of study. 

2. To develop in students a well-rounded practical vocational vocabulary. 

3. To give students a complete survey of the educational opportunities offered in their own high school. 

4. To help students to realize that the best time to get a high school education is now, and that failure to do so is likely to result in their never acquiring one. 

5. To give to students who are forced to leave school early information concerning the advantages of part-time and evening schools in preparing them for vocational opportunities in the community.

6.

To give students information about the various colleges and universities in which they may continue their education. 

7. To lead the student to consider the importance of having some vocation and of occupying a useful place in the world. 

8. To help the student realize his own weaknesses and strong points. 

9. To show the student the relation of school courses to preparation for life outside the school. 

10. To point out to the student the essential items to observe in choosing a vocation. 

11. To make a study of the typical vocations which comprise the work of the world, for the purpose of giving students useful information in helping them choose their life work. 

12. To help students understand and appreciate the processes by which men make their living. 

13. To aid the student in understanding some of those business principles which are common factors in all life work. 

14. To encourage students to be thrifty, to save and invest wisely, so as to provide against economic emergency in old age. 

15. To show the students the various methods of locating vacancies for positions. 

16. To teach the students some of the important phases concerning the writing of applications and the making of personal interviews. 

17. To develop in students an honorable code of ethics towards their fellow workers and society. 

18. To instill in students a desire for vocational happiness. 

The following suggestions should prove helpful: 

1. Use a basic text unless you have an extensive library. The texts most commonly used are those referred to throughout the Work-book. 

2. Adapt the subject matter of the course to the capacity of the particular group under instruction. 

3. Make frequent use of such teaching devices as oral reports, debates, socialized recitations, and objective tests. 

4. Make your instructions to the students interesting, practical, and significant. Place emphasis upon reasoning rather than upon memory. 

5. Develop in students sound judgments through practice in viewing both sides of questions with deliberation and with an open mind. 

6. Encourage the students to read magazines and newspapers. Allow them to place clippings on a bulletin board arranged for that purpose. 

7. Encourage the students to read the biographies and autobiographies of persons who have been successful in certain vocations. 

8. Use various means of stimulating interest in the subject by having visits to factories, stores, banks, and other institutions. It is very important that definite plans are made in connection with these trips. A few wellplanned visits are better than a large number of haphazard ones. 

9. Use discretion in securing outside speakers to lecture before the class. Too often such speakers are not acquainted with educational methods and do not know how to present the subject. When outside speakers are engaged it is often advisable to provide them with a general outline to follow in preparation of their lecture. 

10. Make the first week of class work an inspirational one. Acquaint the students with the aims, purpose, and content of the course. This may best be done by having a general discussion of the Introduction, Foreword, and Contents in the Work-book. Supplement any interesting facts that will help arouse the students' interests.

It is hoped that this book will enable the teacher to secure the maximum results with a minimum amount of effort and that the real aim of vocational guidance will be the better realized. vi

TO THE STUDENT 

Purpose. This Workbook has been prepared for your convenience. It is compiled with the view of making it easy for you to gain a clear and comprehensive understanding of the problems in vocational guidance; thus increasing your chance of achieving security, prosperity, and happiness. It should prove beneficial in the following ways: 

1. It will serve as your guide and standardize work. 

2. It will save the teacher's time in writing instructions, etc., on the blackboard, and will save you the time spent in copying these instructions. 

3. It will make it much easier for you to make up back work after having been absent. 

4. It will inspire you to do neater work. 

5. It will help you to measure your achievements.

Use. This Work-book should be used with the following suggestions in mind: 

1. Read through the chapter in your text book that deals with the lesson topic. Then take up the questions in your book and fill in the answers. If you cannot find all the answers in your textbook, use some of the references provided at the end of each lesson or those suggested by your instructor. In some cases it may necessary for you to prepare the first draft of your work on an extra sheet of paper and then copy it Most of the work done in this book should be done under the supervision of your be in your notebook. instructor. 

2. If a reference book is used that is not referred to at the end of the lesson, write the name of this book on the blank lines provided for the additional references at the end of each lesson. 

3. Be sure that you understand all directions before undertaking the work. 

4. Be careful to take account of any changes in directions that may be given by your instructor. 

5. Be neat in your work and be very careful in matters of spelling and writing. 

6. Work independently unless otherwise instructed. 

7. Use the blank pages given throughout the book at the suggestion of the instructor. The blank pages given in the Appendices are for occupational clippings and pictures taken from newspapers, magazines, etc. If you do not care for this material at the end of the term, give it to your instructor so that it may be filed, classified, and indexed. Compilation of such materials becomes very valuable information for future classes. 

8. Observe the sample lessons and notes on pages 3 to 9 as a model for lesson procedure and as a supplementary source of information.

It is hoped that this Work-book will enable you to gain a comprehensive understanding of the fundamental principles of vocational choice, so that you may be able to meet successfully life situations and become a good, happy, and useful citizen. vli

B. The need of vocational guidance. 

There are infinitely more opportunities in America today than were ever open to any nation in all the history of the world; in fact there are over 20,000 different tasks from which a young man may choose a career; young women have about half as wide a range. Yet on the other hand at no period in our history has there been such a need for vocational guidance as there is today. Our occupational life has been made so complex by the magnitude and variety of our industrial and commercial enterprises, that it is becoming more and more difficult for young people to discover themselves unaided. 

In order to keep pace with the rapid changes in our economic and social life, society has not only found it desirable but necessary to offer some form of guidance to aid our boys and girls in choosing wisely their life work. In recent years our educational and industrial institutions have made much headway in a guidance program by providing: 

1. Vocational education. 

2. Occupational informational courses. 

3. Vocational counseling. 

4. Extracurricular activities. 

5. Personnel departments in factories and stores. 

Conclusion. Thus it is encouraging to note the achievements of the vocational guidance movement during the past decade. It is beginning more and more to form a fundamental and permanent part of our American School System and is perhaps one of the most important educational developments of today.

D. Character vs. reputation. 

A good name is better than great riches, but a good character is far more important than a good name. Character makes reputation; reputation can never make character. Reputation is what people think you are; character is what God knows you are. A pure, strong, upright, Godly character is power, a mighty force, in the world; but reputation may be like the noise of sounding brass or of tinkling cymbals.

LESSON V INSTRUCTIONS FOR, WRITING TERM THEME OR CAREER BOOK ¹ 

One of the most valuable means of acquiring occupational information is the writing of a term theme on some particular topic. The student is thus given an opportunity to arouse his thinking powers, to express clearly and logically his thoughts in writing, to learn how to use the library with its indexes, encyclopedias and bibliographic aids, to develop the power to judge between conflicting statements and to compare the creditability of different sources. For these reasons a theme based upon serious, systematic and extended investigation should be required of the student as a part of the semester's work. 

INSTRUCTIONS TO BE FOLLOWED BY THE STUDENT IN THE PREPARATION OF THEME. 

1. Topic: 

a. Select any occupation in which you are particularly interested. b. Get selection approved by the instructor. 

2. Length: 

From 1500 to 4000 words. Longer themes may be necessary for some topics while others can hardly be extended without padding. 

3. Form of completed paper: 

a. Title page. 

On the first page write the title of your theme, the name of the writer, name and number of the course, and the date. 

b. Preface: 

On the second page give a brief foreword or preface, stating what you have tried to accomplish in your paper, your point of view, special difficulties you have encountered, etc. 

c. Contents: 

On page three write the table of contents, outlining the topics and sub-topics of each division or chapter. This should be in exact conformity with the order of the divisions and sub-divisions in the treatment of the subject throughout the theme and the pages should be indicated. 

d. Body of paper: 

1. The introductory paragraphs should be brief, clear and concise. 

2. The body of the theme should be developed in a logical order, according to main divisions and sub-divisions. 

3. When the subject has been thoroughly presented a few summarizing paragraphs should be given. 

These final interpretations and conclusions are exceedingly important. 

e. Bibliography: 

1. The bibliography is placed at the end of the theme. 

2. The data of this bibliography are obtained from the bibliographical cards that are explained in the latter part of this outline.

4. In listing magazines, give, where possible, the author, the title of the article, the name of the magazine and the date of its publication.

a. Read the references given in your text and those suggested by your instructor. b. Note the additional references in the footnotes and bibliographies. c. Use quotation marks whenever statements are copied exactly. h. Make all of your notes legible and when quoting verbatim, check very carefully with the original. i. Cards of two colors may be used. White cards may serve for the bibliographical material and yellow for ideas and suggestions that come to you in the course of your reading. j. THE ROAD TO SUCCESS 

1. Be punctual. "Time is money." Do not waste it. 

2. Be honest, truthful, frank, sincere, and serious. Make your word as dependable as a United 

States Government bond. 

3. Be careful about your dress. "Dress does not make the man but it does make impressions, good or bad." 

4. Be ambitious. Try to succeed in every honorable way, exercising at all times energy, perseverance, thoughtfulness, patience, and courage. 

5. Be modest. Do not talk about your accomplishments. Let the other person do that. 

6. Be sociable. Make friends, but remember the best of friends will wear out if you use them too frequently. 

7. Be efficient. Do your work with exactness. Nothing is worth doing that is not worth doing weil. 

8. Be progressive. Prove by hard and conscientious work your fitness for a more responsible position. 

9. Be economical. Save something no matter how small your earnings may be. 

10. Be cooperative. Do your best work without friction with your associates. Smother petty jealousy, and avoid gossip. 

11. Be thoughtful. Consider the desires of others. Think first and act second. 

12. Be clean in your habits and mode of life. Do not drink, swear or gamble. They are injurious as well as expensive. 

13. Learn to take criticism. If it is just try to profit by it; if it is unjust try to take it without ill feeling. Take advice but do your own deciding. 

14. Keep yourself in good physical condition. Good health is your most important asset. 

15. Attend church. It is right and helpful, commands respect, and inspires confidence. 

16. Observe the Golden Rule. "Do unto others as you would have others do unto you."

The above suggestions are within the reach of every individual endowed with a reasonably healthy constitution and average mentality.
