They had been married little more than a year. 

It seemed long and yet it seemed short: funny how any thing could be so contradictory. And that year had been blissfully devoid of quarrel and misunderstanding. 

Their last real spat had been two weeks before their marriage, but she had had her way, and he had prom ised not to accept any foreign propositions. After all hadn't she been born and raised here in New York, and weren't all her friends here? True, Joe had traveled far and wide, and he felt that some of his best oppor tunities were in foreign fields. 

Last night when he had come home and told her very bluntly that they were leaving for China today, informing her that they were to leave on the Oceania from 

Wharf No. 12 at 2:15 P. M., it had been too much.

Her reminding him of his promise had been to no avail. 

He said that this time he had no choice, for the company had ordered him to leave, besides, it meant a considerable advancement.

Words were followed by tears, and finally in desperation she packed her bag and came here to the St. 

Charles. His parting words, along with the plea in his eyes had been, "I'll be here packing till sailing time and 

I'll be waiting for your call."

Now after a night of tears she was resigned to her fate. A night of lonliness made her realize that New 

York, South America, China, or anywhere, would be bearable so long as Joe was there. Although it was still well before noon, she would not call, but would meet him at the wharf and they could sail together. 

Explanation, after all, would be unnecessary, for their hearts knew the truth.

He looked at his watch as the shore faded from view. 

It was just 2 o'clock. He felt strangely alone in spite of the crowded deck. After all, she hadn't called. Had she known that the Oceania was unable to leave, and the passengers for that ship had been notified to leave on the Mauritania from Wharf No. 10 at 2 P. M.?

When the police came in, Ralph led them into his uncle's study. The old man sat in his big easy chair, just as if he had fallen asleep, except for the fact that his eyes were open. A rapid examination showed the police that he was dead, and that obviously there had been no struggle.

A few minutes later the police physician came in. 

With him came Charles Wood, the town pharmacist, who always seemed to show up at the scene of crimes, and who regarded himself as quite a Sherlock Holmes.

Both the doctor and the pharmacist had known Gor don Rowe, as had most of the people in this sleepy little town. Gordon Rowe, the retired factory owner, had been in ill health for years, and Dr. Brown, the

police physician, had often cautioned him about his bad heart. They likewise knew Ralph Rowe, a shiftless young man who was to inherit the Rowe fortune, provided that old Gordon didn't change his will as he had so often threatened to do.

They listened again as Ralph told of finding his uncle. 

He was spending the week-end with him, Ralph said. 

He had gone to a dance at about eight o'clock, picking up Gloria Swan down the street. On returning at one o'clock, he had found his uncle as they saw him now. 

Nowhere on the body was there any sign of violence; so Dr. Brown tentatively proclaimed death due to heart failure, and gave permission to have the body removed. 

The police began to leave, but Charles Wood remained kicking around at the ashes in the fireplace. To 

Sergeant O'Malley's gruff call, he answered, "I'm try ing to solve this murder." 

Sergeant O'Malley, who had learned in the past to pay attention to the somewhat eccentric pharmacist, walked over to Wood and entered into a low-voiced conversation with him. According to her confession, Mrs. Darling had prevented this by her wellplanned murder of her no longer loving husband.

She explained that Joe Darling had, at the time of their marriage and for two years afterwards, been working in a biological laboratory. She insisted she go back into the house to get material from their bathroom first-aid kit to attend to it. He thought she was applying an antiseptic when she applied' the cotton pledget which she had soaked in curare while in the house. He stiffened and died almost instantly.

THE SURGEON 

I stood looking at his deft fingers, working swiftly yet deliberately. The long slender hands were those of a born surgeon. His hands moved to and fro, now in the field of operation, now reaching for an instrument: never a lost motion; never the slightest sign of hesita tion. His body was arched over the operating table, tense but not stiff. His eyes, peering from under the mask, were intent on his work, with never a side wise glance. They were steel hard, with not a gleam of human sympathy. They seemed to behold not the mangled form of a poor little boy, but rather an interesting, though perplexing bit of work. 

I had given anaesthetics for many operations and for many surgeons, but this was different. Here I was giving an anaesthetic for the famous Dr. Barrows, loudly acclaimed traumatic surgeon, and head of the

State Hospital. Our patient was a little boy who had been hit by an automobile just in front of the hospital. 

It raced through my mind that this famous Dr. Bar rows had a son, named Fred after himself. I was reminded of the day a weeping mother, in the early years of my training told me, that we might accept it as "just work" as long as it was for someone else's child rather than our own.

THE HOUR OF DEATH. 

Death comes to all living things. Its certainty is absolute but its time is variable. Yet, when the time is known, it often becomes even more difficult to bear. 

And it is given to some to carry this extra burden. 

Tomorrow: it had so been decreed. Tomorrow as the afternoon sun began to fade, life would cease, and death, with its endless sleep would begin.

The causes of death are as varied as death itself. 

Some give up life as a result of their own mismanagement of it, while others give up life that life itself may continue to be.

Need it matter greatly how the last few precious hours be spent, or might they not as well be much the same as those before? 

Tomorrow, in the afternoon glow it would all cease to be. It had so been decreed. So let it be.

THE RUNAWAY 

The train thundered on. It was completely out of control. With no engineer or fireman in the cab, there was no way of stopping it. Behind the engine were six day coaches, two pullman cars, and one mail car. This heavy load only added to the recklessness of the runaway.

Just ahead was a tunnel, and beyond that a trestle. three or four times the length of the entire train. And to make it worse, there was a curve in the road between these two.

If the train made the tunnel safely, it would prob ably tear out the rails at the curve. If this didn't happen, it was dreadful to think what could happen at the trestle. 

If it hadn't been for that steep incline, newly made, this would never have happened. No use now to think of the hundreds of times this same train had made this same run without mishap. At its present rate of speed the runaway would surely be a complete wreck. This realization was too much to bear. 

So he'd better shut off the switch, for if he wrecked his new electric train, little Johnnie Brown would be in for it.

He would catch the 5 P.M. train going West. He understood they were harvesting out West now, so he should be able to get a job. After a little while in the harvest fields, he would push on farther west to California. Mr. Wright, whom he had met last month, was from California and he said it was the most beautiful country in the world. He'd like that. He'd stay around California a while and just enjoy the scen ery. After that he'd ship out on one of the big boats. 

Mr. Wright said ships left the California coast and travelled all around the globe. He was a pretty handy fellow, so he should have no difficulty getting work on one of the ships. Then he would travel far and wide. 

He was sure he would enjoy seeing foreign places and foreign peoples, and, in general, just getting around the world and away from here. 

Well, of course he'd hate leaving his parents and friends here. He'd never been away except with his parents. Maybe he should put it off for a little while yet, for after all he'd be just eight this coming birthday. 

And maybe if he explained to Mother that he really did slip and fall she wouldn't spank him as she had promised to do the next time he got his good suit muddy.
