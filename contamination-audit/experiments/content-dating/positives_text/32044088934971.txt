FOREWORD BY JOSEPHUS DANIELS EGINNING with the incomparable pen-picture of the gracious women of the Old South, drawn by Thomas Nelson Page, of gentle womanhood of the land he loved and glorified, literature has been enriched by the life stories of the charm of a race that lives in loving memory. The impression long prevailed outside the South that these sheltered women were lovely and helpless, ornaments of a civilization where chivalry and knighthood were in flower. To be sure there were Southern women of the days that are gone who were of the type of graciousness without capacity for industry and practical knowledge. They fluttered and shone in the airy social life of gay capitals. But they were the exotics of the South. Most 

Southern matrons measured up to the Bible standard of the place of women, ordering their households and the slave population wisely. Their positions called for executive ability, wise planning and knowledge of the needs of plantation life. It was such women of the Old South, whose kindness and consideration for their slaves ameliorated the system of slavery. 

The reader of the useful and beautiful life of Mrs. Johnston so happily portrayed in this volume, will say of her as the prophet of old said: “Her price is far above rubies. She stretcheth out her hands to the needy . . . She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and in her tongue is the law of kindness. She looketh well to her household, and eateth not the bread of idleness. Her children arise up, and call her blessed; her husband also, and he praiseth her."

The typical Southern woman is depicted by the author in the life of her aunt, Mrs. Robert D. Johnston, crowned

with the title conferred upon her of "First Lady of Alabama". She was born to the purple, so to speak. She opened her eyes in Blandwood, the ancestral home of her grandfather, Governor John Motley Morehead of Greensboro, North Carolina. She was the daughter of Col. Peter 

Evans, a brave confederate officer who was killed in battle near Upperville, Virginia, two months after Stonewall

Jackson had "crossed over the river" to rest under the shade of the trees.

The heroine, Johnsie Evans Johnston, was early married to Brigadier General Robert D. Johnston of Charlotte,

North Carolina, who moved to Alabama and was one of the most successful bankers in the wonderful growth of Birmingham, Alabama. Perhaps the South has had no husband and wife more congenial as true partners, each of them winning high place by useful service. 

Before leaving Charlotte, Mrs. Johnston was the moving spirit in establishing a hospital for the negroes; in Alabama her Christian duty led her to teach prisoners, to whom she held out a helping hand. She established the 

Alabama Boys' Industrial School; held office in the Alabama Federation of Women's Clubs, Vice Regent of Alabama for Mount Vernon, and held other positions of responsibility. The mainspring of her life of usefulness was her unfaltering Christian faith. It gave her zeal and strength for unselfish ministration. She touched no life except to bless it. She was guided by this expression of her faith: “Trusting God is one of the most thrilling experiences men can enjoy. The trouble is we fail to try it enough."

The author, with deep love and admiration, gives many graphic stories of Mrs. Johnston's experiences, the most interesting being the devotion of the boys she had more than mothered at the Alabama Boys' Industrial School.

There are glimpses of her touch with the great, of the friendship of Theodore Roosevelt with characteristic letters from him, and of Leonard Wood, and of the distinguished personages from allied nations who were received by her at Mount Vernon during the World War I. 

The reading of this real life of a real Christian will enhance admiration for Christian Southern womanhood and will strengthen faith in the goodness of God and His guidance and support of those who trust in Him.

CHAPTER I HE carriage of John Motley Morehead, Governor of North Carolina, swung through the high gates of his Blandwood estate in Greensboro, and rolled up the winding driveway of the court in front of its entrance. It was evening of May the third, 1851. The 

Governor pulled out of his pocket a quaint old silver ladle he was bringing home from the personal sale of his motherin-law's effects and surveyed its unusual markings. Three times the name Elizabeth was engraved there, and his thoughts went back wondering what common bond the name might hold through the generations. Suddenly he was startled out of his revery by excited feminine voices shouting in unison, “Oh, father, it's another girl," with a decided emphasis on the "another". "What's another girl?" the man asked, still preoccupied with his thoughts. "Sister Lizzie has a daughter", the girls said in disgust.

Their hearts had been set on a boy and not a fourth granddaughter. "Bless her heart. She is born with a silver spoon in her mouth," said Governor Morehead, as he paused a moment to show his daughters the ancient heir-loom; and then passed on up the stairs to enter the quiet, dark room, now glorified with this new life. 

He scanned the baby's face closely, and chuckled, as he said, "The lift of the left eyebrow-one in each generation. 

Our little girl may be the possessor of the gift of second sight-a power that seems to come from God, and her name shall be Elizabeth Johnston."

I wager she then and there began her wicked practice

of coquetting, as she brought down her first of an ever increasing line of victims to his knees! This gift of coquetry was surelv inborn with her, and of all her earthly attributes was one of the last relinquished. However, neither of them could have known, along with the "lift of the eyebrow" she had also inherited his silver tongue. Fifty years later in the state capitol at Raleigh, where her grandfather, Governor Morehead, had often stood, a beautiful woman pleaded with a golden voice for the delinquent boys of her own native state, in words so eloquent and stirring, that old men and old women of the audience were heard to whisper, "Governor Morehead himself must have come back to life."

It was at the top of spring when the whole world was mad with green that a little girl was born in Greensboro,

North Carolina, who eventually became Elizabeth Johnston Evans Johnston; as yet neither she nor anybody in particular, but merely a soft anonymous morsel of humanity, who from the beginning was enveloped in light and laughter.

Earliest recollections seem the only fitting way to begin a person's life, whether those recollections are of incidents or of people. But our little heroine's first conscious recollection was of neither; rather was it of a great emotion, at the age of four, and possibly except for this, and the way the experience burned itself into her infant soul, her days and her deeds might never have been recorded.

And in the beginning be it known that this is not a chronicle bound upon accurate figures to be used as a reference book, but upon cheerfully expurgated memorials of the woman, and seen through the admiring eyes of one, who throughout her life, in amazed wonderment watched this aunt shoot skyward.

This first recollection of Johnsie Evans, for by this name her tall and handsome father chose to call her, had

to do with an experience of hers while visiting with her parents, their relatives in Scotland Neck. With night, came the realization she was to sleep in a room alone. Never having been out of her mother's room at night, she wept at the thought. Wild imaginings filled her soul with terror and she pleaded with her mother not to leave her. "What do you fear, my child," her mother said quietly and calmly in her accustomed way. "Jesus is with you here as well as anywhere."

The words somehow seemed to bring instant comfort and soon the frightened and excited child closed her eyes, and the tired little body fell asleep only to dream that a drunken man had crept into the room. She awakened, screaming involuntarily with this new terror, but instantly her mother's reassuring words came back to her-"Jesus is with you." "Dear Jesus," the little voice called out aloud, “just put your hand on my head and take care of me." As the unseen hand comforted, the baby fell into a peaceful sleep. From then on, she boldly proclaims, that whenever she has asked, she has never failed to feel "the Hand". The incident became a guide-post, for to Johnsie, her religious sense came to her that night, a conviction that bound her to the idea of All-Mightiness. 

But the path of the "faithful follower" is never smooth, little Johnsie was soon to learn. Closely following her religious venture, came her first adventure with sin. A kind aunt gave her a quarter to spend any way she liked. Holding the vast amount in her hand, as she watched her mother and aunts at their needle-work, she suddenly decided she wanted above all, a needlebook-a little red needlebook with a spool of thread in it, that her eyes had spied at the village store the day before. "But, my dear, you cannot spend your money for that.

We will give you all the needles and thread you want,” her mother told her. "But, Mother, I want my own needlebook." "No." And in those days the self-expression theory was unknown to man or child, and a parental "no" was final.

Deviation from it was the sure path leading to perdition. 

For hours Johnsie tried to forget that lovely little red leather needlebook, which grew more desirable every time she thought of it. She tried to play, she tried to think of something else she might buy-but all in vain, so of course she just slipped across the road, bought the coveted treasure, and flew back home. Safely inside her own yard she examined it carefully. The day before it had seemed so beautiful, but now suddenly she felt it did not quite come up to expectations, and then she was quite puzzled, for she was not certain what to do with it. She could not show it to the grown-ups, so she decided on slipping up stairs and putting it carefully away. She ran all the way up, and knocked right into her "mammy” in her haste. "Now, honey, what yo' don' don'? Why is yo' hurrin' so, is yo' sick?" and her nurse examined her carefully. "Already she knows what I have done," thought Johnsie. 

But after "mammy" left, she hunted around to find a safe hiding place, but nothing seemed to offer security, and she constantly imagined she heard her mother coming. 

Then her eyes happened to fall on the velvet carpet, with its rings and clusters of roses, and an idea came to her. 

With deft fingers she tucked her foolish little purchase under one of the corners of the carpet, and then safe from critics' eye the mite of a child stood up with a sigh of relief that the red leather case was no longer burning her fingers, learning thus early that forbidden fruit is intriguing only before plucking! 

All that day a strange and unhappy little girl moved in

and out of the house, shunning and avoiding her mother and aunts, and if asked what she intended buying with her money, she evaded the question. Finally though, night came, and with it, bed, mother and prayers, and never was an occasion more welcome, for the wretched sinner unburdened her infant soul to her mother. In this day of free will and small conscience, the big suffering of this child may sound positively grotesque. There is small need to suffer pricking of the conscience now-a-days. If a mere baby wants a red leather needlebook, something in her subconscious craves it, and that something should be satisfied. So she gets it, with the hope that pricked fingers will be the only result of self-expression. But those were the not distant days when a child's conscience, as well as its body, was thoroughly clothed, and a daily scrubbing behind the ears of both, a general practice. 

Meanwhile she loved many things, her silver cup, the lovely portrait of her mother, her Little Susie books-and most of all, her baby sister, whose tiny life was to span less than three years. And through all these infant days the child finds closely interwoven an association with her fearless, self-willed and high-spirited father. "Punish this child for temper and lack of self control and don't let her grow like me," she remembers hearing him say. He often played marbles with her, but never gave her any advantages because of her age. "Play the game fair throughout life, even if the odds are against you," he once said to her, and one can see the picture of this gallant young cavalry officer a few years later living up to his words, as he dashed into the jaws of death, waving his saber and shouting to his men; "Now, men, I want you to understand I am going through." 

And he did go through, but was mortally wounded in the passage, dying shortly after. Unconsciously his daugh-

ter picked up the fallen challenge and was "going through" for eighty-three active years.

The elements of courage, poise, vision and romance so mingled in this child of Elizabeth Morehead and Peter Evans that she seemed somehow to catch the high-lights of each ancestor and amalgamate a composite likeness of their virtues into a magnificent whole. The touch of heroworship was handed her by grand-father Peter Evans, who wanted to name his sons after his ideals, George Washington and Augustus Adolphus, but Grandmother Evans, of a practical turn of mind, said decidedly, “Away with your fine names, I borned my sons and they shall be called plain George and Peter." But the grand-daughter, being a chip off both the old blocks, went straight ahead and called her first son, Gordon, after the great Scotch soldier and missionary known as Chinese Gordon.

It is further recorded that this Grand-father Peter loved the beautiful and when he sent his cotton to England, he specified that the money he received should be spent on mahogany furniture of the pedestal style with lion's feet, massive four-poster beds, and great tables and an exquisite pink rose dinner and tea set. Even more significant than his discriminating taste, was his vision of the soil. At that early day he preached deep plowing and liquid fertilization, while all the countryside believed his ideas crazy. 

Then along came a great drought in Eastern Carolina, and it was discovered that he alone had vast corn crops. Day after day all through that summer, neighbors came to buy corn, and in the fall he had seed to divide. The old gentleman's generosity in sharing with those less blessed at the time, inspired a grateful neighborhood to affectionately refer to his plantation ever after as "Egypt". 

From a paternal grandmother, several times removed, comes a story of a daring, unequaled both of mind and body. Karem Habboa Norman married a Morehead and

lived in Maryland. During the Revolutionary War she rode far and wide caring for the wounded. With her remedies made from herbs, she untiringly rode from battlefield to battlefield, administering to the sick and dying. Hearing that a battle was to be fought at Guilford Court House in North Carolina, in which her son was engaged, she rode her horse all the way from Maryland, only to find the battle over and the casualties great, her son reported among the missing. As an angel of mercy she moved among the wounded, doing all in her power to alleviate suffering, scanning each face for her own son. Finally she found him severely wounded, and along with several others, moved him to a cabin, dressed his wounds, and taking a tub, suspended it over the cot, punctured holes in it, and filled it with water that it might drip on the wounds, thus anticipating the ice pack of a later generation. It is no wonder that a monument was erected to her memory as one of the greatest Revolutionary heroines.

From their own Grandmother Morehead, Little Johnsie and her brother, Smith, never tired of hearing her tell the story of her own mother, Obedience Motley. During the 

Revolutionary War, when she was four days old, a party of Tories pushed into her mother's room while her father was away. Seeing the woman in bed, the heartless chief announced that she had fever and he was going to bleed her. Taking out his knife he cut her veins and she soon bled to death. The infant daughter, also Obedience, grew up, married John Morehead and they resided in this same home. Years later, one night a man knocked at their door and when Mr. Morehead opened it was told a companion with him was dying and asked to bring him in. Recognizing the desperate state of the ill man, Mr. Morehead had him brought into the parlor while Mrs. Morehead hurried to have a mattress brought into the room. The dying man looked around and faintly whispered, "In this very house

I bled a woman to death, in bed with an infant. I now ask 

God to forgive me." Obedience Motley Morehead straightened up to her full six feet in height and said, "That was my mother. May God forgive you, I never can."

At the age of ninety-eight, when she recalled the story, the children were conscious that family history was correct, in recording she had a temper of her own and sent it sizzling down the ages! Very tall, walking with a long stick and wearing black silks and caps of real lace, she moved about her son's home, a majestic figure. Above all things she hated idleness, in old and young, and every day she would say to the grandchildren: "Come, let me teach you to knit. Satan still has work for idle hands to do," and they would gather around her, while her withered fingers flew back and forth. She was then too blind to notice that the shameless children had no knitting needles, using their first fingers as imaginary ones, whenever she looked their way. It was the duty of a servant each night to ravel out the dropped stitches the old lady had made in her day's work and knit up to where she had left off, so that she was never conscious of her imperfections. 

She watched her son wax great and powerful in his state, where his memory is revered in the field of developments, as the Father of the North Carolina railroad; in education, as the founder of the famous Edgewood Seminary for Young Women, and in philanthropy, as the inspiration of the first Hospital for the Insane in the state. 

While his achievements were a satisfaction to his mother, they were an inspiration to his little grand-daughter of the lifted eyebrow.

The deeds and destinies of those ancestors were so real a part of little Johnsie's life, that she had but to go into the library at Blandwood and look deep into the life-like eyes of their portraits, as they stared down upon her from their vantage place on the wall, to know that they were very

real flesh and blood people, even as you and I, and know, too, that her best in life was none too good to match with theirs. The child sensed what seemed to be expected of her, maybe because of the lifted eyebrow, or maybe because she was born sentient of everything. Some people are not, but when they are, it is written somewhere like a warning upon their face-nothing can change it. 

It may be the reason that Johnsie Evans Johnston walked much more softly before her Lord all her life, than she may have often times felt like stepping. In her life, she said, she never had the human privilege of zigzagging now and then. Like the wee babe of herself, she repented when she might have gone on and forgiven herself, as other people do.

CHAPTER II 

IZZIE MOREHEAD and Peter Evans, the parents of Johnsie Evans, were married at Blandwood in 1850, having met two years before at a Chapel Hill commencement, where Peter Evans was graduated. 

The South, in the fifties of the last century, was in its hey-day of prosperity and affluence, with New Bern, the home of the Evans's, one of the centers of its greatest wealth and culture. Here the people worshipped in a church built by Sir Christopher Wren, held their civic meetings in a building designed by the same artist, and reared their children under the crumbling eaves of Governor Tryon's magnificent palace, which was acknowledged to be the most costly and most beautiful building of both Americas at the time of its completion. Their material wealth and artistic surroundings assumed an air of royal flavor. They lived in a regal way. 

Peter Evans was no exception to the rule, and his courtship was like that of a young prince. In a fervent moment, when he wished to send a special message to his lady-love, the distance, some two hundred miles from New Bern to Greensboro, and the ordinary post too slow to bear his greetings, it was a matter of no moment at all, to send one of his own servants on horse back to deliver his letter in person, a three days' journey each way. Such delicate attentions were the custom due grand ladies of that day. 

Those were the days when women were really wooed, when a sweetheart was regarded with a sort of holy reverence and the salutation of a love letter began, “Honored Madam". There was enchantment in reserve and ceremony-as extreme in the one way, as the present day worship of freedom and informality—is in the other.

The last of these "Special Delivery" letters came to Miss Morehead just one week before their wedding, and in it Mr. Evans referred to their marriage throughout as "Our 

Important Event". The word marriage seemed a bit too sacred a thing to be viewed with the naked eye. 

As a knight of old, in great pomp and glory, riding in his coach and four with its out-riders, Peter Evans journeyed to Greensboro to claim his bride. Blandwood, the home of the bride, showered blessings upon the young couple wishing them God-speed. Beechwood, the home of the groom, with its nine hundred acres and more than half as many slaves, waited in breathless expectancy for the return of its young master and his beautiful bride.

The House of Beechwood was of brick, large and imposing, resting in seclusion among its grove of trees, its lands dropping gently down into the Neuse River. The slave quarters of this plantation were of brick fashioned in two long rows with a spotless avenue of white sand. between, and the windows in the cabins had glass panes, "jus' like the big house", pridefully boasted the slaves.

One of Johnsie's most delightful memories of the place was being lulled to sleep by the sweet night-music coming from the quarters, the clear high voice of her own negro mammy leading in the plantation melodies, with the mighty swelling of the great chorus following.

Beechwood, like most of the South's great plantations, was the seat of a large home industry. Upon marriage, young Mrs. Evans found herself the head of such an establishment, and with rare ability met the situation. As far back as Johnsie could remember her mother had always been the same. Her manner efficient and unruffled; her spirit always calm-so calm that when a servant was asked how her mistress stood the burning of their great barns, she replied, "She was just as calm as always."

Her daughter never knew but one other woman who

approached her in gentleness and goodness, and it was her good fortune to have this other as her mother-in-law. 

Young Mrs. Evans supervised the weaving, spinning, sewing and knitting, which for many hundreds of people, was no simple task. Besides the young master and mistress were ever watchful of the well-being of their slaves, and the creature necessities of the body were not their sole concern; here the mind and soul were carefully directed. 

Sunday-school was attended by all the slaves, and for a certain number there was schooling. Besides these institutions at Beechwood there was a regular Day-Nursery, presided over by one of the elderly women. Every morning, as the young mothers went to their various tasks, they left their younger children at the nursery. In the evening, on their way from work, the babies were collected. It is remembered that Monday was a proverbial “blue Monday” for Mammy of the Black nursery. Many of her charges were half sick and all of them wholly demoralized by their one undisciplined day in their own cabins.

The devotion of these slaves for their master, and their childlike dependence upon him, could not be better exemplified than, upon hearing he had died of wounds received in the Battle of Upperville. Their mistress was away so in an utter state of terror and bewilderment some hundred or more started right down the hot, dusty road to the home of Col. Evans' sister, Mrs. Smith, in Scotland 

Neck, led by one of the negroes who had often accompanied the Evans' there. Grief-strickened and frightened, they had only one thought, that of being with their "Whitefolk". New conditions and new changes always filled them with new fears and suspicions. "Mas' Peter's sister will care for us," they wailed and mourned, as they trudged and sweated along the seventy-five hot, dusty miles in that month of May. "They loved my father," remarked his daughter, “even

if they feared him, and well they might for he was as kind as he was strict." With a smile she said she had reason to know how strict he was. 

When eight, Johnsie came face to face with this characteristic of his. For a long time she had yearned for a real "store" doll, and speaking of it to a guest of her parents, the lady said, "You can order one with real hair from

New York, that can open and shut its eyes and for only six dollars."

In wide-eyed wonder Johnsie marveled that such dolls were a possibility, but after being convinced, she declared one of them was hers right then and there. The actual demonstrating it into a reality however, was not as easy as she had hoped. She was sure her father would order her one, but when she actually reached his study to make her request, she lost courage to ask for so expensive a toy. 

Then she thought of a better way, so she wrote him a little note, asking him to loan her the money to buy this doll, and she would pay for it in work. She counted on her father taking this little hint, and presenting her with the gift. But she missed her mark. With eager expectancy she waited for his call, and when it came she ran to his study with heart in mouth. "All right, Johnsie," he said, "I will agree to loan you the money to buy this doll, and I expect you to live up to the agreement." At the moment nothing seemed easier than the bargain.

So the doll was ordered and in due course of time, the large package arrived from New York. Eager little fingers cut strings and began tearing off paper, which only revealed a big box, Hastily, she opened the box only to behold more paper. Nervously this was torn off, and to her delight she saw first golden curls, then the peach and cream wax face, closed eyes and long lashes. Impatiently she lifted it up and lovely blue eyes slowly opened, she

then bent it forward to hear it say “Ma-ma”.

The child's cup of happiness was full, and turning to her parents she cried in joy, "I will never do anything but dress and undress my precious doll, put her to sleep and wake her up.” 

Her dream was rudely broken by a look in her father's eye, and instantly she recalled she was to earn the right of possession by the sweat of her brow. After only one short day's enjoyment with her blessed treasure, she was summoned to the reality of life by a masculine voice, starting her out on a vocation.

She said she worked daily to pay off that debt, until war intervened, from picking cotton to cleaning her own room, as her father's fancy dictated; but the memory of her labors influenced her whole life to such an extent, that debt was always an evil she never entered into lightly. 

It was while struggling with this economic problem, she made her maiden speech, unconsciously giving birth to talent, which years later, caused one great orator to remark, "When Mrs. Johnston speaks she dwarfs everyone else of the program."

It was a day when weeding was her business, and a group of excited little darkies rushed up to her crying, "Little Miss, dat overseer (whom her father had brought from the North) is plowing up us colored folks' graveyard."

Instantly Johnsie accepted the challenge to protect thẻ helpless. Across the field she went at top speed, reaching the center of the graveyard, she called to the overseer, "Stop! Stop! Don't you dare plow up one of those graves," she cried indignantly, “If you touch one of them I will have my father turn you off at once." 

Then having got full command of her breath, she made a fervent speech to her astonished listeners on one's duty to one's neighbor. The speech must have been convincing,

for at its conclusion, the overseer quietly moved his field of operation to a safer spot.

Just prior to the war between the states, Colonel Evans formed a partnership with his father-in-law, Governor Morehead, to build the North Carolina Railroad and every indication led to the belief that the investments of the Evans's would increase and multiply. Then suddenly the war was on, followed in 1863 by the untimely death of the young Colonel. When the smoke of battle cleared away, the young widow with a daughter and son, found herself almost penniless. The vast landed estates were worthless without slave labor and unable to satisfy her husband's creditors, she signed away her rights to all their vast holdings, and received from the creditors exactly thirty pieces of silver.

Colonel Evans was passionately fond of blooded horses and loved to ride to the hounds, so most naturally when war was declared he chose the Cavalry branch of the service, becoming Colonel of the 64th North Carolina Cavalry. 

With undimmed vividness throughout life his daughter remembered her one and only visit to her father at camp.

It was in May, 1863. Being stationed at Enfield, not far from their home, he wrote his wife and daughter to come to see him at camp. To the child of ten it was a thrilling experience. They started early in the morning, and after baskets of delicious food were packed in the carriage, they rode off, driven by their faithful coachman. It was just a gay, joyous pleasure trip for the child, and the continual passing of soldiers on the way, an added delight. 

In the late afternoon they arrived at Enfield Camp, and immediately the handsome young Colonel rode up on his dashing steed to greet them. In his grey uniform, with a linen cape flying out from the back of his cap, his daughter thrilled with pride as she looked at her soldier-father. 

The thrill was gone with one look at his face. She knew

something was wrong, even before her mother quietly said, "Peter, what has happened?"

Tragedy was written on every line of face and form of the man before her. An awed stillness enveloped the entire surroundings. "Stonewall Jackson is dead," came the answer.

A more tragic sentence could not have fallen from the lips of a southern soldier. The announcement turned the woman cold, as it had all the soldiers of the Colonel's Regiment; as it had all the soldiers of all the regiments of the Confederacy.

Had the small girl actually been at the great soldier's death-bed, she could not have been more moved by the news. The circumstances under which she heard of Jackson's passing forever gave her the feeling that she too, was really an active part of the fighting Confederacy. 

The muchly anticipated happy reunion proved more like a funeral gathering, and two days later the wife and daughter started homeward. As the horses headed towards 

New Bern, and the Colonel stood waving his goodbyes, without warning Johnsie wheeled around, as if she had seen a vision, leaned out the carriage, screaming to her father with tears coursing down her cheeks. Then falling back on the seat she sobbed, "I will never see him again. 

I know it! I saw it!"

This, she said, was the first of a long list of premonitions that followed, like a thread, through the years, dashing great periods in her life. 

And she was right, she never saw him again. The gallant

Colonel was mortally wounded in his charge, a little distance from Upperville, Virginia, June 21, 1863, less than two months after the passing of his great Chieftian. He was carried to the Federal hospital in Washington, a prisoner of war.

Mrs. Evans and her children were in Greensboro at the time, and upon hearing of her father's capture and injuries, Johnsie lost no time in weeping. At once she was all action. She was going to Washington to her father; going to see President Lincoln to beg for her father's freedom.

All her plans were stealthily made, confiding only in her cousin John Walker. She felt reasonably certain a little girl alone would never reach Washington without being apprehended, so it was decided she would wear a suit of John's and pass as a boy. Between the two of them they scraped up enough money for a ticket to Washington, and neither of them bothered their little heads about how she was to get back. Once there, nothing else mattered. The day of the gallant little rebel's departure came but also with it news that the brave young Colonel had died of his wounds.

In the letter, written by a nurse, she said, “As the Colonel was dying, he called ever so faintly the name, “John,

John," which of course must have been his son," she added. The daughter whom he ever so faintly called could not be comforted. The thought of his dying, was as nothing to the fact that he died in prison! The image of him there caught her imagination, and like Joan d'Arc she too saw a vision. The shadow of coming events was manifested and the little child saw herself distinctly moving among prisoners.

When her father started off to war, Johnsie, in her spontaneous way, wanted him to carry something of her very own with him. In her hurried search for a parting gift she spied her silver cup. Rushing up to him, told him to keep it with him always. After the war all memory of the cup was forgotten.

CHAPTER III 

T THE age of ten, Johnsie found herself permanently beginning life at Blandwood, the place of her birth. Besides her grandparents and several unmarried uncles and aunts, were her widowed aunt, Mrs. Walker, and two Walker children, together with her mother and brother. This constituted the household.

New interests immediately opened for Johnsie, for now in real earnest her "inking" days began. The first day at the Dame's School found her dressed in a frock of linsey. 

This was a sleazy woolen cloth. The color was invariably reddish purple with bright yellow stripes woven crosswise in it, so that she must have looked like a little tight waisted caterpillar. Over this she wore a starched white apron. 

Starch was fashionable in those days and Johnsie often wondered if she would ever be able to make her long starched underskirts crack like whips, as did her Aunt Letitia's, whose skirts actually made music as she moved about.

Her hair slicked back off her forehead, was held in firm place with a round comb. Once in a while the comb was stealthily slipped out and run back through the hair to plaster it even tighter. Her headgear was a Shaker Bonnet, with a brim of straw and crown and skirt of cloth. Her stockings were hand knitted of white cotton and barely covered her knees. They were tied with good looking strings on school days, but with ribbon on Sundays. Her pantalettes came below her knees a good inch or so. In such outfits, the girl grandchildren of Blandwood Manor went forth to the Dame's School. Usually little "Dot", as

the children called Johnsie, went dreaming along in a world all her own, and more generally than not, holding whispered conversations with God. Quite typical of this: one morning when she had been the recipient of some special favor, she spontaneously said, "Dear God, You have been extra good to me today, let me do something for you to show how I thank you." In lifting her eyes from this prayerful determination, they fell upon an old woman weighted down with a big heavy valise. At once, Johnsie knew this was her opportunity to show her gratitude, so running up to the old woman, she smilingly offered to carry the valise. Without hesitation it was handed to her. 

Before she had gone very far she began wondering if she had not been a little too hasty in her selection of a thanksgiving offering, or if the Lord had not taken her a bit too seriously. But, having undertaken, she was confident He would see her through, for already she was developing the theory that emotions without actions are worthless. In time, the school loomed in sight, as she knew it must, and triumphantly and joyfully she handed the burden back. 

With light feet she tripped down the white-sanded school yard.

The "Blue Back" Speller and Peter Parley's Geography were her chief weapons of education in those first school days. Years later, when she accidently ran across Peter Parley's grave in Massachusetts, she wept a few tears in memory of all the tears she had shed in childhood because of him.

The three "R's" did not occupy all her time, however, for life at Blandwood was ever exciting. In front of the house was a court, gay with flower beds, before a row of formal box. Here in the summer, tea was served to grownups, and a never ending feast of watermelons for the children. These melons came in wagon loads from the Yadkin farm of Mrs. Walker. It was this aunt, who presided as

Mistress of Blandwood. Johnsie's grandmother was a timid, retiring little creature, who disliked entertaining and mingling with the distinguished guests, so often gathered there, and she turned over this responsibility to her eldest daughter, a woman of rare personal charm. 

During these days, Johnsie gave no thought to raiment and was generally bare-foot and with apron in a usual state of untidiness-that is, until the day came when she suddenly became dress conscious. It happened on an afternoon when she was decidedly unkempt and somehow noticeably bare-foot. In the midst of a wild Indian game, all the children stopped dead still in their tracks as an angelic apparition seemed to drop down out of the skies. In reality it was only a beautiful little girl in a snowy, starchy frock, with golden curls falling all around her face. Instantly, Johnsie was conscious of her feet and her soiled apron, and above all, of her straight, straight hair. If this heavenly vision had somehow gradually made herself known, Johnsie might have handled the situation better. She would undoubtedly have held one of her trusting conversations with her understanding 'Father', doing a little religious bargaining; like, for a head of curls, a promise to walk just a trifle straighter all her mortal days. This time she was caught unaware, and for the first time in her life—and I feel sure the lastwas consumed with the deadly sin of envy and jealousy. 

Never had she desired anything as much as a head of golden curls, and never did she quite reach that little girl's apparent self-confidence, until sixty years later she had her first permanent wave and put on a straight front corset. “Curls and absence of curves do something for women!", she remembered with a smile. 

But the years of sixty-three, four, and five, were not made up of days that even a girl could long dwell on her flat hair, for Greensboro was a railroad center. Soldiers were traveling back and forth, and often filled the town

hospital with the wounded. All the women were doing their share of the nursing, and even a woman-child had a part to play.

She often accompanied her mother and aunts to the hospital and was allowed to carry some special delicacy that had been prepared at home for the wounded soldiers. On one occasion she was greatly pleased to take some ice cream to a very ill patient. When he tasted it, he said, much to her dismay, "Little Miss, it is so cold, couldn't you warm it up a bit for me?" 

With mysterious awe, the children watched their mothers practice with pistols that they might be able to defend themselves, should the occasion arise, also, they heard them discuss the only books they were reading-stories of Revolutionary heroines.

Blandwood's lands ran right down to the railroad tracks and whenever a train load of soldiers arrived at the station, a great moment in the children's life came with it, for the servants were ordered to fill buckets with fresh butter-milk and carry it down to the tracks. Even the women of the household would do their share, and often the children were allowed to assist. The train would slowly move out of the station onto the Blandwood land, and as the soldiers would see the line of buckets of milk held up to them, they would reach out of the window, dip their tin cups into the buckets of milk with the one hand and with the other, poke love letters out on the end of sticks to any of the young girls about. Nothing quite equaled this moment of excitement to Johnsie, particularly when she happened to be the recipient of one of the mysterious missives from an unknown hero.

Once when one of these trains of soldiers was passing, she happended to be riding and decided to have a race with the train. Her habit was a gorgeous green broadcloth, belonging to one of her aunts. It not only modestly covered

her own legs, but those of the horse as well. The race was on, much to the delight of the soldiers, who were wildly cheering from the car windows. Feeling like a Lady 

Launcelot, she dashed through the gates of Blandwood and the climax would have been perfect, if her habit had not blown out on the breezes at just the wrong moment and been caught on the pailing of the fence. Almost the entire skirt went flying to the winds, leaving her with abbreviated habit. She was completely covered with humiliation, as the increased cheers of the departing soldiers rang out louder and louder from the disappearing train. 

It is not difficult to fancy Johnsie as a 'Hero-Worshipper' and during these war days she often was able to give her imagination full sway. Her first 'face to face', or more correctly speaking, face to back, contact with a hero came with General Morgan's visit to Greensboro. The General had escaped from a northern prison and was stopping over in Greensboro. Everyone was out to do him honor. The crowd was so great that Johnsie soon despaired of getting near enough to shake his hand, so she decided on other tactics. Laboriously she pushed through the crowd, unnoticed, until she found herself right at the back of her herɔ. 

She stood there a moment drinking in the glory of the contact, and then growing bolder, gently caught the tails of his grey uniform in both her hands, and whispering to herself said, "I can't shake your hand, Mr. General, but I'll give the tail of your coat a great big shake."

Just before the surrender, the whole town was excited over a great religious revival, and after intense soul-wrestling, Johnsie presented herself to the minister of the Presbyterian church. Bursting into his study one morning with heart overflowing with fervor, breathlessly said, "Dr. Smith, it's all right." (with her soul, she meant.)

Looking up into the eyes of the earnest little child standing before him—and beyond, into her heart, he gently

said, "Yes, Johnsie, I know it is all right.” With superior wisdom and insight, he surely sensed to whom he extended the right hand of fellowship.

To these war-torn little children acquainted with grief, even in play they carried their scars, which expressed itself oftentime in somber games. A favorite one was making miniature graveyards and erecting monuments to their heroes both of the quick and dead. Its skill depended on one's ability to write the most fitting and telling epitaph. 

But it was indeed a red-letter day for them when all seamy things were forgotten and they were allowed to have an egg and bird roasting. Each of the four grandchildren had his own personal servant-a small negro to do his bidding. 

These little darkies assisted in the shooting of the birds, and picked and cleaned them. Fresh eggs were hunted and with their spoils they grouped round the nursery fire place, presided over by Mammy, dictator of this realm. She would let the fire burn down to coals, and the birds were roasted from the end of a long stick held by the children. Then eggs were tucked under the coals. While this rite was in process, children's voices-both white and black-would ring out in plantation songs, one colored lad, the servant of John Walker, always leading. Years after, this boy's remarkable voice attracted the attention of a musical group, who had it cultivated. Before he died he had sung those same plantation songs, learned at Blandwood, before kings and queens in foreign lands and became owner of an opera house in Australia.

At the age of sixteen, Johnsie was tall and developing a bewitching personality, later to mature into one of her greatest assets. To this was added a sort of wistful beauty that was lighted up from within. Now was the time for completely grown-up clothes, long dress and bustle! 

Fancy the excess weight carried around by women of that day. Besides the tight corsets and huge bustles, there were

the two starched petticoats made of stout "Fruit of the Loom" cotton, substantial drawers and a corset cover, though many also wore a long chemise. This garment holds painful memories for the Morehead family. One afternoon the maid announced a caller to one of Johnsie's aunts. "Tell him I will be down in a few moments," and in an aside said, "I'm en deshabille."

The maid, who had more perception than discretion, gave the remark a literal interpretation, announcing to the embarrassed young gentleman, "Miss Mary sa' she'll be down in a little while, she in her chemmy tail!"

With all of this personal protection Johnsie was allowed to attend the Bingham School Oratorical. The fascination she exercised over those youthful cadets culminated once in such a remarkable note that she kept it always as a priceless treasure. It ran like this: "My dear Miss Johnsie:

I have seen young ladies with more beauty; I have seen young ladies with, maybe, more charm, but you excell all others in the characteristics of ineffable darlingness!"

Possibly therein lay the intangible 'why' of her everlasting charm. It may bear some faint kinship to what moderns call 'IT'. This we know, that she was as skillful at turning a man to love as any musician ever was in producing harmony from a piano. A rare talent, to change every man into a lover by the mere enchantment of your presence. There is actual proof that such was her case, for on a summer evening in 1869 in the aristocratic old town of 

Morganton, a dapper young man presented himself at the hotel to register. The wily old proprietor looked up over his glasses at the handsome youth in front of him, chuckled, and said aloud, "Robert Bingham, hey. Well, I warrant you are here to see Miss Johnsie Evans."

The youth blushed that his private affairs had already become town-talk. But the old man continued, "Well, my

boy," and he took time to aim carefully at the spittoon, "you are the ninth that has come, but each of you will go away happier for just seeing her.” 

Sixty-five years later, Colonel Bingham's grandson bowed in stately fashion and kissed the hand of a still beautiful woman of eighty years, saying, "Madam, I am the tenth.”

Whereupon, as quick as of yore the woman replied, "Would I were younger, or you older, Bob Bingham's grandson!"

Reconstruction days at Blandwood were not as distressing as those experienced by many southern plantations. 

Governor Morehead was rich and powerful in the industrial world, besides being an extensive planter and slave owner. At the close of the war when urged to sell his cotton and buy gold, he indignantly refused. A senator of the 

Confederate Congress, he had no idea of discounting his Southland's currency in such a manner, though at the time he had to borrow the little money his daughter, Mrs. Walker, had saved, to pay the freight on his cotton that his mills might run. At the time of the surrender he had a fortune alone in Confederate bonds, and still believing they would again be valuable, he put them in tin boxes, sealed them and directed that his daughters hide them. 

When Johnston's army surrendered at Durham, it was learned that a northern division was coming to Greensboro, using Blandwood as headquarters. It became necessary to hide the bonds at once, and as a trapdoor in the stair closet lead down into the cellar, and Johnsie being the only one small enough to go through this opening, she was given a lantern, put down through the hole and told to bury them. In the midst of her task, she suddenly heard the weeping of the women upstairs, and wailing of the negroes in and out of the house-and then she heard the tramp, tramp of marching feet. She realized it was John-

ston's ragged army passing by. In terror and excitement of the moment, she accidently put out her lantern. In utter darkness she groped about finishing her task, and all the while screaming for some one to lift her out. For an interminable time, as she thought, she laid there forgotten on the damp, cold earth. That she hid the bonds successfully has never been questioned, for they still remain where she put them.

It was in the year 1870 that Johnsie went forth on a great journey, up into the north and even on into Canada. 

Her companion was her cousin, Addie Avery, and their guide and protector was their uncle, Mr. Eugene Morehead. No lack of excitement prevailed in the family as details for the journey progressed. After prolonged consultation of mothers and aunts the girls must wear gray traveling dresses, as only black and gray were considered genteel for the train. A friend, having just returned from 

New York, informed them that rain-coats were one of the newest things in fashion, so one of these garments was immediately added to their wardrobe. They went by train to 

Norfolk and on to Washington by boat.

On reaching the old Metropolitan Hotel in the Capital, which was the most dazzling building the girls had ever entered, they were indeed glad of all their new finery. 

Walking down the corridor, Johnsie spied two girls coming towards them, and feeling so self-satisfied, whispered to Addie, 'I'm glad we don't look as countryfied as those two girls", when just at the moment they bumped right into a large mirror! No sooner settled in their room, than they heard the fire engine snorting and clanging down the street. Up jumped the girls, out came the raincoats, and down the street they dashed, but no one else was running and the sound of the engine was fast growing fainter. 

Breathlessly they hailed a policeman and asked him which way was the fire. "About ten miles out, Miss," he said, with

a smile. Fires evidently were not as exciting in Washington as in Greensboro.

Shopping consumed much of their thought and time on their travels. It would never do to return home without a dress made in New York, but the selection of material and color proved quite a work of time, and then after all, they selected a sedate gray merino. With a feeling that their wardrobe was assuming too somber a tone, it was agreed to brighten it up a bit with a gay evening dress in Montreal. However, when the final decision was reached they returned to the hotel with silver gray satins! At all hazards they would appear genteel!

This silver satin was to play a conspicuous part in the life of Johnsie. Soon after her return, she attended a dance in Charlotte, and the satin evening dress was as nothing to a gorgeous pair of French kid high top shoes, laced with red laces. She was forever trying to keep the sweeping satin skirt from hiding the timid foot finery. So intent was she on this, she failed to notice that the eyes of one young man were glued upon her. He happened to be a Confederate 

General-a real hero of song and verse-General Robert 

Douglas Johnston. If she had heard the General's ardent language in voicing his impression of her, after the casual introduction, the young girl might have been flattered; but had anyone suggested that one day she would marry him, the idea would have sounded preposterous. Of course, from then on she was pleased by his ardent attentions, but marrying a man sixteen years her senior would have been out of the question. Nevertheless, if she had but known the iron will of the man whose acquaintence she had so recently made, it might have frightened her to learn that from the first he had made up his mind to marry her! Also, it might have frightened him if he had known the campaign ahead of him: In the next two years he asked her to marry him exactly thirty-six times, she always said, and

then she told him, "Well, I suppose I will have to do it in order to get rid of you.”

On November the first, 1871, they were married, a man of thirty-six and a girl of twenty. The occasion was in striking contrast with the pomp and glory of her parents wedding, still there was no lack of modest ado. Love, life and laughter reigned supreme, and the eight lovely bridesmaids in billows of white Tarletons over layers and layers of lace and muslin petticoats, moved among the groomsmen like fair spirits of bewitching charm. The groom arrived at Blandwood a full hour before the ceremony, pacing up and down the great hall like a nervous woman. He could get no comfort from either of his brothers. William, his best man, had already fallen in love with one of the fairest of the maids, and had time for no one else. His youngest brother, Bartlett, who was a bit too envious of the groom, was easing his heart by making eyes at all the girls in general and didn't care if his brother was scared out of his wits.

The man who had risen rapidly to a Brigadier General, who had been wounded eight times, who was ever ready again and again to face unflinchingly the shot and shell of battle, was so agitated that when the preacher reached that part of the service reading: "If any one knows just cause why these two should not be lawfully joined together in Holy wedlock, let him speak now, or forever hold his peace", the groom loudly spoke up, "I do."

CHAPTER IV 

·NTO a splendid colonial home in Charlotte, adjoining the house of his mother and sisters, General Johnston carried his youthful bride. 

If her husband trembled with fear at the altar, his wife quaked with dread for years under the scrutinizing and critical glances of her two efficient sisters-in-law. Their fame as housekeepers kept her forever in a feverish state of mental apology. She could never see how one eternally saw dust and cobwebs in a world of sunshine and clouds.

From the beginning their marriage proved ideal. The disparity in years, instead of separating, seemed to unite the pair more closely. The love of husband was supplemented with the tenderness of a father for a beloved child, while it created in the young wife a feeling of deference towards his opinions and wishes. With similar tastes and ideals and the complete sympathy in each other's interests, time but drew them closer. In this home, six of their eight children were born, Louise, the eldest coming the following year.

When this baby was six months old, General Johnston took his family to New York to consult a specialist about his wife's hearing. An attack of diphtheria, at the time of her little sister's death, had left her slightly deaf, which had increased since the birth of their child. 

After several treatments and detailed instructions for home usage, the physician was very hopeful of Mrs. Johnston's improvement, but specified that they return in a year that she might have a check up. On scheduled time they were back with high hopes. The great specialist, though a man of marked medical ability, evidently was

lacking in facial expression, for after greeting them, he turned to the woman, and without change of countenance, nor note of compassion in his voice said, "I have suffered a great loss since you were here. My wife has died."

Hearing so largely by sight, his patient smiled one of her most engaging smiles and replied, "Oh, I am so glad.”

Still as wooden as before, the physician merely turned to the husband and said, "I am sorry, but I do not think I can help your wife." 

When she learned of her dreadful mistake, she said, "Well, however was I to know he had had a sorrow, if he failed to suit his expression to his emotion." But from that moment she knew henceforth she was to face a more silent world. It is possible then, her inner ear became more sensatized and she began to hear with her heart. 

Louise was two when Gordon was born, and being able at the time to sing correctly something like a dozen hymns, she tried to instruct her infant brother, whom she thought was all too slow in mental attainments. As soon as he was able to toddle and speak in simple sentences, Louise had already settled on his career. 

It soon became known that she intended her brother should become a minister, and Gordon accepting her decision as final, coupled with the family's rejoicing over his father's recent connection with the church and becoming a Bible-School teacher, saw an opportunity to start out on his profession. Every afternoon on returning from his office, General Johnston was met at the front door by Gordon holding out the Bible to him. But there was one hitch in the selection of his life's work. He wanted to be sure there were horses in Heaven before he renounced the world forever. On his mother's assurance that she felt confident that there were, he was entirely satisfied. It was in these early married years that Mrs. Johnston had one of her premonitions that came in the form of a dream. On account of her deafness, her two children slept in the room with her mother. One night she dreamed she heard her mother coming down stairs, calling her name in a voice of alarm, entering the room with Louise clasped in her arms, saying with agitation, “Our baby has a spasm.”

She then seated herself in a rocking chair on the left of the fireplace. In her dream, Mrs. Johnston immediately jumped up, ran to the cupboard where she found kindling, made a fire, and running to the back door called the man servant to go for the doctor. She, then, returned to the room and prepared some mustard plasters, which she put to the child's back, wrists and ankles, and was just putting her in a warm bath, when the door opened and instead of their family physician, who lived next door, a stranger walked in. He went right up to the child and looking at her anxiously said, "I can do nothing for her, she is dying."

At that instant, in great distress, Mrs. Johnston aroused herself to find that she was sitting up in bed, shaking with terror, but at the same time she heard her mother calling on the stairway-then she entered the room, her baby clasped in her arms, repeating the very words of the dream, as she took the chair by the fireplace. Under the impluse of the dream, the mother rushed to the cupboard, and sure enough there she found kindling, made a fire, sent the servant for the doctor, applied the mustard, and put the baby in the bath, when a strange physician appeared in the room. Of course she thought he would make the same pronouncement, but instead he said, after an examination, "You have done everything that I could have done, and the spasm is passing." 

The remarkable thing was that Mrs. Johnston had never

before seen a child in a spasm, and before going to sleep Iwould not have known how to act under such circumstances. 

But alas, in all too short a time, the completed dream was fulfilled, for when Louise was nearly five, the city of 

Charlotte was stricken with a diphtheria epidemic and she was an early victim. Taken ill on Sunday night, she passed away on Tuesday, saying as her little spirit took wings, "I'm getting better, I'm getting better."

The house was silent. Loneliness and a thousand memories. But her short span of life shed an immortal fragrance throughout the years. One must go on living, and Johnsie 

Johnston knew only to live with courage. Her love for her fellow man proved her salvation. The destroying fire in her hand became a beacon, shedding light on many discouraged lives. It was quite by accident her attention was called to the fact that a colored woman had been severely burned. Going to see her, Mrs. Johnston realized that she needed hospital care, so she called in her own physician to take the case and have her moved to the hospital, only to learn that there were no colored wards in the one small hospital.

This lack of provision for the negro population of the town seemed inexcusable to the woman, so she started out that very day to try to remedy this condition. She talked to physicians, preachers, laymen and women, but no one seemed to feel quite as she did about the matter. Then she decided to lay the cause before her missionery society. 

When the day rolled around for her to make the plea, she felt downhearted and frightened, so to give herself courage, she walked down the miserable, dingy alley, where the woman lay suffering, but instead of being helped she seemed to lose heart entirely. In desperation, she stopped dead still in her tracks and cried aloud, "O, Lord, why did you start me at this. You know I

9 have no money to build a hospital myself, and lack the ability to make others see the need!" "I slew a thousand Phillistines with the jaw-bone of an ass," she seemed to hear a voice say distinctly, and she burst out laughing. “All right then," she said, “I have the jaw-bone!" "Many a time since, in extra hard places the Lord and I have had a good laugh about that," she solemnly declared, and the hospital for the negro population of Charlotte became an assured thing that day. "It's funny," said Mrs. Johnston, in recalling this circumstance, “that the building of this negro hospital and the great Charleston earthquake are tied up in my memory with an amusing family incident. They must have happened about the same time, although I'm poor on dates." 

The Charleston earthquake was felt in Charlotte, N. C., with terrifying severity, occurring around eight in the evening, when sleepy children and emotional servants were tired. They were thrown into an utter state of terror. When the Johnston servants felt the house shake and heard dishes rattle, they dropped everything and wailed, "It's judgment day! It's judgment day!" "It's judgment day," loudly echoed the Johnston children, running to their parents in terror-that is, all but eight-year-old Robert. When he heard the pronouncement, he went straight to the table and grabbed the Bible and calmly seated himself in a chair, and oblivious of all, loudly pleaded with the Lord to forgive him one sin which he specifically named. The tremor subsided, and the other children were comforted with the hope that all was over, but Robert quietly sat and waited, and in a short time another quake was felt. With it, Robert released another sin, then another quake, and still another sin was loudly broadcast. This serious little felow seemed unconcerned that he alone was repenting. He manifested no missionary

spirit in suggesting others had better follow his example. 

His sole concern was saving his own soul. 

After all danger seemed to have passed and re-assurance restored, Mrs. Johnston tried to persuade Robert to go to bed with his sisters and brother, but he would not stir. He was still doubtful about its not being judgment day and he had no intention of being caught napping. Clutching the 

Bible tight in his chubby little hands, he looked up at his mother and said, "You see, Mummie, I can't go to sleep 'cause I have one more sin to tell and I'm not going to tell it 'till the very last quake comes."

But around midnight, with a new day in the making, and feeling convinced the fatal one was deferred, the tired little transgressor went confidently to bed, seemingly satisfied that he had kept his own counsel. "But you know," merrily laughed his mother, "I have never been quite sure I was not a bit sorry that we didn t have just one more little tremor to satisfy my own curiosity, for who could knowingly pry into the secrets of a little soul so fresh from God, nor fail to respect such canny discretion!"

Johnsie Johnston's few trips into the outside would had innoculated her with the wanderlust germ, and when Robert was a few years younger than the time of his confessing, she lifted her sight even beyond New York out across the Atlantic, but she was far too wise to announce she yearned to go abroad. That would have sounded like wanton extravagance. Instead, in a subtle way, she made her desires fit into a most worthy object. The church papers were full of accounts of the coming celebration of the one hundredth birthday of Gaike-the father of the Sunday-School-which celebration was to be held in London that summer. With heartfelt gratitude to this great-visioned man for having

a birthday, she convinced her husband that as a Sunday School teacher, this was an opportunity he could ill afford to miss. Leaving their two small boys with Gen. Johnston's mother, the Johnstons sailed away with Mrs. Johnston's brother, Smith, and her cousins, Addie Avery and John

Walker. 

Further than the means of the trip, she only vaguely remembered the Gaike celebration, nor did the fashions of Paris tempt her, not even to buying a gray dress! The Art 

Galleries and great Cathedrals were not her absorbing interests, as she recalled this European trip. Unlike two of her daughters, she did not seek an audience with the Pope, so she never knew whether she would have kissed his hand twice, as did one, or refuse to kiss it at all, as did the other, but all through life she was stirred by the memory of the Catacombs, where the early Christians suffered and died. and the ancient Roman prisons where countless souls were tortured. She said if it had not been for the peace on the face of the Sistine Madonna, a copy of which she brought home with her, the lasting impression of that trip would have been one long memory of man's inhumanity to man. 

For twenty years the Johnstons had taken an active part in the religious, social, and civic life of Charlotte, their roots were stuck deep in its soil, and they were content.

Gordon was being prepared for Princeton by a professor who had come from Virginia. One Sunday he asked permission to accompany this man to Pratt Mines, operated by the convicts. The professor was in the habit of conducting a Bible class for the prisoners on Sunday. A few Sundays later his mother went with them. When she entered the somber, grey place, she suddenly remembered the promise she had made as a little girl, on hearing of her father's death in prison. Here, then, at last, was the opportunity to do something for those behind bars, but somehow it did not seem

to be just this kind of a prison she had pictured. After several trips to the mines, in which her husband joined them, they realized the need of regular Sunday School classes for the convicts, and after much discussion, it was decided she should go regularly and establish a Sunday- School, and he would stay home and instill sound Presbyterian doctrines into the six little Johnstons. Her children, many a time, wished they were the prisoners at Pratt Mines! If one is unfamiliar with an old-fashioned Presbyterian 'at home' on Sunday afternoon, there is no way of making one understand. Presbyterians know early that the chief end of man is to glorify God, but they utterly failed to enjoy Him forever. Her daughter, Nancy, says the joy of her young life was being allowed, on rare occasions, to escape those Sunday afternoons at home, and go with her mother. There was first a street car ride, a short train ride, and then the last two miles of the journey in a spring wagon, driven by a prisoner, with maybe the added delight of an apple, given her by the driver. She looked around at the prisoners, listening to her mother's beautiful stories, as she taught the huge class, and thought possibly they imagined themselves miserable and unhappy, but they just did not know. Besides her agreement with her husband, I have a notion that Johnsie Johnston had one of her heart to heart talks about the matter with her Lord, bargaining with Him to the effect that if she did this outside work for Him, He would do a little extra guiding of her children. With her own mind at ease about the well-being of her children, for over ten years she gave up her time to the teaching of the prisoners. She was a mother superior to the 'Life-Termer', a mother confessor to the man condemned, and a mother of opportunity to the man released. She had a shepherd's instinct towards the man on the bleak and windy side of life. Humanity needs constant cheering and stimulus, and with her ringing words of hope she brought a new light, a real message. She, somehow, never saw the evil, but with eyes of tolerance looked beyond the visible, catching hold of every spark of good there was, showing it to the man himself, as something to build upon. Years later after the establishment of the Alabama Boy's Industrial School, a very difficult lad was sent to them. He seemed to be unapproachable. Sullen, morose and silent;

no one ever saw him smile and conversation was limited to "yes" and "no". Then one day at the swimming pool, as he was resting on the bank, he noticed a boy drowning. 

In he plunged and gallantly rescued him, at his own peril, for he was already tired when he plunged in. The Colonel of the School sent his name in for a Carnegie medal, but even this aroused no spark of interest in him. Mrs. Johnston heard about the incident and sent for him; looking into his face, she said, "My son, I have heard of you and your bravery, and first I want to tell you heroes are not born in a moment. 

I know your name is Charles C............ and there once was a brave and grand man named Charles C.......... He was a signer of the Declaration of Independence, a great American Patriot. You have his name and you must have some of his blood in your veins. You have just done something worthy of the name you bear. He threw out his chest, lifted his chin and without saying much, he started right then acting the part she expected him to play. 

One certain Sunday afternoon, as she sat in the prison chapel surrounded by all that army of men in their stripes of degradation, and thinking of it as a vertiable Dante's Inferno, she noticed a gentle, simple old woman in the front row. Her hair was parted and folded over her ears, a snow white kerchief draped over her breast-a perfect picture of a mother. By her side sat a frightened, pale lad in stripes. At the close of the service a guard stepped up, touched her on the arm, telling her she must leave. A pitiful, sharp scream filled the silent hall. With her arms around her boy, forgetting the crowd about her, forgetting

everything but the reality that she must leave her child in this place of desolation, she cried out in her anguish, "Oh, God, is there no one to comfort my boy now?" The woman in the back of the hall heard the cry, and her own heart was pierced by its agony. She responded to the prayer, with a prayer. "If it be Thy will, Lord," she whispered, "I will make some place for the little boys of Alabama, that they may be spared this place of wretchedness and contamination."

Thus with a mother's travail, the birth pains of a great institution quickened. From that moment, Mrs. Johnston knew her ultimate objective. Only her Lord knew how hard and rough, and nigh insurmountable, the reaching of that objective would be.

She had been vouchsafed an extra anointing with the oil of gladness and by her presence and her Sunday talks a little sunlight was beginning to find its way into prison walls. Religion seemed a happy vocation to her and the convicts felt about her Christianity much the same as her young niece did on her first trip to New York, "Aunt

Johnsie, if Heaven is like New York, I'm going to be a Christian."

One afternoon when she was stressing the power of prayer and urging the men to pray, a lad of twenty spoke up saying, "I have tried to pray, Mrs. Johnston, but whenever I start, all I seem to hear is 'twenty years, twenty years.' 999 The lad, four years before, had been forced by older criminals to loot a store. They escaped, but he and his brother were caught and sent up for "twenty years." The following week she kept thinking of that poor boy, and the thought “20 years" kept coming up before her, so it came as no surprise the next Sunday to learn he had escaped with several older prisoners, a guard being killed. 

Eight years later there appeared at her door a splendid

looking young man. "Don't you know me, Mrs. Johnston?" he asked, visibly disappointed, “I am John B.......... and I have come for you to give me up."

Then he told the story of his escape, of his finding his way into a distant state, of getting work and prospering, of his marriage and being the father of two children. With the respect of the community in which he lived and every. thing in his favor, he had to come back to give himself up. "When anyone looks at me suddenly," he said, "I think they know my past. "No, no," the woman said, "No, I can't give you up." Many of the people approached had entirely forgotten all about the lad sentenced to "twenty years," for something he in no way instigated. HE pathway of the soul-builder is strewn with wrecked hopes, disappointed desires, and broken lives. If such were the facts in Mrs. Johnston's career, she never once voiced them. Always a glorious optomist, with an unshakable faith, untiring persistence and constant encouragement, she refused to see anything between herself and her objective. Her reform work certainly did call for unusual courage, in the face of public sentiment, which at the time, opposed women doing any sort of work outside the home, and work among prisoners was particularly radical. It was because of the untrod path she was taking that she perhaps anticipated little of its real difficulties. Taking faith as her guide, she started for the 

Open Road. 

She had no way of knowing before her lay the most beautiful, fruitful and satisfying adventure of her life.

She simply followed the dream which led her to the cot of a young boy in the prison hospital. The thing that somehow drew her towards him was a pair of haunted eyes which seemed to follow her around. He was enveloped in a silent reserve and sadness. Scarcely a word of response could she get to her inquiries as to the nature of his illness, but she divined the suffering. Here was a wounded soul. 

A few sentences were passed and she realized he was an English gentleman. It was not until after several visits that he disclosed the fact that with an inheritance he had come to America. As many Englishmen were officials in the steel and iron industries in and around Birmingham, she concluded this was probably the reason he had drifted to Birmingham, but she never knew how he found himself

in prison. She did not care to know the past, nor anything of the crime. It was only a brother she saw who was in trouble and needed help. 

Several weeks later, when he had overcome some of his reserve with her, he mentioned how terrible it was to be cut off from communication with his family, particularly his mother, but, he quickly added with a shudder, “I could never write her from this place." That was enough for Mrs. Johnston, so at once a letter went from a mother in America to an anxious one in England, telling her that she had met her boy in a hospital where he was convalescing from an illness, and that he was doing some work for the state, making it difficult to reach him, but any letters she wrote in her care would be given to him. It was not long before Mrs. Johnston was the proud bearer of the longed-for letter from his mother. Sensitive, nervous fingers tore it open and a five pound note fell upon the floor. 

After reading its contents, he said ironically. "My mother sends me this money 'to take my girl to the opera', when I have recovered." The travesty of it made the stripes of dishonor he wore burn into his very soul. "But you can use the money to bring greater joy than ever an opera would," said the woman with an encouraging smile.

In all her reform work she began and ended by discarding as much 'red tape' as possible. No inviolate rules and no precedents to hamper and stiffle soul-building. 

Years later, when speaking before a large audience in Virginia, at the conclusion, a learned divine came up to her saying, "Mrs. Johnston, I was greatly impressed with your 'Heads'.” “With my heads", laughed the speaker, “I did'nt know I had any 'heads'. I only have a heart."

As this great new venture of hers began to take shape, there were others, besides her husband, who realized they, too, would have to sacrifice that this work might come into being-the prisoners at Pratt Mines, who had become so dependent upon her weekly visits, must relieve her of that work. The Legislature of Alabama met in 1898. A farewell to

62 an old century had been sung, and unknowingly also a farewell to an old order of things. The women of Alabama had become mother-conscious under the sway of one woman's vision of a greater love, and their motherhood had burst the walls of home and become state inclusive. The old Capitol at Montgomery became the scene of a new Confederacy. Headed by Mrs. Johnston, these earnest women came before the legislature with their demand for a Boy's Industrial School, with a carefully prepared Charter which included the new and original clause of a woman board of managers. This was Mrs. Johnston's own idea of bringing the mother love of home into the impersonal institution. With the adoption of this Charter, approved by the Legislature, February 23, 1899, the first women's board of a Boys' Industrial School of America became a reality. The women asked for an appropriation of three thousand dollars! Those were the days when money was money, not in the light of present day mathematical magic. This small sum was the amount they dared to ask to save hundreds of boys. An amount that numbers of speakers receive for a fifteen-minute radio talk today. 

Perhaps if they had asked for a larger appropriation, their fight might not have been so great. Staggering amounts assume greater dignity. 

The Attorney General of Alabama, glancing at the earnest faces of the women lobbyists, sensed the way the wind was blowing and rushed into print, crying aloud to the people that what Alabama most needed was some place for the juvenile delinquent. Thinking here was a sympathetic ear awaiting them, Governor Johnston told his sister-in-law to go and talk to the Attorney General. Instantly, the wrath of God entered Mrs. Johnston's soul, and she replied, "What does your wife do with her time?

Still the women got no nearer the Appropriation Committee with their bill than this interview, and time was passing. Finally, near the close of the Legislature, one of the legislators came to Mrs. Johnston and said, "Mrs.

With the smallest per capita appropriation of school of its kind in the United States, and inadequate financial backing, hard work and implicit faith saw seventeen industries installed, together with grade school, junior high, through high school in those infant days of the school. The boy, on leaving, is able to secure good jobs with adequate pay, because of educational and industrial training. any Realizing that responsibility brings out the best qualities of a boy, this home-school allows the boy every liberty he seems worthy of. Some of the boys would never have had the opportunity on the outside that the boys here have.

A great day came to the School when Colonel Gordon Johnston wired his mother that he and General Leonard Wood would make an inspection of the school early the following morning. Colonel Johnston was on General

Wood's staff and they were on an inspection tour in the south, and the General was particularly desirous of seeing this school. It was eight-thirty when the General and their hero, Colonel Johnston, arrived, before any one under ordinary circumstances had time to clean house, and the school had received only an hour's notice. When they arrived, the boys were in military formation and General Wood immediately began the inspection. After the drill-

ing, he went through the quarters from cellar to garret, and when he was through he turned to Mrs. Johnston and said, "This institution is all right. You have very poor equipment and you are very poorly clothed, but there is no institution that smells better."

Hanging on the wall in the library of the school is the letter he wrote Mrs. Johnston a few days after the visit:

I want to thank you for the opportunity which you gave me to see your school.

Very sincerely yours, Leonard Wood

At The Boys' Industrial School the motto is "Give the boys a square deal and a square meal." Coupled with this, Mrs. Johnston's faith in them has enabled her to sell her ideals to the state at large. The school has had enthusiastic recognition of the entire city of Birmingham and the State of Alabama. The boys have always been made to feel they

were real assets of their state and this inspires them to do even better than their best.

This was another bit of Mrs. Johnston's home psychology that she carried into the school. "Mrs. Johnston's children will all grow up to be wonderful," said a neighbor's child one day, "she never criticizes their failures. She always finds something in them to praise, by telling them she knows they will grow up to be good and great." Her confidence in them acts as a spur. Still with that quick twinkle in her eye, she was apt to warn them to beware of the smug complacency of their brother, Robert, when he returned from his first day at school, announcing he would never go back. "Why do you feel that way?" gently inquired his alarmed mother that this son's education was to begin and end at age seven. "The teachers don't know how smart I am, and wouldn't let me tell them."

She might be generous in her praise of others, but public recognition of her own achievements always seemed to stampede her. In 1922, she was awarded a silver loving cup by The Birmingham News, for having rendered the most outstanding piece of philanthropic work of the year. Except that the front seats were all filled with her boys, and the school band surrounded her on the stage, she said she might have run. When the cup was presented, in a most impressive speech by the editor, the only thing that enabled her to stand and face it, was the inability to hear all of the beautiful tributes being paid her, so she just smiled a heavenly smile, with a light in her eyes that gleamed like stars, and as soon as it ended, she quickly raised her hands with an upward gesture to all her boys, calling out, "Jump up, boys, jump up. This cup is not for me. It's your loving cup."

A strapping young man in the rear of the hall seemed so deeply moved by the proceedings that an old man by

 him said. "You are so noticeably affected by this scene, do you happen to know her?" "Know her," was the lad's proud reply, "she is the only mother I have ever known."

In joy and sorrow, she was ever mindful of the Giver of all gifts, and she instilled this practice in her boys. Once while teaching the Sunday School class, a telegram was brought her, stating her grandson, Hartwell Eager, was not expected to live.

After reading the wire, she slowly put it down, as if trying to decide what to do, and then quietly said, "Boys, this telegram tells me one of my grandsons is not expected to live. Now I want you to put everything aside and silently pray for him.” 

All heads instantly bowed-then out of the stillness a clear, shrill little voice shouted, "And she don't mean maybe!"

In the next few hours came the 'miracle' that puzzled the great doctor at an Atlanta hospital. The boy suddenly rallied and he pronounced him out of danger. He was healed by many prayers.

Is it any wonder that these boys, raised in such simple faith, living in the atmosphere of continual answered prayer, feel they have a real place in the world in which they live? Is it any wonder here is an industrial school whose alumni boast of the Alma Mater? "What can we do for the school, Mrs. Johnston," asked a member of the legislative committee, on one of their official visits. "Keep the Bible in the school and politics out,” replied the sweet voiced lady of a thousand sons.

ERSONAL, as well as family activities, were claiming a large share of Mrs. Johnston's attentions during these years.

After Gordon's taste of war under the gallant Roosevelt, he determined, if possible, to enter the army, but the chance of an appointment being somewhat remote, did not mention his intentions to his parents. Quite out of a clear sky came a letter from him in the late summer of 1899, stating Governor Roosevelt had succeeded in getting him appointed a lieutenant in the regular army, never dreaming his parents would not rejoice with him, but he had not reckoned on his unreconstructed mother. 

To her, it was an unbelievable thing. The very thought kindled anew all the childhood memories of suffering and bitterness she had been trying to forget all her life. Her son deserting the cause of his father she reasoned unjustly; her son, whose grandfather had been cut down so early in his youth, wearing the 'blue' of the enemy. She went into a mental tailspin, but she did not visit her wrath on Gordon, but rather upon Governor Roosevelt, whom she hastily decided had over-persuaded him; so in the heat of the moment wrote him her feelings on the subject. 

Like many another troubled night through which we pass into the clear calm morning of a kinder day, she quickly realized what a wrong thing she had done, so another letter immediately followed her first. The answer to both came promptly in true Rooseveltian fashion.

Twenty-five years later, in awarding Colonel Johnston the Distinguished Service Cross he was told he had received every decoration in the power of the United States Government to bestow upon an officer of the Army. These decorations included the Medal of Honor, decorations of extraordinary heroism and Distinguished Service in 

Philippines Insurrection and the World War. Besides, he was entitled to wear three stars on his campaign badge for further instances of heroism during the Spanish American war. He also had Chinese and Japanese decorations, and was an officer of the Legion of Honor of France and awarded the Purple Heart.

Having thus been so signally honored for distinguished service to his country, we wonder when this final decoration was pinned upon his breast, if his first great leader was priviledged to look down and witness the ceremony, would he still have said, "I wish to Heaven I had not got him his commission." Most certainly the proud mother standing by her hero son was breathing a prayer of “Thank 

Heaven you did it, Colonel Roosevelt."

While this first son of hers was learning the great business of soldiering and her second son, Robert, was assiduously mastering Blackstone, a new and unexpected responsibility and honor came to her, in the appointment as Vice- Regent of Alabama of the Mount Vernon Association, left vacant by the death of Miss Herbert, daughter of Secretary of Navy, Hilary Herbert, under President Cleveland.

At the time it seemed this one woman was already doing the work of twelve, but it was not the extra duties that caused her to hesitate. It was the breaking of a precedent she had strictly adhered to. She believed in sticking to one special line of work, and not spreading one's self out too thin. "Be known by your work," she would say. She had been asked to plead for a girls' school, to sell Liberty

bonds, to use her gift of speech for this worthy cause and that, but she always declined, saying. "No, I can't. I must be identified with 'boys', so whenever I rise to speak my audience will always see boys, boys -hundreds of boys."

This one and only time, she deviated from her set path, and she regarded this later activity in the light of needed recreation, "A sort of dessert", as she put it, "in my very plain diet of life." It became her hobby, and to her, a hobby was a vocation. Her nose for Mount Vernon relics became unequaled. Once on the trail of a lock of Washington's hair or a piece of Martha Washington's personal belongings, she never turned loose until the treasure was safely within Mount Vernon.

Her aunt, Mrs. Walker, had been Mount Vernon's first 

Vice-Regent from North Carolina, and as a young woman, she had been steeped in the dramatic history of its reclamation by Miss Cunningham of South Carolina and her 

Board of Women Associates. As a young girl, her romantic soul had thrilled, when on her first trip north, she had been awakened out of sleep, by the tolling of the ship's bell. 

Jumping out of bed and sticking her head out of the port hole, she called to a sailor, and asked if the boat was sinking. “No, Miss," was the amused reply, "we are passing the home of President George Washington." "How thrilling," she replied, and her youthful heart knelt in gratitude for the Father of her country. As the bell continued its tolling, she was filled with a burning desire then and there to do something for the memory of this great American. What she later really did is written in its records. › 

Her hours of rejoicing over this honor and privilege that had come to her were cut short at the moment for the legislature of 1901 was in session and Mrs. Johnston had heart and mind for nothing else. The School was moving

forward, boys by the dozens waiting to be enrolled, the appropriation too meagre for even necessities, and an unsympathetic legislature fighting a larger appropriation. 

Feeling that something must be done, one of the members interested in the school went to the Speaker of the House, Mr. Pettus, and told him he thought the only way to save the situation was to have Mrs. Johnston speak before a joint session. Finally this was agreed upon, and Mr. 

Pettus, a most courtly gentleman, came out into the rotunda where Mrs. Johnston was, and with a stately bow said, "Mrs. Johnston, the House has adjourned to hear you speak, Madam.” "I don't suppose," she said afterwards, “if I had a death sentence pronounced upon me, I could have been more terrified than to be called to speak before the Legislature of Alabama, where no woman ever before had spoken.' 

This was an unexpected turn she had not counted on. 

Button-holing individuals in the lobby had become a habit, but standing up before the whole body was something new. She instantly became all feminine. She glanced at her dress, an ugly home-made snuff colored dress, she remembered her hat was her own handiwork and even her shoes were not her best! She decided then that a pretty, good fitting dress, rather than a clear conscience gives a woman self-confidence! In after years, whenever she thought of that outfit, she invariably suffered acute anguish, but the die was cast and she entered the great hall clothed in home-created snuff color, but "proudly leaning on God." The room was filled with men, and she looked about her for one sympathetic face, when, finally, her eyes fell upon a lone woman in the gallery. She was convinced she must have looked faint, for a little page came rushing up to her with a glass of water and an encouraging smile. 

Then she began speaking. Her voice sounded so strange that she was even more frightened, but once started she

soon forgot herself in her plea for her boys, forgot the snuff colored dress, makeshift hat, and even the lone woman. Her words fell like stepping stones across the field of thought and came from her mouth like jewels. In the beginning, the men sat adamant, then they slowly seemed to come to life, then they leaned forward drinking in every word, but when that great body of everyday men began pulling out handkerchiefs and blowing their noses, the sweet voiced psychologist stopped suddenly with the words: "And now, gentlemen, I leave my boys, and your boys, with you." "That was the very first speech the Lord ever made for me," she said with a smile, "and I was too frightened to listen." It was a masterful appeal, even if she didn't hear it, for it carried the day as well as the increased appropriation, and cast the shadow of the time, when all Alabama legislature troubles would be at an end. 

This legislative experience of hers recalls a similar one she had in North Carolina a few years later. The women of her native state were wrestling with the legislature for an industrial school of their own, and seemed to be making poor headway. Then, one of them suggested they wire 

Mrs. Johnston to come to their rescue. As quickly as the train could bring her, she was there and appeared before the committee having the bill under consideration. She presented the details of the Alabama school, its formation and progress, read parts of the charter and answered all questions put to her.

After the hearing, there was much discussion of the joint meeting of the two Houses to be held that evening at the Capitol and she expressed delight that she was to have the opportunity of attending this open session. She did not explain that her delight consisted chiefly in being able to take a back seat and watch others do the fighting.

As they entered the Capitol that evening she turned to her hostess and said, "Who is your speaker tonight?" "Why you are to address the meeting," was her astonished hostess' reply. The misunderstanding came about in the fact the women had overlooked that rather important little detail in writing Mrs. Johnston to come to their rescue. "You don't mean I am expected to speak without preparation," she gasped. "You certainly are going to speak. That is what the meeting is for," was the stern reply. 

All the anticipated joy vanished. Here in the same Capitol, where her gandfather's voice had rung out and moved his hearers, she was to speak, and possibly bring disgrace upon the name.

She took her seat on the platform with Honorable Josephus Daniels and she was being introduced by Mrs. 

Stonewall Jackson, but no definite inspiration came to her. Then, as she was about to rise, the doors opened and an officer entered, pushing back the overflow crowd that packed the hall. The band struck up and in marched dozens and dozens of little boys, every one carrying a banner of some state's industrial school, headed by Alabama, with a boy carrying one for North Carolina at the end. The woman's whole being quickened at the sight. The little boys were there, and with them, her inspiration. As she rose to her feet, to herself she whispered, "You make the speech, Lord, and I'll deliver it.”

The voice was low in the beginning, but its musical notes came as effortless as a prayer from an humble soul. 

The words alternately charmed and thrilled. She marshalled them as a general would his army, with life and pigment in them.

It is told of her, that at one time of great political stress,

the state chairman of Alabama, searching for its most vigorous, convincing and entertaining campaign speaker, came to the governor for suggestions. "Whom do you consider the state's best stump speaker?” “Why, Mrs. R. D. Johnston, of course," was his quick reply. 

Every May found Mrs. Johnston at Mount Vernon.

During this month, the Regent and Vice-Regent meet for ten days to transact the stupendous amount of work relative to Mount Vernon. For the first ten years of Mrs. 

Johnston's affiliation with the Board of Directors, she said she felt quite like a 'Miss Nobody', and had to eat a lot of humble pie. She was surrounded by women much older than herself, possessed of great wealth and national prominence. There was Mrs. William Randolph Hearst, Sr., with her many millions on the one hand, and on the other, Longfellow's daughter, she who was 'Grave Alice', whom her southern sister always eyed with a sort of poetic awe. 

But her time was to come, and the incident that turned the trick was an invitation to lunch at the White House with President and Mrs. Theodore Roosevelt. After that, Alabama's stock rose!

The visit to the White House on this occasion is refreshing enough to be recorded. The Roosevelt family gathered with Mrs. Johnston in the Red room, with the conversation centering almost entirely around 'our boy', as the President always affectionately called Gordon, when with his mother. The young soldier had even then done much to make them both proud and happy, and in his vital way, the President, more than once, would jump up and clap his hands exclaiming, "Bully, bully," as the mother told of her son's achievements. She never ceased to wonder at this great man's human touch, as well as his unpresidential demeanor on this occasion.

Her son's becoming a part of the country's army did not

change her personal feelings about the matter, and it was not until we entered the World War, that in her heart she lost forever the dividing line that separated the north from the south. She told the story, herself, in speaking before the Board of Governors of the Confederate Museum in 

Richmond, Virginia. "Before we entered the World War," she said, “my youngest son, Ewart, came to me one day and asked, "Mother, why is it I have never been given a flag and taught to love it as other boys have?" "Son," I replied, "it is because I, myself, have felt I have neither a flag nor a country. (You see,' she smiled, 'how unreconstructed I was.') Some time later," she continued, "we entered the war. My son enlisted and went off to camp. Still later, he came to see me dressed in uniform. He went to the embarkation camp ready to sail for France. Then, I suddenly saw the meaning of it all, so I went out and bought two flags-one for myself and the other for my own son and sent it to him with this message, "Son, I thank God that we both have a flag and a country." 

Crowded, indeed, were all these years for this manysided woman, whose mothering side was particularly to the fore. They were the years of marked activities in her fast maturing family. Her children were marrying and being given in marriage; they were starting to colleges and being graduated from them. Wedding bells exchanged places with school bells in such rapid succession, that the tintinnabulation of the one got mixed with the other in their mother's ear. Gordon had been married some years before to Julia Johnson, of Baltimore, and once in speaking of her satisfaction over this marriage, Mrs. Johnston said, "Well, how could it be other than an ideal match? 

I have prayed for his wife since the day of his birth.”

Then, their daughter, Nancy, the beauty of the family, was married to a young Canadian banker, Harvey Skey,

followed soon by Elizabeth to Maxwell Berry, of Atlanta,

Georgia. These two older daughters were among their mother's most loyal admirers, and were ever ready to relieve her in the home when the necessity arose. Indeed, all her family were so prideful of her, and encouraging by their unselfish cooperation, that unknowingly, each of them gave of himself to her great work. 

There was just breathing time with the Johnstons, and 

Robert was married to Margaret Lutkins, of Jersey City, then came Gene's marriage to William Eager, the son of 

Mrs. Johnston's dear friend and co-worker in the first days of the Industrial School. 

Around about 1918, Letitia was married to young Captain Gerald Firth of the English army and a few years later Ewart married Cecil Carr, of California. So it came about that this woman, who had claimed to be sectional in spirit, became international in scope, reaching out with her understanding heart to embrace north, east, south, west, and beyond the seas to receive sons and daughters into her heart and home.

It was about 1915, that General Johnston retired from active law practice, and like most gentlemen of the south, dreamed of a plantation for retirement. The summers were spent at their home in Canada, but the winters in 

Birmingham no longer held strong inducements, with two of their sons over seas, their children scattered far and wide and Robert and Margaret in a home of their own in Birmingham. The husband and wife seemed to rattle around in the big old house that had been peopled with youthful groups for so many years. Quite by accident, through their daughter Gene, they learned of an apple orchard for sale near Winchester, Virginia. At once, this appealed to them and it was no sooner decided upon, than done. Later, Captain Ewart Johnston bought Clay Hill estate, a plantation almost adjoining and came there to

live after his marriage. For five years, General Johnston enjoyed life on this place. In the spring, he watched his apple trees, like popcorn in a shaker, burst into bloom.

In the fall, he saw them blush in delight with their rich gifts for man. Among his trees in week days, teaching a large Bible class on Sundays, a gallant southern soldier passed his last days.

Somehow it was as it should have been, that he died near the scene of his life's greatest action. General Johnston was the hero of the 'Tar Heel Thin Gray Line', celebrated in song and story in the south. His famous achievement was in his masterly covering of General Early's retreat from Winchester, Virginia, in 1864, when his brigade of less than a thousand North Carolina veterans held back swarms of union cavalry under Sheridan, Custer and Merritt. At the sound of the bugle, the horsemen would swoop down upon the brave Johnston boys, who would halt, 'bout face and deliver a withering fire, scattering the cavalry, then cooly face to the rear, continuing their orderly retreat. A half dozen charges were made at the back of the retreat line, and a half dozen times the charging squadrons had been driven back, and the 'Thin Gray Line' was never broken.

Thus, fittingly, the remains of this southern soldier and Brigadier General, his coffin wrapped in an old Confederate flag, was laid among his comrades of the Civil War in the Stonewall Jackson section, North Carolina Division of Mt. Hebron Cemetery, at Winchester, Virginia. The citizens of the town, the Home Guards, and the Boy Scouts marched behind the bier, to pay their respects to the eighty-year-old Christian gentleman, and to the Brigadier General, who so ably and successfully defended their valley during the sixties.

He sleeps his last sleep among the boys who died wearing the grey under his brilliant and gallant command. At

his grave stood the one remaining veteran of his brigade in that valley. Thus, one by one, these souls of the south's history have been passing on, leaving their record in black and white for all time. "Character-the greatest thing in the world," whispered his wife to their children as they left him there.

CHAPTER IX 

RS. JOHNSTON'S seventieth birthday found friends offering felicitations, but unconsciously they seemed to inject a minor note in their congratulations, as if at last surely old age might creep in upon her, causing her to slow up a bit. Mrs. Johnston sensed the thoughts of well wishers and gallantly replied: "I have no time to grow old. I only want to be outlived by my front teeth and my thrills!"

Youth is usually the time for physical activities and battling with ideals. Old age is generally supposed to be tired, disillusioned and ready to rest, with an idea that the world should slip a pillow behind its back, and put a hassock under its feet. But, this was not the way of our valiant lady. 

She was vividly alive at seventy years young, full of interest and enthusiasm. She had no patience with the present day manner of receiving things only with the lift of the eyebrow. Every day brought new experiences, and with them new enthusiasm, and an ever increasing faith in everybody and everything. A joyous outlook on life in general, she came upon old age like a galleon under sail. 

Evans, her only unmarried child, was her constant companion, and without him, the things she accomplished would not have been possible. He was both son and daughter, and gayly she added, ‘her eyes, ears and a staff upon which her body leaned.' Indeed, her last fourteen years were active and absorbing. Her children were coming to her in Virginia and Canada, and their problems were ever her first consideration.

Mount Vernon, with its vast plant was increasingly claiming more of her attention, and the Boys' Industrial

School awaited her arrival to have many knotty tangles unraveled, so she learned to parcel out her years by allotment. The spring and early summer, she spent in Winchester, except the May meeting of Mount Vernon. The late summer at Port Dover, Canada, where many of her children and grandchildren gathered around her. In the fall, she was apt to visit her children, and the winters were spent among her 'Boys' at the school. After one of these visits to her son, Gordon, and his wife, she received the following letter which gives one an insight into her ways as a house guest: 

Dear Mother: 

Fort Oglethorpe, Georgia 

Sunday, Oct. 1, 1933

Doubtless you are planning to write us a 'bread and butter letter' (being a most polite lady!) but I always feel like writing you a note of thanks for the food you furnish our souls whenever you stay with us. You have no idea how much good you do us, nor what stores you leave on the shelves of our minds, to nourish our spiritual selves after your visits. So here are a thousand thanks. 

Always lovingly yours, Julia 

To be sure of her peace and comfort, her beloved Highland Book Club built a cottage for her on the grounds of the Industrial School, which later would be used as the school's library. She named it "Little Mount Vernon" and here she always said her "sitting days" would be spent, surrounded by her 'boys'. The four-poster bed, a copy of 

General Washington's was made for her by the boys in their work shop. A set of draperies that once hung around the great General's own bed was given her by the association when new ones replaced them. A mirror that hung over an old highboy was made from wood of a walnut tree from the Mount Vernon estate. Everywhere were 

Washington prints and pictures of Mount Vernon. A little

enclosed garden at the back was filled with Mount Vernon flowers, ivy from Washington's tomb clung to the walls on every side. The spirit of Washington filled the house and its Vice-Regent instilled in the boys the courage and Christian integrity of the man himself. On one occasion when her youthful audience was thrilled by a Washington incident, a lad remarked breathlessly, "Mrs. Johnston, I feel just like I do at a ball game with the wind blowing through my hair!" Each boy's visitor was always shown

Little Mount Vernon, while he enjoyed the privilege of explaining everything about the house. 

For a few years, Mrs. Johnston suffered the ‘annoyance', as she lightly termed it, of cataracts growing over her eyes, and the boys, knowing this, were very apprehensive for her. With her deafness they feared this added misfortune might cast a shadow even on her dauntless spirit. One little fellow had taken it particularly to heart and had prayed earnestly about it, then after a time, light seemed to come to him. Rushing to her cottage one day, he screamed in her ear, "Miss Johnston, you need not be afraid of being deaf and going blind. God is going to take care of you like he did Abraham and Jacob and Isaac." He had settled the question to his satisfaction. 

For thirty years Mrs. Johnston gave her time, energy, enthusiasm and spiritual qualifications to Mount Vernon, and there was nothing that gave her greater pleasure. It is recorded, she found more authentic Washington relics than seemed possible, and with each new find came a vivid story. Miss Buchanan, of Winchester, a priceless find in herself to Mrs. Johnston, gave her several rare Washington mementoes and located many others. The four-poster camp bed and lock of Washington's hair was the beginnings of her successful quest, which in all numbered fourteen. Two objectives, she bore in mind-"Keep your ears open for Mount Vernon relics and your eyes open that the

atmosphere of the Home be not destroyed." The field bedstead with mattress, curtains and blankets, viewed with such deep interest by vistors from all parts of the world, was given Mrs. Johnston by Miss Birdie Washington. It is regarded as the most prized recovered relic and was by far the most difficult task Mrs. Johnston ever undertook for the Association. The field bedstead was used by General 

Washington in all his active campaigns, and upon his final return to Mount Vernon, he presented it to his grand nephew, George LaFayette Washington, the grandfather of Miss Birdie Washington. Miss Buchanan was Miss Birdie Washington's aunt, and for years had tried to induce her to give this prized relic back to Mount Vernon. After knowing Mrs. Johnston, she took her to see Miss Washington, thinking she might succeed, but with the Vice- Regent it was just the same. Miss Washington expressed the intention of ultimately giving it to Mount Vernon, but for the present she was loathe to part with it. “Some day I shall, but not just yet."

Time went on and that 'some day' seemed no nearer and Mrs. Johnston went again and again to present her plea. Finally, she once said in leaving, “Miss Washington, you assure me that you want General Washington's camp bed returned to his home, but as we have no record of it, I cannot help feeling apprehensive. Suppose you were to die suddenly, leaving no record of your wishes, Mount 

Vernon might never get it." "Well," she replied, “I will attend to it." But she let her friend leave with nothing more than that. For three weeks, Mrs. Johnston scanned the daily mail hoping to receive that written assurance, when instead, one morning, came a letter announcing the sudden death of Miss Washington the day before. “Alas, I have lost a friend," sighed Mrs. Johnston, "and Mount

Vernon, a treasured relic." But she was wrong, for written in Miss Washington's diary, on the day of Mrs. Johnston's

last call was the item: "Today I have given to Mount 

Vernon, General Washington's field bedstead, curtains, blankets and mattress."

Another relic that kept Mrs. Johnston in a fever of excitement for some time, was the recovery of the famous Braddock sash. This sash was used in the French and Indian War as a stretcher to carry General Braddock from the field of battle where he fell mortally wounded; nine blood stains on it. Just before he died, he gave it to his only surviving aide, Colonel Washington. Through Nellie Custis Lewis, it descended to Colonel E. George Washington 

Bittern, of New Orleans, who gave it to General Zachary 

Taylor, whose bravery he greatly admired. At General 

Taylor's death, his daughter, Mrs. Phillip Dowdridge, moved to Winchester and lived with Miss Buchanan, and at her death, left the sash to Miss Buchanan. In 1883, the 

London paper advertised for information concerning the sash. Having located it, the Prince of Wales, later Edward 

VII, sent two officers of Braddock's former command, the 

84th Essex Regiment of the Cold Stream Guards to Win chester, with the request that they be allowed to purchase it, offering Miss Buchanan three thousand dollars. She refused, because she felt as it was given to Washington by General Braddock, it rightfully belonged at Mount Vernon.

When the Braddock monument was unveiled on the spot where he fell in Pennsylvania, sixteen Englishmen were sent to represent their government. They invited 

Miss Buchanan to come as their guest and bring the sash. 

During the ceremonies, she handed it to General Codrington, who represented King George. He was deeply moved and pressing it to his heart said, "These blood stains make it seem a human thing. This sash was worn by Braddock's father, who fought with distinction under Marlborough."

Woven in the corner is the date 1709. After this occasion,

Mrs. Johnston presented the matter to the Association and it was immediately purchased by them. 

Soon after, the Vice-Regent of Georgia discovered that Washington's own sash was in a home in her state, and she solicited Mrs. Johnston's aid in procuring it. If she had wanted the Braddock sash still more did she desire this one. She spent a feverish year trying to devise means of purchasing it and begging the owners not to sell until all hope of their being able to buy was abandoned. At the next annual meeting Alabama and Georgia were fighting side by side for its recovery, but they felt it almost a lost cause. The Association had undertaken a vast improvement program and though the other members of the Board were as interested in the recovery as either Alabama or Georgia, they felt they could not let sentiment run away with their better judgment. But Mrs. Johnston had discussed the matter with Colonel Dodge, the superintendent, urging him to present the matter to some wealthy American, but so far he had received no encouragement. 

The last meeting of the Board was called to convene, and feeling it was the deadline for the purchase of this valuable relic, Mrs. Johnston fervently made a final plea. “Oh, is it possible," she cried, “that we are going to lose this sacred relic? We were willing to pay three thousand dollars for the sash of Braddock and are unwilling to pay that for the sash of the greatest man who ever lived." She was continuing her fervent appeal, when Colonel Dodge entered the room waving a telegram. She paused to hear him say, "Pierpont Morgan has just bought the Washington sash for you." In fitting and gracious appreciation, Georgia invited Alabama to present it to Mount Vernon. Thus runs the stories of recovered Washington relics credited to Alabama, from a lock of his hair to a sword which Mrs.

During the years following World War I, when visits of foreign royalty were the usual, and not the exceptional order of the day, it was Mrs. Johnston's privilege to meet most of them, for with one accord, no foreign ruler coming to America, fails to pay an official visit to the home of Washington, and the Vice-Regents living near, usually come to wecome them, with the Regent. Alabama's Vice-

Her greatest joy always in touching hands with those of high degree, was the pleasure of retelling the experience to her boys, as they gathered round her at 'Little Mount Vernon' on cold winter evenings. The story they loved best though, was the Fourth of July memorial service held at Washington's tomb, the year following the Armistice. 

To her, also, it was the most impressive ceremony she ever witnessed or was a part of. "The ceremony was to begin," she said, “about two o'clock, immediately after the arrival of President Wilson, by boat. The Regent and Vice-Regents, foreign dignitaries and American officials were gathered at the landing. President Wilson stepped off, dressed all in white, and as he marched through the

wooded land, with the long, silent line of followers marching behind him, he looked like a crusader. They slowly approached the tomb, the President going in and placing 

America's wreath upon it, and as he stepped back, fortythree nations, through their representatives, all in full military dress, one by one, laid their nation's wreath upon the tomb. The effect was electric and the vast crowd silent. In the stillness of the moment, the golden voice of 

John McCormick, standing on the slope above the tomb, rang out with the thrilling strains of ‘America'. It was one of those rare moments in life, when suddenly all the world seems bathed in the spirit of 'Peace on earth, good will to man'." And with a faint smile, she added, "If only such moments might be frozen to keep always."

In May, 1934, she made her farewell visit to this place she held so sacred, only two months after the tragic passing of her son, Colonel Gordon Johnston. The way she carried this loss among her associates excited in them something like the effect of being in the midst of a spiritual revival. "What has this woman that we have not, that lifts her so sublimely above a great sorrow," was the question they were heard asking one another. Then, two nights before their departure, as they were seated around the great living room, the Regent, Mrs. Richards, spontaneously sensing the others' feelings, asked Mrs. Johnston if she would talk to them. This unusual request caused her momentary surprise and she hesitated an instant, but with face lighting up, the most musical of voices replied that though she did not know what they would like her to talk about, she could always 'Witness for her Lord.' What she said to them must have been satisfying, for the next day one of the number said there was a radiant glow on every woman's face, as if they had glimpsed a bit of heaven. They somehow felt for her, earth was receding, not from physical weakening, but rather from a spiritual strengthening.

CHAPTER X 

ER twilight and evening shade was to Johnsie Evans Johnston just a vast sunset processional marching down the sky. No one really grows old by merely living a number of years. People grow old by deserting their ideals. So long as the heart receives and radiates messages of beauty, hope, cheer, grandeur, courage, and power from God and from your fellow man, so long you are young. Mrs. Johnston wore laughter on her lips, though her heart might be in pain.

She received the tragic news that her son, Colonel Gordon Johnston, had been severely injured in a polo game at Fort Sam Houston, Texas, dying a few hours later. She had easy access to the source of all comfort, as everyone knew she would, for when they came to comfort her, found themselves comforted. She surprised those who knew her best, when the morning after, being Sunday, she quietly dressed, put on her bonnet and said to her daughter, Gene. "Come on, we must go to church. If I am not there the boys will think my heart is broken." And so she went, singing with her boys "When my Cross Becomes a Crown", while from innumerable pulpits in Birmingham churches, prayers were going up that all she had done for others might flood back upon her at this hour and bring her peace.

Just before her eightieth birthday, the Legislature of Alabama passed the following resolution, adopted by acclamation:

Be it resolved by the House, the Senate concurring, That the Legislature of Alabama expresses the gratitude of the state to 

Mrs. R. D. Johnston, of Birmingham, Alabama, for her unselfish service in inaugurating and establishing, under great

difficulties, and in being largely the guiding spirit for the past 30 years of its existence of the Alabama Boys' Industrial School, and also for her religious and educational work among prisoners of Alabama during the ten years preceeding the founding of the Alabama Boys' Industrial School. 

Mrs. Johnston has never received any compensation and payment of any kind for her forty years of unselfish, devoted work among the unfortunate, and as she is now approaching her eightieth year, the Legislature of Alabama deems this an appropriate time not only to congratulate her and wish her many years of health and happiness, but to assure her of the love and gratitude of the people of Alabama.

The Birmingham News in printing this resolution had this to say: "Perfect though this tribute may be, to one whose mothering heart divined the need for an Institution of the character she devised, The News cannot resist recalling that eight years ago Mrs. Johnston was awarded The Birmingham News Loving Cup for her profound and unfaltering devotion. By that powerul service of hers, she answered the deepest and tenderest crying of the hearts of the wayward youth of the state, vicariously through the 

Alabama Boys' Industrial School. 

"Since 1900, this great mother served Alabama's children. Her work has been the redemption of demoralized lads. She divined underneath the jackets of the boys that society had called criminals and vicious-sparks of nobleness and beauty. Surely this 'Goodness and mercy shall follow her all the days of her life'."

These beautiful tributes were received by her with sweet amazement, only as one of the joys in the game of life. 

Always so praiseful of others, she never stopped to think any was her due. Only once did she show childlike delight in an expression of this kind of gratitude. One Christmas morning a package came to her. The family stood around while she opened it, and to their surprise, what did the

package reveal, but twelve silver forks, with a card enclosed from the 'Prisoners of the Penitentiary'! On the back of each was engraved "Matt. 25-26. In prison and ye came to me."

As the summer of 1934 approached there was much discussion back and forth among Mrs. Johnston's children, as to the advisability of her going to Canada for the summer. They felt the long motor trip might be too much for her in the first place, and feared the comings and goings of a number of her grandchildren, with their friends would interfere with her rest, but they reckoned without her knowledge.

Instead of being too much for her, it was just what she decided she needed, and so she joined nine or ten of her nineteen grandchildren there. Among them were Louise and Larry Skey, Betty and Maxwell Berry with a few younger Berry's, Johnsie Eager, and Daphne, Lindsey and

Gerald Firth. In speaking of it afterwards she said, with a delicious laugh, "Here I was, flirting with all the beaux, forgetting I was as old as the everlasting hills." But in that, she was wrong for in the center of her heart was an evergreen tree, and its name was Love. So long as it flourishes, you are young. Besides, she had always cultivated an appetite for adventure, rather than love of ease. Thus it was that a large group of youthful spirits gathered at Port Dover in the summer of 1934. The grown-up grandchildren with their sweethearts made merry, while the younger of them watched on in wide-eyed wonder. In the late afternoon, or immediately after supper, it was their custom to gather around their grandmother, as they did as little children, and call for stories. Not now tales of fairies and long ago heroes, but generally those of present day men and women. Stories of men having lost their way, finding it again; of boys, by the hundreds, who having taken the wrong turn in the road were guided back. Then, she

would thrill them with the many great physical changes she had lived to see. How she was born under the tallow dip; how as a young woman she had fretted under the ceaseless trimming of unruly lamp wicks; how she marveled when she heard the first voice over the telephone wire; and how she had made her first radio talk. As she stood up before the little black microphone to say something about the Boys' School, with just that little black bulb and herself alone in the room, she became speechless, and then an idea came to her. Putting her mouth up to the microphone she called for help, "If any of my boys -my hundreds of boys-are out there listening in, come up closer to me that I may feel your presence!"

Quick as a flash, she would be reminded to tell them of the fun riding in an ox-cart had been in her day, or the hair-tearing thrill of her first plane ride of a recent date.

Picture her, sitting on the porch of this home, with a lake at its foot, all dressed up in a soft violet lace, and when one of her daughters chided her about such vanity, she commented, "I love to dress up, and I love fine feathers. 

I'd even wear ermine if the Lord sent it to me!" 

Her spirit was too eternal to ever be caught and pinned down with white net fishues and old-ladyish fluffiness. A heavy gold linked chain was about her neck, in reality it was not a necklace at all, but a bracelet, the engagement gift of her husband. "You might be able to take a ring off and give it back to me," she remembered his saying most seriously, "so I will bind you with this bracelet,' and romantically locking it, he took the key, "which he promptly lost," laughed his wife, "and then he worried else some other man would find it!" Attired in lavendar lace over a straight front corset, hair waved, eyes sparkling, humor effervescent, laughter on tap, youth and maturity sat together and spoke the same language, because both

had faith, courage and hope, and we all are just as young as these attributes.

The doors of Little Mount Vernon swung open for her late in September, while all nature was arrayed in her crimson and gold. The soft rose of the cosmos and the pale blue of the little 'farewell', flowers vied with the boys in bowing and waving their glad welcome.

That fall, there was time for more talks, special little confidences between the boys and their 'other mother'.

More visits to the sick, and endless friendships renewed. 

Her days were so full that December was upon her before she realized it, and that was the time set for the operation on her eyes. 

Reflecting none of their mother's courage, with fear and misgivings, her sons, Robert and Evans, and her daughter, Gene, went with her to the hospital. "What, if, after all the suffering she sees no better-what if-" but they dared not give expression to more anxious thoughts. 

While she lay suffering, eyes bandaged and at times hands confined for fear she might unconsciously touch them, her one great easement were the letters that came from her boys-forty or more. This one that follows gave her great joy, and is a reflection of the place she held in all their hearts: 

Dear Mrs. Johnston: 

You can't realize how unhappy it makes me to know that you will have to stay in bed ten days. But, Mother, with God's help, you will go through with the operation without any further serious injuries. 

Mother, I have never prayed before, but I am going to pray for you night and day until you fully recover.

You can't realize how happy it will make us to be seen and recognized by you again. There is not a boy out here who is not praying for your return, for they all love you so. Excuse my personal feeling, but you are really a mother to us all. 

Yours lovingly, Charles C

They brought her back in two weeks with the bandages still over her eyes, while her little boys kept silent vigil about her cottage. When asked how she was, it was always the same dauntless spirit that answered, “Just fine."

She was happy resting in the spot that held so much of her heart, and she had wished to have the bandages removed here that her cleared vision might first fall upon the boys. But, on the night of December 19, 1934, her will to live failed at last in a dream, and she awoke seeing all and hearing all. "There must be no mourning of farewell," over and over again she had said. “Do not think or speak of me as dead, but alive as never before."

This letter from a niece, Mrs. Hardee Johnston, who was appointed to her place on the Executive Board, to one of her daughters will bring certain details: "I have just returned from the school. Never have I experienced such a glorious service-a service of triumph, in spirit of the sorrow that welled itself in tears and coursed down the cheeks of young and old alike-for no one was there who did not love and venerate, as of God's chosen, our precious one. "Your spirit would have been uplifted, for there was an inspirational thrill, as if divinely directed, throughout the entire service. "So simple, so serene, so radiant with her love and faith in her Savior. It was as if for a brief space mortals touched the divine and earth and all its materialism was swallowed up in the light of heaven. "There was the dignity and beauty of the military setting. 

Hundreds of her boys at attention, wearing the black band of mourning on their khaki shirts and wearing on their faces a touch of her divine spirit, uplifted by an emotion they may never reach again and yet may never forget. "It was so beautiful, so gracious, and so fitting. The guard of honor stood at each end of the casket, one with the American and the other with the School flag. The casket was covered with an exquisite pall of flowers, lilies lifting their white faces

and roses and narcissus-beauty without display-nature's tribute of her lovely blossoms. "Dr. McDonald and Dr. Alexander spoke as if they had felt the pulse of her great heart. The music was heavenly, the boys sweetly and softly sang the hymn, 'I Hear You Calling.' Dr.

Alexander read a prayer she had given him—a prayer to sustain old age-a prayer for reason and sanity, service to continue unto the end and finally to be abundantly welcomed Home. "And the final leaving of the Chapel. The two manly lads carrying the flags led, followed by those boys whose privilege it was to bear the casket, and the lines of khaki-clad boys-each wearing his mourning band, stood rigid attention, the band softly playing, 'I Hear My Savior Calling.' Surely seeing it with her spiritual vision she must have said, 'It is well, my boys.' "There was a quick intake of breath everywhere, as if one had participated in a celestial vision—and there was silence." 

The last light of day enveloped her boys, who, yesterday, were bewildered children; today, as one more veil was lifted, sensed the glimmering transparency of death.

AN APPRECIATION By David M. WEAKLEY 

HE way is never easy for those who set out to explore uncharted paths, or sail unplumbed seas. 

They are sure to encounter difficulties; they must suffer ridicule and heart breaking disappointments. They must steel themselves against the advice of well meaning friends who say it "can't be done.”

The memory of those who achieve and accomplish despite all opposition and misunderstanding should be preserved and passed on to other generations. The success of the future often depends on keeping alive the memory of the heroic deeds of the past, and it is just as foolish to waste these memories as it is to waste our natural resources. 

My hope is that the friends of Mrs. Johnston will receive from the pages of this book renewed strength and encouragement, and that it will be the means of stimulating others to greater efforts when they feel they are fighting a losing battle.

Johnsie Evans Johnston was a heroic soul who would never accept defeat. For twenty-nine years it was my rare privilege to work with her. 

I think I know something of her matchless courage, her love for humanity, and the tact and wisdom which she so effectively used to overcome the many obstacles she so often encountered.

It is given to few people to possess so many characteristics of greatness as did Mrs. Johnston, not the least of which were faith and humility. A faith that transcended things of this world and reached into eternity. A queenly

humility that gave her the common touch, that inspired confidence and enabled her to influence the lives of people. 

Her womanhood was supreme, and her simple trust in the cardinal virtues of life soothed and sustained her. 

Her courage was magnificent, and her modesty was marked with genuine simplicity. She had in her heart no room for hatred and strife, and she carried on her shield the words of truth and honor. By right of inheritance she possessed pride of ancestry and the gracious gift of spiritual and physical beauty.

One of her greatest assets was the power of oratory. 

When she spoke people listened and were convinced, and her well modulated voice enabled her to play upon the emotions of her listeners like a master musician on a cathedral organ, but she never used this gift unless she felt it was for the glory of her God. 

She never failed to recognize the worth-whileness in people and her enthusiasm was so contagious that simple tasks became animated and glorified. 

In establishing the Alabama Boys Industrial School she measured her strength against a mighty task and won. 

Her city honored her, a grateful State memoralized her with a resolution of appreciation, and thousands of boys, here and everywhere, whom she had inspired to a way of better living, felt the loss of a mother when she was transferred into the "surrendered" life.
