Foreword 

In a world at war, torn by death and devastation, as recorded day after day by the war correspondents and retold with all the glamor and high lights added, tend to keep us in a state of jitters. We feel that the subject has been well covered. 

There is another, maybe not as spectacular feature, of this struggle for supremacy that one hears very little of and that is the part being played by our "Dogs for Democracy" whom have added so much and received so little praise.

The writer of this little volume has presented something new and very human in fictional fighting Soldier Dog, man's best friend who loves human beings more than anything in the world. He seems to develop almost human personality, noble-but incapable of deceit and who never descends to falsehood to man. 

Lord Byron pays this tribute to man's most loyal friend:

But the poor dog, in life the firmest friend 

The first to welcome, foremost to defend, Whose honest heart is still his master's own, Who labors, fights, lives, breaths for him alone,

Unhonored falls, unnoticed all his worth, Denied in heaven the soul he had on earth.

This story should have heart stirring appeal to all men and women dog lovers and particularly so to all those in the armed services. It is interesting, wholesome and thoroughly typical of our American way of life. -Jay T. Heffron.

CHAPTER I A Dust Bowl Blizzard 

Freezing and starving the vagrant whined in anguish as he raked the front screen with his numb claws. 

The sound was harmonized with the moaning icy wind and sleet as it blasted in fierce puffs against the north wall and window. Buck Bullard was comfortable. He pulled the covers up tighter and listened to the screaming blizzard. 

Between gusts of wind he heard what he thought to be a lunge against the screen. The hook became unfastened. The door slammed against the wall of the little grocery-filling station so violently that several cans fell to the floor from flimsy shelves. This made sufficient noise to cause him to roll out of bed in the rear room. He grabbed a flashlight and was in front in a jiffy to see what had happened. 

Buck unlocked the inner door and got a wire to tie the screen. As he pulled the door in, something out of the bleak storm, scurried swiftly by him, brushing its icy covering against his pajama-clad legs. He glanced at the intruder as it moved across the few feet of light and scooted under his bed in the rear.

He wired the screen, locked the inner door and walked briskly to the counter. Underneath the cash register he picked up an old 38 pistol and went into the rear room. The brief glance he got at the varment, caused him to judge the little animal to be a young coyote, seeking shelter from the biting storm. 

Buck had no fear of coyotes. He hated them. During his youthful days as a pioneer of the west, he had permanently disposed of several of them. His eyes were flashing now as

he was preparing to give battle to what he thought was a coyote, in his house and under his own bed. He took a drink of white corn liquor, squatted to his knees and brought his flashlight square into line of two gleaming eyes. 

A friendly pleading whine was all that greeted his battle preparations. He gave a grunt of relief. He knew it was a dog of some breed.

A jab with a broom and the intruder scampered out into the store room. Buck followed to make an inspection of the uninvited straggler. "Well-well, you're a dog, are you?" the station man mused.

The frightened pup lay on his side trembling and whining. He beat a rat-a-tat against the floor with his tail in a gesture of friendship.

Frozen rain pellets had been driven deep under his hair and against his frail body by the force of wind. Heat from the stove soon began to thaw the sleet and the whiteness gave way to a grayish yellow. "You dirty little Bum," Buck murmured to himself as he surveyed the helpless mut in silence.. 

The pup continued his supplication for friendship and harmony with his wagging tail and pleading whine. He appeared to Buck to be a German Shepherd with probably a strain of coyote or lobo blood. He looked about two months old. 

Buck went into the rear room-his bed room, kitchen and dining room-brought out a kettle half full of cold stew and set it on the stove. He then got some gunny sacks and placed them in a pasteboard box. 

The pup smelled the stew when it started to steam. He set up an enthusiastic campaign to inform the man of the house that he was hungry. In a few minutes the kettle was set

off to cool and the contents poured into a tin pan on the floor. 

The pup dived into it, gulping it all down and licking the receptacle clean. He looked up at the man with a "thank you, brother, that really hit the spot" appreciation.

Buck lifted him into the box, turned out the light and went back to bed. He listened to the searing wind and sleet as it pounded the north wall and roof. He could not sleep. 

His mind flashed back to several jalopies, filled with dust bowl refugees which had stopped at the station late during the afternoon and earlier that night. They all needed water, air, information, cigaret papers, matches and about everything a country filling station had to hand out without cost. 

As he reflected, he remembered a special one. It was a wheezing old car, driven by a leather-faced hill billy who needed everything. Besides his wife and several children, the overburdened car was carrying two long eared hounds. The family had been picking cotton on the South Plains and were trying to get back to their native Ozarks. 

Buck remembered hearing a whining underneath the tarpaulin that covered the children as he helped a gangling boy pump air in the tires and then went inside and sold him some sardines and crackers. "Yes," Buck thought, "that's where the little stray came from. The kids in the car unloaded him while I was on the inside. He's a wide open plains dog."

Buck was not a filling station man or merchant. The whole business to him was a little unsavory and distasteful. He was an involuntary retired newswriter, then a victim of the world depression. He had done some wandering before the station came into his possession in 1935. He was the sole master but the work and environment were unsuited to his taste.

In a deep sleep in the same bed, unaware of the severity of the storm, was his 24-year-old son, the Buzzer. He was enjoying one of his brilliant rendezvous with the God Morpheus. 

The boy had recently arrived to visit the old man after a four year hitch in the navy. He was a typical soldier of fortune and an ace sleeper.

Because of his disposition not to be crestfallen, no matter what the circumstances were, the Buzzer had acquired his share of happiness so far in life. He had ordinary parentage, never any place to call home and had been undisciplined except for his schooling and navy service. He had sold newspapers at 5, became an Eagle Scout at 13 and was a newswriter and student of radio at 18. He had a fog horn sort of voice but musical enough to have made him song leader in his high school assembly and yell leader at the foot ball games.

Buzz had always slept well. As a baby during the colic period, he could be put to bed, patted a little and had slept off the worst kind of bellyache. The old man attributed his wonderful energy and boundless enthusiasm to this peaceful slumbering and to an appetite that never shirked its duty. 

Buzz was the type that lived today-forgot yesterday-and didn't give a whoop what was on the program for tomorrow. 

He was as familiar with the ports of the Pacific from Panama to the Philippines and Puget Sound as his Dad was with the little towns of the Southwest. As a naval radio operator he had worked, eaten and slept through tropical hurricanes and arctic blizzards. He simply could not be disturbed now by this storm which was making life uncomfortable for dust-bowl people. 

With the boy and stray pup, safely tucked away in bed and sleeping, the old man was restless. It was past midnight

when he heard a knocking at the front door. He let the halffrozen stranger in and turned the heater on. The stranger related that he was one of a dozen passengers in a bus that had slid off the pavement a half mile east of the station. The highway was completely blocked and some trucks and cars were caught behind the barricade. 

Buck took down the telephone receiver. All he could hear was the popping and cracking of wires. The wind and sleet had grounded the line. The north and south highway to the county seat was swept clear of snow and open. Fortunately a car came by. In less than an hour all the passengers and motorists, who had gathered in the little station, were evacuated to the city.

The old man turned out the lights and went to bed and to sleep. When he awoke four hours later, he found that snow had drifted into the building around the north and west windows and lay two inches thick on the floor. It was a gloomy outlook but heat was turned on and the floor soon cleared. 

He opened the front door to look out. The wind had subsided slightly but hard sleet had turned to snow and was coming down so furiously that he couldn't see 100 yards. The north and south highways, open at two o'clock, when the passengers left the station, were now covered with six inches of snow. He knew that in a few hours, all traffic and communication with the outside world would be cut off. 

The whirling flakes were drifting into the low places. Down the highway to the east where the bus, trucks and cars were stranded, nothing but humps of white snow loomed, like ghosts on the horizon.

Buck pulled out the box from under the bed. The pup was scared. Trembling and whining, he was lifted from the box

and given his freedom. He let it be known that he wanted to be a friend. He was soon begging for food. 

The radio was turned on. The first of the Christmas carols heralding the approaching holidays was coming in over the air. The old man visioned a few days of isolation. He anticipated he would enjoy it with his son, plenty of food, fuel, water and a good radio. The weather report indicated that the storm, still raging, had covered a wide area over the middle-west and southwest. Traffic would likely be tied up for several days. "Hi ya, Pooch," Buzz wide-eyed exclaimed as he neared the stove putting on his clothes. "Pop," he bellowed, "how come the little mut here?" "Broke in last night," Buck replied. "Was about frozen and nearly starved." "Seems very friendly." "He was desperate. He was about at the end of the trail when he broke the screen loose. Slid by me as I opened the door like an icicle on a frozen pond." "Well, I had a great night sleeping." "Yeah, I know that," countered his father as he related the incidents that happened since the storm blew in. 

The boy and young dog started playing. Their friendship was completely mutual. Along with gratitude for being welcomed, the pooch kept dropping hints that he could take on a lot more food. He stalked about the two rooms eyeing everything and smelling all the boxes and containers where anything to eat might be.

Buzz opened a can of meat and emptied it on a plate. The hungry pup wolfed it in a few gulps. He looked at his benefactor with entreating eyes and a whine for more. 

Buck watched the boy and dog enjoy their play and

13. comradeship. No thought of making money out of the business that day nor for several days was entertained. With complete harmony about the place, he thought a nice bit of a drink would be an inspiration. From a stash underneath the counter, he lugged out a half gallon jar of corn whiskey. He poured a good size drink into a glass and swallowed it. The young man also took several big drinks. The pup watched with wagging tail and indicated that he, too, would like to get in on the party. The boy poured some liquor on a slice of bread and then soaked it in milk. "Let's see if you can take it," he chuckled, laying the bread on the floor.

The avaricious pup gulped it down and in a few moments. ate two more slices. "That kind of food is all I need to put new life in this joint. 

Bring on some more," he subtly indicated with his eyes and tail. "Dad, I believe he's a well bred dog and I want to keep him," the boy broke in with characteristic gusto. "He's so adaptable. He'd make a fine comrade or mascot for the army. "You know you never let me have a dog when I was growing up. I want this one. I believe we can make something out of him. I'll give him a high sounding military name and we'll tell the neighbors he's a thoroughbred we ordered from some famous kennel.” "Tell the neighbors anything you like," snapped the old man. "I named the little cheater last night when he broke in.

He is 'BUM,' the fastest moocher of food, drinks and shelter that's ever been on this corner. If I could do half so well in a storm, I'd walk off as soon as the highways are open."

By mid-afternoon, the dark gray skies were giving way to

light and visibility was improving. The wind had decreased. 

Falling snow flakes were smaller but harder, causing a firm surface on the more than a foot of snow on the ground. 

A really white Christmas was approaching. The young man was in a burst of song and was singing with the radio the yuletide carols that were coming in. Buck felt the motivation that had been in his silent heart since childhood-that of sermonizing. He was blasting away furiously at the cock-eyed world generally and the condition it was in. He poured a tirade of hot words against the war which had started in Europe and predicted that America would be soon sucked into the whirlpool.

The man and boy had taken several drinks. Just as regularly some soaked sliced bread was given the stray pooch. 

Not being accustomed to the effects of alcohol, he was the first to feel the golden glow. He wobbled about the rooms whining and barking with his belly expanded like a beer keg. 

The pup had proven that he had an understanding heart and an adaptable spirit. He had also proved that his appetite would take on anything offered and that he could really be one of the party. There was not a dull moment in the little station that day. "Dad," the son pleaded, "you have not promised yet to let me keep this dog. You know there's a war coming and he'll make a good war dog." "Keep him then," his father consented.

CHAPTER II The Holidays Pass 

During the following days while the long thaw out was under way and the blocked highways were in process of being cleared and opened for traffic, the trio enjoyed a hilarious holiday season. There were no customers to wait on. No business to worry over. They all ate sumptuously and drank liberally each day.

No presents were given or received because they were snowed in. The boy made the dog a collar and gave him a hambone. He showed a distinct dislike for the collar. He gnawed the bone at intervals. Then one day after the ground had thawed, he carried it outside and crossed the highway into an adjoining field. 

The pup laid the bone down and surveyed the location critically. He acted much in the same way that a tightwad human does in making an investment. He sniffed the ground in several places, looked in every direction and then dug a hole and buried it. This was the beginning of his rainy day bone reserve.

Bum learned his name quickly and was always prompt to respond. He also began to show some keen political intuition. With his distinctive friendliness he had glad-handed, tailwagged and frisked his way into the hearts of several neighbors, their dogs and the highway workers who, during the lunch hour, made the station their headquarters. His motive in these ultra friendly contacts was to wheedle them out of all the scrap food he could. Near noon he was always waiting for these half dozen workers with a hospitality that they

couldn't resist. He soon learned to catch food with dexterity. 

On one occasion at the luncheon period a devilish Irish worker who knew of his liking for. "spiked" bread, soaked a slice in hot pepper sauce and tossed it to him. Bum made a perfect catch and gulped it down before he discovered his mistake. He tried to get rid of the hot bread, then trotted to his water pan and took a few laps to ease his burning throat. He continued to catch food pitched to him by other workers, but ignored with disdain the offerings of the fellow who had given him the sauce soaked bread.

The dog was growing rapidly because his rapacious appetite was kept satisfied with food and drink. He was taking vigorous exercise each day. As his gangling legs developed there were indications that he would become a kingly specimen of his race. He was moving from puppyhood to a nearly grown dog and was ranging farther away from the station in his daily strolls.

Buzz and the young dog were constant companions. The boy loved him, cuddled him, taught him mischievous tricks and altogether treated him as a kid brother. Weeks of such ecstacy flew by. One day out back of the station the man heard the dog barking in a sort of rythmic manner and heard the boy say: "Now repeat after me." The dog barked at each word spoken by his mentor. "I pledge allegiance to the flag of the 

United States, the Republic for which it stands, one nation, indivisible, with liberty and justice for all." They went over the scout oath several times.

Winter soon melted into spring and Buzz began to show signs of restlessness. The drabness of country life was taking hold. The wanderlust, with which he had always been affected,

was coming back. On spring afternoons he would gaze off towards far horizons, inspecting the glimmering sun rays sparkling on the pastures and watch the little whirlwinds as they traveled across broken fields. It was to him a beckoning call from land and sea to travel. He also showed this restive spirit at night when he would sit for hours by the radio, tuned in on short wave, copy messages in code, passing through the air between stations thousands of miles away. 

The dog was growing out of adolescence. One morning a neighbor on the way to an auction sale left a trailer parked near the station. It contained three goats.

Bum immediately challenged their presence. He made a charge at the trailer, running around it barking and snapping. 

He knew they were not his kind and seemed to feel they had encroached on premises that it was his duty to guard. The terrified nannies set a turbulent "baah" for help and the boy quieted the disturbance by calling the dog off sternly. 

Bum was not satisfied. He backed off and looked the pack over. He seemed to know the goats were frightened and there was not a chance for a fight. As he gazed at them, his emotions seemed to change to fondness. He whined, wagged his tail and again approached the little boxed vehicle with a "Yo Ho, there baby, look me over" attitude. He reared upon the trailer side to get a better view. One of the angry nannies lunged fiercely at him. He understood that his advances were repulsive.

A short time later, there was an accidental canine party at the station. Several dogs with their masters happened to meet at the junction joint of the highways, on their road to and from the city. Bum immediately assumed the role of host. 

He showed the dogs over the premises, to his doghouse, water-

ing pan but not to his bone reserve. He was careful that none of them discovered this precious spot across the highway in the field.

A little runt dog did start in that direction once. Bum headed him off. His tail was curled over his back, his bristles on his shoulders were raised and he showed his sharp long teeth. 

The small dog was chased several yards down the highway. 

Bum strutted back to the other dogs and pompously let them know that he was still in authority. 

One day something happened that changed his disposition and eventually the remainder of his life. A hunter shot a jack rabbit on an adjoining farm and threw it out of his car at the station.

Since he had been kicked overboard and sought shelter at the station on that wild winter night, so far as his master knew, the young dog had never tasted any kind of food, except prepared dog food, scraps of left overs and an occasional slice of milk and whiskey soaked bread.

The dog saw this gray looking package when it hit the ground. He trotted over to it with his bristles raised. He smelled it. Fresh warm blood was still oozing from a gaping gun-shot wound just behind the forelegs. He licked the blood. He then turned it over with his nose and licked the other side. He stood for a moment as though he were absorbing the taste of this, his first fresh blood. He eyed the carcass of the dead rabbit. Then he opened his jaws and got a firm hold on the body, near the middle.

The rabbit was a good load for him. He carried it across the highway, into the field near the spot where he had been burying his bones for several months. His two masters were watching in wonderment. They thought he would dig a hole and bury it.

The dog did not do this. Instead he placed both feet on the rabbit's body. He took a firm hold with his mouth and began heaving to tear it apart. He succeeded. Then began like a savage barbarian to gulp it down. The man and boy watched him for half an hour. He ate all the big jackrabbit. 

Hair, hide, entrails, ears, all. He then came back across the highway and stretched himself out in front of the doghouse. "Keep him, if you want him," the father observed. "He's part prairie lobo and will go wild some time." 

The young man shook his head, dejectedly. The savage like act of the dog had hurt him. "Dad," he pleaded. Tears had started to well. "Maybe we can get him out of it. He's the kind that will make a fighter. I'll get him some fresh bone meat. I want him to have another chance."

For more than a week the dog was fed some raw meat with his rations. His appetite seemed to be appeased. 

A few more days sped by. Then another mild shock came to the old man. The boy was up earlier than usual one morning. He spoke to his father. "Dad, guess I'll be on the move.”

His parent was not surprised when the boy told him his stay at the station was ended.

The European war had been under way only a few months. 

Hitler and his army were blitz-kreiging everything in front of them. France had already folded. The Dunkerque disaster still unknown. "We're certain to be in the fight before long," the boy asserted with emphasis. "I'm trained in signal work. I know 

I'll be called back and have decided to beat the government to it by enlisting as quickly as I can.

20. "I'd gladly take Bum with me but I may get an assignment where I can't take him. You can care for him better here. I think he'll be needed before many months. I regret to leave you and him-but you know me I follow hunches.”

The few dollars at the station were divided. His scanty belongings were thrown into his handbag. He was whistling as he started hitch-hiking south. 

Loneliness swept over the old man for the next few days. 

The dog also was lonesome. He showed he missed his comrade. He would stand and watch down the highway in the direction he disappeared as though he expected him to return in any car that passed. He showed a lot less interest in the public who stopped at the station. 

A period of hot, dry weather followed. By mid-summer when harvesting was over, cotton was wilting. Pastures were drying up. Creeks quit running and water wells were going dry. A depressive feeling was over the whole area. 

Bum had been slipping all summer in character, stability and trust-worthiness. He had been getting his raw meat diet and was sleeping when he should have been on the alert. 

The old man lectured him about the slothfulness-his easy to bruise and quick to heal attitude. He warned him of his cynicism of believing the "baa" of a nanny goat was an “Indian love call" to him.

Enervating hot weather continued. Gray thunderheads would appear on the horizon late afternoons and distant flashes of lightning could be seen almost every night. The mornings dawned regularly with clear skies and hot winds through the middle of the day..

A weather forecast came in on the radio that a low pressure would move into the Southwest from the mountains and

that a different weather status could be expected. An afternoon later, a menacing cloud gathered in the southwest. The air soon became a stuffy stillness. 

No weather forecasting was needed. Any old Indian or range cow knew that there would be "hellapoppin" soon. 

Everything alive was seeking shelter. To the northwest, the clouds resembled the black dusters that had tormented the country.

Splattering drops of rain with little puffs of wind came out of the southwest cloud first. The wind ceased.

The breezes struck out of the northwest. Small drops of rain came with the wind. It was moderate for several minutes and in less than a half hour the downpour was on. Water was soon pouring two inches deep through the driveway of the station. It continued for four hours or near midnight. When it ceased the southwest was drenched and washed clean. 

Bum was snug in his doghouse quarters Buzz had built for him long before he left. It was the first storm the dog had experienced since he had been left at the station on that memorable winter night. 

Next morning the sky was clear. A cool breeze was blowing out of the north. Large pools of water were standing in the grader ditches along the highway. The low places in pastures and fields were filled. Terraces thrown upon land to prevent soil washing had broken on many farms. Fields of cotton, wilted and yellow from the fierce breath of sun and wind, leaning north, were now turned back east and south. 

Frogs were putting out a tropical chorus, not often heard.

Old Mother Nature had again asserted herself. There was happiness once more in a much depressed land. Farmers, borrowing money on crops, whipped by the drouth, frowning and

irrational, were smiling. The earth had been cleansed. The crops had been saved from their worst enemy-drouth. 

A few days of sunshine changed the condition. Leaves on trees, cotton and the foliage on stock feed, turned yellow by the blistering sun, suddenly became green again. Wheat shattered out on the ground during the harvesting season, sprouted, took root in the moist stubble and became green. 

The country in less than a week had the appearance of a 

June bride. All kinds of livestock and poultry enjoyed the tender vegetation. Jack rabbits appeared as if by magic in the community. Dozens of them, nibbling the young wheat, hopping and playing, could be seen from the station late afternoons and early mornings.

Buck cleaned up his .22 rifle. He began bumping one off nearly every morning from the station driveway. Bum was the beneficiary. He had rabbit every day and got more worthless as the days went by. He would eat and without taking exercise, retire leisurely to his doghouse and sleep long hours. 

Buck was disgusted with his attitude. He quit talking in. low tones. He yelled and scolded. The dog would at times pull back his ears, wag his tail and seem to say: "Rave on, old top. I live and enjoy my life the way it suits me best." 

This "don't care" attitude of the dog got under the old man's skin. He killed a rabbit one morning and let the dog eat it. When the dog went to his doghouse, the man nailed the door down. On the second morning Bum was making a plea by howling and scratching at the door to get out. He had a pan of water and was given scrap food. He got no attention from the man until the third morning of incarceration. 

When Buck pried open the door he came out with a bound of appreciation. He ran about the premises for several min-

utes. He went to his bone reserve first to see if it had been disturbed. He was given about 20 minutes of liberty. When he came tail-wagging back to the old man, a collar was snapped around his neck and he was put on a 50 foot leash. "I'll try to teach him a lesson," the master thought as he continued his chores. "He thinks there's a Santa Claus every day in the year; he believes that gravy trains are running on schedule; he believes that there's a long political pie counter with vacant seats, just waiting for someone to occupy them without effort. "I'm going to let him know that his rations are cut off; that we're in debt; that our patrons can't pay their bills they owe and that he has got to do some hustling to sustain himself." 

The dog was kept on the leash that night. He was tied to one of the bed posts on his master's bed. He was making a plea for liberty before morning.

He had been nailed up and on leash for more than 72 hours. He was then taken outside and tied at a corner of the station. He was hungry when his piercing eyes caught sight of a big gray jack rabbit, nibbling leisurely at volunteer wheat, right in the midst of his bone reserve. 

Buzz had been gone now for 60 days. A three page typewritten letter came from him on the early morning mail. He was in the Canal Zone. He had enlisted for four years in the 

Army at Fort Sam Houston. According to his letter, he had proved that he was an efficient signal corps operator. After a few weeks around the circuit, he had been assigned to do a night job on a scouting plane, flying east and west over the Canal. He said he was in excellent health and happy. The closing paragraph of his letter read: "Dad, I'm sorry to have left so suddenly. Tell Bum, the

best way you can, I think of him often. Give his nose an extra tweak for me. I still love him. I've a hunch that good dogs will be needed before this is over."

A half hour had passed since the man received and read the letter. The dog was hungry and still had his eyes on the jack rabbit. He heaved against his leash, took a few laps of water and whined. "You want to eat that rabbit,” Buck bawled out at him.

I've been feeding you and you've lain down on the job. 

Damned if I waste another bullet on one, until you do something. You are on your own from now on. If you really want to eat, go get him."

CHAPTER III Bum Catches A Rabbit 

Buck unbuckled his collar. It fell to the ground as the old man gently patted him on the head, "You can eat, boy," he thought, "if you can catch him."

The dog's ears straightened. He looked at the rabbit; then up and down the highway. There were no cars approaching. 

He moved across the pavement and the grader ditch. He crouched under the fence as though he was trying to slip up on the rabbit. The sensitive rabbit sat on its all-fours and raised its ears watching the dog. They were not 150 feet apart. The rabbit sensed the danger and pertly started off on that threelegged gallop to put more distance between him and his enemy.

Bum was off in a flash and the race was on. He spurted to within 50 feet of the animal before it realized the danger. Then the rabbit put down his fourth leg, laid his ears straight back on his body and began doing the job which had kept his family alive for centuries and made him famous for doing so well, outrunning his enemy.

The dog was soon left far behind as they disappeared over a plowed ridge in a fog of dust. 

The old man had seen the young dog start a few of these chases before. He had watched him run a half mile and put on good speed for a German Shepherd and then throw up his tail and quit. He had seen him come trotting back to the station with his tongue out and make for his water pan. He expected this to happen on this occasion. He watched for the dog in the direction in which he had disappeared over the plowed ridge.

A car stopped at the station and while Buck was giving highway information, pointing east, he observed dust fogging from the fence row a half mile east. He watched the gray spurts and there was no doubt that it was a rabbit and dog. He kept watching and the movement became somewhat slower as the section line and highway intersection was reached. As the race proceeded down the fence row, it was plain to see from the dust made by the feet of both runners, that the dog was moving closer, yard by yard and inch by inch as each fence post was passed.

The pace became much slower as they neared the highway intersection, south of the station. The ground was barren and there was no chance for the rabbit to hide-nothing he could do except to outwind and outrun the dog. The rabbit made slight gains on the dog in running under the fence and making the turn into the grader ditch. But the jack was full of new green wheat and the dog was outwinding him on an empty stomach and an animal desire to eat. 

The old man watched the race carefully as they showed up in the ditch, a half mile south. It seemed to him that they were three or four minutes making this last half mile. The dog had moved up almost within nipping distance and the rabbit zig-zagged and dodged under the fence in front of the station.

This was a fatal mistake for the rabbit. The point of passing under the fence, where he should have made a gain, was directly between the station and the bone reserve of the dog in the field. The dog had been under this fence a thousand times. He knew where every barb on the wire was. He knew just where to bend to keep his back from getting scratched. He never lost an inch and before they were 50 feet in the stubble

field, Bum tipped the desperate exhausted rabbit over with his nose and dropped on top of it. He gave its body a crunch between his jaws and it was dead. 

He sank down for a minute, his tongue all the way out and the rabbit between his paws. The catch was almost in the middle of his bone field and not 100 feet from the point where the three mile 20 minute race had started. He was looking toward the station as though he wanted some attention, preferably some applause from his master for his feat. 

When he had recuperated a few minutes he came across the highway to his water pan. He lapped water and lay down panting in the shade of the station building. His eyes were riveted on the place where he left the rabbit. 

Buck had given him a fatherly pat on the head. That was his applause or compliment. It seemed to satisfy the dog. But the old man knew he was hungry and kept his eye pinned on him. After the dog had relaxed for a few minutes, he stood up and stretched the soreness out of his muscles, re-crossed the highway and went direct to his rabbit. He tore it apart and began his uncivilized feast on raw flesh, blood and hair that lasted for half an hour. He was abundantly full. His sides bulged out. He appeared drowsy when he retired again to his doghouse and was not heard nor seen by his master until late the next afternoon.

When he came out, several rabbits were nibbling the wheat across the highway in the field. He saw them but did not indicate any desire for another race of running one down. 

He was still recovering from his last effort and his gorging himself with flesh and blood.

If this almost human dog, did not understand the conflict going on in the world he seemed to be meditating on a similar

problem as it related to him. He believed he could get what he wanted to eat, when he wanted it, without paying the price of a three mile run and exhaustion. He, the old man thought, had decided to take the lines of least resistance. He would play both ends against the middle. He would get raw food without putting out so much effort. He was definitely covetous.

Buck saw him coming in one morning from across a field. 

His stomach appeared to be overloaded. There was blood on his jaws and on his front paws. When the man called him, the dog slunk away and went into his doghouse. He was admitting his guilt. He remained in all day. A neighbor told the old man the next morning that a coyote was in the community and had caught and ate one of his turkeys as it came off the roost. 1 The dog was awake. His head was stuck partly out his doghouse, watching and listening. He may not have understood the language but implied that he knew the conversation was about the crime he had committed. He showed in his demeanor that he was learning a lot of tricks and really getting smart.

More farmers, during the following days, complained of coyotes marauding, just before daybreak when poultry was leaving the roosts. Then at daybreak one morning, Buck heard the blast of a shot gun. He got out of bed in an instant and was at the window looking in the direction from which he had heard the shots.

Dust was being stirred up by something coming down the fence line toward the station. It was Bum. He ran under the fence, crossed the highway and straight to his doghouse. 

A short time later this neighbor appeared with his shotgun.

"Buck," he snapped angrily. His blue eyes were shining and his face flushed. "That damn dog of yours is part wolf. 

You'll have to do something with him. He ate one hen and killed a couple of others before I shot at him. I'll kill him if I get a chance.” "I'll get rid of the dog," Buck replied quietly and in a conciliatory tone. "I'll pay for the chickens and promise you he won't molest your poultry any more. My son asked me to keep him and I've tried to make a watch dog out of him."

An hour passed and the old man went to the doghouse. 

Bum was shaking and pleading for sympathy. He was licking his hip. One of the shots had taken effect but the wound was not serious. He seemed to show his guilt for the crime he committed.

Buck stood in front of the doghouse in silence for a moment and then nailed the door fast. That afternoon he went to the doghouse again and slipped a pan of water under the door. He was left alone to "think it over" with the door nailed. 

For the next week he was given some scrap food and let out on the leash for exercise. Another week passed and the wound had healed. He was put on a 50-foot rope leash and tied to a fence post across the highway where he could range over his bone reserve. He occasionally dug up a bone and gnawed it. Each night he was locked in his doghouse. 

Buck observed him closely. He did not try to break loose in the late afternoons when jack rabbits were hopping about. 

He did cast longing eyes at the neighborhood poultry houses. 

Buck believed he might again take the easiest way to get raw food if he were not restrained. How to cure his savage appetite or get rid of him without letting his son know, was a problem he had been pondering on for days.

Several produce trucks passed the station each morning on their routes. They stopped occasionally and all of them knew the dog and the predicament in which the old man found himself. "Lemme give him a ride into the country," challenged 

Jerry McContra, red-headed Irish truckster.

Buck's eyes lit up. The suggestion met his instant approval. 

The dog was placed in a poultry crate and loaded into the truck. The old man visioned the route of the truck and thought the dog might have a good chance of being adopted by some family on a farm, if he'd give up his banditry. 

Bum was unloaded about 25 miles from the station that afternoon. By six o'clock he came limping in, tired, footsore and hungry. He went to his doghouse and was locked in, after being given food and water. He appeared nervous as though he had been stoned and chased by other dogs enroute home, but seemed in a penitent mood and made advances to be forgiven.

Two mornings later, another driver offered him a free ride on his route. Buck agreed quickly. Any thing to get rid of the "problem child," without killing him. This time he was forced out of the truck 40 miles away, in another direction. He never showed up that afternoon but some time during the night the "bad penny" returned and was snug in his doghouse next morning. He was even more amiable. He let his master know that he appreciated his home and would like to call the whole thing off. He was put on the leash in day time and locked up at night for another week. "I can take that dog to a spot on my route where he won't come back," Lanky "Swede" Neilson, boasted. "He simply can't find his way."

"I'll take you up," Buck agreed. "Let's load him."

When Bum saw a turkey crate being taken out of the big truck, he set up a protest. He growled and snapped at the old man as he was pressed into the crate. He had memories of rocks and bricks being heaved at stray dogs. He wanted no more of it.

Tears came into Buck's eyes as he silently watched the vehicle go lumbering along over a hill, a half mile east of the station. "I hate to see the damn pooch leave,” the old man mumbled. "The kid loved him so."

CHAPTER IV The Old Man Quits 

Buck was lonesome. He missed the dog. He tried to keep busy and forget. The radio and war news absorbed him. He was drinking more and going deeper into debt. He was definitely on the down grade, mentally, physically and finan cially. 

Business men whom he contacted occasionally in the purchase of store supplies, classed him as a No. 1 sap. Why should he issue credit to everyone who asked for it? Some of his neighbors and many highway travelers had taken advantage of his easy going disposition. 

Buck did not feel any guilt over the situation. He knew he had taken a four year beating. He reviewed this period to himself, thought of the charity and unappreciated services he had given. He had read notes from mothers, carried by school children, gathered at his station to catch a bus, asking him to let the children have bread or crackers and lunch meat, so they would not be hungry at school.

Literally hundreds of small items which he had trusted his customers to remember, were forgotten. He tore the charge slips up. He tore into and threw away a roll of "rubber checks" that had bounced back. He closed the place and in less than a week was seeking work in a large city. 

Buck applied for work everywhere. He answered blind ads, wrote letters and sought appointments, but always it was the same old answer: "Sorry, we want younger men."

He resented this kind of a reply. He did not believe he

was "washed up." He was disqualified from examinations because of his age. He wanted to work badly. He wanted to keep a clear conscience while the world was being cut to slithers by the knives of intolerance, greed, and race hatred. 

It was a severe winter. Dark misty, cold days came on early. December 7 was such a day—the Japs had attacked 

Pearl Harbor. His status changed almost overnight. At the army recruiting station next morning, he was turned down because of his age. The navy and marine corps also rejected his offer to join. Before night however he had a job—a night job—as guard at a war plant. Construction on it had just started.

In the marine corps recruiting station that afternon he happened to meet his old friend, the Lanky Swede Neilson. 

Sixty miserable days had passed since he had seen Neilson, who had unloaded the dog at a spot to which he never returned. "Not waiting for the draft," Neilson exclaimed. "Think I'll be accepted, too," he boasted, flicking off ashes from his cigaret. "Never even got back in that truck after I heard of the war. Just hit a bee-line for this office."

Buck told him he had been turned down by all the recruiting services. "Say, did you know that pesky dog of yours is here in town?" "By jove, how's that?" "Brought here last week to a dog hospital," the Swede replied. "That's top news-tell me about him. What's ailing him?" "Nothin' serious; broken or sprained front leg."

"Where is he?" Buck asked eagerly. "I want to go see him." "Well, on my last trip over that route, I happened on to him at a country store, 15 miles north of the point I'd unloaded him two months ago. "This was a wilderness of trees and rugged canyon country about 100 miles from the station. It was a sparsely settled region, but with plenty of water and an abundance of vegetation and wild life. "I was slowing down for the stop at this store when I saw a colored chauffeur loading the dog into his car. The car was driven away before I had time to talk to the occupants. "A boy at the store gave me the information. He said

Bum came to the store the day before and the boy thought he had been separated from a caravan of army trucks. The car struck the dog and injured him in passing, but stopped, backed up and took the dog in. The chauffeur left the name and address of a retired Army colonel and told the boy when the dog got well they'd try and return him to his outfit. "The dog had lost weight, looked hungry and was nervous when he saw me and the truck. He knew me and I could identify him anywhere. "Here's the name and address the boy gave me.” "That's swell, thanks a thousand times," the old man replied.

Buck was happy. He had thought of the dog often. He had hoped Bum would be drawn back into better surroundings than the hideout he was left in. "If I'm accepted, I'll probably not be seeing you again very soon," Neilson said as he extended his hand to tell his old friend goodbye.

Buck thanked him, wished him the best of luck and was off. Next afternoon at the war plant, he was given a uniform, assigned to a post and told to report for duty that night. The weather was cold and the ground wet as he walked the line that night. He decided not to see Bum for a few days.

Hundreds of workers were busy constructing the big plant. 

Before morning, a crew of hole diggers and fence builders came by his post. He learned from them that the property would be under fence soon, but that the guard duty would not be lessened.

No word had come from his son since war was declared. 

He phoned his army headquarters and was told he was not there. He had been moved. A week later he received a box full of civilian clothing, a radio and personal items a soldier has no use for in wartime. A brief note was in the package: "Dear Dad: I put on army togs Sunday night for the duration. I have no use for these. Wear them if you can. If you get a chance, get Bum into training. I'm ordered out. Hope to see you again, but no matter what happens, you'll be uppermost in my mind-Buzz."

He called the hospital and asked about the dog. He told the doctor the truth and was invited out to see him. The veterinarian said the dog was about ready to leave the hospital. 

Buck went there next morning. He did not let the dog see him but he saw the dog in a wire enclosure. Splints had been on the dog's leg but were now removed. "The lady who left him here said his name was 'Major'," the veterinarian asserted. "She explained that he had an accident on the highway west of the city. She ordered that he be given the best of care and that she'd return or send for him when he was able to get about. Her name is Mrs. Pangburn

and she is the wife of a retired army colonel. Here's her address and phone number. I'll have to see that the dog is returned to her." "Certainly," Buck replied. "I wouldn't want you to do otherwise."

Buck looked at the dog through a window. He noted that he was changing. It had been three months since he last saw him. Hospital food and the service he was getting were agreeing with him. "Yeah," his former master thought. "A 60 day banishment in the jungles, hunted as a bandit. Now in a modern dog apartment, served the best of food and a watchman passing by every hour to see that he was protected. It's a fast moving world and the dog is moving along with it." 

Before Buck left, a car drove in and a fuzzy nosed airdale that had been in the hospital for treatment was taken into the car. The old man noticed that this dog had a cage only a few feet from Bum's. He seemed concerned about her departure. 

He heard a teen age boy call the dog "Miss Pat."

A month of real winter passed. Buck was well equipped with clothing and was doing his guard job satisfactorily. He had pocketed money from the first pay day at the plant. He spent much time, when he was not sleeping, reading and listening to news over the radio. From a Sunday newspaper, his eye caught this headline and story: "Already several hundred dogs have been enlisted in the war and several thousand are being trained for various war services. The dogs work with the soldiers, sailors, marines and guards and do many kinds of work at night. "Because they can see so well and have a keen sense of smell and hearing, they are proving an invaluable aid.

They are used for sentinel duty at hazardous points around defense plants, messengers, watchdogs for war supplies and some have already appeared on battle fronts. The large smart dog between 1 and 5 years old, are the ones wanted by the Army."

Buck talked to the captain of the guards next night before going on duty. "I don't have a dog right now, but I know of one that I did have once. I think I can get him back. I believe he'd qualify for this work and I'd certainly like to have him with me at night."

The suggestion met with the approval of the captain. "If you get a dog, have him trained. We'll give you one of the most vulnerable spots on the property line," the captain declared.

When Buck got to his room next morning, he called Colonel Pangburn. 'If he's yours, I'll be happy to let you have him," the officer agreed. "The dog should be in training. He is young and strong and can be made useful to the army." 

Buck called a dog training school. He was invited to get the dog and bring him out. The training service was free. If the dog would take training within six weeks he could be assigned to service with a master he trusted. 

Buck rented a car and drove to the Pangburn home. It was then past noon. Bum was lying down out on the lawn in front as the old man stopped the car. "Bum," he called.

The dog stood up. He was wearing an elaborate set of dog harness. He had grown some and was fat. Shining

buckles and buttons set him off well. His ear straightened. He wagged his tail and recognized his former master. 

The dog leaped across the sidewalk and upon the old man as though he were attacking him. The colonel came to the door and called him "Major" but he paid no attention. He continued to rear up and play. "He's your dog," the colonel laughed as he came on out to the walk. "He's been a fine gentlemanly dog ever since we've had him. We're happy to know his real owner has him. "When he was injured at the store on the highway, we were under the impression, from what the boy told my wife, that he had been lost from army trucks and have tried to locate the company that lost him. "Mrs. Pangburn is not here this afternoon, but she'll be happy to know that the dog will get military training and will be with his former master. She is very fond of the dog." 

The colonel refused to accept any money for the hospital care and the harness the dog wore.

Buck drove away with the dog by his side. He stopped in a park and he patted the dog on the head. His grayish yellowing that once made him look so much like a wolf was disappearing into a more golden color. There was plenty of flesh-in fact too much on his canyon-hardened bones. There was no doubt that he had had the best of care. 

The harness was taken off and wrapped up. The dog seemed to appreciate this. "Darned if you've not come a long way in getting over your appetite for raw food," the old man thought. "You're too fat on fine food and some of it is going to be trained off." 

The car was started and was soon rolling up a road out of the city limits. It entered a long driveway that led up to

the dog training headquarters. The superintendent came out.

Bum was laying down in the front seat with his head in the old man's lap. "This is the dog I told you about over the phone," Buck said. "I want to leave him for training." "We'll be glad to enter him and see what we can do for him. Seems to be a good friend of yours. Also seems a little 'fat but that'll be easily remedied. "I'll come back every morning at the training period to help your trainers get acquainted with him and get him started. 

I believe he'll make a good war dog. He's only a little more than a year old."

The dog was taken out of the car and placed in a wire cage, large enough for some exercise. Buck patted him on the head again. He was comforted by the fact that there were dozens of other dogs near his own.

Buck returned to his room in the city. He still had not heard from his son since the war started, except to receive the note when the boy's clothing arrived. Two letters addressed to his company, the old man thought should have been forwarded, had been returned. Before he went to work that night he wrote another letter telling his son that his wish had come true. Bum was in a training school.

CHAPTER V Bum Goes To School 

Buck went direct from the war plant, when he got off duty at daybreak, to the dog training school. Bum was happy to see him.

The dogs had all been fed. Dozens of them seemed eager for training practice. Bum knew something was up but did not know what it was all about. All the dogs were on leash as they were led to the training area. Part of the terrain was rough with some trees and lots of underbrush, holes, wire entanglements and other barricades. 

The master trainer advised Buck to keep his dog on the leash and let him observe. Bum was taken to a point on the grounds where he could see most of the activity. He showed an interest in everything that was going on. In fact, he acted like a country boy at a circus for the first time. He was trying to see all the show but there was too much for him. 

A trainer not far from where the man and dog stood released a big Mastif from a barricade and sent him to retrieve a parcel resembling a machine gun from a hole. The dog galloped across an open space of 50 yards, seized the dummy gun and started back with it to his trainer. An enemy rose from some bushes and fired two volleys. The big dog increased his speed and delivered the parcel to the trainer. 

The report of these shots seemed to upset Bum. He was frightened and nervous until the old man put him at ease with some encouraging pats.

Whether he had been shot at during his two months of bandit life, no one will know. Buck, however, knew one rea-

son for his being allergic to gun shots. The man remembered the speed he made that early autumn morning coming down the fence row after he had raided the farmer's poultry yard. 

He had cause to show nervousness. 

The dog was soon at ease and soon understood this was not that kind of shooting. He watched keenly what other dogs were doing. The master trainer had passed by several times that morning and noted the interest the dog was taking in the exercises.

On their way back to headquarters at noon, the dogs were released from leashes. There was no authority or viciousness shown toward the newly arrived rookies. Bum acted as though he was proud of being welcomed into this select bunch of canines. "Your dog at first acted nervous when guns were fired," one trainer commented. "He soon relaxed." "Yes," Buck retorted. "He has cause for his nervousness."

Bum was placed in his cage and fed with the other dogs. 

Buck gave him a pat before leaving to get some afternoon sleep before going on duty that night. The dogs were put through an afternoon schedule of training and Bum was taken along to observe.

The dog was out on the grounds again and on the leash with his master early next morning. The little airedale "Miss

Pat" had been entered in the school to train for messenger service. Bum remembered her while the two were in the hospital. He watched her carry a message all the way across the field. He saw her outrun a man, who had jumped from underbrush to intercept, and then deliver the message to the trainer. 

He watched with interest as she was given the message to

return and detoured around the spot where the man had tried to stop her. 

Bum's big thrill came on the third morning. He watched a German Shepherd run from out of a cluster of bushes, leap through a hole in a wire entanglement, then make a graceful and vicious spring on a dummy enemy. He saw this faultless attack made numerous times by several German Shepherds that looked as if they were all akin to him. "That's my dish” he seemed to be trying to tell the old man. “Here's where I fit into the picture. Turn me loose and give me a chance," he seemed to plead.

He was whining, wagging his tail, growling and tugging at the leash. He wanted to get into those bushes and put an enemy down. He indicated to his master and the trainers that he wanted to become an attack dog. 

The trainers told Buck that they had tried to make him carry messages during the afternoons but he had shown no interest in this service. He was restless at first when he was taken with a trainer to patrol a line and do sentinel duty. The trainers advised that he should be put through a thorough course in this. Even though he had shown he wanted to be an attack dog, he would probably have to be on the alert several hours before the opportunity for attack came. 

The group of attack dogs were being put through a severe grind. A few had already completed training and had been sent into the service with their masters. Others were training daily with morning, afternoon and night schedules to equip them for the fighting the army expected of them. 

The attack training centered in a cluster of bushes and underbrush in the middle of the training grounds. Surrounding

these bushes were open spaces, containing numerous holes, wire barricades, vine entanglements and other hazards. 

The dogs were taken into the cluster of bushes and dummy enemies raised out of the holes for them to attack. One trainer released a dog and other trainers manipulated the dummies.

All the dogs were constantly on the alert. They were being taught to watch and listen from every direction. Bum had been taken into the enclosure a time or two but had not been turned loose on one. "We'll give your dog a try this morning," a trainer told 

Buck. "You can control him better, so take him in.“

Bum seemed to sense this his big moment had come. He was led into the bushes by his master. He was unleashed as a dummy began sticking its head like a scare crow out of a hole 50 yards away. The dog saw it and dashed out of the enclosure. He hurled the first barricade of small bushes gracefully. He made splendid time across the open space with his eyes on his enemy. Unhappily his intended leap through the wire opening was inaccurate.

He hit the wire so hard that the rebound sent him sprawling backwards in a somersault. He was on his feet in an instant, backed up and took a slower pace. This time his body slid through the wire opening with ease and he made the attack with vigor. His excessive weight knocked the dummy back into the hole and he was putting all of his heart into the business of tearing it to shreds when the trainer broke him loose. "That dog'll be a Hell cat fighter," the trainer chuckled. "Put him through again."

A group of other attack dogs, some already well trained, were watching this freshman. If they could have talked, Bum would no doubt have come in for a round of razzing. He was

made to go through the same practice several times that morning. "He has the fighting spirit. He'll make a fighting war dog," the head trainer assured his master.

Bum was given attack practice for several hours every morning. During the afternoon periods he was made to patrol, watch and do sentinel practice. He saw other dogs carrying messages and running errands for their trainers much more efficiently than could have been done by men.

CHAPTER VI The Dog Starts Guard Service 

Three guards had been patrolling a post on the property line during the night hours where the fencing crossed a creek. 

They made their headquarters in a small two room shack. 

The post was regarded as dangerous by the captain. "You and the dog may have the use of the cabin," the captain told Buck. "Make arrangements for your rations tomorrow and take this post over tomorrow night."

The man and dog went to work together. They walked, watched and listened from dark until daylight for more than a month. Nothing unusual happened. 

Then one night it was near clocking time out for swing shift employes and clocking time in for the graveyard shift. 

The dog suddenly gave his master an alert signal. 

They both stood still. A minute passed before Buck could hear footsteps approaching. The noise was coming from up the creek toward the fence. The invader pushed the wires apart and came through. As he straightened up the old guard commanded him to halt as the dog attacked. He sprang on the excited victim and snatched for his throat, but grabbed his shoulder and the man fell to the ground. 

The intruder yelled in anguish. Buck threw his flashlight on the fierce fight and saw the victim was unarmed. He pulled the dog loose. The man struggled to his feet and the guard ordered him to walk on to headquarters.

On top of the hill near the cabin, they met other guards who had heard the man scream. One of the guard officers knew the injured man-a plant employe-who had forgotten his badge.

"I couldn't get in through the main entrance," the helpless victim told the officer. "I tried to come through the fence in the creek bottom."

The dog's teeth had left a painful shoulder wound. He was bleeding and groaning in pain as he was taken to a first aid station. He was later taken to a hospital in the city and dismissed from the service for violation of the entrance rules. 

It was the first days of spring. Dark clouds had been gathering in the Southwest for more than an hour. There were constant flashes of lightning as the storm approached.

The dog was on leash as he and his master went back to the creek post to patrol the line. In less than half hour winds from the storm clouds struck, followed by a torrent of hail and rain. They stayed through the storm; for the next hour they took a hard pounding and drenching. 

The night captain came to the cabin about four o'clock expecting to find them inside. He came on near the creek fence line and saw them. "Bullard, don't you know enough to get in out of a storm?" he queried sarcastically. "You and the dog are drenched and cold." "Nothing was said about weather when we agreed to do the job," old Buck shot back. "If I was a saboteur I'd take advantage of a little storm like this to do some dirty work." "Well, bring that dog in the cabin and both of you warm up." 1 In the cabin they discussed the attempted entry of the employe. The officer commended the old man and praised the dog's alertness. "He never raised any hell about staying on the job through that storm either," Buck boasted with a smile.

Before work time the next night word had reached the guards on all three shifts about the dog's attack on the wouldbe invader. Buck put the dog's harness on and took him for a long walk during the afternoon.

They could walk only on one side of the creek fence that night. The floodwater that had rushed downstream before they were called away from the post the previous night had about disappeared. The path across was boggy and the man and the dog did their night vigil from the banks.

Two or three days after the fight the dog had with the employe, a brief story appeared in an afternoon newspaper. 

The story was told by the victim in a hospital and was not denied by plant officials. A dog magazine got the facts from some source and carried a complete story of the incident with the name of the plant deleted. 

The night duty at the plant during the next two weeks grew monotonous for both the man and dog. They were impatient for more action.

One afternoon a messenger came to the shack and ordered them to report immediately at the plant's commanders office.

Hand to hand clashes with bayonets and knives had occurred a few hundred yards away during the morning. At two o'clock in the afternoon a company of 15 charged the hole the Corporal, the old private and his dog were in. 

The cross fire of machine guns from behind stopped most of them. The Corporal's machine gun dropped two in 20 feet of the hole. Buck was firing his revolver. One shrieking Jap ran by the hole just as Buck was unsnapping the leash on the dog.

The dog made a lightning leap out of the hole and onto the enemy. The Jap fell. The dog snatched him by his neck and shoulders. The Jap was attempting to slash the dog with a knife. He made a fierce stab but the knife went into his own body near the center of his stomach. Two soldiers came up from the trench and pulled the dog loose.

The old private got out of the hole and went to his dog. 

Blood was coming from his left shoulder. Part of the old wound he had sustained in the war plant fight had been re-opened. 

The Jap lay helpless. Guards stood over him until an ambulance arrived.

Four hours later, patrol scouts reported that there were no Japs alive on the island. They had all been killed or were prisoners. Before daybreak the entire company was back in the field headquarters. The dog had been given first aid. The wound was dressed but did not require sewing up. He ate his rations and indicated he was ready for more action. The com-

pany remained at the field headquarters for more than a week, loafing and resting.

Bum got well acquainted with many of the soldiers. He met two other dogs who had been in battle with a company farther South. Butch was bandaged on the shoulder. He had been wounded by a machine gun bullet. Ned had lost an eye from fragments of an exploding grenade. 

The record of these fighting K.9.'s became known quickly. The dogs acted like brothers towards one another. They were well disciplined and showed their comradeship. 

No advance warning or information was given when the whole outfit was quietly moved westward to the coast one afternoon. They were transported in many small boats across a few miles of water that night and landed on another island. 

By daybreak thousands of fighters from all the branches of the service were on the march Northwestward. The three dogs and their masters, led by the Corporal who had been with 

Buck and Bum were in the line.

They carried rations for one day and a canteen of water. 

They came to a field headquarters long after dark. It was well hidden from the enemy. A much larger force had been assembled than were on the island from which they came. 

A battle was in progress and many of the troops were thrown right into the fight. The Japs had been pushed back along a 20 mile front. They had fought stubbornly during their daily retreat and were now trying to entrench in the brushcovered hills by placing small groups in holes a few hundred yards apart.

There was little noise but lots of action in this camp. They listened to airplanes overhead. Booming artillery punctured

the stillness. A pincer drive by Americans had shortened the battle line to less than ten miles. 

Four other soldiers with war trained dogs appeared in the camp one morning. The dogs were fed at the same time. 

They mixed well and enjoyed the furlough from being on the alert and in battles at numerous locations in the islands. Two of these dogs, Duke and Bob had recovered from wounds sustained in attack. All of the men who had come across to this island had been at the front lines fighting and some of them had been returned for rest. The field camp had been moved 

15 miles further inland.

All the masters and their canine soldiers were ordered to fall in line for an inspection one morning. An officer addressed them: "We have a problem a few miles out," they were told. 

These dogs, I think, can help us solve it. Some Japs, we don't know just how many, are 'holed in' and can make it expensive for us to get them out. They have a machine gun and maybe ammunition and supplies to hold out for a long time. "We've bombed them from the air, but they're still there delaying our advance. They can't escape. They're surrounded. 

They must be killed or taken prisoners. We don't want to sacrifice a lot of men in doing this job. We don't think the dogs will be sacrificed at night. "This little group of dogs have all been in one or more fights. They all know a Jap. If any of you guardians think your dog won't attack, I want you to tell me now."

There was silence as the young officer eyed the soldier guardians, one by one. "Private Hubbard, your dog Bum has a good fighting record. He's the youngest and one of the largest dogs in the out-

fit. What do you think of the idea of releasing these seven dogs on that hole, to disturb the Japs? We can get 100 men in there in two minutes to help the dogs clean it out." "The idea is perfect," the old man answered eagerly. "I think you'll see that the dogs do more than just 'disturb' them." "We've planned and orders have been given to release the dogs on them at daybreak in the morning. Corporal.. who has been with you, will be in charge." 

The time for the attack was set for 5:45 a. m. Watches were checked. The soldiers were instructed to feed and exercise the dogs at regular hours that day. They slept about four hours and were ordered out just after midnight. 

They walked in a single file, following the Corporal to the point of attack. It was about four miles and was after two o'clock when they took their places in a trench occupied by several soldiers. The dogs were restless. They seemed to sense the impending fight. 

Every dog spotted the enemy's location immediately.

Whether through the sense of smell, eye, or hearing, or because of their keen animal intuition, they were on the alert, listening and looking at the designated spot 150 yards away. No one knew whether there were 5, 6 or even a dozen Japs there. 

Guardians were to follow the Corporal and dogs as rapidly as possible. Every man was equipped for fighting with an army revolver, two hand grenades and a 10-inch knife. 

At 5:40 the Corporal was watching the tick-tock of his watch. Buck did not unsnap the leash on his dog but unbuckled the collar to which it was fastened and held the dog by the nap on his neck. All the dogs were likewise stripped for the battle. They were all growling and wagging their tails

for release. They acted like race horses at the post, eager to break away.

The Japs had invited this battle. The K-9 brigade was ready. They indicated their courage as perfectly as any group of fighting men ever asesmbled to do battle with an enemy. They were asking for no quarter and expected to give none. They expected to kill and be killed. Daybreak was approaching when the Corporal ordered, "let 'em go." 

Bum sprang to the embankment. He got a few feet start on some of the other dogs. The Corporal, Buck and the other soldiers climbed the trench embankment and started to follow the charging animals. Before the soldiers were half way to the place the dogs had plunged into the hole. 

The machine guns started spraying bullets at the approaching soldiers as the dogs made their attack. The young lean Corporal was several feet in the lead and two other soldiers had passed the old man. The Corporal went down 40 yards away. A soldier running just behind the Corporal also fell. Buck felt the sting of something hit his shoulders but kept going.

The dog fight was on. Pandemonium followed. In less than five minutes the place was surrounded with 200 grizzled fighting men on the rim. The machine gun fire had suddenly ceased. Deep throated growls of fighting dogs and cries of anguish from the victims were bursting from the hole as the dogs continued to ferret out their enemies. 

It was light enough on top to distinguish one soldier from another, but still dark all over the space where the dogs were fighting. Two hand grenades exploded almost simultaneously.

The shrieking voice of humans and whining cry of dogs were

heard. The noise subsided immediately but broke loose again when some of the dogs had found another Jap hiding in one of the shell craters. The hiding place of the Japs was twice as large as was expected. Accurate air bombers had enlarged the hole, made it deeper and caused numerous small craters. 

It was from these that the fighting dogs were still attacking their frightened enemies.

The noise subsided again in a few moments. An officer then threw a flashlight into the center of the hole. Three human bodies and a dog were visible. He ordered the guardians to call their dogs. The first dog to show up was Duke, one of the four that had joined the brigade two days previous. His master did not call his name, but some of the hundred soldiers who knew him did. His master was the soldier who fell with the Corporal. 

Duke came out wagging his tail. Metal from the exploding grenades had struck him. His body was covered in blood. Sam, Butch and Rube also answered the call of their masters and came up the banks of the hole, all with their tongues out, wounded and bloody from fight. Buck called his dog and got no response. A depressing chill came over him. He became suddenly pallid. "Man, you're shot!" a startled soldier exclaimed. "I'm not hurt bad," the old man replied weakly. "I want to see my dog. Help me locate him."

Blood appeared on his shirt over his shoulder blade. He called the dog's name again. Flashlights were now beaming over the whole battle scene. Big Ned, the dog that resembled 

Bum so much and had lost an eye in a previous battle was dead. A Jap bayonet had been thrust through his body just

behind his forelegs. Bob, another fighter, was torn almost to pieces by a grenade.

Then the familiar friendly whine came to the old man's ears. It was comforting. Flashlights spotted the big iron gray dog soldier. He could not stand. He was lying on a ledge near the top. The machine gun nearby was silent and the Jap who manned it was lifeless. His head had been almost torn from his shoulders.

The dog had been seriously stabbed and slashed in the death fight. One stab wound in his neck looked particularly dangerous. His left foreleg was cut into the bone and a long slashing gash had laid open his skin of his left side. There were stab wounds in his shoulder. 

Two soldiers helped Buck carry him to the top of the hole. 

He was given first aid and the flow of blood was stopped while a truck was being brought up to take the wounded dogs away. 

The officer who had planned the dog attack came up. "The dog attack was certainly a success," he told Buck and the other soldier guardians. "Yeah, the dogs really did a good job 'disturbing'," the old guardian replied. "What's the final score?" "Nine Japs were in the hole. The dogs accounted for six of them. Four of these committed hari kari with a grenade. 

Two were definitely killed by the dogs and the other three were so badly torn they were helpless. "We are sorry to have lost two good men, the Corporal and Duke's master, and the two wonderful fighting dogs, Ned and Bob."

Buck rode on a truck with Bum and the other two guardians and wounded dogs to the field headquarters.

"Good job, boy," old Buck said as he patted the dog sympathetically. "What about your wound Bullard?" one of the guardians. asked. "It don't hurt any yet," Buck answered.

The men and dogs were placed in an ambulance and were taken 20 miles back to a coast point, ferried across a few miles of water. In an hour they were back to the island port city where they had landed three months ago. By noon the old man was on a hospital cot and the dogs were being treated nearby by a veterinary.

When Bum was unloaded, his master patted his head. and warned the veterinary and attendants not to lock the dog up. "He'll get well of those wounds a lot quicker if his door is left open. I'll come back and be with him this afternoon when you get ready to sew the slash in his side up." 

Buck was not badly wounded. He did not take it seriously. A machine gun bullet had passed through the top of his shoulder blade and was easily treated. 

Because of the old man's age, the doctor had ordered that he be kept in bed and watched closely until the wound was healed. He read and listened to such radio programs as were permitted. He was given some alcoholic stimulants. His appetite for this was regular-in fact, got more than his quota by making friends with the nurses. He had been out of touch with his dog for more than a week. He had received two reports daily that the dog was doing well. 

He had a background worry. He had not heard from his. son since they bid each other good-bye at the war plant four

months ago.

He contented himself with "No news is good news." 

Several days more passed. He was still ordered to stay in bed and not to leave the hospital ward. He awoke one night looking into the eyes-the very blue eyes of a blonde haired nurse. She was new on the job at his cot. She was smiling. "You are Private Bullard?" she inquired. "Right, sister," Buck shot back. "How'd you guess it?" "Looking over the convalescents records in the office, I noticed the name. It's my fiance's name. I received a radio message from him yesterday. He said his father was in the islands with a Bum. I didn't understand it." 

The old man laughed. "So you and Buzz have really decided to take the plunge?" he inquired. "In nurse headquarters at 9:00 o'clock tomorrow night. 

You are really his father?" "Yeah." "I just slipped off duty and came to this ward to see you. 

I've been on the island here six weeks, but had a year's training before I shipped over." "You seem to be a smart girl," the old man mused as he eyed the ceiling lights. "Slipping off duty for love of a man is something. Why not get me a permit from that hard-boiled doctor to leave the building in the morning. I'm in condition." "I'm a little in love too-with B-Bum. He's a dog soldier. 

Was badly wounded a short time ago. He and Buzz were great pals before the war started. We saved his life in a storm. Since he's dragged me out of the gutter, got me two good guard jobs and into the army. He's about recovered now. I know he's eager to go in again.

"I want to see him-want to convince him if I can that I'll be as grateful and loyal as a man as he's been to me as a dog," his quivering voice muttered. "Get me out and we'll both be present to see you and son married tomorrow night."

The nurse pressed the old man's hand and walked out briskly with happiness lighting her eyes. She returned in half an hour with a little slip of white paper, which she handed to her future father-in-law.

Buck was up and ate breakfast ahead of schedule next morning. He walked out of the hospital ward with the pass. 

He walked back of the buildings and down the slope of a hill. A hundred yards away, sheltered by large umbrella trees were a half dozen wire cages. Only two were occupied. A soldier guard was sitting on a stool some distance up the slope. Buck presented his permit. "Oh!" he exclaimed. "You're the master of the big German Shepherd, Bum." "He's my dog," Buck snapped. "I came to see him.” "Best dog I ever attended. I've exercised him every day for a week. He hopped on three legs at first but now uses all four. He's been schooled thoroughly and behaves perfectly. 

He's my idea of a real dog soldier." "That's Bum's cage to the left," he indicated pointing. "Miss Pat, our hospital messenger dog, is visiting him."

The gray war dog was lying a few feet in front of his quarters. His head was up and his ears straight. The fuzzynosed Airedale was standing near. "I'll be my Nanny's gone," the old man blurted out laughing. "Soak my feet in Jap soup and see if my head isn't soft!" "What'n hell so funny?" the soldier asked, puzzled.

"Funny? Nuthin' to them. They're serious. Rubbin' noses again here in the jungles. I gotta laugh. They're smarter, daggon' it, than people." "I still don't know the joke." "There ain't none. But these two pooches met first in a dog hospital. Both were victims of motor accidents. They trained at the same school at the same time for war service." 

The old man laughed again. It was funny to him. 

The dog heard and recognized his voice. He stood up and looked at the two men standing on the side of the hill, 50 yards away. He wagged his tail and broke into a gallop to meet his master. The dog was so happy that he reared up and came near pushing the man over. "A real soldier, you are, Bum," he said, patting the dog tenderly.

A golden sun was rising over the Pacific. Across the road to the north a bugle was trumpeting. It was the reveille.

The old man and dog stood at attention and watched the flag as it was slowly raised into the breeze.
