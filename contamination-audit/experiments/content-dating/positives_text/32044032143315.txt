Chaos in Sentencing Youth Offenders Introduction 

The histories of delinquency and crime presented in this study are taken from records of one hundred cases of youths going through the mills of criminal justice in New York City. These records were painstakingly gathered from numerous sources by the Committee on Youth and Justice of the Community Service Society of New York. All the cases are of boys; all names are disguised. 

Some of the cases were selected from verified newspaper accounts of crimes committed by youths; others were obtained from the records of courts, parole authorities, and private social agencies. Only serious cases were included. Either the criminal offense itself was serious or, if the crime was a lesser one, the consequences to the offender were serious. 

There is very little descriptive material of the boys themselves. 

The cases, as written, are more in the nature of chronicles of events as the boys make their way through various episodes of court arraignment, probation, commitment to reformatory or prison, parole, rearrest, and recommitment. The reader can, however, form a fairly clear mental picture of the individual boy as he is shunted from one authority to another in our unintegrated criminal justice system.

It will be seen from these accounts of youth offenders that a judge of one court in sentencing a boy knows nothing of what a judge in another court is doing. The several criminal courts initially set the mold of correctional treatment in individual cases and scarcely any consistent policy is observable among them. Sentenced youths are gathered together at a reformatory for correctional 570790 [3]

training and treatment and the institution's officials have to do the best they can with them regardless of whether the boys are well selected or are ill-suited to the type of treatment available at the institution. 

At that point the judges are busy with new cases and are no longer responsible for reviewing or modifying their earlier decisions in originally passing sentence. After a period of imprisonment another independent body, the parole authority, makes the decisions about the final course of treatment. Just as the institution officials take without question what the judges send to them, so the parole officials take what the institution has on its hands. 

There is at present no way of greatly improving the whole task of correctional treatment because there is no central administration of it.

As to specific forms of treatment after sentence, individual boys are subject to examination and supervision by a collection of independent courts, clinics, probation services, institutions, and parole services. Often a boy is found to be technically subject to the jurisdiction of two or more agencies at the same time. Parole supervision of a boy following release from an institution is sometimes interrupted by his commitment to another institution, although no new offense has been committed subsequent to parole. 

A first offender may be shipped off to Elmira Reformatory by one court as a felon, while another boy, after repeated offenses, may be placed on probation by a different court or may be dealt with as a misdemeanant for the commission of what is actually a felony. 

Unquestionably there is flexibility in the present system but it is flexibility without a clear policy or design for the treatment and rehabilitation of youth offenders. Dispositions are made by one governmental agency, execution of the task of treatment is carried out by another up to a point, when another body takes over, followed in many instances by another and still another control. 

The business of rehabilitating youths should be chargeable to [4]

a single administrative body alone responsible for its mistakes and successes, for striving to correct mistakes in future administration and to enlarge the proportion of successes. Under the existing system no official agency is responsible to another and no one is responsible for determining the entire pattern and method of correctional treatment of a youth whose reclamation is being attempted through control by public agencies. 

The remedy for many of these defects may be found in the Youth Correction Authority plan which has been presented to the 

New York Legislature for consideration. The Youth Correction 

Authority would bring order out of chaos and more readily restore youthful offenders who are now tossed into unsupportable conditions of freedom or into the hazards of mass-imprisonment by numerous official agencies often working at cross purposes with one another. The Authority plan is not offered as a substitute for crime preventive work among children or older adolescents; it is proposed as a substitute for the present system of sentencing and correctional treatment of 16 to 21 year old offenders. [5]

CHAPTER I Expanding Criminal Careers The Case of James 

James C, on the day of his parole from the New York State Training School for Boys, was arrested for the burglary of a store in company with two companions, one a youth of 21 and the other a juvenile aged 15. At the time of arrest James was within three days of his sixteenth birthday but he became an adult, as the criminal law defines it, by the time the Grand Jury was called upon to indict him. 

Burglary. The trio had broken into a candy store. They were seeking something of lesser bulk and greater value than candy. Their loot consisted of a quantity of cigarettes valued at $55, currency amounting to $4.50, and 40 cents worth of postage stamps. The sixteen year old James and his older companion, being unable to raise $1000 bail, were held at the Tombs prison; the juvenile companion was taken to the Children's Court. 

Trial and Disposition. Trial of the case in the Court of General 

Sessions followed speedily after indictment. The original plea of not guilty to the felony charge of burglary was changed, presumably upon the advice of his attorney after a whispered conference between attorney and judge, to a pleading of guilt to petit larceny. 

An examination by the Court's psychiatric clinic disclosed that James was neither psychotic nor mentally deficient but was "prob ably of dull normal intelligence." He was sentenced to the New 

York State Vocational Institution at Coxsackie where 16 to 19 year old offenders are received. The older companion was given a suspended sentence and placed on probation for two years. The younger one had been committed to Children's Village by the [6]

Manhattan Children's Court two days after the arrest. James had an earlier delinquency history extending over a period of years, which is set forth below.

THE DELINQUENCY CAREER of James 

Family Situation. James was one of three children in an impoverished family. The parents, both living, had received assistance and relief from private and public agencies during a portion of the time when James, because of various offenses, was marching in and out of Children's Court. The family guidance was evidently unsatisfactory. The role of the mother fluctuated from that of an accuser to that of a protector. The record reveals nothing about 

James' relation to his father except that, at one point in the boy's institutional experience, he expressed a considerable hatred for him. 

First Delinquency. At ten years of age James, with a juvenile companion, stole some cash from the poor box in a Catholic Church. 

He was taken to the Manhattan Children's Court, adjudged to be delinquent, and remanded to a shelter of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. His companion was committed to the New York Catholic Protectory and James, emerging from the Shelter in three days, was placed on probation. He was introduced to the recreational program of the Police Department's Juvenile Aid Bureau, responded satisfactorily enough on probation, and so was discharged from supervision in about three months. 

Theft. Two years later James, then twelve, was arrested with a companion for stealing merchandise from a truck. He was again adjudged delinquent by the Manhattan Children's Court and again remanded to the Shelter of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. The twelve year old remained at the Shelter over Christmas Day. On December 26th, despite a recommendation of commitment to a correctional institution made by the investigating probation officer, James was placed on probation. After [7]

five months, with improvement noted at home and school, he was discharged from probation by the Court. 

Disobedience and Probation. Less than a year later James' mother took him to Children's Court because she could no longer discipline him. The boy's age was then 13 years and six months. 

For the third time he was adjudged delinquent and, for the third time, remanded to the Shelter. The remand period originally scheduled to last for five weeks at the Shelter was extended to five months upon James' removal from the Shelter to Children's Village at Dobbs Ferry. James was reported to have shown improvement at that institution and at the end of the remand period wa placed on probation by a justice of the Children's Court, hereafter referred to as Judge Primus. Six months later he was discharged from probation.

New Offense. One month elapsed and James, 14, was arrested with two older boys for car theft. He later appeared with his mother before Judge Primus in Children's Court when he was for the fourth time adjudged delinquent and remanded to a Shelter of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children for one week for further investigation. James insisted that he had been invited to ride with the older boy and that he did not know the car had been stolen. Both the investigating probation officer and the Children's Court clinic recommended commitment to an institution. 

That advice was disregarded by the judge. 

Probation Conditions. Probation was granted to James on the following terms: the mother was to move to another neighborhood in about two weeks; the boy was to be at home early every night and attend school and church regularly. He was to read 

Modern Pioneers, Captains Courageous, and David Copperfield.

He was to attend the movies once a week to see a picture selected by the probation officer. A review of the books and pictures was to be sent to Judge Primus. One wonders how well this proba [8]

tionary home-work assignment may have fitted in with regular school home-work and how much recreational satisfaction may have been derived from the movie of enforced selection. Two weeks were required for dissolution of this beneficent regime. 

Remand. James was again in Court before Judge Primus for violation of the conditions of probation. He was remanded for four months to the New York Catholic Protectory. At the expiration of that time James was in Court before Judge Secundus. While at the Protectory James had become interested in the study of wireless and received a grade B rating. He was restored to probation and reported every two weeks. He attended school regularly and received average grades. His mother reported him to be more obedient.

New Offense and Commitment. Five and one-half months later 

James was arrested with two companions for stealing three cartons of butter from a truck. He was remanded to a shelter and on the following day was committed by Judge Tertius to the New York State Training School for Boys at Warwick. Members of the institution's staff were not hopeful regarding a satisfactory adjustment by James. They thought him "hard to reach." A Warwick social worker found James' mother uncooperative. She regarded his commitment as severe and uncalled-for punishment. James evidently entertained a similar opinion for some months later he was reported to be absent without leave from Warwick. 

Arrest. The time was near the end of October, 1939. For three weeks James remained undiscovered and a warrant was issued for his arrest. The mother became hysterical when the warrant officer visited the home and during the scene of hysterics James, who was at home at the time, made his escape. He was later picked up and returned to Warwick. After five months he was released for an "extended visit" to his home, amounting to a period of a month, after which he was placed on parole. [9]

Correctional School. The saga of James has now been traced down to the commission of the burglary and subsequent commitment with which this story commenced. After an eleven-month stay at 

Coxsackie James was approved for parole. The record discloses that "he made a poor adjustment at the institution, although he was rated as fair in the bakeshop training program.' 

New Offense-Penitentiary. Within five months after his release from Coxsackie, while on parole, James was arrested in Manhattan on a disorderly conduct charge. The sentence was suspended. Two days later he was arrested for burglary, making his second appearance on that charge in the Court of General Sessions. Again the charge was reduced to petit larceny and he was sentenced to the New York Penitentiary at Rikers Island in his eighteenth year. Automatically, he became a parole violator from Coxsackie.

Summary. As James looks through the bars of the Penitentiary and ponders his career of delinquency and crime he must have a wellrooted feeling that the court sentencing system is a strange mixture of leniency and harshness. The mother had become so thoroughly accustomed to probation for her son, after four different appearances in Children's Court for stealing, that in the words of one official "she refused to accept the necessity for his commitment" and shielded him when he made his escape. 

The boy himself from 10 to 18 years of age had not desisted from thievery and burglary. Yet he was let off time and again. 

When at last he was sent to the Training School he chose his own time to depart. The consequences of that decision were not onerous; he was returned to the place from which he had escaped. 

Perhaps other boys at the institution sustained greater injury through association with the "big shot" upon his return. His final leave culminated in parole, the initial day of which he contemptuously celebrated by burglarizing a store. 

By this time the boy had reached his sixteenth birthday and was [10]

ready for experience with criminal justice procedures for adults.

His first felony was labelled a misdemeanor and he was committed to a State correctional institution for young offenders of the 16 to 19 year age group, where he joined company with boys from all over the State, some committed for felony, some for misdemeanor, and some showing no criminal offense in their records, having been adjudged wayward minors. 

Although numbered among the youngest admissible inmatesjust turned sixteen-he was already wise in crime and in "copping a plea." He was known to the police, city probation officers, state institution officials, and parole officers as an untrustworthy boy. 

He did not adjust well at Coxsackie and certainly had not been rehabilitated. Yet the State, under the existing sentencing and correctional system, had no other place to which he could be sent for closer, more rigorous supervision. He was paroled and that may have been a good thing, if not for him, perhaps for some of the more promising inmates who were then deprived of his company. 

He was returned to his parents who had never been able to divert him from crime.

True only to his pattern of criminal behavior, he soon turned again to burglary and larceny. Again the judicial sentencing system went through some of the motions of a felony procedure, ground out a misdemeanor conviction, and sentenced the boy to a city institution for short-term misdemeanants. Granted that our correctional systems are lacking in suitable places for dealing with young offenders, one cannot understand why he was sent to the Penitentiary. He might have been returned to the State's institution at Coxsackie; he will be returned there anyway as a parole violator at the expiration of his Penitentiary term of imprisonment. Or, if the felony charge had been pressed, he might have been sent to the State's Elmira Reformatory, or again, he might have been sent to the City's Reformatory for 16 to 30 year old misdemeanants on the charge for which he actually was convicted. [11]

Instead, he was sent to the City Penitentiary where he was incarcerated along with several hundred youths and several thousand adult prisoners!

The General Sessions Court chose to place the boy under the care of city correctional authorities for an interval of unconstructive, damaging mass-imprisonment pending resumption of responsibility by State correctional authorities who later will deal with him as a parole violator of the State Vocational Training School. 

This case is given as an introductory example illustrating the chaos of sentencing and anticipating a forthcoming companion pamphlet, Chaos in Correctional Treatment of Youth Offenders. 

The Court Preserves Its Pound of Sentencing Power The Case of Jerry 

Late in January, 1939, Jerry P——, aged eighteen and a first offender so far as known to the police, in company with two other youths held up the clerk of a liquor store in Brooklyn. Jerry and one of his companions were armed. The robbers obtained three dollars and made their escape.

Robbery and Arrest. On the following March 15th, the same trio, again armed as before, held up another liquor store and were arrested. They were held without bail at the Raymond Street Jail and about a month later were indicted for robbery first degree, grand larceny first degree, and assault second degree for the March hold-up. They pleaded not guilty. 

Change of Pleas. Later, upon the recommendation of the District 

Attorney and with the consent of the Kings County Court, all three youths withdrew their original pleas. Jerry and one of his companions pleaded guilty to robbery third degree, armed, and the other to robbery third degree, unarmed.

Sentence. The boys were arraigned on May 10, 1939, for sentence. [12]

They were committed to Elmira Reformatory on an indeterminate sentence for the hold-up of March 15th. On May 15th they were admitted to Elmira Reformatory. 

Second Indictment. During the period of their detention, awaiting disposition by the Court, all three youths admitted their participation in the robbery of January 27th. For that offense they were indicted for robbery first degree, petit larceny, and assault second degree. A warrant was lodged with the Superintendent of Elmira 

Reformatory for their eventual return to the Kings County Court to face the second indictment.

Elmira Clinic Report. The Elmira Reformatory clinic reported that Jerry was of average intelligence, I.Q. 96. He had been reared with "indulgence and scant supervision." It was noted that he had lived in a "delinquent and congested neighborhood * * * this inmate is by far the most dangerous and the most aggressive of the three, and he has a poor prognosis." 

Parole. Jerry must have shown some improvement within a year's time for he was accredited as eligible for parole upon obtaining suitable employment. The family butcher offered him a job at $15 per week. On October 11, 1940, Jerry and his two companions were released on parole but first had to appear at the Kings County Court in compliance with the bench warrant relating to the offense of January, 1939. They were held to answer the indictment which had been returned against them after their admission to Elmira. 

Held in Jail. Jerry was rearrested on October 12th and detained in the Raymond Street Jail to await trial on the indictment for the earlier robbery. His companions were also held. Upon arraignment 

Jerry pleaded not guilty. Bail was set at $5,000. Failing to produce bail he was remanded to Raymond Street Jail. On a writ of habeas corpus, a justice of the Supreme Court ordered Jerry and his companions admitted to bail, which was provided the following day. [13]

Parole and Imprisonment. Jerry reported to his State parole officer on November 2, 1940. Thus, after a lapse of three weeks, parole supervision began. The State officials had already determined that 

Jerry was ready for re-establishment in the community. No one would deny that his eagerly awaited new start on parole should have been attended by every favorable factor and encouragement. 

It must have been psychologically disturbing, to the point of inducing extreme bitterness and resentment against a seemingly vindictive criminal justice system, to leave the Reformatory on parole only to enter the Raymond Street Jail. The new imprisonment, the legal contest over bail, and pleading to the earlier offense must have pretty effectively negated the parole possibilities. 

Change of Plea. Jerry and his two companions were arraigned in the Kings County Court. Upon recommendation of the District 

Attorney the original plea of not guilty was withdrawn and a plea of guilt to robbery third degree for the offense of January, 1939, was accepted. The Court rescinded bail and remanded the youths to the Raymond Street Jail pending pronouncement of sentence.

The District Attorney's petition relating to Jerry and his two companions stated in part that "the ends of justice are sufficiently met by the acceptance of this plea at this time, after their having served a term in Elmira Reformatory."

Jerry and his companions were arraigned on December 4, 1940, for sentence. The youths were given suspended sentences without probation inasmuch as all three were at the time under the super vision of the New York State Division of Parole. 

The Court's sentencing power had enjoyed its full opportunity of expression but the youths had been sacrificed to useless technicality in criminal justice procedure.

The Final Item. While allegedly attempting to commit armed robbery on May 17, 1941, Jerry was killed by a member of the Police Department. [14]

Sentencing at Cross Purposes with Rehabilitation The Case of Tony 

During the afternoon of January 3, 1937, Tony S—, aged 20, with a youthful companion, burglarized a residence, taking $600 in cash and jewelry. That evening the youths broke into another residence. Tony's companion was arrested at the scene and Tony himself was apprehended some days later. Events relating to the companion will now be dropped from further consideration in this case.

Following an indictment for burglary third degree and petit larceny relating to the afternoon offense, Tony pleaded guilty to the misdemeanor charge of petit larceny in the Kings County Court. 

Disposition. On February 24, 1937, Tony was sentenced for an indeterminate term to the New York City Reformatory at New Hampton for the offense committed on the afternoon of January 3rd. A warrant was filed at the institution to assure subsequent arrest for the burglary committed on the evening of the same day. 

Parole. In a little less than a year, Tony was released from the 

New Hampton Reformatory on parole by the New York City Parole Commission. He appeared before the Kings County Court on January 18, 1938, to answer the charge relating to the evening burglary. Withdrawing the original plea of not guilty, Tony pleaded guilty to grand larceny second degree for the earlier offense. He was remanded to the Raymond Street Jail. 

New Commitment. Tony on parole, yet imprisoned at the jail, was sentenced on January 31st to Elmira Reformatory as a felon. 

Tony had committed two felonious offenses in one day. The first felony charge was reduced to misdemeanor. Why was the youth sentenced for an indefinite term at the City Reformatory for [15]

misdemeanants, only to be sentenced later for another indefinite term to the State Reformatory for youths convicted as felons? 

Presumably he had been rehabilitated while at the City institution, otherwise he would not have been released on parole in less than a year of a three-year indefinite sentence. If he was not reformed he should not have been released from New Hampton. 

If, however, his release was justified because of a satisfactory adjustment he should not have been re-sentenced to Elmira. The New 

Hampton officials and New York City Parole Commission made one decision and the Kings County Court took an opposite course. 

Tony was dealt with as if he were two different criminals because he committed two burglaries, one in the afternoon and one in the evening. After nearly a year of correctional treatment by the City institution, as the afternoon thief, the Court determined that he was to undergo correctional treatment, as the evening thief, by the State institution. He was twice rehabilitated and twice paroled. 

That amounted to chaos in sentencing which interfered with sensible administration of correctional treatment. 

The Worst That Could Have Happened

The Case of Harris 

Less than six months after release from Elmira Reformatory, Harris Mcommitted murder in an attempt at robbery and shortly thereafter was convicted and sentenced to death. He had become accustomed to a cell as his home, having been incarcerated at the Workhouse, the Penitentiary, Elmira Reformatory and Sing Sing Prison. The delinquency career of Harris began when he was eleven years of age and developed, after he became sixteen, into a full-fledged crime career. 

Family Background. Harris was next to the youngest in a family of eight children. His mother's death occurred when he was ten [16]

years of age. So long as the mother lived the family had not depended upon public or private agencies for assistance. Later the family received aid from the Department of Welfare. The record of a private welfare agency indicates that there were questions about the father's adequacy as a parent. He was alleged to be an alcoholic.

School. Described as "restless and undisciplined," Harris was, for a time at least, something of a problem at school. He fought with other children and threw stones through windows. The school authorities believed that he was neglected at home. At the age of eleven Harris ran away from home, returning at 11:00 p.m. two days later. This episode resulted in the father's presenting a delinquency petition to the Manhattan Children's Court. Harris was remanded to a Shelter of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children for eight days pending an investigation of his case.

Dependency Commitment. Harris was nearing his sixteenth birthday when he was arrested with two companions for rolling a couple of spare automobile wheels. Harris told the arresting officer that he was sixteen years of age. Accepting the boy's statement about his "maturity" the case was brought before the adult criminal courts. He was arraigned in a Magistrates' 

Court and remanded to the Tombs from January 12 to January 29! Then he was arraigned in the Court of Special Sessions on a charge of disorderly conduct. Imprisonment at the Tombs would appear to be an extremely damaging prelude to prosecution for disorderly conduct.

The Court of Special Sessions learned his true age-under sixteen-and discharged him to the custody of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children pending disposition in the Children's Court. CRIME AND PUNISHMENT 

Five months after his discharge from Children's Court, Harris was arrested with two companions for breaking into the basement of a building through the coal chute and stealing sacks of copper and brass valued at about $50. The charge was burglary, the plea not guilty, bail of $500 was not forthcoming, and Harris was imprisoned at the Tombs pending action on an indictment. The case was dismissed by the Grand Jury.

Sentenced to Elmira. Six months later Harris was involved in an attempted burglary. Then, at seventeen, he was convicted of attempted burglary in the Queens County Court and sentenced to Elmira Reformatory for an indeterminate term. 

The story of Harris now discloses rapid and serious deterioration. The first sentence to Elmira Reformatory produced no reformation, a subsequent sentence to the Penitentiary brought no penitence. Harris became a truly hardened criminal, bold, ugly in his sullenness, and altogether anti-social in his outlook. 

What could have been done at the crisis when, as a youth of seventeen, he was sent away to Elmira? Let the reader consider his home life, as sketchily revealed in the record, the evident neglect, and the routine probation supervision limited to ascertainment [ 20 ]

that he had not been in trouble for long periods. The catastrophe of a commitment to Elmira fell upon him at a time when he should have been placed in a hospitable setting where he could have had a fair chance to respond to personal guidance. He needed someone who would work with him in an intelligent, sympathetic, helpful way.

Parole and New Offense. Harris remained at Elmira nearly two years and was paroled. Nothing is heard of him until eight months later when he broke into a grocery store by night and stole articles valued at $50. At this time the M- family was receiving assistance from the Department of Welfare. 

Upon commission of the new offense the Elmira Reformatory officials filed a warrant for his arrest as a parole violator. But prior to the execution of that warrant the criminal law was to take a turn revealing a curious example of judicial sentencing. Harris entered a pleading of guilt to petit larceny upon reduction of the felony charge of burglary in the third degree. 

Penitentiary Interlude. He was sentenced by the Court of General 

Sessions to imprisonment for one year at the City Penitentiary, an institution for misdemeanants. On August 3, 1938, after a stay of seven months, he was released from the Penitentiary and was returned to Elmira Reformatory for violation of parole where he served out the maximum of his indeterminate sentence (until November 20, 1940) for the attempted burglary committed on 

August 25, 1934, when he was sixteen. What purpose was served in sending Harris, a parolee, to the city institution for misdemeanants as a preliminary to returning him to the state institution for youths convicted of felony?

Workhouse Sentence. Two months after the expiration of his maximum term at Elmira, Harris was arrested under an assumed name for speeding and operating a car without a license. He was fined $15 on each count by the Manhattan Magistrates' Traffic [21]

Court and, failing to pay the fine, was sentenced to the Workhouse of the City Penitentiary. He was released in nine days upon payment of the $15 fine for speeding. 

Murder. Three months and eight days later Harris shot and killed a victim who attempted to resist in a holdup. Prior to the robbery and murder Harris, according to a statement of the assistant district attorney who prosecuted the murder case, had participated in fifteen other robberies. Skillful detection by the police tracked him down. An assistant district attorney, patient and competent, had procured a confession of the murder in nine hours after the arrest. 

Harris was taken to the line-up at police headquarters. "Did you make a statement to the District Attorney?" came the question. "Yes, I did." Harris kept his head down but his voice was clear. "Are you also known as Joe M—————?” "That's right." "Take him down," ordered the captain.

There was a brief arraignment in Felony Court. "The prisoner, who had thick brown hair, heavy eyebrows, and a prominent nose and gives the impression of leanness-he stands 5 feet 9 and weighs 160—had nothing to say."

Execution. Harris was indicted by the New York County Grand 

Jury for murder in the first degree. A blue ribbon jury found him guilty. He was sentenced to die in the electric chair. The conviction was upheld by the Court of Appeals. He was put to death at Sing 

Sing Prison at 11:05 o'clock in the evening some months later.

Harris did not make a formal statement before being taken to the death chamber. Another prisoner who had been sentenced to die on the same night had been taken out of the death house earlier in the day. His sentence had been commuted to life imprisonment. * Quoted from the New York Times. [22]

Would not Society have been better served if Harris himself could have had a break in his childhood of neglect and at the time of his attempted burglary when he was sentenced to Elmira and before he committed himself to a life of crime leading to the electric chair? [23]

CHAPTER II Probation or Punishment; 

Felon or Misdemeanant? 

The Case of Andrew 

Andrew R, aged eighteen, made the headlines in connection with an unsuccessful attempt at robbery. His victim resisted and shot the youth in the chest. Andrew ran from the scene but was caught by a patrolman, placed under arrest and held in the prison ward at Bellevue Hospital. This offense marked the first contact of the boy or his family with the police or courts.

Disposition. The wound proved to be not serious and Andrew was arraigned on a charge of robbery and felonious assault. He was held at the Tombs and in due course was indicted. After pleading guilty to attempted assault second degree on January 17, 1940, in the Court of General Sessions, Andrew was placed on probation. 

New Offense. About two months later, while on probation in New

COMMENT ON THE CASES OF ANDREW AND VITO 

Within a period of three months' time Andrew was twice indicted and convicted of felony offenses, one committed in Manhattan and the other in the Bronx. He was placed on probation by one court upon the first conviction and was sentenced as a misdemeanant to New Hampton Reformatory by another court upon the second conviction. Officials of the Reformatory first became responsible for Andrew's correctional training. Next the New York

City Parole Commission and the Probation Department of General 

Sessions took over supervision of him in the community, working independently at the task of post-institutional rehabilitation. 

Vito, a first offender, was packed off, manacled, to Elmira Reformatory under state control and later was supervised in the community by the State Board of Parole. Thus we find variety in the courses of treatment in similar cases. But it was variety of treatment administered by separate agencies of criminal justice rather than flexibility of individualized correctional measures which should be determined upon by a single authority exercising uninterrupted responsibility.

CHAPTER III 

Some Are Committed as 

Wayward Minors

The criminal justice system presents many examples of astonishing, and seemingly inexplicable, contrasts in the sentencing of youth offenders. In no phase of judicial sentencing is this more evident than in the practice of excusing a defendant from making answer to a misdemeanor or felony charge by means of declaring him to be a wayward minor. 

The legal status of a wayward minor is not entirely clear to a layman. The Appellate Division of the Supreme Court has held that a "wayward minor,' as defined by this section, (913-a of the Code of Criminal Procedure) is not a criminal.”* In a later construction by the State's Attorney General, however, conviction as a wayward minor "is a criminal offense within the meaning of the Correction Law."** Whatever the construction of the Law's meaning, wayward minors are sometimes imprisoned along with youths convicted of misdemeanor and with other youths who have committed felony but are convicted of misdemeanor on a plea of guilty to a reduced charge. 

Moreover, youths who have committed no offense, merely being disobedient to parents, are sometimes imprisoned as wayward minors. These latter are released from imprisonment on parole, exactly as convicted persons are released and, if their subsequent observance of the conditions of parole or their home situations are not satisfactory, they may be sent to the Penitentiary where, presumably, they could not have been sent in the first instance. * People ex rel. Deordio v. Palmer, 1930, 230 App. Div. 397, 244 N.Y.S. 727. 

** 1932, Op. Atty. Gen. 315. 

[36]

The legal definition of a wayward minor is as follows: 

"Any person between the ages of sixteen and twenty-one who either (1) is habitually addicted to the use of drugs or the intemperate use of intoxicating liquors, or (2) habitually associates with dissolute persons, or (3) is found of his or her own free will and knowledge in a house of prostitution or assignation or ill fame, or (4) habitually associates with thieves, prostitutes, pimps, or procurers or disorderly persons, or (5) is willfully disobedient to the reasonable and lawful commands of parent, guardian or other custodian and is morally depraved or is in danger of becoming morally depraved may be deemed a wayward minor."

The Case of Chan Lin 

Chan Lin M, a Chinese boy nearly seventeen years of age, was complained against in a Manhattan Magistrates' Court by his father.

Family Background. The boy's family, established in the Chinese quarter of lower Manhattan, was in comfortable circumstances. 

Chan Lin was apparently the only person in the family who had become thoroughly Americanized and this the father appeared to resent. There were frequent quarrels and the boy's dissatisfaction was manifested in a poor school record. No member of the family had previously been in contact with the courts or social agencies.

Court Appearances. The father had been unable to discipline his son in the manner he thought appropriate. He therefore had 1 [ 37 ]

Chan Lin arrested on a complaint of disorderly conduct. Upon arraignment in the Magistrates' Court the boy was placed on "unofficial probation" for a period of one year.

About two months later the boy was again haled into Court on the written complaint of his father. Chan Lin was remanded by the magistrate to the Tombs pending investigation. In three days he was returned to court, charged with "willful disobedience" to parents, stealing from home, refusing to work and truanting from school. It was a case of ill-adjustment at home, a not uncommon and frequently transitory experience with adolescents. No criminal offense had been committed. 

Sentence. Chan Lin was adjudged to be a wayward minor, although he fitted into none of the specifications defining a wayward minor, save willful disobedience, and was sentenced to the New York City Reformatory at New Hampton for an indeterminate term not to exceed three years. 

Parole. After a stay of approximately three and one half months, Chan Lin was paroled. He returned to live with his family. A year and a half later he was still on parole and was reported to be in good standing.

He obtained a job as a messenger but was discharged because he was unable to read the addresses on packages and failed to deliver them. For a while he lived with his father in a furnished room in lower Manhattan. 

At seventeen George was sent to Bowdoin Farm School at

New Hamburg for homeless and transient boys, maintained by the Children's Aid Society. He decided that he did not wish to become a farmer and returned to New York City. About this time George contracted syphilis. 

Aided by the Emergency Home Relief Bureau, George stayed at the Mills Hotel, a "modern hotel for respectable men of small means."

Hospital Treatment. Upon learning that his venereal disease was in an actively infectious stage, a social worker arranged for George to enter the New York Hospital for treatment. The hospital, when ready to discharge him, hesitated to do so because of his "indifferent attitude toward his condition". and feared [45]

that he would not return regularly to the clinic for follow-up treatment. An officer of the Police Department's Juvenile Aid 

Bureau became interested in George's case. 

New Hampton Reformatory. Several placements of George had been tried without success. At this point in his story he was living at a municipal lodging house. He was said to be in habitual association with dissolute persons and in danger of becoming morally depraved. An officer of the Juvenile Aid 

Bureau arrested George and brought him before a magistrate on a wayward minor charge. George, seventeen, was sentenced 

New Hampton Reformatory for an indeterminate term not to exceed three years. He was held at the Tombs to await transfer to the Reformatory.

Later Developments. After nine months of imprisonment George was released on parole. He was instructed to attend a clinic for anti-luetic treatment. He lived for a time at various Bowery lodging houses and held many jobs, all of short duration. He reported on parole with fair regularity. 

The parole officer noted that George spoke very incoherently, as he had not done in the past, and recommended that the youth's parole be revoked and that he be sent to the Penitentiary for physical and mental examinations. That was done. 

The resident psychiatrist of the Penitentiary reported that, "From a psychiatric viewpoint this boy is obviously inadequate to cope with normal conditions in a competitive existence outside an institution. While he does not admit hallucinations as such, he spends a good deal of time day-dreaming and creates for himself a fantastic picture of how he will occupy himself when he leaves the institution. He requests that he be allowed to serve out his time so that he will not need parole supervision on discharge. This inmate is unquestionably of psychopathic personality with nomadic tendencies and incidental systemic syphilis." [46]

The New York City Parole Commission transferred George to 

Bellevue Hospital for observation on December 6, 1940, two weeks before the expiration of his maximum three-year term which he commenced to serve as a wayward minor at New Hampton. 

Bellevue reported that "his defects in adjustment are probably in conjunction with immaturity and inferior intelligence. It is not uncommon to see youthful individuals who spend most of their lives in prison showing some schizoid characteristics.' 

At the expiration of sentence George was given emergency cash relief and counsel by the Bureau for Men and Boys of the Community Service Society. After a few contacts, and before a suitable plan could be worked out for him, George disappeared from view. 

Homeless and diseased physically and mentally, this boy had never committed a crime but had been imprisoned at the Reformatory, the Penitentiary, and the Tombs Prison. 

Thus the youth who had committed no violation of the Penal Law and who needed constructive social treatment was incarcerated. The one with whom he is paired in the above accounts engaged in repeated criminal offenses and was similarly dealt with, although with a lesser variety of imprisonment. 

A Mentally Dull Boy and a Drug Addict 

Imprisoned as Wayward Minors

The Case of David 

David C―, aged sixteen, was a border-line mental case. He was arrested for possessing a loaded revolver which he had voluntarily surrendered. Following two mental examinations he was adjudged a wayward minor and sentenced to New Hampton Reformatory.

Family Situation. David was one of twelve children, six boys and six girls. Social agency records indicate that the parents, tolerant [ 47 ]

and understanding with their children, maintained a pleasant and satisfactory home. The father was employed as a chauffeur at $40 a week which doubtless required a good deal of stretching to cover the needs of the fourteen members of the family. The mother died six months prior to the time of David's arrest. At school David made an unusually poor showing. He was in an ungraded class for backward children.

Offense and Arrest. As a protection against the threat of a school mate, David had obtained a revolver which he said he had taken from a parked car. When the gun failed to impress the enemy, David himself became frightened and told the attendance officer at school about the weapon. The attendance officer notified the school principal who in turn called the police. 

Arraignment. David was arraigned in the Queens Felony Court before a magistrate who remanded him to the observation ward of the Kings County Hospital for psychiatric examination. 

Psychiatric Report. According to the Stanford-Binet scale David's mental age was rated at 11 years, 2 months. The hospital's report stated that, "While not a moron, he is not up to par intellectually, and his behavior in the present circumstances is symptomatic of his infantile makeup *** It is our opinion that- *** It is our opinion that (David) is at present not insane, nor imbecile, and is capable of understanding the charge against him and the proceedings against him, and of making his defense.”

One is disturbed to think that our criminal justice sentencing system had to make charges against, and conduct proceedings against, a child chronologically sixteen but mentally eleven years of age. 

Additional Psychiatric Report. The magistrate was not satisfied with the clinic's report because it contained no recommendations for treatment. He therefore remanded David to Bellevue Hospital for further observation and report. [ 48 ]

Bellevue reported that David was "not a suitable case for commitment to a State Hospital for care and treatment*** He will be most unlikely to make a satisfactory social adjustment on the outside, and it is believed that some type of disciplinary care, such as can be extended at Hampton Farms (New Hampton Reformatory) will be the most desirable disposition. If he should fail to benefit at Hampton Farms with further psychiatric observation and examination in that institution, commitment to a State Hospital can then be considered." The clinic believed that David's behavior was in the nature of a compensation for a sense of inferiority due to physical immaturity and mental disability. 

Sentence. David appeared in the Magistrates' Felony Court in 

Queens. With the consent of the district attorney, the original felony charge was vacated and a wayward minor complaint substituted. David was committed to the New Hampton Reformatory for an indeterminate term not to exceed three years. For four days prior to court sentence he had been detained at the Raymond 

Street Jail.

The Case of Philip 

Strictly speaking, Philip M——————, nineteen years of age, had committed no criminal offense. He was addicted to the smoking of marijuana "reefers" and that habit engaged him with undesirable companions.

Family Background. Philip was an only child living in a good home and neighborhood. Although his stepfather appeared to be indifferent, his mother was devoted to him. The mother gave evidence of distress over the boy's use of marijuana and his association with what were called Harlem and Broadway friends. Philip had been able to hold a job and was in the good graces of his employer both before and after his commitment as a wayward minor. [ 49 ]

Drug Addiction. When eighteen, Philip willingly accompanied his mother to Bellevue Hospital for treatment of his drug addiction. An intelligence test classified him as high average, I.Q. 114. 

The clinic reported, "this individual, although innately of superior intelligence represents a severe psycho-neurotic problem associated with long-standing maladjustment, from which he has tried to escape by various means, including running away from home * * * recommended to mental hygiene clinic for therapy." Philip, however, failed to return to Bellevue for treatment.

Offense So-called. A year later, Philip, nineteen, left home. Previously he had been absent from home for weeks or months at a time. His mother consulted the police who advised her to have the youth arrested as a wayward minor for being "willfully disobedient." He was found by detectives, arrested, and arraigned in a Bronx Magistrates' Court. He was adjudged to be a wayward minor and committed to Bellevue Hospital for an examination. 

The results of the examination confirmed the earlier findings. 

He was "neither mentally defective nor psychotic" and by his own admission had been using narcotic drugs. The youth did not seem to resent his having been taken to court by his mother. He expressed a desire to be cured of his narcotic addiction. 

Sentence. Philip was sentenced as a wayward minor to New 

Hampton Reformatory. One week later, at age nineteen, he was admitted to the Reformatory. 

Sequel. About eight months later Philip was paroled from New 

Hampton. His parole officer arranged for him to attend the Bellevue day clinic for psychiatric treatment but Philip's daytime employment made it impossible for him to attend. 

Philip stayed away from home overnight a good deal. He married without informing his parole officer who later discovered it. 

Philip and his wife both reported to the parole officer for a time. [50]

The marriage soon went on the rocks. Philip resorted to postcards in making his parole reports. Again Philip left his home, whereabouts unknown, and a warrant was sworn out by the parole officer for his arrest. 

Philip was arrested and held at the Tombs. Later he was transferred to the Penitentiary at Rikers Island. This is a strange case of repeated imprisonment in the absence of a specific criminal offense. 

Wayward Minors - Of All Sorts and Conditions 

Youths are gathered together at the New Hampton Reformatory for disobedience, for commission of misdemeanor, and for commission of felony scaled down by a plea of guilt to misdemeanor. 

In the cases listed above, boys of sixteen, seventeen, eighteen and nineteen, promising of success and of failure, are alike thrown into an institution of mass-imprisonment—presumably for their own good. It is a senseless system. Citizens and officials who are either ignorant or indifferent to such conditions are equally to blame. 

Conclusion 

In dealing with a youth arrested on a felony charge, the normal legal procedure would be arraignment in Magistrates' Court, then presentation of indictment by the district attorney, next action of the Grand Jury and, if an indictment is affirmed, there follows a trial in a superior court having jurisdiction over felony offenses. 

In actual practice we sometimes find magistrates assuming jurisdiction by disregarding the felony offense and adjudicating the offender to be a wayward minor. The courts having full jurisdiction to try felonies sometimes proceed on the felony charge, more often accept a plea of guilt to a misdemeanor, and less frequently make a wayward minor adjudication. [51]

We find that the different criminal justice processes crisscross each other in arriving at different ends in the same types of cases or at the same ends in totally different cases. Thus the several courts on the one hand, commit boys of great dissimilarity as to their capacity, age and delinquent history to the same institution and, on the other hand, they commit boys, alike in their personal characteristics and behavior record, to different institutions which include City Penitentiary, City Reformatory, State Vocational 

Training School and State Reformatory. 

We find that, following indeterminate commitment to a reformatory, the criminal courts in some cases reassert their jurisdiction on an earlier charge or the city and state parole bodies, according to the institution from which released, assume parole supervision. At present it is everybody's business to take jurisdiction in the task of rehabilitation of youth offenders. 

It is nobody's business, it seems, to devise markedly improved measures of treatment and to provide new types of facilities for reclaiming youth offenders. The existing system consists of several layers of public authority, represented by city and county courts, city and state correctional institutions and city and state parole administrations. Each unit is entirely independent of the other. 

No one is responsible for unified control and for follow-up of an individual youth by means of an uninterruped administration of a whole course of treatment.

We advocate creation of a State Youth Correction Authority as a hopeful remedy for the existing chaos and deficiency in correctional treatment measures employed for the reclamation of youth offenders. A digest of the powers, duties, and treatment resources of the Authority may be found in a forthcoming pamphlet — 

Chaos in Correctional Treatment of Youth Offenders.
