JOHN WOOLMAN 

In a big farmhouse on the bank of the beautiful 

Rancocas Creek lived Samuel and Elizabeth Woolman with their three little daughters, Elizabeth, Sarah and 

Patience. On October 19, 1720, when Elizabeth was almost five years old, the family welcomed the first son, John, who was to grow up to be a very remarkable man and make the name of Woolman famous. Perhaps he received a great deal of attention from his mother and sisters and the two aunts who lived with them; but they did not have much time to spoil the only boy, for when John was two years old a little brother was born. 

And after that five more brothers and three sisters brought the family total up to thirteen children. 1 

BOYHOOD ON THE FARM. Life for the Woolman family was happy and busy. The father, Samuel Woolman, was not only a farmer, but a weaver as well, and his sons learned to help with the farm work and to run

the looms which wove the cloth for the family and neighbors. The girls busied themselves with household tasks which, in those days, included spinning and dyeing as well as sewing, and soap and candle-making in addition to baking and cooking. Of course the girls could help the busy mother take care of the younger children; and Elizabeth often played with her brother

John and took him for walks along the creek or through the woods. The brother and sister learned to know each other well, and a very close tie of affection bound them together for the rest of their lives. 

The Woolman family attended a little Quaker school about a mile from home. Here came all the Quaker children from the nearby "plantations," as the farms were called; and a few Indian boys and girls also sat with them on the long wooden benches and tried to understand the queer signs of the white man's language. 

John Woolman was a good student and soon learned to find pleasure in reading. He learned to write well, too; and as he grew older he was often called upon by friends and neighbors to write important documents for them.

In addition to family and school activities, there was the Meeting which, for those early New Jersey farmers, was the center of their social life as well as of their worship. Every member of the family went on Thursday and on Sunday to the log meeting-house a little

beyond the school. Here in the silence, surrounded by his family and his friends, John Woolman thought of the beauty of the world, of the goodness of God and of his care for all his creatures. Into the meeting sometimes would come a tall young Indian brave, who would quietly take his place among his white neighbors and worship the Great Spirit in a silence that he could understand better than the words that were spoken. 

Yearly Meeting in Philadelphia was a great occasion for all the country Quakers, for here they could visit their city friends and feel themselves in the midst of the great political and social events of their day. John 

Woolman attended the Yearly Meeting when he was old enough to ride to Philadelphia on horseback, and was impressed with much that he saw and heard there. 

When John was a little boy, an event occurred which influenced his thought and action during the rest of his life. Perhaps you would like to read his own account of it. "Once as I went to a neighbor's house, I saw on the way a Robbin sitting on her nest, and as I came near she went off, but having young ones, flew about, and with many cries expressed her concern for them. 

I stood and threw stones at her, till one striking her, she fell down dead. At first I was pleas'd with the

Exploit, but after a few minutes was seized with Horror, as haveing in a sportive way kild an Innocent Creature while she was careful for her young. I beheld

her lying dead; . . . and after some painfull consideration on the subject, I climbed up the tree, took all the young birds, and killed them, supposing that better than to leave them to pine away and die miserably. I then went on my errand but, for some hours, could think of little else but my cruel conduct toward the poor old bird."

From this time on, John was never cruel to any living creature, and we shall see how he grew to be more and more sensitive to the sufferings of everything about him, animals as well as persons.

At home, at school, and in the social life of the community John took an active part. He made many friends and enjoyed the good times which they had together. 

When he was about sixteen, however, he met a group of boys and girls that were certainly not the right friends for him. He liked them and had a good time with them, but whenever he went out with them, he realized that he did things which he should not have done. It was not easy for him to change his ways or to refuse their invitations, because when he was in their company he had lots of fun. After almost two years of struggle with himself, he finally decided that he must entirely break away from this group. He was helped and strengthened in this resolution by his parents, by reading the Bible and other good books, and by long walks,

during which he prayed for the courage to do what he knew to be right.

SHOPKEEPING AND TAILORING IN MOUNT HOLLY. 

When John Woolman was twenty years old, he decided to leave home and take a position in the neighboring town of Mount Holly. He was not particularly interested in farming, and when an offer came to tend shop and keep books, he accepted and went to live behind the shop. He was busy during the day, but the evenings were long and lonely. When the old gay crowd found that he was in town with plenty of free time in the evenings, they invited him to come to their parties again, but he was finally able to make them understand that he no longer wanted to play around with them.

At first he spent his evenings alone, but soon he began to find friends whose companionship was helpful to him. Mount Holly was a busy and prosperous town and the center of trade for the planters from the surrounding plantations. When they came to town to the sawmill or the gristmill, they would often stop in at the shop. 

They learned to know the young man and found him helpful and intelligent. The townspeople, too, liked the new clerk, and began to call on him for advice on the writing of legal papers or to help in a sick-room, if the doctor was away from town on his weekly rounds. 

After he had lived in Mount Holly for a while, John

Woolman began to notice that there was entirely too much drinking in the taverns, especially at holiday times. One Christmas he was particularly distressed, and felt that he should speak to the innkeeper about it. 

He hesitated to go, thinking that perhaps some more elderly Friends should be the ones to make the call.

However, once he had made up his mind that a certain course was right for him, he had no peace until he took that course. So he called on the innkeeper, talked to him in a very tactful way about the disorderly drinking, and came away thankful that he had not neglected his duty. The innkeeper took his admonition kindly, "and," says Woolman, "afterward showed more regard to me than before."

About this time his employer sold a Negro slave and asked John Woolman to write the bill of sale. The request came as a shock, and he immediately felt that he should have nothing to do with the sale of a human being. However, since this was an order from his employer and the buyer was waiting for the bill, he decided to write it. His conscience troubled him as he wrote, and he eased his mind somewhat by telling his master and his customer that he believed "Slavekeeping to be a practice inconsistent with the Christian Religion. 

He was always sorry that he wrote this bill, and afterwards refused to write others or to make wills for those who wished to bequeath slaves to their children or

relatives. From this time on John Woolman saw a clear path of duty for himself: never to be responsible in any way for the enslavement of a human being and to work to convince others, especially in the Society of Friends, that such slavery was wrong. 

After he had been in Mount Holly for about three years, John Woolman thought it was time for him to decide what should be his permanent business. He thought that retailing of goods was "attended with much cumber," and he wanted to do something else. 

He tried to find an occupation that would be useful to the community and give him enough money to live comfortably, but simply. He therefore decided to learn to be a tailor. After a few years he bought the little tailoring business and the shop from his employer; but he soon found that his management of the shop brought him too many customers and too much money. Because he could not live the kind of life he wanted to live if he had to run a large and flourishing store, he decided to sell the shop and work only as a tailor. He bought a small place at the edge of town, which had an orchard of apple trees. His work as tailor left him time to prune trees in his orchard, help his neighbors with their legal writings, visit the sick, teach a class in school when no other teacher was to be found, and make many journeys to Friends' meetings to present his concern against slavery.

Now that his business affairs were settled to his satisfaction, John Woolman felt that the time had come for him to marry. He found what he calls a "well inclined Damsel," Sarah Ellis, and they were married on October 18, 1749.* Janet Payne Whitney tells us that the groom wore a coat of camlet, a woolen fabric, woven by his father, dyed by his friend Josiah White, tailored by himself and ornamented with gold buttons bequeathed to him by his sister, Elizabeth. The bride wore a dress of soft blue, trimmed with a white fichu and set off with a green apron. John Woolman himself mentions none of these details, and does not even tell us of the birth of his daughter, Mary, on December 

18th of the following year.

During the time that John was learning his trade and establishing his family, he was also becoming an interested and valuable member of his Meeting. From the day that he had been able to throw off successfully his temptation to lead a light and frivolous life, he had thought more and more of the spiritual side of his growth and had entered into the worship and the business of his Meeting with great concern. He began to speak in the meeting for worship and was finally accepted and recorded as a minister. He studied carefully the Quaker position against war; and during the French *Their marriage certificate in the Pennsylvania Historical Society says they were married in Eighth Month, which was October before the change in the calendar mixed up these Latin names so that they no longer apply correctly to the number of the month.

and Indian War he advised Friends to uphold this testimony by refusing to pay war taxes, and by avoiding anything that would help the war. In his opposition to war, John Woolman was as consistent as he could possibly be, and was willing to suffer any criticism, inconvenience or hardship to uphold what he believed to be the Christian way of life.

TRAVELS IN AMERICA. The early Friends in the 

American colonies lived in small communities and held their meetings in their little log meeting houses or in their own homes, if no meeting house was nearby. In 

Eastern Pennsylvania and New Jersey there were many Friends, but in New England and in the southern colonies they were less numerous and not always welcomed as members of the community. This was especially true in New England where they were often persecuted. It is easy to understand how welcome was any visitor from another Meeting, who could bring news of how other Friends fared and could give encouragement and help where it was needed. Even where the groups were larger and closer together, a visit from a Friend from another place was an occasion, and the community gathered in some home to hear his message. For he never came unless he felt he had a "call" to make the visit, and then he brought with him a minute from his Monthly Meeting, authorizing his trip and commending the Friend and his message.

John Woolman felt the call to make many journeys to Friends in nearby New Jersey communities, including even his own Mount Holly neighbors. He also visited most of the Pennsylvania Meetings, and made two trips to the southern provinces, Maryland, Virginia and North Carolina, and two to Long Island and New England. Sometimes he was gone only a few days or a few weeks, but the New England journeys took four months. He usually traveled on horseback and sometimes mentions in his Journal the distance he has gone. 

A trip to the Eastern Shore of Maryland lasted six weeks, and covered 550 miles; on the second journey to the South, John and his brother Uriah traveled 1150 miles; the visit to New England totaled 1500 miles on horseback and 150 miles by sailboat. Usually one other 

Friend, sometimes two, made the journeys with him, but occasionally he was moved to go alone. 

The first journey was made in the company of an older man, Abraham Farrington. They visited homes and held meetings in Rahway, Plainfield and other eastern Jersey places, where Friends' meetings were not usually held. This was an interesting and pleasant experience; but the next journey took John Woolman to the South, where his sensitive nature was stirred by many new experiences. As they traveled, John and his friend, Isaac Andrews, were entertained by Quakers who lived along the way. Sometimes they stayed with

very poor people who shared with them their meager supply of food and their hard and uncomfortable beds. 

The next stop might be at the home of a rich Quaker planter, some of whose slaves would care for their horses while others led the tired travelers to cool rooms and provided them with cheering refreshment. The contrast between the manner of living of his various hosts was very great, and led John Woolman to think how wrong it was for some people to be so poor and miserable while others were too rich and idle. He believed if everyone worked and earned enough to live simply and comfortably, there would be plenty for all, people would have time to enjoy the better things of life, and their spiritual natures would have a chance to grow. 

This trip also showed him the great evils of slavery. 

He was not so much concerned about the effect on the slaves, for Quakers usually treated their slaves well. 

However, he was very unhappy about the result in the character of the slave owners. He talked about this with many Quaker owners and some were led for the first time to see slavery in its true light. The second time 

John Woolman visited these Southern homes he decided not to accept hospitality due to slave labor, and left a sum of money to pay the slaves who served him. 

This was embarrassing to him and to his host, but such action was often more eloquent than any words had been.

One day when John Woolman was in Philadelphia, he met some Indians from the Indian town of Wehalosing, on the Susquehanna River about two hundred miles from Philadelphia. He was impressed by their faces and their conduct and decided that he would like to visit their town some time. Accordingly he obtained the consent of his Meeting and set out with some Indian guides. Since this was a very dangerous journey, John's friend, Benjamin Parvin, determined to go with him. The mountain trails were narrow and slippery; the party had to sleep in the woods, when their clothes and their blankets were drenched with rain. They met hostile Indians who eyed with suspicion these white men traveling far beyond their own borders. Messengers bearing rumors of war and of trouble between the Indians and settlers constantly reported discouraging news to their guides. But the travelers persevered and finally arrived at Wehalosing. John and Benjamin stopped at the edge of the town while the Indians went in to announce their arrival. As they waited, an Indian woman with a baby came past and sat down on a log with them, and all joined in a "deep inward stillness." 

Large meetings for worship were held in the town at the home of the Indian chief, Papunahung, who was a Christian. 

Why did John Woolman make this long hard journey to the little Indian village? He was not trying to

make Quakers of them; they held no slaves to give him concern. He answers this question for us: "Love was the first motion, and then a Concern to Spend Some time with the Indians, that I might feel and understand their life and the Spirit they live in, If happily I might receive some Instruction from them." 

This desire to enter into the lives of other people, to share their experiences and their hardships led John 

Woolman to make his famous walking journeys into

Maryland. Traveling on foot was wearisome, hot and slow. It was also lonely; but it brought a peace of mind and an understanding of heart that seemed to make clearer the path of duty. Great sorrow that Friends should feel that they could profit by the labors of oppressed Negroes moved the traveler to speak eloquently in several large meetings and helped the more tender-hearted Friends to decide on their own right course.

WORK AGAINST SLAVERY. Although some of his visits were made for the purpose of friendly intercourse and fellowship, most of them were undertaken because of John Woolman's growing concern that Friends should really see all that slave-holding meant. Most of his work was with individuals or with small groups, gathered in someone's home. After a period of silent worship John Woolman would address the gathering, pointing out the three main arguments against slavery:

first, it denied the brotherhood of man which was a basic Christian and Quaker belief; second, it had a demoralizing effect on the slave-owners; third, it was degrading to the slaves, whose physical needs might be supplied by their Quaker owners, but whose intellectual and spiritual natures were completely neglected. After the meeting small groups would continue to discuss the matter.

When the consciences of enough people had been stirred, the question of Friends' attitude toward slavery was introduced into the Monthly or Quarterly Meetings, and there was discussion of it there. T that slavery was wrong spread slowly and graduy. Many 

Friends admitted that engaging in the slave trade was bad; some were beginning to think that buying slaves should be discontinued among Friends; but only a few were ready to think that keeping slaves and using their labor was just as wrong. In Philadelphia the opposition to slavery was taken up by other concerned persons; but the South, which used the labor, and New England, which profited by the trade, were harder to convince. 

John Woolman believed that "conduct is more convincing than language," and his very presence in a room made people conscious of his opposition to slavery. He was constantly careful of his own way of living and watched every detail to see that he was not profiting from the slave trade. When he became aware that

dyes came from the West Indian slave plantations, he decided to wear no more dyed clothes. In our time, men in white or natural-colored suits are not conspicuous, especially in summer; but in colonial days to wear uncolored clothes was very queer indeed. John Woolman knew that it was queer, but after he decided that it was right the queerness was unimportant. He did not insist that anyone else adopt this form of dress, not even his wife and daughter. He did forbid the use of silver in his household for it, too, was a product of slave labor; and he always asked for a china or pewter dish when he was dining away from home. Sugar and molasses, luxuries supplied by the slave-owning plantations, were likewise given up.

He was particularly interested in a slave named William Bowen, and taught him to read and write and to understand the fundamental principles of Quakerism. 

When William wished to marry Dido, a former slave of John's uncle, Joseph Burr, a Quaker wedding was arranged. Friends refused them the use of the meeting house, but the wedding was held at the home of Joseph 

Burr. John Woolman made all the arrangements for the marriage and even wrote the certificate himself. 

Negroes and white folks met together in the parlor of the big farmhouse. This must have caused much comment in all the surrounding community, for most people paid little attention to the family life of their

slaves, even selling husband or wife when it suited their purposes. And here were two colored people, one of them a slave, having a real wedding! What strange ideas this John Woolman had!

THE JOURNAL. During his busy life in Mount Holly and in other distant communities, John Woolman found time to write down some of his ideas on the things nearest to his heart. Two essays on "Some Considerations on the Keeping of Negroes" were printed and circulated among Friends. He even wrote a "Primer or 

First Book for Children." His most famous work, however, was his Journal, which is a record of his life, with particular emphasis on his spiritual development. It is so simply and plainly written and presents a life of such sincerity and integrity that it has come to be recognized as a classic in American literature. When Charles W. 

Eliot chose his five-foot shelf of the world's greatest books, he included the Journal of John Woolman. 

THE JOURNEY TO ENGLAND. On the first of May, 

1772, in the ship "Mary and Elizabeth," John Woolman set sail for London. He had long felt a concern to visit Friends in England, again to present his thoughts on slavery. Although there were no slaves in 

England, Friends were not "clear" of the trade and many had made large fortunes by allowing their ships to be used to transport slaves. After a thirty-nine-day trip in the steerage, where he had ample time and

opportunity to experience the hardships of a sailor's life, John Woolman arrived in London, a bit late for the first session of Yearly Meeting. Friends had already gathered and were seated in silence when he entered the meeting house and went forward to present his minutes to the Clerk. Friends in America were used to the white clothes and hat; Friends in England were not, and the sight of this strange-looking figure created quite a stir in the meeting. His minutes were read, however, and then one Friend rose quickly and said, "Our visitor has answered his call. He has come to our meeting and presented his credentials. We think it would now be well for him to return home." What a cruel reception after such a long, hard journey! The visitor was not ready to go home. He had a message to give, and he rose to tell the meeting that he could not go home, but would wait until the Friends felt ready to hear his message. Of course he couldn't accept their hospitality during this time, but he would be glad if they could give him some tailoring to do during his stay. This humble, simple reply touched the consciences of the meeting as they sat in the silence; the hasty 

Friend apologized; and the Clerk decided the meeting was ready to hear the message. As a result of his work during this and the following sessions, London Yearly Meeting made its first pronouncement on slavery. 

After the Yearly Meeting, John Woolman stayed on

in England visiting individual Friends and many meetings. In September he went to the town of York, where he became ill. His disease proved to be the dread smallpox, which caused his death on October 7, 1772, when he was almost fifty-two years old. He was buried in the Bishophill Quaker burying ground at York.

Thomas Priestman, in whose house he had stayed, and Esther Tuke, who nursed him in his last illness, have preserved for us this prayer, which expresses the spirit in which he lived and died. "O Lord! it was thy power that enabled me to forsake Sin in my Youth . . . and though I have gone through many Trials and sore Afflictions, thou hast been with me, continuing a Father and a Friend. I feel thy

Power now and beg that in the approaching trying moments thou wilt keep my Heart stedfast unto thee."
