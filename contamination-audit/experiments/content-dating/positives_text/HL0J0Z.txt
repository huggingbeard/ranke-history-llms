reviewing fiscal 1969 

highlights: 

Civil Aeronautics Board program and management accomplishments were along a broad front in the past fiscal year. They ranged from awards of new and improved operating authority in many important air markets, through mail and military rate reductions and the breaking of new ground in certain joint tariff filings, to solutions for congestion problems, investigation of illegal charters, and revamping procedural and housekeeping operations to either save money or put certain operations on a self-paying basis. 

Perhaps the best at-a-glance statement of the Agency's accomplishments case is the report of the docket processes during fiscal 1969, the greatest year in CAB history from the standpoint of dockets completed-1,496. The previous record was achieved in fiscal 1961, with 1,353 dockets completed. 

During fiscal 1969, there were 1,138 dockets added to the 1,349 on hand at year's start. The record number of completions during the year reduced the docket load by 358, leaving 991 on hand at year end. Only four times in the past 15 fiscal years were there fewer dockets on hand at year end.

From fiscal 1961, and during the intervening years, the CAB's manpower has been virtually unchanged. 

Congestion a Major Problem

One of air transportation's major problems continues to be airport and airways congestion and surface traffic congestion between city center and airport. In the past year, the Board either initiated actions or continued earlier actions dealing with each of these areas of the congestion problem. 

Hearing was concluded in the first of two phases of the Northeast Corridor VTOL Investigation—the phase concerned exclusively with determination of the need for and feasibility of a new type of air service using vertical takeoff and landing aircraft or other equipment usable on small landing sites to serve metropolitan areas in the heavily populated northeastern United States coast. The second phase-selection of a carrier or carriers to provide the service will begin only after the first phase is completed. 

The Northeast Corridor VTOL case is an attempt to find a longer range solution to the congestion problem. Since October 1968, a CAB staff group has been looking ahead to determine at what specific cities, in addition to those now being actively studied, congestion problems may loom in the next 5 years. The full report is moving toward completion during fiscal 1970. 1

In another approach, the Department of Transportation participation is being encouraged in cases that involve obvious problems of airport congestion. 

The CAB also authorized United States and foreign air carriers to hold discussions related to air traffic congestion at New York, Chicago, Los Angeles and Washington, D.C. The discussions also were open to the affected airport operators and interested Federal departments and agencies. CAB observers attended all the discussions; and restrictions were imposed against any scheduling or frequency determinations by the participants as a group. 

Service through Satellites

Many CAB route actions in recent years have been aimed at relieving congestion at major hub ariports. In the second half of the fiscal year, the 

Board authorized nine air carriers to serve satellite airports in the Los 

Angeles area on their domestic routes.

Surface traffic congestion is increasingly related to air traffic congestion. 

In December 1968, the CAB announced that large metropolitan communities appearing before it as intervenors in future formal airline route cases would be specifically invited to furnish information concerning steps being taken to solve intracity transportation problems. Historically, the Board's determination of public convenience and necessity in connection with awards of new operating authority has been largely based on existing traffic; but now congestion sometimes impedes traffic flow at major cities. Hence, a question arises whether the overall public interest is actually well served by introducing additional air service in such an instance-even though traffic figures appear to warrant such increase.

Federal subsidy to air carriers continued its downward trend during the fiscal year, largely through improved route structures for the local service carriers. Total subsidy paid to all carriers fell $9.03 million from $54,999,000 to $45,970,000 during the year.

Local service route structure improvement, in turn, was expedited through increased use of the new subpart M of the CAB's rules of practice in economic proceedings. Although subpart M became effective in mid-fiscal 1968, its use did not hit full stride until fiscal 1969. Twenty-eight of the 39 subpart 

M applications filed since the rule became effective in January 1968, were filed during fiscal 1969. Of the 39, two applications were dismissed in fiscal 

1968; 13 were approved in fiscal 1969; and eight were denied or dismissed. 

Thus, 16 were still pending at June 30, 1969. The 13 applications which were approved moved to completion in an average of 6.5 months. This compares with about 15 months for regularly processed cases. 

Route and Fare Matters 

Some of the agency's most significant fiscal 1969 accomplishments involved route and fare matters.

The largest and best known of the route cases was the Reopened Transpacific Route Investigation, involving both domestic and foreign authority. 

The examiner's recommended decision was issued in fiscal 1968, and the Board's decision in the domestic and international phases of the case in fiscal 2

1969. In the domestic phase, the CAB granted five air carriers routes to 

Hawaii but stayed the effectiveness of their certificates to permit final disposition of the domestic phase in coordination with the South Pacific deferred portion of the case. 

In the international phase-except for the South Pacific deferred portion the CAB denied petitions for reconsideration of its supplemental opinion and order which effectuated the directives of the President after his review of the original opinion and order—thus permitting the international awards to become effective, except for the South Pacific deferred portion. 

The CAB heard oral argument in May in the South Pacific deferred portion, and submitted a decision to the President. On July 1, 1969, the President disapproved the decision and returned the case to the Board for further action in accordance with instructions as to certain characteristics of the route.

New Authority Granted 

Among other significant decisions reflecting the scope of the year's accomplishments, were the Central Route 81 case and the Gulf States-Midwest Points Service Investigation. The former granted new authority to three local service carriers in a large region in the southwest, and realigned the old Central Airlines Route 81 (reducing 10 segments to four). Central had merged with Frontier after the hearing in this case was concluded in Sep tember 1967. 

In the Gulf States-Midwest case, extensive new airline service in the Nation's midsection was authorized, involving four trunkline carriers (American, Braniff, Delta, and Eastern) and three local service carriers (Frontier, Ozark, and Southern). The cities involved include Chicago, New Orleans, Memphis, Nashville, San Antonio, Houston, Dallas/Fort Worth, Detroit, St. Louis, and Kansas City.

In the Reopened Pacific Northwest-Southwest Service Investigation, two trunkline and three local service carriers (Braniff, Continental, Air West, Frontier, and Texas International) received new authority in three large geographical areas. The awards involved long-haul Pacific Northwest- 

Southwest routes; Southwest Regional routes, and Pacific Northwest

Regional routes. The authority given the local service carriers in the case was granted on a subsidy-ineligible basis. 

Service to Caribbean 

Extremely important cases involving service to the Caribbean area and to South America were decided. So also was an international case involving service to Bermuda. A number of complex and important cases, both domestic and international, were heard by the CAB in fiscal 1969, with decisions to be issued in the very early part of fiscal 1970 (e.g., the Southern Tier case, the Miami-London case, and the Service to Albuquerque case).

Extensions and realignments have strengthened and improved several local service carrier routes both in service to the public and subsidy reduction, such as the Ozark Extension to New York/Washington Investigation. In that case, Ozark Airlines was authorized to provide first single-plane service from

eight midwest cities to Washington, D.C. and New York, N.Y. (Washington,

D.C. to be served through Dulles International Airport, rather than through heavily-trafficked Washington National Airport). In the Ozark Extension case, the award in the examiner's initial decision was contingent on Ozark's receiving nonstop authority in the Sioux City-Cedar Rapids and Cedar

Rapids-Champaign/Urbana markets in the Ozark Air Lines, Inc. Route 

Realignment Investigation, which was so decided. 

The CAB realigned Allegheny Airlines' east-west route structure, which was in effect prior to its merger with Lake Central Airlines, in the Allegheny Airlines Route 97 Investigation. The local service carrier received liberalized authority in a number of markets though elimination of stops formerly required at segment junction points.

Southern Airways' route was extended from Eglin Air Force Base, Fla.; 

Dothan, Ala.; and Columbus, Ga. to New York-Newark and Washington,

D.C. the latter point to be served through Dulles International Airport. 

Fare Increases Permitted 

On fare matters, the CAB in February permitted to become effective certain fare increases proposed by the domestic trunkline and local service carriers. Affected were most of the trunklines' local and joint normal fares, Discover America fares, military and youth standby fares, military leave reservation fares, youth advance reservation fares, and most other promotional fares. Fare increases of a similar nature became effective about the same time for all the local service carriers except Trans Texas (now Texas International). The increases were estimated to produce a 3.96-percent increase of total 1968 passenger revenues for the "Big Four" trunk carriers; a 3.81-percent increase for the remaining seven trunklines, and a 9.83percent increase for the locals.

In May, however, the CAB suspended fare revisions proposed by five trunkline carriers, which would have resulted in net fare increases, so that it could investigate the reasonableness of the changes. The CAB also suspended for investigation certain promotional fare changes proposed by 

Northeast Airlines. 

As a followup to an earlier decision-which approved and set for investigation certain International Air Transport Association resolutions on North Atlantic and Mid-Atlantic contract bulk inclusive tour fares-the CAB in June proposed new regulations governing the furnishing of bulk inclusive tours in foreign air transportation by tour operators and foreign tour operators.

Charters Under Scrutiny 

With a rising number of complaints against allegedly illegal charter operations, the CAB turned increasing attention to investigation of these practices, most of which have been centered on the west coast. The violations have included excessive fares, improper commissions, selling transportation on nonexistent flights or without charter contracts.

The CAB's Bureau of Enforcement also was active in other areas and accepted a total of $79,000 in compromise of civil penalties for tariff

violations. Three of the penalties, of $25,000 each, were collected from foreign air carriers; the fourth, at $4,000, was paid by a travel agent. 

In management matters, the CAB reduced overhead, reallocating the savings among program areas. The agency put more than 2,900 square feet of office space to additional use and effected telephone equipment savings. 

During the year, the average grade level of employee was held to 10.3 and about $185,000 of pay increases was absorbed by the CAB. This follows respective absorptions of $175,000 and $113,000 in fiscal 1968 and 1967. 

Filing, license and other fees, inaugurated in fiscal 1968, last year brought in approximately $1 million which was forwarded to the Treasury. 

For the first time, the 1970 budget estimate for payments to air carriers was presented on an accrued expenditure basis. 

Details of the CAB's work during the fiscal year are presented in the following pages.

domestic air commerce

The CAB's domestic route program during the fiscal year centered on: 

1. Increased benefits to the public through authorization of first singleplane service, first competitive service, or new nonstop service, in markets where traffic growth warranted; 

2. Realignment of local service carriers' routes to permit flexible scheduling and thus provide more profitable operating results; 

3. Reduction of Federal subsidies for local service carriers through major route extensions, and through grants of nonstop authority between pairs of points already certificated; 

4. Relief of the equipment burden on trunkline, local service and 

Alaskan carriers through substitutions of service by air taxi operators in low-density markets; and, 5. Efforts aimed at relieving congestion at some major hubs through satellite airport authorizations or by bypassing large airports.

Trunkline Carriers 

The domestic trunkline carriers increased their revenue passenger miles from 76.5 billion in fiscal 1968, to 85.6 billion in fiscal 1969, or an increase of 

11.9 percent. Likewise, operating revenues grew from $4.7 billion in the last fiscal year to $5.3 billion this fiscal year, or a percentage increase of 13.6 percent. By way of contrast, operating expenses increased 16.9 percent over the same period. Return on investment, excluding investment tax credits, for this fiscal year was 4.74 percent. Net income increased (or decreased) from $192 million to $84.7 million.

Alaskan Service 

The CAB instituted the Alaska Service Investigation. This will be a comprehensive review of the major route patterns within Alaska and between Seattle and various Alaskan points. The issues include the realignment of the routes of Alaska Airlines and Wien Consolidated Airlines, the surviving carriers of a series of recent mergers. The investigation also will consider applications for new authority, suspension or deletion of certificate authority at points which no longer require service, and suspension or deletion of carriers where uneconomic competition exists. 

In the Transpacific Route Investigation, the CAB granted Western Airlines a route between Anchorage, Alaska and Honolulu and Hilo, Hawaii, thereby linking the 49th and 50th States with direct air transportation for the first time.

Exemption authority was granted to Wien Consolidated to serve Prudhoe 

Bay/Sag River. Alaska Airlines' long-haul restriction on Seattle-Sitka 

Anchorage flights was modified to designate Nome/Kotzebue as an alterna-

tive terminal to Fairbanks, and to permit turnaround authority at 

Anchorage on flights which also serve Juneau, Yakutat, and Cordova.

A hearing was held to determine whether Alaska Airlines' certificate condition restricting its subsidy payments should be suspended or modified. The examiner's recommended decision has been issued and the case was awaiting oral argument before the Board as of June 30, 1969. 

The CAB also proposed a revision to part 298 of the economic regulations to permit more liberal air taxi authority in Alaska. Alaska air taxi operators currently are prohibited from operating regular service in air transportation-i.e., in interstate commerce-between points which are served by a federally-certificated carrier. The proposed revision to part 298 would lift that restriction in certain cases. 

Hawaiian Service 

Aloha and Hawaiian Airlines continued during fiscal 1969 to provide a high level of certificated interisland service without benefit of subsidy payments from the Government. These carriers had, however, by the end of fiscal 1969, brought to the CAB's attention the fact that one or both might, in fiscal 1970, seek payments under section 406 of the Federal Aviation Act. 

The services offered by these carriers is augmented by air taxi and helicopter operators.

Air Taxi Operators 

A number of significant amendments were made during the fiscal year in part 298, the economic regulations governing air taxi operations. These made basic changes in the regulatory scheme for this class of carrier. 

Compulsory liability insurance was established covering both passengers on board the aircraft, and persons and property on the ground. Effective 

July 1, 1969, every air taxi operator must register with the CAB and file a certificate attesting to his meeting the liability insurance requirements. On receipt of an appropriate application, accompanied by a proper certificate of insurance, registration will be completed.

The CAB also, for the first time, made a distinction between those air taxis performing scheduled air services on an individually ticketed basis and all others. It designated the former as commuter air carriers and placed them under a reporting requirement which will give the Board traffic and schedule data concerning this service. 

In a separate proceeding, the CAB permitted air taxi operators to use jet aircraft above the usual limitation of 12,500 pounds maximum certificated takeoff weight; the limitation for such aircraft was raised to 27,000 pounds provided that the aircraft has a maximum certificated passenger capacity of not over 12 persons and is used only in planeload charter service. 

In addition, the CAB issued 15 exemptions to air taxi operators to use large aircraft, such as the DC-3, in specific geographic markets or in unique and special services.

In considering whether to authorize an air taxi operator to replace a certificated carrier at a city, the CAB considers such factors as whether the traveling public will receive improved service from the air taxi operator;

the effect on Federal subsidy payments; the cost to the certificated carrier of continuing its service; and the attitude of the community towards the proposed replacement.

These actions are all indicative of the rapidly increasing importance of air taxi operators in the total national air transportation field. 

All-Cargo Service 

The major developments in all-cargo authority have been in the international field during fiscal 1969. However, the Flying Tiger Line Additional

Points case has developed into a major domestic route proceeding. At issue is whether there is a need for domestic all-cargo service between the points. on Flying Tiger's route and some 27 other cities in the United States. Public hearings have been concluded and the case should be decided this fiscal year. 

Acquisition of Air West 

A joint application was filed for approval of an agreement providing for the acquisition by the Hughes Tool Co. of the properties and assets of Air 

West, including its route and certificates. The proceeding was awaiting final CAB decision and Presidential approval at the close of the fiscal year. 

Helicopter Service 

After public hearing, the CAB awarded Washington Airways, Inc., a new certificate for route 160 authorizing the carrier to transport persons, property and mail in the Washington-Baltimore area on a subsidy-ineligible basis for a 5-year temporary period. The carrier has not started the new service to date. The effective date of the award to WAI has been stayed by the Court of Appeals pending disposition of a petition for judicial review. 

Los Angeles Airways, Inc. and Chicago Helicopter Airways, Inc. were granted expansion of their existing authority in their respective geographical areas, by allowing them to operate with either fixed or rotary wing aircraft. 

Control and Interlocking Relationships 

During the year, the CAB acted on 96 applications for approval of control and/or interlocking relationships under sections 408 and 409 of the Federal Aviation Act. Sixty-five of these pertained to relationships involving direct air carriers and, generally, surface common carriers and persons engaged in a phase of aeronautics, including aeronautical equipment suppliers, and aircraft leasing companies. A number of these applications involved leases by air carriers of new aircraft from banking institutions and groups of banking institutions. These institutions have entered the leasing business as a means of availing themselves of the investment tax credit which is not normally available to the air carriers on equipment purchases. Through this device, the carriers are generally able to operate the new aircraft at a cost lower than would be available were the carriers to acquire the aircraft in their own right. 10

Thirty-one of the applications seeking approval of control and/or interlocking relationships were filed by airfreight forwarders and generally involved relationships with local pickup and delivery carriers, airline sales agents, and companies affiliated with the forwarders in various activites. 

From a substantive point of view, the CAB's decisions in individual cases were directed at maintaining intercorporate relationships free from conflicts of interests which might be detrimental to the development of air transportation.

Intercorporate Agreements 

The CAB reviewed and acted on a total of 1,116 intercarrier agreements affecting air transportation. As in past years emphasis was placed on those multilateral agreements constituting concerted action by members of various associations of air carriers and foreign air carriers, such as the Air Traffic 

Conference of America (ATC) and the International Air Transport Asso ciation (IATA). Emphasis also was on certain other agreements involving important antitrust and other substantial public interest considerations. 

Generally, the agreements reviewed by the CAB involved such matters as administration of the aforementioned associations; passenger baggage and cargo practices; reservations and ticketing; interline traffic procedures; traffic documentation; passenger and cargo agency matters; airport usage and services; and a variety of other subjects affecting air transportation. 

For example, the CAB approved an agreement among certain carriers serving the New York area which established an Airport Security Council. The 

Council's objectvie is to increase security and cooperate with existing enforcement agencies at the various airports.

Supplemental Air Carriers 

Overseas National Airways, Inc., Standard Airways, Inc., and Vance International Airways, Inc., were awarded inclusive tour charter authority in their international certificates.

Interstate Airmotive, Inc., was granted a new type of limited certificate to provide supplemental service within 250-mile radius of St. Louis, Mo., and to carry small groups (50 passengers or less).

There were 14 carriers in the supplemental field as of June 30, 1969. 

One of these however, was in such financial position that monthly financial reports to the Board have been required; and certain funds representing advance deposits were directed to be held under an escrow arrangemet. 

Reequipment programs continue and all but three of the supplemental air carriers now have some type of turbine equipment. Several have stretched jets with passenger capacities ranging up to 250. 

A significant step in the history of the supplemental carriers occurred in September 1968, when legislation was enacted amending the Federal Aviation Act to clarify the CAB's authority to permit the supplemental carriers to operate inclusive tour charters. 

As of June 30, 1969, the supplemental carriers had assets of $406 million. 

Operating revenues of supplementals for fiscal 1969 amounted to $338 mil- 11

lion. A comparison of significant financial data for the supplemental carriers is given in appendix A. 

Travel Agency Matters 

The CAB has no authority to regulate directly the selection or retention of travel agents by the airlines, or the relationships between such persons. 

However, the domestic and international airlines through their respective trade associations over the years have developed comprehensive rules to govern such matters. Since these rules involve concerted action by the airlines affecting air transportation, they must be filed with the CAB for approval or disapproval in the public interest. 

The CAB approved a number of resolutions adopted by members of the two airline trade associations relating to such matters as travel agency qualification standards, compensation for the sale of convention tours, and interpretation of agency rules on request by any individual agent. 

The CAB has under consideration an agreement among the domestic carriers establishing new credit standards for use in evaluating the appointment of travel agent and a joint indemnity program. Also under review is a multiparty agreement establishing use of a common automated reservations system throughout the United States by travel agents and others. 

Airfreight Forwarders

The airfreight forwarder industry continues to grow, both in size and in financial strength. Combined domestic and international forwarder revenues from airfreight forwarding increased from $243,530,000 in calendar 1967 to $298,324,000 in calendar 1968. Airfreight forwarders performed

Airport Congestion 

189 75 77 23 164 146 148 The CAB continued to focus on the problem of congestion at major hub airports. The problem is manifested in several ways-the air corridors grow clogged, an overload of surface traffic teems to and from airports, terminal facilities jam up. Through its route licensing powers the CAB has acted in several digerent ways toward easing the problem. 

The CAB named Ontario, Calif., as a hyphenated point with Los Angeles on routes of Airlift International, Air West, American, Continental, Delta, National, United, TWA, and Flying Tiger. 

In addition, United, TWA, and Flying Tiger were granted permission to operate flights between Los Angeles and the San Jose Airport, thus taking pressure off at San Francisco International Airport.

In many cases, the CAB has specified satellite airports or included issues of airport designation. This is done with an eye toward diverting air traffic from over-burdened major terminals. 

A case in point is the Additional Service to San Diego case now underway. 

This involves proposals for new direct service between east coast points and San Diego, thus relieving Los Angeles International Airport of a substantial volume of connecting and through passenger traffic. In a number of cases, the CAB authorized new service to Washington, D.C., and specifically designated Dulles International Airport as the airport to be served. This relieves strain on Washington National Airport.

In the Additional Service to San Antonio and Austin Investigation, it has been proposed that the carrier or carriers receiving new nonstop awards involving Washington/Baltimore be allowed to use Dulles or Friendship Airport but not Washington National Airport.

Other cases have been instituted to consider new traffic routings which would bypass major hubs. Investigations involving such issues are in progress in the Milwaukee Short-Haul Investigation, Buffalo-Twin Cities Nonstop Service Investigation, and Twin Cities-Milwaukee Long-Haul Investigation. 

One of the goals of these cases is to reduce the volume of traffic flowing through Chicago.

In the Service to White Plains, New York case, the CAB is exploring the possibility that new service to White Plains and/or Islip, N.Y., from Chicago, and other midwest cities might siphon some traffic from the existing New York airports and help ease the congestion problem in that area at Kennedy 

International and LaGuardia Airports.

The Washington-Baltimore Airport Investigation was instituted to explore means of reducing congestion at Washington National Airport and increasing utilization of facilities at Dulles and Friendship Airports. 

The Northeast Corridor VTOL Investigation which went to hearing in June, is to determine the feasibility of connecting city centers with each other and with major airports by use of vertical takeoff and landing aircraft. 14

International Routes and Operating Authority 

In accordance with Presidential directives, the CAB issued its final decision in the International/Orient Phase of the Transpacific Route Investigation. Four carriers received new or amended routes across the Pacific. 

Northwest has a new route from Los Angeles and San Francisco to Tokyo via Honolulu. Pan American has been granted a new direct route from 

New York to Japan which supplements its present transpacific routes. TWA received new operating authority between Los Angeles and Hong Kong via Hawaii, Guam, Okinawa, and Taiwan making it the second round-theworld U.S. carrier. Flying Tiger was awarded a new all-cargo route from the cities on its domestic route to Japan, Korea, Okinawa, Taiwan, Hong Kong, the Philippines, Vietnam, and Thailand.

Still to be decided at the close of the fiscal year is the South Pacific deferred phase of the Transpacific case. 

The Pacific Islands Local Service Investigation, originally severed out of the Transpacific Route Investigation, involves air service issues for Palau, Mariana (including Guam), Caroline, Marshall, Gilbert, and Ellice Islands from Honolulu, American Samoa, and Okinawa. Public hearings have been completed in this investigation.

New Authority Granted 

The CAB, examining the air service need between certain U.S. cities and 

Bermuda, found in the Bermuda Service Investigation that new and amended authority should be granted to Northeast, Eastern, and Pan American. This is the first time that Northeast has been certificated into Bermuda. The route is between Boston and Hamilton.

The Domestic Coterminal Points-Europe All-Cargo Service Investigation, instituted to determine the need for all-cargo service across the Atlantic, was decided during the fiscal year. Seaboard World Airlines' route 119 was amended to designate Chicago-Cleveland-Detroit-Los Angeles-San 

Francisco/Oakland and Washington-Baltimore, as additional coterminal points. Prior to the award, Seaboard served only a limited number of coterminals on the east coast. Seaboard is required to serve Washington through either Dulles and/or Friendship Airports.

Seaboard also was issued a new certificate for route 119-A to engage in the interstate transportation (except as to New York) of property and mail on a subsidy-ineligible basis on flights over route 119 serving a point or points in Europe. 15

The case will be heard in the forthcoming fiscal year.

Traffic Growth 

Passenger-Air passenger traffic between the United States and foreign countries has grown about 2½ times in size during the past 7 years, from 5,752,177 passengers in 1962 to slightly more than 14 million in 1968. 

Of the total sea and air passenger traffic between the United States and foreign countries, the percentage of passengers traveling by air has risen from 

78 percent in 1962 to 91 percent in 1968.

The share of U.S. airlines in the air passenger market between the United 

States and foreign countries also has steadily increased from 50 percent in 1962 to 56 percent in 1968.

The air passenger traffic flow between the United States and Europe continues to make that market the largest of all United States-foreign markets, accounting for 42 percent of the total. United States-Europe traffic has grown about 2/3 times in size during the past 7 years, from 2,515,275 passengers in 1962 to 5,913,245 in 1968.

The 1968 air passenger traffic between the United States and foreign countries increased 13.7 percent over 1967, with U.S. airline traffic increasing 19 percent, and the air passenger traffic between the United States and 

Europe increasing 11 percent.

Significant figures relating to this international passenger traffic are reflected in appendix B.

Airfreight-U.S. international airfreight traffic has grown even more dramatically than passenger traffic. In 7 years it has almost quadrupledfrom 293 million pounds in 1962 to slightly more than a billion pounds in 1968 (excludes only shipments by the U.S. Government for its own account). 16

The growth each year (during this 7-year period) ranged from a low of 11 percent to a high of 45.4 percent.

During the same period the U.S. airlines' airfreight traffic increased their share of the total being carried, the tonnage transported by U.S.-flag carriers more than quadrupled, from 107 million pounds in 1962 to 453 million. pounds in 1968.

The largest international airfreight traffic flow continues to be between the United States and Europe, accounting for 557,042,000 pounds.

Foreign Charter Carriers 

Fiscal 1969 showed a continuance of the trend in recent years to an expansion of the number of charter-only foreign carriers. The number of permits of this sort granted to new carriers was six. Six existing carriers had their permits amended to broaden their authority.

Bilateral Relations 

The international airlines of the United States operate into 119 foreign countries and their respective dependencies. The United States has bilateral air transport agreements with 67 of these countries. This includes 18 agreements based on assumption of rights. 

A bilateral air transport agreement provides a legal framework-a contract-between the governments of the two countries within which the international airlines of both countries operate. The bilateral agreement therefore, provides a basis for resolving disputes or other problems which may arise.

In the absence of a bilateral agreement, aviation relations are conducted on the basis of unilateral grants of operating authority. 

The United States' most recent bilateral air transport agreement was signed February 28, 1969, with Czechoslovakia.

In addition to the concluding of entirely new agreements, existing agreements may be modified. Most recently, on December 20, 1968, the United 

States and Greece modified the bilateral air transport agreement of March 27, 1946. 

During fiscal 1969, consultations and negotiations were held with 18 countries, one agreement was concluded, and four were modified or amended.

Responsibility for bilateral relations between the United States and foreign countries is divided into four geographic areas: Area I, comprising 

Canada, Mexico, the Caribbean, and Central and South America; Area II, comprising Greenland, Iceland, the United Kingdom and Ireland, western and northern Europe, and eastern Europe, including the Soviet Union; Area 

III, comprising southern Europe, the Near and Middle East, and Africa; and Area IV, comprising the Far East and the Pacific Basin generally, including Australia, New Zealand, and Indonesia.

Multilateral Relations 

The CAB always has been actively interested in U.S. multilateral civil aviation relations. This has included participation in the United Nations 

International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), organized in the 1940's with the United States as an original member. The CAB also maintains relations with various regional and other multilateral organizations. ICAO The 16th Session of the ICAO Assembly convened in Buenos Aires, Argentina, in September 1968. Official delegations represented 103 of the 116 member states. Three CAB personnel, led by the vice chairman, were in the 

U.S. delegation. Observers from three nonmember states and 12 international organizations also attended. This conference reviewed ICAO's work since its last General Assembly in 1965. The work program for the next 3 years was planned.

The Assembly also reelected the United States to the ICAO Council for the next 3 years. Most of the U.S. objectives at this session were achieved. 

The United States was pleased with the Assembly resolution against hijacking which calls for early ratification of the Tokyo Convention. The resolutions on sonic boom and aircraft noise in the vicinity of airports are regarded as important and realistic forward steps in maintaining an environment for the operation of air services acceptable to the public without interference with the rational development of technology. The U.S.-sponsored resolution concerning ICAO headquarters premises should permit ICAO to find more suitable accommodations in Montreal. In addition to these resolutions in the legal, technical and administrative fields, the Assembly adopted resolutions in the economic or air transport field which were almost without exception in accordance with the U.S. position. 

Much of the ICAO work is of a continuing nature, such as its activities with respect to joint financing, facilitation of international air travel and special aviation studies. The CAB has participated during the past year in

Normal Domestic Passenger Fares 

A number of trunkline carriers during the first half of the fiscal year filed revisions in fares designed to produce an overall increase in passenger revenues. The various proposals involved revenue increases ranging from 4.8 to 

7.9 percent, and followed differing approaches or formulas to achieve this result.

Anticipating the possibility of such filings, the CAB undertook a comprehensive review of the industry's then current and its projected economic condition. The review indicated that, while traffic was continuing to grow at a healthy pace, there was an unprecedented reequipment program underway resulting in a sharp increase in capacity. 

Sizeable new purchases of flight equipment have come, in times past, as new types of planes have become operational; and deliveries of twin-engine jets and advance orders of jumbo jets contributed to this large expansion in the investment base. Coupled with a declining trend in passenger yield and rising costs of doing business, this resulted in a significant deterioration in the industry's return on investment. 

It appeared that this situation might be in the nature of a temporary earnings trough. Nevertheless, in January 1969 the CAB concluded that some increase in revenues was warranted for the immediate future, although not in the amount sought by the carriers. 

To gain as much light as possible on the matter, the CAB acceded to the carriers' request for a meeting. After a general round of comments at this meeting, the Board obtained additional information it desired and then announced a tentative decision to permit certain fare increases, subject to reconsideration on the basis of any complaints subsequently received. 

Such increases, designed to orient the fare structure (as distinguished from fare level) more closely into line with the cost of providing air service, were expected to produce aggregate additional revenues of about 3.8 percent for the trunkline industry, during the first full year of applicability of the new fares.

These new fares became effective for the trunkline carriers on February 20, 1969, and on various dates shortly thereafter. Local service carriers also were permitted to put fare increases into effect.

Reduced fares for youths between the ages of 12 and 22, both on a standby and confirmed reservation basis, continued in effect pending the outcome of an investigation initiated after the U.S. Court of Appeals remanded an earlier Board order dismissing complaints against these fares. It is expected that this limitation will materially reduce the use of these fares for business and other essential travel, yet retain their generative effect in the personal travel market for which they were designed. 

Fare Structure 

A long-range review of the domestic passenger fare structure, with particular emphasis on the possibility of developing a cost-oriented formula which would be used by the CAB as a guideline in evaluating carrier proposals for fare adjustments, was made during the fiscal year. A computer program was designed to test with accuracy the predictable impact which various approaches or formulas would have on revenues of the individual carriers and the industry as a whole. 

Domestic Cargo Rates 

The CAB suspended, pending investigation, significant selective increases in freight rates proposed by several major airlines. These proposals were canceled by the carriers, who were subsequently permitted to put into effect relatively moderate across-the-board increases. 

The Board instituted an investigation of the rule in effect for carriers and forwarders which permits the substitution of surface for air transportation. The evidentiary hearing was completed by the end of the fiscal year. 

The CAB permitted a number of carriers to increase minimum charges per shipment and also permitted Flying Tiger to put into effect a revised freight rate structure, consisting of both increased and reduced rates. 

The Board approved numerous intercarrier agreements which define and limit accessorial cargo services. Two other agreements concerning advance charges and assembly/distribution service were pending at the end of the year.

Intercarrier and shipper-carrier discussions on containerization continued under CAB authorization.

As a result of Board-authorized discussions, the carriers have arrived at numerous tentative recommendations for improved rules and practices in the area of freight claims and liability provisions. 24

The air cargo workshops inaugurated by the CAB in 1966 continued through the carriers' efforts on a regional basis, as well as at local and industry levels. These workshops provided a continuing dialogue between shippers and carriers on airfreight problems and marketing concepts.

International Fares 

North Atlantic.-The carrier members of the International Air Transport 

Association (IATA) filed an agreement proposing substantial revisions in the North Atlantic fare structure, intended to be effective for the 2-year period beginning May 1, 1969. The more important revisions are: elimination of the 5-percent round-trip discount; extension of the availability of the 14-21-day excursion and individual inclusive tour (IT) fares to a yearround basis, with added charges when travel is undertaken in the heretofore precluded summer weeks and on weekends; introduction of contract bulk fares for inclusive tour travel (CBIT fares), and reduced fares for group travel. The CBIT fares and reduced affinity group fares carried an intended effectiveness date of November 1, 1969.

After hearing oral argument from persons favoring or opposing the agreement, CBIT fares and elimination of the round-trip discount were limited to March 31, 1970. A formal investigation was ordered on an expedited basis in the interim.

The agreement affords savings to the traveling public in two respects: (1) the elimination of the blackout heretofore applicable to weekends and summer peak periods in connection with the 14-21-day excursion fares and individual inclusive tour fares will make available to a significant number of passengers who wish to travel at these times much lower fares than would otherwise be applicable; and (2) the contract bulk fares are lower than any fares ever offered in scheduled service on the North Atlantic.

Other Areas.-The IATA agreement an transpacific fares expired on 

March 31, 1969, and the carriers were unable to reach a new agreement. 

Fares, however, were maintained at the status quo through the remainder of fiscal 1969. In light of the high earnings which have been realized for a number of years by the U.S. carriers in this area, the CAB made clear its expectation that substantial reductions in transpacific fares should be agreed to at the IATA conference (which was convened shortly before the close of fiscal 1969).

Fares were generally maintained at the status quo within the Western Hemisphere. The carriers filed an agreement to be effective October 1, 1969, through March 31, 1971, which was under consideration by the CAB at the end of the fiscal year.

Military Transportation

Warsaw Convention 

Twenty-six additional carriers became signatories to the interim agreement providing for increased liability limits of $75,000 per passenger, and absolute liability for personal injury or death of international passengers subject to the Warsaw Convention. This agreement, which was adopted in 

May 1966, by the principal carriers providing service to and from the United States, provided a basis for the United States to withdraw a "Notice of Denunciation of the Warsaw Convention." The United States had previously filed to withdraw from the Convention because of its low liability limits.

There are now 45 U.S. carriers and 81 foreign carriers which are parties to this agreement, and these carriers provide most of the transportation involving travel to or from the United States.

Air Taxi Mail Service 

On October 17, 1967, part 298.24 of the CAB's economic regulations became effective. This new part gave the Post Office Department the opportunity to use air taxi operators to carry mail in markets competitive with a certificated route carrier by following certain procedures. By the end of fiscal 

1969, air taxis were carrying mail on approximately 150 different routes. 

Nearly $8 million was paid for these services. 

Part 298.24 provides for two types of notices to the CAB, the first a regular notice of intent to use air taxi mail service and the second, an expedited notice of intent to use air taxi mail service. The regular notice authorizes the Post Office Department to use air taxi mail service in competitive markets if, within 10 days of the filing of such notice, no objections are filed by interested parties or the Board does not issue an order denying its effectiveness. The regular notice must contain the reasons, together with supporting 26

nto data, why the Post Office Department deemed the proposed service required to meet the needs of the postal system. 

In the case of the expedited notice, the same information is required as for the regular notice except that, in addition, there shall be a factual representation that the Post Office Department has ascertained that no interested certificated route carrier objects to air taxi mail service between the subject pair of points. Expedited notices become effective in 5 days unless objected to or denied by Board order. 

Prior to the adoption of part 298.24, in order for mail to be carried by an air taxi operator in competitive markets, the air taxi carrier had to file under section 416(b) a request for an exemption, a time-consuming procedure. 

Part 298.24 was adopted at the request of the Post Office Department to facilitate its overall program for overnight delivery of all first class and air mail.

Since the new regulation became effective, regular notices and expedited notices have been filed by the Post Office Department. Although the purpose of part 298.24 is to cover authority to carry mail in competitive markets, the 

Post Office Department has been using this procedure to get established mail rates in noncompetitive markets in order to expedite the institution of mail service by air taxi carriers. The CAB determines the rate to be paid by the 

Post Office Department for air taxi mail service. 

Under part 298.24, the mail rate included in the notice of intent becomes effective at the end of the 10-day period even though the CAB has not fixed a final mail rate by that date. This procedure permits the Post Office Department to use air taxi mail service in a shorter period of time than could be done by filing separately for a mail rate in a noncompetitive market and waiting for the CAB to fix a final rate covering such air transportation. 

Of the 168 notices filed, 121 duplicated in whole or in part some segment of a route of a certificated carrier. These notices are listed in appendix E. 

Objections were filed with respect to 19 notices, 15 by certificated carriers, and four by competing air taxi operators.

The times for air taxi mail service as contained in the notices of intent are typically between 10 p.m. and 4 a.m., the period when passenger traffic is relatively light for the certificated air carriers. Therefore, these are the times when the least number of schedules are provided by such carriers suitable for the use of the Post Office Department. No apparent pattern has emerged from the filings to date as to any particular carrier being subject to numerous filings by the Post Office Department. Of the trunkline and local service carriers, only Northeast has not had at least one segment duplicated by a Post Office Department filing. Frontier, with 25 duplicated segments, has the largest number of route segments involved, but has filed no objections to the notices of intent. The local service industry has had 110 segments duplicated by notices of intent; the trunkline industry has had 106. 

Forty-three notices were filed where the available certificated carrier had a published schedule within 1 hour of the proposed air taxi schedule (app. 

F). Of this number, there were objections to 10. There were 27 notices filed where the available certificated carrier had a published schedule within between 1 and 2 hours of the proposed air taxi schedule. Of this number, only two notices were objected to; the remaining 89 notices duplicating certificated 27

carrier routes were for segments where the published schedules of the certificated carrier were more than 2 hours removed from the proposed air taxi schedule times. There were objections to two notices. The foregoing figures appear to indicate that the Post Office Department is generally keeping the certificated industry advised of its mail service requirements. 

Beginning October 30, 1968, 20 regular notices were filed by the Post Office Department to replace previous notices in instances where the designated air taxi operator subsequently requested to be relieved from serving the route. Some of the reasons for these failures were changes in the mail rate, flight patterns, schedules, aircraft, transferred assets, and air taxi mergers. Of those 20 filings, two new notices split one previous notice into two segments, and one notice replaced a service which had been authorized by exemption prior to the promulgation of part 298.24 of the Board's regulations.

Effective July 1, 1969, the CAB amended part 298 of the economic regulations to extend to June 30, 1974, the exemption granted air taxi operators to engage in the transportation of mail. Also effective on the same date, approximately 3,500 air taxi operators in the United States (of which about 165 provide scheduled service) were required to register with the CAB a certificate indicating compliance with the new liability insurance requirements that became effective March 7, 1969, and to file other basic information as to the nature of their operations. 

Trends in Yield (per Revenue Passenger Mile) 

The following table sets forth the average yields per revenue passenger mile by class of service for the scheduled services of the domestic trunklines since calendar year 1957.

For the 12-month period ended March 31, 1969, the average yield for all services was 5.47 cents per passenger-mile, which represents a very slight reduction of 0.18 percent from the yield of 5.48 cents experienced in the 12 months ended March 1968. The first-class yield increased 0.57 percent, from

Automatic Data Processing 

One of the CAB's most impressve in-house achievements has been the development of automatic data processing (ADP) as an integral tool of the Board's information collection, verification, processing, dissemination and analyses functions. ADP is a common, basic component of the Board's operational systems and is the primary tool in making those systems respond to the requirements of the decisionmaking process. In the climate of increasing need for maximum economy in Government operations and increasing complexity of the air transport environment, the CAB has emphasized automation of all information handling.

Creation of basic data banks, automation of major statistical releases, and ad hoc preparation of special statistical studies were the major aspects of the Board's ADP program in its early periods. The staff used in this activity over the past 10 years has increased from nine employees to 34. The annual equipment expenditures, correlative with productive capacity, grew from less than $30,000 to about $180,000. The scope of data made available and applied in analytical processes has expanded accordingly. 

During the fiscal year, data banks accessible to the CAB's computer facility were expanded significantly. Two notable examples involve (1) cargo import-export statistics; and (2) drastically revised passenger origin and destination survey.

System plans and designs also were initiated for further improvements in data banks through: (1) revision of the basic (form 41) traffic data system to embrace data at the detailed segment as well as at air carrier summary levels; and (2) construction of data banks of domestic passenger rate tariffs, operating authorities and conditions and charter traffic movements. 

These data banks, when added to existing computerized information, provide a broad variety of data for ADP-supported analyses in virtually all phases of the CAB's operations.

During the fiscal year, too, much effort went into improving techniques of using the data bank. The main emphasis was on the use of a Mark IV user-oriented system which enables individual analysts to participate more directly in retrieving data and instructing the computer on its use. This system will give the user access to the data and computational facilities of the CAB's centralized computer with minimum direct participation by technical 

ADP staff. 32

Facilities also are being developed which will provide individual analysts on a time-shared basis, with "real-time" computational capabilities for those research-directed projects which cannot be effectively developed in the "batch mode" environment of the central computer. 

A contract was made during fiscal 1968 for the use, through a typewritertype terminal, of a remote General Electric comptuer. A further arrangement then enabled the use on a time-shared basis of a remote computer provided by the U.S. Department of Agriculture. 

This facility, in addition to providing "real-time" computational capabilities, also makes possible for the first time the automated exchange of information with the centralized computer. This permits much more flexible and economic use of both types of facilities. 

Concurrently with the development of data banks and methodologies for their use, computer applications of progressive complexity and sophistication are being developed to perform mission objectives. Examples of applications by major program areas during the fiscal year:

Carrier Accounts 

One of the more significant new developments during the past fiscal year was the CAB's issuance of a proposed regulation to provide faster, more efficient communication of traffic data between the industry and the CAB. There would be uniform statistical classifications for the industry and the CAB would be provided with recall of detailed computerized data for use in individual route and rate proceedings and general purpose studies through the media of magnetic tapes and punched cards. 

With an eye to fully automating the CAB's informational data collection and processing system, steps are being taken to mechanize the data collection and reporting of financial data. The main efforts will be on improving the data content for separating costs of carriers' passenger, all-cargo and military operations.

A rule adopted June 23, 1969, and effective with the start of fiscal 1970, is aimed at effecting more comprehensive reporting by local service carriers. 

The new rule reflects the growing importance of this carrier class and places the local service carriers in a new reporting class comparable to the reporting class in which the trunkline carriers are grouped. 

Another sharply expanding segment of the industry, the commuter air carrier operations (scheduled air taxi operations), also came under new reporting requirements which alleviate a basic traffic statistic information deficiency. 

In another significant action, stemming from the experimental airfreight forwarder authority awarded to three long-distance motor carriers, the 34

CAB proposed a new regulation which would impose minimal reporting requirements consistent with protection of the public interest. These requirements will allow the CAB to evaluate possible diversion of traffic from airfreight forwarders to the long-distance carriers operating as airfreight forwarders.

In cooperation with the Interstate Commerce Commission work is continuing toward the development of a more comprehensive system of accounts and statistics for freight forwarders in light of continued growth of this segment of the industry. In a parallel development design is nearing completion for the submittal of additional statistical data which would eliminate certain analytical problems that now exist in regard to supplemental air carriers as well as fulfill the data requirements made known by the National Trans portation Safety Board. 

In its role in development and maintenance of uniform standards and practice in major accounting questions affecting the carriers, the CAB issued several interpretive letters to the industry during the fiscal year. One of significance was an accounting letter concerning an inconsistency of practice in circumstances where carriers were receiving allowances by aircraft manufacturers in consideration of aircraft purchases and leases. Another letter examined the question of the proper interest rate for the industry to use in determining the amount of interest to be capitalized on all outstanding long-term debt. A third letter prescribed a uniform practice for recording and accounting for the 10 percent income tax surcharge. 

The CAB recently completed a General Accounting Office questionnaire which sought information about the accounting practices followed in the industry and the accounting policies or principles which guide such practices. 

The questionnaire is for the GAO research study on the feasibility of establishing uniform cost accounting standards in fulfillment of Public Law 90-370. 

Under consideration also is a draft proposed amendment broadening and clarifying the CAB's reporting entity definitions and entity designations for domestic, territorial, and international operations in light of the changing profile of the industry's route pattern.

Field Audits of Air Carriers 

Field audits are made by staff assigned to the New York City and the 

San Francisco Regional Audit Offices and the Miami permanent duty station. 

At June 30, 1969, there were 39 certificated route carriers and 14 certificated supplemental carriers with general offices in 32 cities in 21 States, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. One route carrier and one supplemental carrier were certificated during fiscal 1969. Thirteen of the 39 route carriers received subsidy.

Air carrier accounts and records are examined by professional auditors skilled in verification techniques. The usual professional audit is performed on subsidized and supplemental air carriers. Its principal objectives are protection against overpayments of subsidy, compliance with CAB accounting and reporting regulations, and evaluation of financial fitness, particularly in regard to the supplemental carriers. 

The nonsubsidized route carriers are generally given subject area examinations. These involve examination of major accounting and reporting subjects in lieu of the usual audit. By rotating the subjects, all phases of the carrier's accounting and reporting are completely reviewed every 3 to 5 years. In the selection process, priority is given to subjects most important to the Board's regulatory program and to new and controversial accounting subjects under study by the accounting profession. Flexibility, consistent with available manpower, and timely compilation of data needed to keep CAB accounting and reporting regulations abreast of changing conditions are the principal advantages of the subject area review. A review of the public accountants' audits is a prerequisite of this type of examination. The scope and depth of CAB auditors' reviews are determined after their review of the public accountants' working papers. 

Special investigations, studies and examinations are made, as warranted, in connection with the Board's route, rate and compliance activities. 

Forty-one audits and 15 subject area reviews were completed during the fiscal year. Fifty-one special examinations also were completed. Four audits were for periods of less than a year and represented closeout audits of subsidized carriers absorbed by other air carriers through mergers. The special examinations covered subjects such as subsidy claims data, air carrier financial fitness, flight equipment pricing policies, capitalization of interest, trade agreements, and research and development tasks related to subject area reviews. 36

Cost Finding and Statistical Services 

Under the CAB's cost finding program, development work began on a computer-based integrated costing system which will use a single data base and a consistent set of costing concepts and methods, yet be capable of providing alternative outputs to serve the agency's specialized costing needs. 

Concurrently, computerized costing programs already developed were used extensively during the fiscal year to develop cost information for the domestic fare structure study and for the study on the impact of the new large jet aircraft.

During the fiscal year, a new CAB statistical publication, Trends in All- Cargo Service, detailing financial, operational, and traffic trends in scheduled all-cargo service, was published. This new report represents results of scheduled all-cargo services for selected U.S. certificated route air carriers for the fiscal and calendar years ended during the period June 30, 1963 through June 30, 1968. Scheduled all-cargo services encompass all flights scheduled primarily for the transportation of cargo, including freight, express, and mail. These selected carriers currently produce 99.7 percent of the total revenue ton-miles flown in scheduled all-cargo service by the certificated route air carrier industry. The report includes all-cargo data for both the all-cargo carriers and the combination passenger/cargo carriers. 

The first two issues of the quarterly passenger origin-destination survey reports were produced, using a revised, modernized passenger survey. In addition to the published survey reports, the more detailed survey data were distributed in microfilm for the first time. Basic data banks on magnetic tape containing the full detail in the survey were available on a much broader basis to Government and industry users. 

The Canadian Government, working concurrently with the United States on modernizing the passenger survey program in Canada, implemented a revised survey essentially the same as the U.S. plan, thus improving and making compatible the recurrent exchange of trans-border survey data between the two countries. Data for the first two quarters of 1968 were exchanged. 

ICAO continued to promote a regional passenger survey within the Western Hemisphere, and the CAB sent representatives to two meetings of 37

The CAB staff produced the first issue of a new periodic publication, the Quarterly Airline Industry Economic Report, which provides timely highlights of traffic and financial trend data for the various air carrier groups. 

The report includes the system operations of the 11 domestic trunk carriers and Pan American World Airways, the domestic operations of the trunk carriers, the international and territorial operations of the passenger cargo carriers, the local service carriers, the all-cargo carriers and the supplemental carriers. Tables of operating and financial data compare the latest quarter and 12-month period with the prior like period.

court actions

As in previous years, litigation during the fiscal year for the most part consisted of direct statutory review of CAB orders in the U.S. Courts of 

Appeals. Eight cases were disposed of during the fiscal year, with seven cases pending. There were 32 cases disposed of and four cases pending during fiscal 1968. 

In Air Line Employees Association v. Civil Aeronautics Board, 413 

F.2d 1092 (C.A.D.C., 1969), the CAB had approved the merger of Lake 

Central Airlines into Allegheny Airlines but, although imposing certain labor protective conditions on its approval of the merger, had refused to require assumption of Lake Central's collective bargaining agreement with the Air Lines Employees Association as a condition of the merger. The Air 

Line Employees Association contended that refusal to impose such a condition invalidated the Board's order of approval and that the labor protective conditions were insufficient. The Court sustained the CAB's order, holding that the Board's duty in airline merger cases is to impose conditions that make the mergers consistent with the public interest and that the CAB is not obligated to pass on every question of labor law which may arise out of a merger.

As reported last year, there were pending at the close of fiscal 1968 two cases in the Court of Appeals for the First Circuit (one of which had been transferred from the Court of Apeals for the Fifth Circuit) challenging the CAB's refusal to institute an investigation into the family fare tariffs of 28 air carriers. On June 13, 1969 the Court set aside the CAB's order. Trailways of New England, et al. v. Civil Aeronautics Board, 412 F. 2d 926. The Court held that in cases involving alleged rate discrimination, the complainant has the burden of showing that the rates are prima facie unjustly discriminatory, but that once such a showing has been made, the CAB has the affirmative burden of showing either that the tariffs should not be investigated for public policy reasons, or that the discrimination is justified in terms of established Board precedent or policy and the relevant facts. It held that petitioners had stated a prima facie case and that the Board had failed to carry its burden. 

Accordingly, the Court remanded the case to the CAB for further proceedings.

Mohawk Airlines v. Civil Aeronautics Board, 412 F. 2d 8 (C.A. 2, June 5, 

1969) was a challenge to the Board's decision in the Allegheny Airlines Route 97 Investigation, docket 17436. That investigation involved realignment of Allegheny's route segments and the CAB's order was assailed by Mohawk insofar as it approved a realignment under which Allegheny could 50

conduct nonstop operations between Islip, N.Y., and Cleveland, Islip and

Detroit and Bridgeport, Conn. and Detroit. The Court set aside the CAB's order, holding that it had given inadequate notice that nonstop service between those points was in issue and thereby had deprived Mohawk of an opportunity to seek comparative consideration for an award of such authority to itself.

Legislation Enacted

One measure was enacted shortly after the close of the fiscal year which directly affected the functions and activities of the CAB. This was Public 

Law 91-62, approved August 20, 1969, which requires, as of August 5, 1969, CAB approval of the acquisition by any person of control of an air carrier. 

The CAB is authorized to exempt any acquisition of control of a noncertificated air carrier from the approval requirement to the extent that such may be in the public interest. Unless the CAB finds otherwise, any person owning beneficially 10 per centum or more of the voting securities or capital of an air carrier is presumed to be in control of such carrier. This public law also requires any person owning, beneficially or as trustee, more than 5 per centum of any class of the capital stock or capital of an air carrier to submit annually, and at such other times as the Board may require, a description of the stock or other interest owned and the amount. 

Another measure enacted during the fiscal year which was of interest to aviation in general was Public Law 90-568, approved October 12, 1968, extending the Aircraft Guarantee Loan Program for another 5 years.

Legislative Proposals 

The CAB is required by the Federal Aviation Act to include in its annual report such recommendations for additional legislation as it believes to be necessary for discharging its regulatory functions. The CAB's present legislative proposals for 1970 are encompassed in the following program. Bills implementing several of the proposals are pending in the 91st Congress. 

The CAB also has under consideration various other legislative proposals. 

Depreciation Accounting.-One legislative proposal would erase the regulatory gap caused by court decisions ruling that the CAB lacks authority to regulate the depreciation accounting practices of air carriers. 

At present, the carriers are free to select their own depreciation practices and employ methods which lack uniformity. Thus, some carriers have improved their reported profits by changing depreciation practices, while others depreciate their entire aircraft fleet or particular aircraft over periods different from those used by other airlines. The result is that the CAB cannot readily obtain reliable financial data needed to conduct its regulatory activities; and the public cannot rely on the carriers' reports as portraying the carriers' true financial condition. The CAB believes that it should have the authority, possessed by comparable agencies, to regulate depreciation accounting (S. 1172, H.R. 8668).

Consolidation of Certain CAB Proceedings.-Legislation is needed which will strengthen and clarify the CAB's procedural powers with respect to the consolidation of proceedings pending before it. One of the most persistent problems the CAB has encountered in route proceedings has been the contention of applicants, based on the doctrine of the Ashbacker case (Ashbacker Radio Corp. v. FCC, 326 U.S. 327 (1945)), that they are entitled as a matter of legal right to consolidation of certain applications with such proceedings.

The CAB is not opposed to the principle that mutually exclusive applications must be disposed of on simultaneous consideration. However, the application of this doctrine has in many cases resulted in protracted delays by reason of interlocutory appeals from consolidation orders. 

The CAB believes that its consolidation orders should not be subject to interlocutory appeal but should be subject to court review at the conclusion of the proceeding. The CAB recommends that legislation be enacted which will bar interlocutory appeals and clarify the guidelines for consolidation (S. 1174, H.R. 8667).

Ineligibility of Air Carriers for Subsidy.-Another CAB legislative pro posal would amend the Federal Aviation Act to withdraw subsidy eligibility from carriers which have become self-sufficient. Only one of the trunklines has received subsidy during the last 5 years, and none receives it now. Other carriers have and will continue to pass the developmental stage where they need Federal financial aid to operate. Nonetheless, those carriers remain eligible under the statute to apply for subsidy.

Although the CAB might decline to grant such assistance, the carriers' continued eligibility may place the Federal Treasury under a major contingent liability. 54

To alert the industry that it is expected to rely on its own resources, the CAB supports legislation withdrawing subsidy eligibility from trunklines and most other carriers not now receiving subsidy, and making operations conducted by presently subsidized carriers ineligible for such assistance after a 5-year subsidy-free period (S. 1244, H.R. 8669).

Repayment of Charges in Excess of Tariff Rates.—Legislation is needed which would permit the CAB to issue appropriate remedial orders in enforcement proceedings directing the repayment of charges in excess of those in lawfully filed tariffs. While the CAB may entertain complaints against overcharges, it is not authorized, in specific terms, to require repayment. Passengers desiring to collect the overcharges must resort to the courts. In most instances, and particularly in the case of group travel, this is not practicable because of the slight amounts involved for each passenger. The aggregate amount involved for each group, however, could represent a sizeable sum which the CAB should be empowered to require a carrier to repay to passengers (S. 1241).

Regulation of Rates and Practices in Foreign Air Transportation.— 

The CAB should be given comprehensive legislative authority to regulate rates and fares in foreign air transportation. The Federal Aviation Act does not authorize the CAB to regulate directly the changes for international air transportation except for a limited power to prevent discrimination. Its only ability to influence international rate and fare levels is through its power to withhold approval of rate-fixing agreements to which U.S. air carriers may be parties. As a practical matter, the effective power to control rates for air services between the United States and foreign countries is in the hands of the foreign countries, with neither the U.S. Government nor U.S. carriers being in a position to bargain on equal terms in fixing rates. Legislation has been drafted.

CAB Court Representation.-Legislation is needed which would grant the CAB the right of independent representation through its own counsel in a manner comparable to that exercised by other independent regulatory agencies. The judicial review provisions of the Federal Aviation Act do not expressly provide that the CAB may participate in court proceedings through its own counsel as a matter of right. In contrast, a number of major regulatory agencies, such as the Federal Communications Commission, the Federal Power Commission, the Federal Trade Commission, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the National Labor Relations Board, have a clear right to appear and defend their orders in judicial review proceedings. 

Legislation has been drafted. 357-332 0695 55
