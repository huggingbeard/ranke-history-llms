Introduction 

Many of the major issues of statutory implementation of the Judicial Article of 1962 were successfully resolved in 1963 through legislation prepared and sponsored by the Illinois Judicial Advisory Council. But several major problems still faced the General Assembly at its regular 1965 session, as well as a host of detailed changes on the ministerial and technical level. As a result, the number of bills in the 1965 legislative program of the Illinois Judicial Advisory Council was dramatically increased. In 1963 the successful Council program contained 68 bills dealing with the new court system and getting it properly underway. The 1965 legislative program of the Council consisted of close to 300 enactments. Although the ratio of major issues in this large number of bills was not as high as it was in the 1963 package of bills, the efforts required to enact the 1965 bills necessarily required a large amount of the energies and skills of the 

Council membership. The Council points with pride to the fact that, despite its heavy duties and substantial legislative program, it completed its work and still managed to lapse $4,490.14 of its 1963-1965 biennial appropriation --almost 25% of the total appropriation. 4/ 

1965 LEGISLATION Matters Relating to Judges 

Compulsory Retirement. One of the most controversial subjects, the compulsory retirement of judges at a stated age, was resolved with the enactment of S. B. 352. As a result, judges reaching their 70th birthday are automatically retired, except judges in office on July 

20, 1965, the effective date of the Act. Judges in. office on that date are retired automatically on one of the following described dates whichever occurs last: (1) on January 1, 1976, or (2) on the date the judge completes 18 years of service in a court of record, (3) whenever the judge reaches age 70.

Salaries of Judges. S. B. 249 of the 1965 session establishes a uniform state salary for all associate judges of the circuit court. 

This figure is set at $17,500. This salary provision went into effect immediately because the Judicial Article prohibits only a decrease in salary during the elected term but does not prohibit an increase during a term. This uniform state salary means that associate judges in the smaller counties got substantial raises as much as $5,500 in some cases. Associates in the largest counties realized no increase from this uniform figure.

Fourth Circuit Judge Circuits. In 1965 S. B. 296 was enacted to amend the provision in Ch. 37, Sec. 72.2a of the Illinois Revised

Statutes, which provides for an increase in the number of circuit judges in particular circuits. Specifically, the section prior to the 

1965 amendment provided that any circuit with a state institution with an enrollment of over 15,000 was entitled to elect an additional circuit judge thus making it a "fourth circuit judge circuit", three being the normal complement of circuit judges per circuit outside Cook County. 

At the time of the amendment Southern Illinois University had an enrollment of just under the 15,000 qualifying figure. The circuit has a large number of associate judges (about 15, the most for any circuit in the state outside Cook County). Because of this, it was felt that the creation of an additional circuit judgeship was unwarranted. Thus, the qualifying figure was raised by S. B. 296 from 15,000 to 20,000. 

This forestalls, at least for the moment, the creation of the unneeded fourth circuit judgeship in the First Judicial Circuit.

Retention Ballot Changes. S. B. 295 was enacted to correct some technical deficiencies in the Election Code dealing with the ballots for the retention (non-adversary election) of judges in office. The bill provides in substance as follows: (1) the judges of the various courts are grouped on the ballot in order of importance of the court; (2) within each group the order of appearance is fixed according to seniority or by alphabetical order where judges have equal service on the court; (3) the ballot must be printed in green to make it distinctive from any other ballot; (4) in order to emphasize the nonadversary aspect of the retention ballot, the bill requires that a brief statement appear on the top of the ballot to the effect that the judges listed are not running against each other, and that the sole question is whether each judge should be retained in office. 1 

Matters Relating to Magistrates Matters Assignable to Magistrates. 

S. B. 1072 accomplishes a

significant expansion of matters which may be assigned to magistrates for hearing. Pursuant to the bill magistrates may be assigned civil matters where the amount claimed does not exceed $10,000 (previously magistrates were limited to hearing civil matters where the amount did not exceed $5,000).

Perhaps even more significant is the new provision in the bill allowing the Supreme Court to establish by rule additional categories which may be assigned for hearing by magistrates. This applies only, however, to magistrates who are attorneys. The Supreme Court has not yet availed itself of this new authority but has the matter under study.

Number of Magistrates Increased. H. B. 933 provides for the appointment of a limited number of magistrates in a circuit in addition to the maximum number otherwise authorized if certain conditions are met. Among these conditions are the following: (1) the chief judge of the circuit must file a request for the additional appointing authority with the Supreme Court; (2) the court administrator must determine on the basis of a number of pertinent factors, such as court case loads, if a need for the additional magistrates is authorized; (3) the Supreme Court then considers the recommendations of the court administrator and either grants or denies the request of the chief judge. The bill limits the total of such additional magistrates to 

20 throughout the state. To date a total of 12 such additional magistrates have been appointed pursuant to the operation of this bill. 

Magistrate's Travel Expenses. Through oversight of the 1963 

session, no provision was made for the payment of travel expenses of a magistrate when traveling on official business inside or outside. his county of residence. The new system envisions flexibility in the employment of magistrates which means considerable travel within his home county, as well as other counties in his circuit should the need arise. S. B. 24 was enacted to provide for the reimbursement of all these travel expenses.

Matters Relating to Court Reporters Appointment, Qualification and Salary of Court Reporters.. An entirely new system for the appointment, qualification and salary of court reporters was enacted in S. B. 1278 and is a significant addition to implementation of the administration of justice in Illinois. The major points in the bill are as follows: (1) the number of court reporters to be appointed in each circuit is determined by the Supreme Court; (2) the reporters are appointed by the chief judge of the circuit with the advice and consent of the circuit and associate circuit judges of the circuit to serve at the pleasure of the chief judge; (3) the chief judge is authorized to assign reporters for service anywhere within the circuit; (4) a test, established by the court administrator, must be passed in order for certification of eligibility. י.

According to his ability as evidenced by the test, an applicant may be classified as a Class A or a Class B reporter. Class A reporters receive a state salary of $9,000 per year and Class B reporters receive a salary of $5,000 per year from the state; (5) a grandfather clause exists in favor of court reporters who have served for at least five years in a circuit court prior to January 1, 1966, the effective date of the Act. Such reporters are entitled to Class A certification without examination.

Matters Relating to Court Fees 

S. B. 1298 raises a few court fees in counties of the first and second class (Cook County is a county of the third class and, in fact, is the only county in that class). The fees raised are as follows: (1) confession of judgment (amount of over $5,000) from $15 to $20; (2) for each action of forcible entry and detainer, $5 plus $5 more if money judgment is sought (there previously was no provision to cover this situation); (3) in each criminal case from $15 to $20; (4) for filing a release or satisfaction of judgment, $2 (no fee previously in statute). 

H. B. 15 provides that the state's attorney's fee for a conviction in a criminal case before a magistrate of the circuit court is $5. There had been some contention that a higher fee was due the state's attorney and this bill removed all ambiguity. 

S. B. 19 lowers the fee from $17 to $14 in confession of judgment cases where the amount involved is less than $5,000. This bill carries forward the policy of lessening court fees in the area of smaller claims.

Matters Relating to Court Administration 

Court Records Systems. Even before the new Judicial Article took effect, the system of record keeping in the circuit courts, as well as other courts, was chaotic and archaic. The new Article pointed up the existing deficiencies even more sharply. As a result, the Supreme 

Court undertook a long range study of record keeping and related statutory provisions. At the request to the Council of the Supreme Court, S. B. 1234 was enacted which provides that existing statutory provisions. on record keeping will continue in force until supplanted by rule of the Supreme Court. At the present time the court administrator reports that a pilot system of streamlined record keeping will be instituted shortly on a trial basis. If it proves successful, the necessary appropriations will be sought to implement the new system completely, and it will be adopted through the rule making authority granted pursuant to S. B. 1234. 14

Filling Appellate Court Clerk Vacancies. Pre-1963 statutory provisions for filling a vacancy in the office of appellate court clerk were rendered inapplicable by operation of terms of the new Judicial Article. H. B. 20 was enacted, therefore, to cover the hiatus in the law. The bill provides that the vacancy in the office of appellate court clerk shall be filled for the remainder of the unexpired term by appointment of the judges of the appellate court elected in the judicial district in which the vacancy in the office of clerk exists. (The bill does not otherwise effect the elective nature of the office.)

Supreme Court Employees. Prior to 1965 the only employees of the Supreme Court whose salaries were fixed by statute were the librarian, marshall, law clerks for the judges, and the custodian of the Supreme Court building. S. B 30 and H. B. 259 were enacted to put the salaries of these persons directly under the administrative control of the Court.

Notice for Certain Vouchering Purposes. H. B. 9 and H. B. 10 

were enacted to cover administrative problems not previously properly defined by statute. Under the new system, the Supreme Court, and not the Auditor of Public Accounts, is the paymaster for the judicial officers. H. B. 9 thus provides that notice of, death or resignation of a judge or magistrate is to be made by the chief circuit judge to the Supreme Court so that the Court will be properly informed of personnel changes for purposes of issuing salary vouchers. 

H. B. 10 requires the State Electoral Board to notify the Supreme 

Court rather than the Auditor, as was the old system, of judicial election results so that the Court will know with official certainty what salary vouchers should be added or deleted depending on election outcomes.

Matters Relating to Constables 

Abolition of the Office of Constable. H. B. 45 abolishes the office of constable thereby eliminating several hundred elective public offices. Under the old court system the office of constable was constitutional in nature and could not be abolished by statute. The new 

Judicial Article stripped away this constitutional sanctuary. Since the new system abolished justice of the peace courts and, since a constable's main function was service of process for J. P. courts, there was no need to continue the office of constable under the new system. 

The office of sheriff in each county has in the past served process for the circuit courts and has taken over the duties of service of process formerly performed by constables. It took an additional set of 38 companion bills (H. B. s 46 through 83) to complete the abolition of the office. บ. 

Matters Relating to Technical Corrections 

The new Article abolished justice of the peace and police magistrate

courts, county, city, village, town and municipal courts and made other major changes. Although the old courts and old phrases were no longer appropriate, their existence lingered on in the statutes of the state.

The number of sections in the various statutes still containing the old references, prior to the 1965 session, was estimated to be well over 800. Because of the staggering task of preparing and handling the some 250 bills necessary to correct the archaic language of most of these sections, the Judicial Advisory Council postponed this job from the 1963 session, when the major substantive implementation was being considered and enacted, until the 1965 session. INTRODUCTION 

The work of the Council since the adoption of the Judicial Article in November, 1962 largely has been the undertaking of the primary responsibility for the implementation of the Article and the preparation of a study of its effectiveness in working. Many of the major issues of implementation were passed in the 1963 legislature. However, the 

1965 session handled a host of detailed changes. Approximately 300 bills were prepared and presented to the 1965 legislature. Many of these were enacted, largely through the energies and skill of Council membership. Much work was done in the interim prior to the 1967 legislative session to carry out the Council's duties, and in preparation for the 1967 session. 

1967 LEGISLATION

JUDICIAL QUESTIONNAIRE 

The Council, after much consideration and discussion, decided to prepare and circulate among all lawyers admitted to practice in Illinois a questionnaire to inquire into the workings, effectiveness or ineffectiveness, and problems, if any, in the Court system and amongst the members of the judiciary particularly, since adoption of the new Judicial Article. Many hours of deliberation were devoted by the Council members as to whether such a study should be undertaken at all. Further, if it were to be undertaken, the nature of the inquiry. Many meetings were had on this problem, and at least seven drafts of a proposed questionnaire were prepared, discussed, and considered. Being of the opinion that the most logical test of the effectiveness or ineffectiveness, strength or weaknesses of the workings of the new Court system, best could be obtained from the lawyers who use the Courts and participate - 5 

---

in and encounter the situation, the Council determined to mail to all lawyers licensed in Illinois a questionnaire into the workings of the judiciary. Some 17,800 questionnaires were mailed out shortly prior to June 20, 1967 with requests for return by reply prepaid mail by June 30, 1967. When these questionnaires are returned, their answers will be placed on punch cards and analyzed by computer. Thereafter, the Council will consider the results in a study of legislation to be recommended to the next session of the legislature. 

OTHER CONSIDERATIONS 

Many other legislative matters have been considered, passed upon, and acted upon during the 1967 legislative session itself. Some of these are those of Court fees, selection method of Judges, and payment of fees by counties into the State Treasury due to payment of Judges' and reporters' salaries out of State funds under the new Judicial Article rather than out of the County Treasury. The questions of appointment, rather than election, of Appellate and Supreme Courts Clerks and their respective salaries all have been matters considered and acted upon by the Council. 

CONCLUSION 

It is the sincere hope of this Council that its activities will continue with the serious consideration and supported effort heretofore afforded it by the legislation creating the Council and defining its duties and responsibilities.

The completion of its study through the Judicial Questionnaire, 6 -

recently mailed by its staff, no doubt will reveal many areas where improvement may be made in the Illinois judicial system. This must be followed to conclusion and its study implemented by proper legislation, soberly considered and carefully prepared prior to the next session of the Illinois legislature. Only in such a manner will the statutory duty be properly fulfilled so that the Council "shall, by continuous study of the problems involved, devise means to effect the improvement of the administration of justice in and with relation to the State, and to formulate all proper suggestions and recommendations concerning legislation and other measures designed to bring about such improvement. 

On many occasions members of the Council have traveled far, and delayed other important duties, to be present and add their best wisdom to deliberation of the Council. Many times almost complete member attendance has been accorded to the numerous serious questions presented. 

We recommend the continuance of this Council, and entrust to all of its future members the highest consideration of its important study and the further continued successful solution of the problems in this field.
