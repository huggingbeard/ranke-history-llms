Dedicated To MEMORY OF MY WIFE

the As a boy Herman Gordon had nothing of which to boast. By strange conditions he could not control, he was compelled to make his home with an uncle, Timothy Sweet, one of the grouchiest guardians the sun ever shown on, and who loved money the best of all. But notwithstanding disadvantages that were against him, he grew up to be a young man of sterling worth, strong and and reliable, perhaps as some have said: "lacking polish, like homemade furniture, but perfectly qualified to carry the load, whatever it might be." Yet this environment he had mastered and had learned to be content, even on his guardian's farm with a half dozen cows he had to milk both morning and night. Besides, he had to look after a husky team of horses between times or when there was nothing else to do. "The worst darn thing about the boy is, he is continually trying to look beyond the horizon far off," the guardian would often say, "which is a mighty hard thing to do, with a vision of limited [1]

means an' a fliver that refuses to start at least two-thirds of the time."

He was a hero only to the old shepherd dog, who followed his boy master over the fields, and down by the running brook, and up the hillsides where a few chestnut trees grew and the ground hog burrowed holes unmolested. Not the best ground on which to raise a boy, but good enough to produce one of the finest, whose word and law and whose heart was as a rose. 

As the plow and the harrow make ready the ground for the coming crop, so old Tim himself and everyday circumstances made Herman Gordon ready for the events that came his way in the days of his early manhood and in middle life and old age when it came his lot to pass that way. 

Thus Herman Gordon comes before us, not to play the most important part in this story by any means but to act as a principal at least in the first scene of a realistic life's drama. 

Betty Camdon, the girl, we find in somewhat similar circumstances. Her desire was to peep beyond the hills that hemmed her in on three sides. Her staunch old grandmother, with whom she lived, always insisted that there was time enough for a girl of seventeen to listen to the music of the world after her twenty-fourth birthday. "The girls of today are different than they were when I was young. Then they were contented to wear a dress of calico, [2]

and to attend a husking bee once a year,' she told the girl, proudly.
