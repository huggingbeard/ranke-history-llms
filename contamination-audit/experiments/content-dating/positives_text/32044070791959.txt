FOREIGN TRADE AND INDUSTRIAL ORGANIZATION 

The following memorandum will survey first the organization of industry in Great Britain; second, the organization of industry in Germany; third, the foreign exchange problems of the interwar period; fourth, the nature of competition in manufacturing; and lastly, the effect of all these factors on American business operations abroad. 

I. The Organization of British Industry 1. Prior to the First World War 

Several good sources of information exist regarding the organization of British industry prior to the First World 

War. 

J. H. Clapham's "An Economic History of Modern 

Britain" (Macmillan, N. Y., University Press, Cambridge, England, 1938) is the standard work on its subject. Part 

III of this work, dealing with the period 1887-1914 (with an Epilogue 1914-1929), is especially valuable for an understanding of modern industrial development in the United Kingdom. Its approach is, however, primarily historicaldescriptive.

Specifically as regards the beginnings of the concentration movement in modern British industry, the pioneering work is H. W. Macrosty's "Trust Movement in British Industry" (London, 1907). The same author's "Trusts and the State" (London, 1901), and D. H. Macgregor's "Industrial Combination" (London, 1906), though more theoretical, are also interesting. Among American sources the "Report of the Industrial Commission on Industrial Combinations in Europe", prepared by J. W. Jenks, Washington, D. C., 1901, is still worth consulting.

The best historical descriptive treatise on British industrial organization prior to 1914 is Hermann Levy's "Monopoly and Competition", published in London in 1911. The following description is largely based on the material of Professors Clapham and Levy.

English industrial organization has gone through transformations that were at times extreme but are now in part forgotten. Industrial capitalism in England was cradled in monopoly, not in competition. The system of monopolies was from the beginning the expression of a definite royal policy, pursued with ever growing eagerness, in spite of statutory opposition from the days of Queen Elizabeth, and so successfully developed under the Stuarts that, in almost every important trade, national monopolies arose. 

Opposition to this policy came from the most diverse quarters. As monopolies steadily increased, the various strains of opposition met in a single movement which succeeded in extirpating, after a comparatively short but exceptionally effective existence, the monopoly system. The main center of the anti-monopoly movement was the House of Commons. This opposition was so successful that, so far as our knowledge of industrial England in the eighteenth century goes, national monopoly based on legal privilege no longer existed, at that time, in any industry. Adam 

Smith knew only colonial trade monopolies and a few town monopolies.

In addition to colonial and foreign trade, substantial monopolist combinations existed in English mining at times. The most famous is the Newcastle Coal Vend which lasted with certain interruptions from 1771 to 1844. 

It rested on its ability to supply coal to the London market by coastwise shipping more economically than competing

coal mining areas.

Similar combinations existed in tin mining and copper mining. English manufacturing industry was, however, by and large competitive during most of the nineteenth century. "During the eighteenth, and still more in the first half of the nineteenth century-that epoch of unexampled industrial development-the industries of England present a picture of increasing production, rapid increase in the number of single undertakings and, in each separate area of production, the keenest competition between the various manufacturers only giving way occasionally and under pressure of certain exceptional conditions to a monopolist organization." (Levy, p. 169) 66 The classical economists' doctrine, that competition was equally to the interest of the consumer and of the producer, had been regarded as an invariable truth, not because it was in any way correct, but because for a long time it was borne out by existing facts. ." (Levy, p. 172) All authorities agree that an aggressive spirit of competition pervaded British industry during the middle of the nineteenth century. The repeal of the Corn Law in 

1846 assured cheap food for the workers. The abandonment of the Navigation Act assured relatively cheap ocean freights. The essential fact of being the first in the field of manufacturing by a wide margin, both in point of time and in costs, made free competition and the greatest possible freedom of trade eminently desirable to British manufacturers. "... as the subjective conditions of monopoly have obviously existed in spite of the doctrine of individualism,

4 we can only conclude that the external essentials of monopolist control were not as fully developed in England as in other countries, and that even at present they are in part lacking. ." (Levy, pp. 177-8)

These deficiencies in monopolistic foundations Levy attributes first of all to a poverty in scarce mineral resources. 

He points out that when English coal, tin, and copper were in a position to benefit from monopolistic forms of organization these industries were monopolistically organized. 

With respect to the fabricating industries, Levy points out that the establishment of free trade, i. e., the disappearance of a protective tariff and of import prohibitions was a sufficient explanation of the non-existence of cartels and trusts in a large section of British industry. To the extent that the purpose of combination is to raise prices there is no advantage to be derived from combination if an increase of the price over prevailing levels will bring in additional imports. In other words, where the costs of 

British industry were so high that imports were competitive, only cost-reducing combinations and mergers were possible.

The deterrent effects of a tariff with respect to industrial combination are easy to over-rate. A tariff is merely the equivalent of an exceptionally high freight rate. In many cases, where English costs were sufficiently low, actual freights were sufficient to protect English producers from competing imports. Wherever the gap between English costs and delivered import prices was wide enough, there was an opportunity within that gap for industrial combination aimed at raising prices. 

The fact that British industry developed competitively during most of the nineteenth century was partly due to

England's leadership in the industrial revolution. At that time English costs of industrial production were the lowest in the world. Once, however, a large number of firms came into being in each major industry, their very existence became a deterrent to combinations. The goodwill of each one of them was a valuable asset which it was afraid to lose in a merger. Cartel agreements, on the other hand, are notoriously more difficult to negotiate among specialized competitors, each one of whom claims to have specialties slightly different from those of the others, · than they are among producers of homogeneous staples. Lastly, owing in part to free trade and to the resultant lesser need for vertical combination, the size of undertakings in England has been traditionally smaller than in Germany or the United States.

In a country where protective duties and monopolies of raw goods hinder the manufacturer of finished goods from obtaining supplies of materials, and where at the same time it is impossible for the finishing manufacturer to make the consumer pay directly for the monopolist higher price of material, an intimate connection with raw production and half manufacture is unavoidable. ." (Levy, pp. 200-201) In England the exact opposite was the case. 

Even so, by the late nineteenth century the combination movement progressed considerably in England and was highly successful in a number of industries. Robert Liefmann in a study made in 1897 (Die Unternehmerverbände) listed forty international cartels in which German companies participated, twenty-two of which were formed in combination with British enterprises. Moreover, as the competitive advantages of large size became more apparent, and especially as the financial success of monopolies resulting from the combination of a few powerful firms became

obvious, a large number of huge industrial mergers took place. The control of the market exercised by the merged enterprises was further strengthened in many cases by cartel agreements with the companies that remained outside the merger.

Levy discusses fifteen major English cartels and trusts existing in 1911 (Portland cement, steel, industrial alcohol, wall paper, cables, salt, fine cotton spinners, sewing cotton, bleaching and dyeing, calico printing, locomotives, whiskey, rails, soda, and tobacco). His survey did not aim in any way at comprehensiveness, and only included the largest and most important economic concentrations of which he had knowledge.

It is worth noting that even in that most competitive and subsequently sickest of British industries, coal mining, a national monopoly was proposed as early as 1893. This was Sir George Eliot's proposal, discussed in Clapham,

Volume III, p. 220. It was followed in 1896 by D. A. 

Thomas's remarkable proposal to cartelize the coal industry. D. A. Thomas, later Lord Rhondda, was a member of 

Parliament and a Welsh coal mine owner. He published in Cardiff in 1896 "Some Notes on the Present Situation of the Coal Trade with a Plan for the Prevention of Undue Competition and for Maintaining Prices at a Remunerative Level." (Clapham, Volume III, p. 271) World famous instances of the evolution of concentration can be found in banking (cf. W. F. Crick and J. E. 

Wadsworth: "One Hundred Years of Joint Stock Bank ing", London, 1936; J. Sykes: "The Amalgamation Movement in British Banking, 1825-1924", London, 1926), in soap manufacture (Lever Bros.—see Clapham, Volume III, passim), petroleum refining and distribution (Royal Dutch-

Shell-Mexican Eagle, see Clapham, Volume III, pp. 275-6).

The cartel movement penetrated many minor industries as well, and to such an extent that Clapham, looking back in 1938, could write (Volume III, p. 316): "The trade without an association of some kind was by 1914 rare." 

2. The First World War and Its Aftermath 

The basic source of information regarding industrial combination in England immediately after the First World War is the "Report of the Committee on Trusts", issued in 1919. The Committee was appointed in February 1918 by the Ministry of Reconstruction. After a year's study it published a report which is fundamental to the knowledge and understanding of the effects of the First World War on the industrial structure of Great Britain. The following quotations are from its report. (Cd. 9236, H. M. 

Stationery Office, 1919) "We find that there is at the present time in every important branch of industry in the United Kingdom an increasing tendency to the formation of Trade Associations and Combinations, having for their purpose the restriction of competition and the control of prices. "Many of the organisations which have been brought to our notice have been created in the last few years, and by far the greater part of them appear to have come into existence since the end of the nineteenth century. For reasons which we shall presently discuss, there has been a great increase in the creation of trade associations during the period of the war." (p. 2) "Many of these Associations appear to have been created as a result of a temporary depression in their trades. 

One witness stated as his opinion that it was 'seldom that

an association came into being until a trade was faced with all-round disaster if it did not combine.' "There was considerable evidence before us that the growth and power of these Associations has been greatly strengthened during the period of the war, and that this result appears to have come about primarily from the novel circumstances of war, under which the Government, acting through the Ministry of Munitions or other Departments, found it necessary sometimes to consult the most informed opinion in a trade, and sometimes to ration material through an organisation representative of the trade. Unassociated firms which found themselves not consulted by the Government were thus led to join existing Associations, or in some cases to form representative groups for the purpose of advising the Government on matters concerning the trade. "There appears to be no doubt that a large number of definite Trade Associations have been formed during the war which may certainly be expected to control and regulate prices and the conditions of industry in the same manner as the pre-war Associations were already doing, and it is also probable that the habit of co-operation and discussion on matters of common concern, which has been the result of the formation of purely advisory groups of manufacturers or distributors for the purposes of the war will lead to a transformation of at least a part of such advisory groups into definite Trade Associations as soon as Government control over trade or industry is withdrawn. The advisory committees will disappear, but will reappear in the form of new Trade Associations." (p. 4) 

But we are satisfied that Trade Associations and Combines are rapidly increasing in this country, and may within no distant period exercise a paramount control over all important branches of the British trade." (p. 11)

The Report of the Committee as well as its recommendations were unanimous. The four "liberal" members of the Committee, Mr. Ernest Bevin, Mr. J. A. Hobson, Mr.

W. H. Watkins, and Mr. Sidney Webb wrote an Addendum, which begins as follows: "We have signed the above Report because we find nothing to disagree with in its recommendations; but we feel that it does not adequately express the gravity of the situation, ." (p. 13)

It continues in part as follows: "The fact is that Free 

Competition no longer governs the business world. (p. 13) "We do not suggest that any action should be taken to prevent or obstruct combination or association in capitalist enterprise. Apart from the experience that no such interference can be made effective, we have to recognize that association and combination in production and distribution are steps in the greater efficiency, the increased economy, and the better organization of industry. We regard this evolution as both inevitable and desirable. It is, however, plain that the change from competitive rivalry to combination calls for corresponding developments to secure for the community, both safeguards against the evils of monopoly, and at least a large share of the economic benefits of the better organization of industry which it promotes." (p. 13)

The Secretary of the Committee was Mr. John Hilton who submitted "A Study of Trade Organisations and Combinations in the United Kingdom" (pp. 15-30) which was also attached to the Report. This study is more analytical than descriptive, but a brief section is devoted to the extent

and significance of combinations. The following passages are derived from this section: Associations concerned with the regulation of price or output, or both, are to be found in almost every branch of British industry. Their number cannot be computed, for many are not registered either as companies or trade unions, and some are purposely carried on as secretly as possible. It may be taken, however, that there are considerably more than five hundred associations, all exerting a substantial influence on the course of industry and price, in being at the present time in the United Kingdom. The most painstaking enquiry yet undertaken into combination in any section of British industry is that made in respect of building materials, and the conclusion there reached was that 25 per cent. of the materials that go to the building of an average house are subject to full control and 33 per cent. are partially controlled. If particular industries in which combination has made most headway be taken it transpires that in innumerable lines of manufacture anything from 80 to 100 per cent. of the whole national output of the articles concerned is either in the hands of one dominant consolidation or of manufacturers grouped together for purposes of concerted price and other control in a trade association. It would, however, be fallacious to take these latter as typical of the whole range of industry. There are many industries, trades, and services, great as well as small, in which combination has made hardly any headway, and competition is still the determining factor in the fixing of price. 

The shipbuilding industry may be quoted as a case in point. " (p. 20) 

“Influence of War Conditions on Combination.—More over, there can be no question that the whole trend of industry and commerce is towards combination. The movement,

indeed, is inevitable, for what machinery and invention have done in the past for production, this disposition of smaller bodies to consolidate in larger units is doing for administration.' Within recent years the number of trade associations in this country has increased enormously, and, under the influence of war conditions, not only have numbers of existing listless bodies been galvanised into greater activity and enterprise, and the membership of others been increased, but many new associations have been formed, some at the instance of the Government Departments, since it was found easier to deal with a group than with separate firms. Still more, the necessity for fixing the prices of many articles and materials required by the State during the war and the need for rationing materials has compelled the Government to call representative conferences of manufacturers in many industries, and to impose on them duties which have entailed frequent consultations and meetings, and when people who, before the war, were competitors have been thus compelled to meet and discuss common arrangements, it is inevitable that they should discover the advantages of working in concert and establish relations which will make for concerted working for the future. Again, the war period has been marked by a remarkable increase in the number of amalgamations. One effect of the excess profits tax has been to encourage the buying up of unprofitable businesses by highly profitable concerns, for such purchases were in effect paid for by monies which would otherwise have gone in excess profits duty to the Revenue. 

Another war influence making for the extension of combination after the war is the enormous development of standardisation and repetition manufacture. The lessons learnt in this connection during the war will not be lost in the change back from war to peace production, but their application to peace production will necessitate collaboration among

the various firms composing the particular branch of industry, and collaboration for specific purposes prepares the ground for combination for the regulating of trade." (p. 20)

There are also attached to the Report "Notes as to the Law Relating to Combinations" by Sir John Macdonell (p. 

31); a "Report Presented to the Committee on Trusts on

Combinations in Building Material Trades" (p. 35); and a "Memorandum on Industrial Combinations" presented by 

Mr. Percy Ashley. (p. 38)

This last memorandum is largely factual. It mentions a very large number of existing combinations for the regulation of trade, and attempts a simple classification. 

3. The First Interwar Decade: 1920-1930 

Information with respect to privately sponsored mergers of large enterprises is plentiful in the files of periodicals like The Economist. Clapham, op. cit., in an Epilogue gives a brief summary and interpretation from the angle of the economic historian which is useful. The standard work on the subject is Patrick Fitzgerald's "Industrial Combination in England", London, 1927. A revised edition of Levy's book (1927) brought some of his material up to date. An increasing number of Parliamentary Committee reports on various industries, especially the important reports of the Committee on Industry and Trade, also known as the Balfour Committee, and legislative intervention in many instances, shed a great deal of additional light on the concentration movement. With respect to the coal and cotton industries in particular, Temporary National Economic Committee Monograph No. 40 provides easily accessible information.

The strong statements of the Royal Commission on Trusts indicate the progress of cartelization and trustification of England during the crucial decade of the First World War. The requirements of the war economy promoted the concentration of industry, for which conditions were propitious anyway. While after the war the purposes of the wartime combinations disappeared, the institutional changes remained. The big companies continued to grow bigger and the small companies continued to strive for agreements with competitors and for mergers in order not to go under in the competitive struggle. 

This latter factor became of considerable importance after 1921, when England failed to recover from the postwar slump with the rest of the world, and chronic unemployment appeared for the first time as an identified malady of modern industry.

It is only too often forgotten that the real purpose of many mergers and cartels is defensive. The defensive advantages of large size, and especially of diversified assets are, after all, obvious. Similarly, in times of stagnation, agreements to regulate competition are chiefly valuable as defensive measures.

The transitional difficulties of the post-war period provided a powerful incentive for defensive measures on the part of industry. Many mergers and cartels were aimed not so much at raising prices and gouging consumers in times of boom, as they were aimed at diversifying risks and/or controlling markets more successfully in times of depression. This was especially the case with many combinations in British industry, which was pervaded by a justified feeling that it was losing out in the international competitive race with the more modern industries of the

United States, Germany, Japan, and even of the smaller European industrial countries like Belgium, Sweden, and Switzerland.

Whatever the specific causes, the results were the same. 

A large further growth of concentration in British industry occurred during the 1920s. Many enormous mergers took place, e. g., in the steel industry and in chemicals. Sometimes these mergers were government-sponsored, e. g. in communications (Cables and Wireless, Ltd.) and in rail roads (pursuant to the Railway Act of 1921). Imperial 

Airways, Ltd., the airline monopoly, was also a governmentsponsored merger, pursuant to a Report of the Civil Air 

Transport Subsidies Committee of 1923.

The first government-sponsored cartel appeared in 1922, when the notorious Stevenson plan for the control of rubber production was put into operation. 

A brand new, subsidized beet sugar manufacturing industry was established in 1925 by special legislative enact ment (British Sugar Subsidy Act of 1925). 

By 1926 the Liberal Party was thoroughly alarmed with respect to its political future by the challenge to orthodox liberalism inherent in post-war developments. It appointed a committee to study the situation, including W. T. Layton, the editor of The Economist, Lloyd George, H. D. Henderson, Philip Kerr (subsequently Lord Lothian), J. M. It commands great attention, reflecting the ideas of leading English liberals and academicians as to how much of the liberal ideology of the nineteenth century could be salvaged. By the end of the decade British industry was among the leaders of the world-wide concentration movement. It

exercised a tight control over its domestic and imperial markets.

In addition to the growing complexity of industrial production, marketing, and trade, the tariff protection of key industries, which was enacted after the World War, considerably assisted trustification and cartelization. 

The tariff protection of industries had begun with the McKenna Duties of 1915-33%% on motor cars, cycles, clocks, watches, musical instruments, and a flat rate on films. The Key Industries Duties of 1921 applied the same rate of 33%% to many instruments and chemicals, and 50% to optical instruments and magnetos. These were the first breaches in the free trade system of England, where the only duties previously in force were a few levies raised for revenue (e. g. on tobacco). The profit possibilities to be derived from economic concentration were enhanced by the tariff which raised the delivered price of potentially competitive imports.

The highly tolerant view British legislation and courts took with respect to restraints of trade, the narrowness of their interpretation of what was an unreasonable restraint, and their refusal to consider any but the most specific evidence, contributed to the development of highly restrictive practices throughout British industry. This meant that what would be considered competitive behavior in the United States was at a discount and would have tended to provoke violent retaliation by an entire industry against the aggressor enterprise. A tacit code of non-competitive industrial discipline was built up which was and is of the greatest effectiveness.

4. Economic Concentration in the Thirties 

The source material regarding cartelization and the concentration movement in general becomes very abundant in this period. In addition to voluminous information in economic and financial periodicals and some books previously mentioned, like Hermann Levy's "The New Industrial 

System", London, 1936, and the Temporary National Economic Committee Monograph No. 40, there is especially abundant material on international cartels organized by British producers, frequently under British Government supervision. (See Alfred Plummer: "International Combines in Modern Industry", second revised edition, London, 1938; William Oualid: "International Raw Materials Car tels", International Institute of Intellectual Cooperation, League of Nations, Paris, 1938; Erwin Hexner: "The International Steel Cartel', Chapel Hill, 1943; D. L. Burn: "The

Economic History of Steelmaking 1867-1939", Cambridge 

University Press, 1940; W. Y. Elliott and others: "International Control in the Non-Ferrous Metals", New York, 1937; Ballande: "Les Ententes Economiques Internatio nales", Paris, 1937; "Intergovernmental Commodity Control Agreements", International Labour Office, Montreal, 1943.)

Parliamentary papers regarding individual industries are also increasing in numbers and importance. Noteworthy among them are the report of the so-called May Committee on the steel industry, and its sequels, including a "Report of the Import Duties Advisory Committee on the Present 

Position and Future Development of the Iron and Steel 

Industry" (Cmd. 5507, 1937), the "Memorandum on Clause 

6 of the Finance Bill, 1936" (Cmd. 5201, 1936) (concerning the steel tariff), &c. Relevant legislation includes the Cot ton Spinning Industry Act of 1936, the Cotton Industry Re-

organization Act of 1939, the Coal Act of 1938, the Sugar Industry Reorganization Act of 1936, and numerous other enactments. 

Regarding government sponsored international cartels in which British producers participated, data can also be found in British Government papers dealing with the respective agreements. In addition to the familiar cases of rubber, tin, tea, &c., the international regulation of whaling (Cmd. 5757 and 5827, 1938) deserves mention as a new type of production control.

Economic concentration developed during the 1920s largely in a haphazard manner. It developed as and when conditions permitted. During the 1930s two major changes occurred, both of which propelled industry further in the direction of concentration and coordination.

First was the adoption of protectionist foreign trade policies. England adopted a full-fledged protectionist tariff in 1932. At the Ottawa Conference in the same year, moreover, a system of Imperial preference was established which went far to guarantee the Imperial market for British manufactured products. Having become a protectionist country, England enabled a number of her industries, in which previously the absence of a tariff enforced some measure of competition, to reduce their competitive activities.

The tariff rates imposed in 1932 were: a blanket 10% ad valorem on imports, excepting raw materials on the free list, and manufactures. The latter were mostly taxed at 

20%, furs and jewelry at 30%, iron and steel products at 

333%. An Import Duties Advisory Committee was also formed to advise the Treasury on tariff questions. The 

Committee interpreted its task as essentially protective and

has advised the Treasury accordingly in most cases. The 

Treasury has tended to follow the advice it received. 

The second major development was the gradual realization that cartels, trusts, and other forms of industrial agreements and mergers are helpful tools of national policy. 

Compulsory cartelization was attempted in cotton spinning, coal, and steel, among others. The British Government took an active interest in the international steel cartel negotiations, it made tariff and quota protection of the industry conditional on rationalization and reorganization, it backed the numerous international commodity agreements that involved British Empire producers. 

The British Iron and Steel Federation was first organized under a slightly different name in 1934. The full history of the cartelization of the steel industry is so well written up in the literature that it is probably not worth reviewing here in detail. (Hexner, op. cit., Burn, op. cit.)

The Sugar Industry Reorganization Act of 1936 enacted a compulsory merger of all beet sugar manufacturing companies in the British Sugar Corporation, Ltd., which was to be privately owned and was allowed a maximum 7% dividend on its common stock. It was also during this period that the Federation of British Industries became a major power in Great Britain.

The Federation of British Industries published an undated pamphlet entitled "Industry and Action", quoted in 

Robert A. Brady: "Business as a System of Power", Columbia University Press, 1943 (p. 177), in which the Federation boasted of its influence on British fiscal policy and commercial policy. The influence of the Federation on import duties, and its success as a channel of consultation with the government in commercial treaty negotiations, are particularly emphasized.

The extensive business affiliations of Conservative members of Parliament and the resultant possibilities of combination to exclude imports and to promote cartelization are emphasized by Brady and also by Simon Haxey's "Tory M. P.", London, 1939. The latter showed, among other evidence, that the 181 Conservative members of Parliament who held company directorships held no less than 775 such directorships. This did not include directorships previously held by members of Cabinet, who, according to British custom, resigned all corporate affiliations when they entered the Cabinet. The book also brought out the fact that the vice president of the Federation of British Industries, Sir Patrick Hannon, was also a member of Parliament. 

With the German example before their eyes, British

Government officials and corporation executives became increasingly aware of the advantages to the execution of national policies that could be derived from well-functioning industrial organization.

Advantages and disadvantages in international trade have an exceptionally voluminous literature in England going back to the early seventeenth century. The economic literature of no country pays as much attention as does the British to the "terms of trade", i. e., to the relationship of export prices to import prices. Methods of making the terms of trade favorable to England and to the colonies received a great deal of study. The advantages of selling at monopoly prices and buying at competitive prices did not go unobserved.

They saw that monopolistic agreements among raw materials producers were a very effective way to increase the total proceeds of exports, and the total revenues accruing therefrom both to the producing interests and to the na-

tional economy as a whole. These considerations were powerful in the formation of raw materials cartels throughout the British Empire, such as the rubber cartel, the tin cartel, and numerous other agreements among British 

Empire producers.

They also became aware of the advantages of regulating trade and payments relations between England and foreign nations on a comprehensive basis, in which regulations organized trade played a considerable part. 

Among the first of such English payments agreements was the one negotiated with Argentina in 1933, the so-called

5. The Future 

While it is obviously dangerous to make prophecies, current indications are unanimously in the direction of

cartelization in the post-war organization of British industry. 

Attorney General Biddle is reported by the Associated

"The countries who undoubtedly are behind the cartel movement are England and Belgium and most of the other European nations, but primarily England because England is in a position where she is afraid of American competition." (Wall Street Journal, January 25, 1945) 

An interesting article by Mr. Geoffrey Crowther, editor of The Economist, in the latest issue of the Yale Review, Winter 1945, points in a similar direction. The 

British delegation to the International Chamber of Commerce Conference at Rye, New York, in November 1944 clearly indicated that this was its position. As early as 

December 1942 the Commission to study the Organization of Peace (8 West Fortieth Street, New York) published a study of "British Business Associations on Post-war Reconstruction" which even then strongly indicated this much. 

British Government action has hitherto not given any signs of reversing the previous trend towards government-encouraged cartelization of private industry. Indeed, all its actions towards post-war planning and reconversion planning indicate that it expects to pursue a vigorous policy of intervention in economic affairs. Integration of industries in cartel forms is an inevitable concomitant to such a policy, short of outright nationalization.

In other words, tight organization of British industry, which has been forged for purposes of the war economy, is not going to be abandoned in favor of a freely competitive

system after the war. On the contrary, it seems that the 

British Government will take an active part in the leadership of the industrial combination movement. The policy will be one of active government participation in business, including participation in and cooperation with industrial combinations, rather than one of checking and controlling them from the outside. In this respect the antithesis drawn by Crowther in his recent article is revealing. The real purport of the article is that complete socialization of specified activities, and governmental responsibility for them, are preferable to government interference by bureaucratic control from the outside. 66 There are two ways of combining governmental direction with individual enterprise; the marriage may be happy or unhappy. The unhappy alternative is the negative method of 'control'. This is the infertile compromise to which countries are driven that recognize the necessity for some intervention by the government and yet shy away from the brutal logic-and the difficult political problemsof socialism. It is the infertile compromise to which both the American and British democracies have had increasing recourse since the First World War-that of leaving the business man with all the initiative and responsibility, with the obligation of covering his costs and satisfying his creditors, but burdening him with any number of prohibitions, restrictions, and controls. This is the bureaucrat's paradise. He carries no real responsibility; he has no pay roll to meet and no shareholders to confront. But his permission has to be sought, his rules have to be observed, his daylong questionnaires have to be filled out. It is a negative system; for while both the business man and the bureaucrat have to be in agreement before a new enterprise can be inaugurated, either one can prevent it.

"The second alternative method of combining government and business is the positive one. Its weapon is organization rather than control. Where public policy requires the intervention of the government in some economic situation, this method would have the government itself assume the initiative and the responsibility, instead of attempting to control the actions of others without assuming their burdens. II. The Organization of German Industry 1. Before 1914 

The literature relating to the organization of individual German industries is enormous and even the literature relating to the concentration movement in German industry is vast. The bulk of this output is, however, largely hidden in German scientific periodicals and doctoral dissertations, in the writings of the so-called historical school of German economists headed by Professor Schmoller of the University of Berlin, and in the works of economic historians like Werner Sombart. The most important official publication relating to the early period of cartelization is the Bericht Ueber Kartellwesen published in Berlin in 1905. Among the more accessible sources are Francis Walker: "Monopolistic Combinations in the German Coal Industry", Ameri can Economic Association, 1904; W. F. Bruck: "Social and Economic History of Germany from William II to 

Hitler, 1888-1938", Oxford University Press, 1938; Alfred 

Marshall: "Industry and Trade", Cambridge University 

Press, 1919; Hermann Levy: "Industrial Germany", Cambridge University Press, 1935; Robert Liefmann: "Cartels, Concerns and Trusts", New York, 1932; as well as English and American literature discussing the history of international cartels.

The economic history of German monopolies goes back to privileges granted in the eighteenth century. These monopolies granted by edict disappeared in the course of the nineteenth century, but there does not appear to have existed great popular hostility against them. 

The modern industrial development of Germany began with the wars waged for the unification of the Reich (1864, 1866, 1870-71), much as the modern industrial development of the United States dates from the Civil War. The new industries of Germany grew from 1870 to 1914 at a pace that was second only to the growth of industry in the United States. These industries developed in the form of largescale enterprise, the largest outside the United States. 

The occasional cyclical interruptions to this rapid growth promoted further mergers and cartelization in the industries affected. German industry became a leader of the movement towards industrial concentration. This movement was not wholly unopposed, but the opposition was on the whole quite ineffective. Even Socialist opposition was largely restricted to the capitalistic features of concentration rather than aimed at the idea of concentration itself.

The technological advantages of large-scale organization were clearly realized and well appreciated in a nation of technicians, such as the Germans increasingly became. 

Moreover, there being no well-entrenched ideological free competition tradition in the many small German states that eventually made up the Empire, this concentration movement had no major political obstacles to overcome. In fact, from the viewpoint of national policy industrial concentration was something of an advantage. It permitted a stabler, more even, and more calculable rate of growth at a time

when the politically controlling elements of the Reich were intensely preoccupied with the political and military power position of the country. 

Supervision of mergers and cartels was spasmodic and largely unofficial, though a Parliamentary commission conducted a fairly extensive investigation into the cartelization of industry in 1903-04. 

2. War and Inflation: 1914-1923 The frightful economic strains of the First World War accelerated the concentration movement in German industry. They resulted, under the direction of Walter Rathenau, then Minister of Economics, in the first comprehensive plan for the direction of an economy for the purposes of total war. (See H. Kessler: "Walter Rathenau", London, 1929, Chapters 8 and 9.) This integration of the German economy for war purposes was, however, rapidly forgotten as soon as the war was over. 

The first socialist government of the Weimar Republic had ambitious plans for the socialization of the economy. 

These found their most interesting expression in the work of W. von Moellendorff, the Permanent Secretary of State in the Ministry of Economics (see Bruck, op. cit., pages 

157-162). He is considered the wartime originator of the concept of a planned economy. His plans were unanimously rejected by the Cabinet in 1919 because they were considered too bold, but his spiritual influence remained considerable and had an effect on subsequent developments in Germany.

Problems of industrial organization were very acute during the four years of post-war inflation. These years were, however, a period of such economic turmoil that their

influence on subsequent development was more of a deterrent than otherwise. Many huge mergers took place during this time, often on a purely speculative basis, and most of them had to be undone or recapitalized after the stabilization of the currency.

Inflationary conditions had a disruptive effect on existing cartel agreements. It was patently impossible to exercise rational price and sales control in times of wild currency inflation and material shortages. On the whole, few lessons of any significance could be derived from a detailed account of the futile attempts that were made to organize German production and marketing during inflation. 

3. The Liberal Regulation of Cartels: 1923-1933 The literature of German cartelization during the 1920s is extremely voluminous. In addition to the general sources previously listed (Liefmann, op. cit., Bruck, op. cit., Hexner, op. cit., Hermann Levy, op. cit.) periodicals like the Kartell

Rundschau and the Weltwirtschaftliches Archiv cover in numerable phases of the evolution of cartelization. The 

League of Nations published an important set of relevant documents partly in preparation for and partly as a sequel to the World Economic Conference in 1927, which contain a substantial amount of information on German cartelization. An impressive amount of material specifically on cartels was published by the German Parliamentary Commission delegated to study the cartel question, the so-called Enquete-Ausschuss. This series of documents is briefly reviewed in Temporary National Economic Committee Monograph No. 40. H. Wagenfuhr: "Kartelle in Deutsch land", Nurnberg, 1931, is in many respects a useful compilation. See also Arnold Wolfers: "Das Kartellproblem im 

Lichte der deutschen Kartell-Literatur", Munich, 1930.

The liberal governments of the post-war era recognized the national problem that lay hidden in the cartel system. 

The first Kartellverordnung was issued in 1923. It gave cartels a well-defined legal status and placed them under government supervision. The text of this cartel ordinance is reprinted in Robert Liefmann, op. cit., page 351 and subs. and discussed ibid. page 168.

however, well known that in all major German industries extensive agreements existed, regulating competitive practices, allocating outputs and markets. 

German thinking viewed agreements among independent competitors as a phenomenon distinct from the general movement towards industrial concentration. This was reflected primarily in a state-as-policeman attitude, leading to solutions of the cartel problem by purely formal tests applied in judicial and administrative regulation.

After 1923 there were two factors that acted powerfully in the direction of increasing the economic concentration of German industry. One was the need for new capital for industrial reconstruction and modernization. The other was the disruption of the structure of both production and marketing that resulted from the First World War.

With respect to the former, the shattered condition in which German industry emerged from the First World War was the major factor. Machinery was worn out, repairs had been neglected for years, and the financial position of most enterprises was unsound, owing to the rapid progress of inflation. Inflation itself and the ability to finance plant expansion with paper money promoted the process of physical reconstruction up to a point. It stimulated, however, an unplanned, widely competitive overbuilding of industrial plant, regardless of its post-inflation costs of operation and future uses, merely as a matter of flight from the depreciating currency. 

Moreover, when stabilization came, the working capital position of the whole German economy was found to be woefully inadequate. Both cash and inventories, especially inventories of imported raw materials, had been squandered

during the war and inflation. Large new liquid capital resources had to be found. Especially in view of the heavy reparations burdens imposed on the German economy it was essential to attract new foreign capital investment. Such foreign capital was, however, only obtainable on wellfounded prospects of profits and would not have been available to industries in which conditions of cut-throat competition prevailed. In the absence of legal obstacles to mergers and cartel agreements, these financial requirements were alone sufficient to give powerful impetus for a tight organization of German industry.

Added to this there was a second, and, in the long run, probably even more important incentive-the disruption of productive and marketing processes due to the loss of the First World War. 

For instance, the Ruhr steel industry relied heavily on Lorraine iron ore and shipped coal into the Lorraine in return. This two-way traffic was interrupted by the return of the Lorraine to France (see Weltwirtschaftliches Archiv, April 1927). A somewhat similar cleavage occurred in the

Silesian coal and steel industry, which was partly given to Poland. Lastly, the breakup of the Hapsburg Empire created six small nations where there had been one large economic area before. This broke up trade interchange into completely unfamiliar and in many respects unworkable patterns. 

International cartelization was thought to be the one effective solution of the problems so created. French economic interests were among the most powerful promoters of international cartelization in the interests of post-war international economic collaboration and a return to stable conditions required for a durable peace.

A conference to discuss problems of the post-war economic organization of Europe is believed to have been first proposed by Mr. Jouhaux, the French labor leader, in 1924. 

Mr. Louis Loucheur, a leading French industrialist and a 

Cabinet member of several French governments, proposed such a conference officially at the League of Nations Assembly of September 1925. He more than anybody else was responsible for the eventual convocation of the World Economic Conference held by the League of Nations in 1927. 

One of the objectives of this Conference was to attempt to create order out of chaos, to attempt systematic economic reconstruction by the coordination of European industries and the rational organization of their mutual trade. 

Even before the World Economic Conference French and German steel makers were able to conclude agreements. (See Hexner, op. cit., p. 220.) A large number of cartels sprang up also among the industries of the successor states, as well as between the successor state industries and German industries.

In July 1930 the government issued a second executive order to regulate cartels, which revised and strengthened the ordinance of 1923 in the light of subsequent experience. 

The network of national and international cartels that was created during the 1920s was subjected to violent strain after Germany's foreign exchange difficulties in 1931, which will be discussed in a separate section below. Since, however, the central position of the German economy on the Continent of Europe remained unaffected, all that was necessary, and indeed possible, was to readjust the cartel agreements to the changed conditions. These readjustments, however, did not occur under the old Kartellverordnung any more. The depression washed away the founda-

tions of the liberal capitalistic reconstruction of Europe that was attempted during the twenties. 

4. After 1933: Compulsory Cartelization 

The source material with regard to the Nazi reorganization of the German economy is described in Fritz Weike's "Der Aufbau der Gewerblichen Wirtschaft", Berlin, 1935, which prints the texts of the relevant laws and regulations to the end of 1935. Other sources are Hans E. Priester: "Das Deutsche Wirtschaftswunder", Amsterdam, 1936; the "Jahrbuch der Nationalsozialistischen Wirtschaft", Munich, 1937; Fritz Haussmann: "Konzerne und Kartelle im Zeichen der Wirtschaftslenkung'", Zurich-Leipzig, 1938; and "Die wirtschaftliche Konzentration an ihrer 

Schicksalswende", Basel, 1940.

Much of the voluminous American literature on German industrial organization under the Nazis tends to project backwards the phenomena of Nazification. This obscures the revolutionary character of the Nazi movement and the revolutionary nature of the changes it has caused in German economic organization.

The advent of the Nazis to power created a radically new situation. They first resorted to deficit spending, on a scale never previously attempted in peacetime, for the purpose of reemployment. Presently they converted their reemployment expenditures into rearmament expenditures, and proceeded to integrate the whole German economy in the service of this rearmament effort. 

On July 15, 1933, they issued a new cartel decree establishing the right of the Reich to compel cartelization wherever the authorities considered it desirable. By their law for the reorganization of the German economy, issued in

February 1934, they gave themselves authority for the most far-reaching economic reorganization, which authority they then proceeded to use to the full. 

In November, 1934, a new cartel order reorganizing the whole German economy was issued. It compulsorily cartelized the whole of German industry, both horizontally and vertically. An industrial supercartel was created, called the Reichsgruppe Industrie, and a whole system of public cartels was fitted into it; the previously existing private cartels had to fit into this system as best they could. The approach of the Nazis towards industry was radically different from that of the liberal Republic which merely concerned itself with the question of how to police cartels. 

From 1934 on the German Government used German industry and German cartels as instruments of national policy, frequently against the wishes of the industries and industrialists involved, and thus created patterns of economic control that had previously been considered only permissible or even possible in wartime.

In the international sphere this use of German industry for the promotion of what the Nazi Government considered to be Germany's national interest was further complicated by foreign exchange policies also aimed at the promotion of these genuine or supposed national interests of Germany.

The actual evolution of German foreign exchange and trade controls will be discussed below. Suffice it here to say that international trade by German industry was subjected to regulation not only from the viewpoint of overall national policy, but also from the specific angle of providing the national government with foreign exchange resources of the quantity and composition that they thought

they required. As often as not, international cartel agreements of German industry had to be revised to meet government requirements, regardless whether the German companies affected and their managements considered these modifications to be in their or their associates' interests. 

Since about 1937 German industry has been wholly dominated by military requirements. What the post war position of German industry will be, nobody can say; but it is self-evident that a freely competitive industrial structure cannot exist in Germany for decades to come, if ever.

The beginnings of what later became the I. G. Farbenindustrie A. G. go back to cartel agreements between large chemical companies shortly after the turn of the century. 

The original agreements preserved the independence of each constituent company, but contained profit-sharing provisions at least in the case of the 1904 agreement between the Hoechst and the Casella company, and probably in others. (Haussmann, 1938, p. 40) A tighter integration of German chemical enterprises was undertaken in 1916, under the necessities of the First World War; the "community of interest agreement" determined the key according to which the profits of all members were to be distributed. 

After 1928 the merged enterprise had a total capitalization in excess of R. M. 1 billion, i. e., over $250 million at the then rates of exchange ($420 million after the devaluation of the dollar).

From the viewpoint of the technical excellence of its management and research operations it was the leading chemical enterprise of the world. The two most spectacular discoveries in the industrial chemistry of the present century, namely, synthetic nitrogen and the hydrogenation process, were both developed in the laboratories of I. G. Farben.

The technological excellence of the research work of I. G. Farben made it a most formidable competitor. Its widespread and well-entrenched sales organization and its comprehensive cartel connections within Germany and throughout Europe gave it an extraordinarily high degree of control over its European markets. It had excellent political connections with the Weimar Republic as long as the Republic lasted; and after the Nazis came into power, it was coordinated with the Nazi system of industrial organization, which meant that it was converted into a vehicle for German power politics. These three factors

made I. G. especially formidable as a competitor to manufacturers of new products seeking markets. Conversely, these same factors of strength in I. G. Farben made it a very desirable ally with respect to developing new products and opening up new markets. Many foreign companies entered into agreements with I. G. Farben and some even established jointly owned production facilities in Germany during the liberal era of the Weimar Republic, when Germany joined the League of Nations and cooperation between Germany and the Western Powers was at its height. (For a list of I. G.'s agreements, see Report of the Kilgore Committee, Part II, pp. 3-35.)

I. G. Farben was not a Nazi company prior to 1933. 

The most authoritative historian of Nazism prior to 1932, Mr. Konrad Heiden ("Geschichte des Nationalsozialismus; 

die Karriere einer Idee", Rowohlt, Berlin, 1932), who investigated very carefully the origins of the movement, its sources of funds, and the hidden support it was receiving from various sources, never suggested that I. G. Farben was in any way involved in the Nazi movement. Evidence is, on the other hand, available that I. G. Farben was to some extent involved in politics on the liberal side. It has been said that it subsidized the leading liberal daily of Western Germany, the Frankfurter Zeitung. It was the leading German company in the camp that wanted collaboration with the Western Powers. It even attempted to internationalize its operations and ownership as much as possible. Obviously, after 1933 it was "coordinated" to Nazi aims and methods, like everything and everybody else in Germany.

By contrast, the German heavy industries were undeniably engaged on the Nationalist side in politics. Whether and how far back they subsidized the Nazi movement is

difficult to determine. Konrad Heiden shows that prior to 1930 such help was intermittent; but there is no doubt that the leadership of the steel industry was sympathetic to all nationalistic political movements and often gave such movements financial support. 

The economic logic of the contrasting political attitude of I. G. Farben and the heavy industries under the 

Republic was fairly obvious. The steel industry by and large only had debts abroad and was no doubt delighted at prospects of selling bigger armaments to a nationalistic 

German Government. I. G. Farbenindustrie, on the other hand, had assets and markets all over the world, including extremely valuable patents. Its highly sophisticated and experienced management could not have been blind to the jeopardy in which these assets would be placed by adventurous foreign policies. After all, its predecessor "community of interests" was the principal victim of the expropriation of German assets and German patents by 

Allied nations during the First World War. 

III. Foreign Exchange Problems 

The patterns of national income and expenditure for each country are set by natural resources, technology, previous economic development, and consuming habits. 

These patterns change very slowly even if the aggregate totals of national income and expenditure show fairly ample fluctuations from year to year.

Expenditure on imports is part of the national expenditure pattern, and as such it is in a very stable relationship to national income. It will rise and fall as national income rises and falls, though in most countries it will tend to go up and down faster. (See "Imports and Income in the United States and Canada" by Imrie de Vegh, Review

of Economic Statistics, Harvard University Press, August 

1941, p. 135, chart 3.) It takes major economic dislocations to affect this pattern. 

1. The Dollar Shortage 

Subsequent to the hectic years immediately following the First World War foreign nations were able to import vast quantities of materials for reconstruction and development purposes, largely financed by loans from the United States. The resultant business activity provided a powerful stimulus to world recovery during the years 1924-29.

Subsequent to 1929 there occurred a drastic reduction in the total supply of dollars available to foreigners. The reduction resulted from the cessation of foreign lending and from the drop in imports into the United States that was caused partly by the beginning of the depression, partly by the Hawley-Smoot Tariff.

Since dollars and sterling were the principal media for the financing of international trade, and sterling itself was endangered by the shortage of dollars that developed, the effects of this dollar shortage on international trade were catastrophic.

In the United States the quantity of imports continued to be closely related to industrial production. Their value declined faster than national income only because of the greater price decline of internationally traded commodities. The quantity of imports into foreign countries dropped disproportionately as compared to their industrial, production; and their imports from the United 

States dropped much faster than their total imports. In other words, under the impact of the world-wide dollar

shortage there was a drastic reduction in the ratio of world trade to world production, and a further reduction in the proportionate share of the United States in total international trade. (See "The United States in the World

Economy" by Hal B. Lary, United States Department of 

Commerce, Bureau of Foreign & Domestic Commerce, Economic Series No. 23, Washington, 1943. Chart 1, table 

1, and chart 2.)

The methods by which the use of dollars was restricted in foreign countries were numerous and sophisticated. 

Their primary objective in each case was to reduce total imports from the United States and to substitute for them either domestic production or at least imports from other sources. Helping to reduce both the dollar shortage and domestic unemployment in one fell swoop was usually the official policy. 

2. Sterling Exchange during the Depression

In the United Kingdom the abandonment of the gold standard in September, 1931, immediately made goods from the United States and Germany 33% more expensive and eventually 50% more expensive than they had been prior to the devaluation of the pound. This situation was rectified by the devaluation of the dollar in 1934, but in the meantime, in 1932, Great Britain established a new protective tariff and Imperial preference; she progressively strengthened her economy on bilateral and exclusive patterns, which tended to discriminate against American goods. "Buy British", by no means a new slogan, received added popularity. The Federation of British Industries and lesser trade associations also made determined efforts to organize British markets so as to present maximum resistance to foreign merchandise. Renewed impetus was

( given to cartelization among the exporting industries and raw material producers of the British Empire-whose best customer was the United States-so as to maximize the dollar proceeds of export sales by a monopolistic price policy. 

3. German Exchange Problems of the Depression

In Germany the situation in 1930-31 was similar to the English situation, only much worse. The collapse of the 

German banking system in the spring of 1931 was primarily the consequence of excessive short-term borrowing in London and New York, and to the freezing of the funds so borrowed in loans that became uncollectible after the onset of the world depression.

On July 15, 1931, after all the banks had been closed, a presidential emergency decree was issued regulating commerce in foreign exchange.

The basic principle of the allocation of import exchange was that every enterprise in Germany was entitled to spend on imports an amount of foreign exchange equal to its average monthly foreign exchange expenditures on imports during the period July 1, 1930, through June 30, 1931.

This allocation of import exchange was already far below pre-depression levels, but still very liberal as compared to subsequent restrictions. The shortage of exchange of the German authorities became so serious after the depreciation of the pound sterling in September 1931 that in May 1932 this allocation of import exchange had to be reduced to 50% of the base period maximum. 

The depreciation of the dollar in 1933 caused major additional difficulties to the German export trades and thus

reduced further the supply of foreign exchange. Moreover, the Nazi government was intent to build up the war potential of the German economy; the supply of foreign exchange began increasingly to be diverted to the requirements of defense. As a result, beginning in February, 1934, drastic measures became necessary for the further curtailment of ordinary commercial imports. The import exchange quotas to which enterprises were entitled without special license were in very short order reduced from the previous 50% to 5% of the base period level; many imports were altogether prohibited except under license, and foreign trade became partly a tool, partly a weapon, of economic warfare. (See "Die Massnahmen Deutschlands zum Ausgleich der Zahlungsbilanz, mit besonderer Beruecksichtigung des Neuen Plans," by Gaston Adolf von Bleichert, Berner 

Wirtschaftswissenschaftliche Abhandlungen, Vol. 27, Bern, 1940.) 

4. Developments After the Devaluation of the Dollar 

The dollar shortage ceased to be acute in England after 1934. The combined effect of the protective tariff, Imperial preference, and the raising of the price of gold in the United States (which accrued largely to the benefit of the gold producers of the British Empire) was to ease considerably the foreign exchange situation. Sterling once more became a reasonably safe currency. By then, however, the damage was done. While imports from the United 

States revived, they never attained previous totals. After 

1937 new pressures developed on the dollar resources of England, partly as a result of the renewed slump in the United States, which cut down all our imports, and partly owing to rearmament needs.

On the Continent of Europe and in the non-British world generally the dollar shortage remained acute throughout the nineteen thirties. In the ultimate analysis this dollar shortage was responsible for the abandonment of the gold standard by the three "rich" countries, Holland, Switzerland, and France in 1936, as well as for the maintenance, and (after 1937) renewed intensification of exchange restrictions, in all the "poor" countries. It was this dollar shortage that kept the share of the United States in total world trade lower than it had been in the previous decade. 

After a temporary alleviation of the dollar shortage in 

1936-37 the slump at the end of 1937 once more intensified the worldwide dollar shortage, when solely due to a drop in our imports we rapidly changed from a small import surplus to a billion dollar a year export surplus. 

During the thirties the armaments race became an increasingly important factor in confining more and more to products of military necessity the imports to the Continent of Europe from the United States. The renewed dollar shortage in 1938 further increased the need to restrict the utilization of dollars to the purchase of military necessities.

The import licensing systems and foreign exchange control systems that were in effect in most continental countries over most of the period strongly militated against American exports of products that were directly competitive with domestic products in those countries. Full employment policies as well as foreign exchange considerations both led to import restrictions. Even less good or more expensive domestic products were preferable as long as they did not require dollar exchange, and as long as their domestic production provided additional employment. (See

also Howard S. Ellis: "Exchange Control in Central 

Europe", Harvard University Press, 1941.)

The Continental European countries were thoroughly cartelized. (See J. W. Reichert: "Die Kartellgesetze der 

Welt", Berlin, 1935; Fischer-Wagenfuhr: "Kartelle in 

Europa Ohne Deutschland", Nurnberg, 1929.) Exchange controls suited these cartels and entrenched their control over domestic and neighboring markets. The cumulative effect of dollar shortage, reemployment and rearmament made the Continental European markets virtually impregnable to American products aimed for general consumption. 

In view of the factors described, any United States company starting an aggressive policy of exporting to the Continental markets would have faced major obstacles at this time if its products had not been militarily useful. The total volume of exports that could have been sold would have been negligible before restrictions would have been clamped down by these countries, based on considerations of foreign exchange and employment, strongly supported by domestic cartels. Thus there was little incentive for 

American companies to engage in an export trade whose total proceeds would have been trifling and whose net effect would have been the establishment of lasting bad relations with well-entrenched European interests. 

IV. Effects of the Economic Environment 

The sweep of industrial concentration through the English and German economies has been paralleled by a similar evolution in virtually all European countries. The question suggests itself whether the cause of so universaland so universally accepted—a movement can be sheer wickedness, whether there is not an inner logic or necessity

that prompts corporate managements to organize in such patterns and that induces governments to accept this process.

The concentration movement does, in fact, possess considerable inner logic. From the viewpoint of management this logic may be irresistible. From the viewpoint of the public interest it may be compelling in the long run, though not necessarily for short periods or without supervision. 

There are two distinct phenomena in the concentration movement. For one of them a good technical term exists, namely, "imperfect competition." The other we shall call, for lack of a universally accepted name, "monopolistic behavior." The distinction between them is profoundly significant. "Imperfect competition" is a technical term that describes a kind of competition that does not meet the formal tests of "perfect competition," but that, from the viewpoint of behavior, is fully as competitive as so-called "perfect competition." "Monopolistic behavior" is behavior aimed to exclude competition, or to prevent it, or to use economic power or political means for the purpose of destroying competitors. 

The forms of competition known in real life are necessarily all "imperfect." This imperfection can vary in degree. Probably the most obvious measure of the degree of imperfection is the number of competitors and the share of the market any small number controls. When the number is very small or a very few control the market, we speak of oligopoly. Other measurements of the degree of imperfection may, however, be more useful at times.

The degree of imperfection is in no way a criterion of the competitiveness of the competitors. In fact, the "free competition" of the law is descriptive of the "imperfect competition" of the economist. Monopolistic behavior, on the other hand, connotes kinds of destructive action that may be taken by competitors at almost any degree of imperfection. The higher degrees of imperfection often make it easier for rivals to behave in such a manner, but this is not necessarily so. Nothing could be less helpful than to imagine that there exists not only a continuous shading from "perfect competition" through "monopolistic" or "imperfect" competition to total monopoly, but also a parallel shading from competitive to monopolistic behavior. 

1. Imperfect Competition 

It has been increasingly recognized in recent economic literature that neither the perfect monopoly nor the perfect competition of the theoretician, nor any reasonable approximation to them, has ever existed, or can exist in modern industry. Competition has always been, and can only be, "imperfect." (Joan Robinson: "The Economics of Imperfect Competition", Macmillan, London, 1933;

Edward Chamberlin: "The Theory of Monopolistic Com petition", Harvard University Press, 1933; A. R. Burns: "The Decline of Competition", McGraw-Hill, New York, 1936.)

Perfect competition as defined by the economist is a timeless concept in which price is determined by the costs of the most expensive producer still needed to satisfy the demand that exists at that price. If the price were higher, the demand would be less than the larger amount offered at that higher price. If the price were lower, the amount that could be offered would be insufficient to meet the greater demand. In describing such a perfectly competitive market

the theoretician carefully postulates homogeneity of product, continuous market, perfect knowledge, perfect mobility of the factors of production, perfect independence of the participants, and such a size distribution of buyers and sellers that the market price is given for any one of them, i. e., no action taken by any single one or small group could have any effect on the price.

It will be appreciated why these conditions cannot exist in the mining, manufacturing, and service industries. It takes time to get factors of production into or out of industry; sometimes it takes a very long time. 

This in itself makes nonsense of the stipulation of a perfect mobility of the factors of production. Knowledge of the market is also restricted; but that is less important than the size distribution of the buyers and sellers. The efficiencies of large-scale production are usually so decisive over handicraft operation that most manufacturing cannot be conducted on a small scale except at a prohibitive cost to the consumer. Large-scale manufacturing implies that only a relatively small number of producers can exist concurrently and that the action of any one of them is likely to have a substantial effect on the market. 

This inevitable technological factor removes the automatism with respect to price that the theoretician imputes to perfect competition. It does not mean, however, that imperfect competition is necessarily less competitive than perfect competition. Highly imperfect competition may be so contrary to what is generally recognized as "monopolistic" as to be competitively far more violent than market situations much less imperfect. 

The reason for this is relatively simple. Since it takes time to get into and out of an industry, there must exist most

of the time excess capacity in every industry requiring large plant investment. This follows from the fact of industrial fluctuations. If additional capacity is built in an industry during a boom,-and it is the nature of booms that additional industrial capacity is built while they are in progress, then during the ensuing recession and for indeterminate periods thereafter that industry will necessarily have excess capacity. This excess capacity can be called into production at the will of the owner. It would pay the owner to utilize his excess capacity as long as the price of the products stays above prime costs. His depreciation charge would run on whether he operated his factory or not; and if he operated it, he would amortize some part of his depreciation charge provided he attained a price above prime costs. Consequently, in industries suffering from over-capacity there is a tendency to price wars. Price wars bring prices below the levels that would prevail under conditions of "perfect" competition. They destroy capital values. Occasionally prices are pushed below prime costs in order to preserve the goodwill of the business. 

Excess capacity in an industry sometimes rests on the market for a very long time, because the extreme durability of capital goods prevents healthy competitive conditions from developing. This is harmful not only to the industry primarily affected, but also to capital goods industries, because no new machinery can be introduced into the over-built industry until the old machinery has completely obsolesced. In a capitalist economy prolonged cycles of unemployment in capital goods industries are an inevitable consequence of booms and of the durability of capital goods.

Young industries, small enterprises, and highly specialized enterprises are highly vulnerable to the demoralizing

effects of imperfect competition. They can come into being only if their costs of production are lower than those of existing firms; the cost advantages of mass production have to be assumed to have been offset by special skills. Their risks consist partly in their inability to compete with their larger competitors with respect to selling and overhead costs and partly in their lack of diversification. 

The larger the firm, the more efficient its sales effort can be. Large corporate enterprise, controlling several plants, possibly in several sections of an industry, derives tremendous competitive cost advantages from being able to distribute its overhead over a variety of products and over greater areas than a small enterprise could. It also derives great competitive advantages from a diversification of its production: each product helps to advertise the name of the enterprise; booms and slumps will not hit every line of business at exactly the same time; nor are the longterm trends of various lines of its business by any means likely to parallel each other. Consequently, new discoveries, new fashions, even cyclical fluctuations are far more dangerous to a small single-product enterprise than to diversified businesses.

The imperfections of competition and the hazards they create for various types of enterprise put a premium on large units and large-scale diversification of corporate enterprise. In the interests of a better diversification of risks, of a wider distribution of overhead costs, of a better resistance to the competitive struggle,-in other words, in the interest of self-preservation,-small enterprise is forced to try to become big, by merger if not by growth. 

The reduction of competitive hazards consists in the stronger control the more diversified, larger enterprise can exercise over its own fate.

While larger size does not mean reduced competitiveness, defensive measures against the hazards of the competitive struggle invariably tend to increase what we have termed the imperfections of competition. Agreements with other enterprises are the minimum means available to small enterprise to obtain the benefits of bigness without sacrificing identity.

The problems of over-capacity or lack of organization lead to efforts to stabilize business and to create more favorable growth conditions by cooperative action. Failing these, the difficulties may become so extreme that agreements and even legislation to restrain competition may be necessary. We have seen numerous British efforts along such lines. Public policy in the United States has recognized this need in the cases of the export trades, agriculture, coal mining, air transportation, shipping, marine insurance, petroleum, railroads, and labor. 

2. Monopolistic Behavior 

Monopolistic behavior may be defined as the use of destructive methods of competition or attempts at exclusion from competition. As we have seen, combination is occasionally forced upon producers by the extreme dangers resulting from imperfections of competition. At other times deliberate restraints are imposed by certain producers or groups of producers for no other reason than that they have the power to do so; these are different in character from steps merely changing the degree or the impact of imperfections of competition. They are especially frequently applied against competition from abroad.

The concept of monopoly as such is a singularly awkward one. "Perfect monopoly" does not and cannot exist any more than perfect competition can. Every monopoly

1 is rigidly hemmed in by the availability of an ever increasing number of substitutes. Conversely, within a very narrow limit, almost every enterprise is a quasi-monopoly. 

The relevant issue is not whether an enterprise is big or small in itself, nor even whether it controls a large percentage of a given market, or even the entire production of a single homogeneous commodity, but whether its conduct is such as to be in the public interest. 

Truly fair competitive conduct is difficult enough to enforce in the United States, where the long tradition of antitrust prosecutions has established-albeit haltingly and with many contradictions-what type of conduct is competitive and what type of conduct imposes restraints that are deemed unreasonable. In foreign countries, where no such tradition exists, regulation of the competitive-or monopolistic-behavior of enterprise is managed on a different level.

Well-entrenched large enterprises and cartels abroad use any and every method to prevent new competition from entering what they have come to consider their own reserved territory. In many, if not most, countries of the world, domestic industry is strongly favored against foreign competition. In many countries industry is heavily or even systematically cartelized. Practices classed as unfair or monopolistic in the United States, and accordingly subject to prosecution, are considered entirely fair and proper in the legal systems and business mores of the rest of the world. Cartelization is an accepted phenomenon. A substantial proportion of the industrial production of the world is controlled by monopolistic combinations of one form or another.

In countries where monopolistic combinations are allowed or even encouraged, they are often able not only to control domestic production but also to exercise influence over the foreign trade policies of their own governments. 

Increasingly they have become instruments for the execution of national policies. 

A new competitor, especially a new foreign competitor, may not claim entry into such controlled markets as a matter of right. Unless he comes to terms with existing combinations, he is unlikely to be able to develop a substantial business. 

3. Effects of the Combination Movement 

For young industries, for new developments, there exists not only a substantial incentive but an unavoidable need to seek, first, substantial financial backing, and, second, a working arrangement with leading local interests in each country.

The first purpose of such arrangements is to secure entry. The second is to secure the most rapid development possible for the new product, unhampered by attempts at exclusion and other similar practices of entrenched organizations. 

Owners of incomplete sets of patents are especially vulnerable to competition from owners of other groups of similarly incomplete patents. The hazards of patent litigation are always present, especially if one group of patentees has resources adequate to finance prolonged litigation. Moreover, if two sets of incomplete patents are used competitively, the development of the new product is likely to be much slower, less efficient; and the hazards of capital

losses and misdirections of investment are likely to be much greater than if patent rights and technical information are exchanged. To that extent a reduction of potential competition among rival patent owners eliminates one of the more obvious wastes of imperfect competition. 

It should be realized in this connection that though a patent confers a monopoly it does so in law only. 

In the first place, new inventions are frequently developed more or less simultaneously by several inventors working in the field. Thus incomplete sets of patents or processes frequently become complementary and one is not particularly useful without the other. Even independent new inventions often obtain greatly enhanced usefulness by being put into operation jointly. Cross-licensing may be the best means of exploiting them, the best means to get the new product to the consuming public promptly and at the lowest possible price.

In the second place, even patent exchanges made for the speeding up of the development and commercial exploitation of new inventions seldom result in a real monopoly. Competition of slightly different products that are in practice substitutes for each other prevents or at least restricts and limits effectively the operations of a patent monopoly. To quote John Maurice Clark: "As to substi tution it is a commonplace that the triumphs of modern industrial chemistry have vastly increased its range and flexibility.

It seems that the differences between substitute products are nowadays no more serious than similar differences between different varieties of what we think of as the 'same' product. . . ." ("Toward a

Concept of Workable Competition", American Economic 

Review, June 1940, p. 247.)

In other words, even if a group of patents constitutes a complete world monopoly, as long as there exist adequate substitutes for the product it will be sold in a competitive market. In this market all producers of substitutes will make every effort to maintain their position and to preserve the value of their assets. As long as there is no understanding between producers of the new product and its substitutes, the limitations to the monopoly of the patentee are fully as real as those of almost any manifestly competitive producer. After all, it is obvious that almost any purveyor of almost anything is a near-monopolist with respect to certain rigidly defined markets; hardly any monopoly is ever more complete than that of the village store.

Under the conditions of the interwar period, owners of patents, and especially owners of rival sets of patents in different countries, had no genuine alternative to a network of agreements. 

4. The Combination Movement and the Public 

With respect to the advantages or disadvantages to the public, it is necessary to distinguish between development products and those already fully developed. 

Development products are more likely to reach the public in quantity if the development is organized and in strong hands than if it is weakly promoted on an individualistic, free-for-all basis. Dependent on the nearness of substitutes, and on the rate of progress in solving cost problems, even the pricing of the development products can only seldom be exploitative.

Fully developed products present a slightly more complex case.

The consumer often benefits temporarily from the very sickness that besets an industry, from the fact that producers selling their product below total replacement cost are giving him, as it were, a small piece of their assets as a bonus with each purchase. But in the long run the public is hurt both by the violent and irregular replacement cycles of capital assets that develop if cut-throat competition prevails over a large enough segment of industry, and by the high prices that have to be paid for years in order to induce new capital investment in unstable industries.

Moreover, the direct protection of the consumer is not the only concern; the wastes of imperfect competition of the cut-throat variety should be measured in terms of losses to investors and wage earners, as well as to the consumer, all of whom would probably receive more, and with greater assurance, if imperfections of competition did not retard industrial progress. 

As mentioned before, the trait of modern industry discussed in the present section is usually referred to as oligopolistic, to express the small number of firms that control each industry. This oligopolistic character creates social, political, and economic problems for the oligopolists themselves. The great instability and competitive hazards of imperfect competition impel towards concentration, mergers, ever larger enterprises. The large units into which industry is organized obtain, by their very size and diversification, a certain degree of stability in output, employment, and revenues. At the same time their size exposes them

to envy, and to much closer scrutiny, than if they were less conspicuous. For this reason, if for no other, higher reasons, they have social responsibilities with respect to giving the public the maximum supply at the lowest possible cost. They also have a responsibility towards their own employees with respect to affording stability of employment, and to their stockholders with respect to not squandering their assets by waging purposeless economic warfare among themselves.

The final section of the present survey presents an outline of how the public interest can best be served in United States foreign trade, in the light of the findings of the foregoing discussion with respect to the organization of industry abroad and oligopolistic tendencies in general.

V. American Business Operations Abroad 

There are three types of business operations in which 

American corporations may engage internationally: (1) exports, (2) imports, (3) production abroad. Production abroad may take place by wholly-owned subsidiaries, by partly owned foreign enterprise, or by licensing enterprise wholly owned by foreigners.

Each of these possible methods of operation has its own advantages and disadvantages as well as its own rules of conduct. 

1. Exports 

With respect to exports the situation is seemingly unambiguous, but actually major national issues are involved. 

Three of these issues usually occupy the foreground of discussions.

(a) First in importance in American discussions is usually the quantity of exports. Possibly as a result of the country having been a debtor country after the Civil War, we seem to have been highly conscious of the economic advantages of paying off debt, and stimulating employment at the same time, by a high level of exports. Whatever the reason, the American approach seems to have been that from a national point of view a high level of exports was very desirable.

As we became more and more an exporter of manufactured products, fierce competition tended to develop in third markets to which both American and European industries tried to export their products. Fierce competition by local producers and efforts at the exclusion of the American product became increasingly frequent also in national markets that American producers tried at various times to invade.

More recently, foreign governments have used tariff increases to force American exporters to build up manufacturing production within the foreign frontiers, first by transferring finishing operations, and then increasingly by fabricating locally the products previously exported from the United States. (b) In recognition of the problems created by the competitive situation in export markets, the Webb-Pomerene Act of 1918 permitted the formation of export associations, and to that extent exempted the export trade from provisions of the antitrust laws. Producers may cooperate in controlling prices and sales in the export trade without any limitation upon their activities, provided the domestic prices of the commodities concerned remain unaffected. As 

Arthur R. Burns remarks ("The Decline of Competition",

New York, 1936): “. the full significance of this proviso has never become evident." (p. 21)

With respect to the national interest in foreign trade, the Webb-Pomerene Act clearly envisaged that, where United States exporters are competing against foreign interests, it would be an advantage to them, and consequently of benefit to the total national economy, if they strengthened their bargaining position by acting collectively. The economic logic of that position is that it improves the terms of trade for the United States; that is to say, it tends to make the unit price of American exports higher as compared to the unit price of American imports, though it is not obvious that the legislators of 1918 saw this aspect of the matter at all clearly. 

Moreover, the Department of Justice issued a ruling in 

1924, in connection with the silver cartel, saying that it is legal for a Webb Association to join in an agreement with foreign cartels, provided other American exporters are not discriminated against and any effect on the domestic price is only incidental. (See Temporary National Economic 

Committee Monograph No. 6, pp. 124-8.)

Good reasons exist in support of this position. It is clearly not in the national interest to force exporters to compete singly against well organized, well entrenched, and government-sanctioned cartels abroad, nor for American exporters to be forced to compete against each other for orders from buying monopolies and cartels abroad. The result of such competition can only be that the prices we obtain for our exports will decline as compared to the prices we have to pay for our imports. (c) A further problem often arises in connection with patented processes and products. When they are licensed

by Americans to foreigners, or vice versa, an attempt is frequently made to restrict the right of the American patent owner or patent licensee to export to foreign countries. 

The legal aspects of such restrictions are outside the scope of the present memorandum. 

2. Imports 

To the extent that American corporations are importers of foreign products, or processors of foreign raw materials, their interest is to buy at the lowest possible price. 

To the extent that they are producers of products that are competitive with potential imports, their interest lies in having tariff protection and other legal means to reduce the threat of foreign competition.

The legal aspects of geographical limitations on the use of patents by foreign licensees of American corporations are outside the scope of this memorandum. Their economic aspects present a difficult problem of economic policy. 

If a foreign licensee or a foreign company partly owned by an American company can produce more cheaply than the American owner of the patents, why should not the American consumer be allowed to benefit from the lower foreign costs?

The obvious answer that this low-cost foreign competition reduces employment in the United States does not hold water; from that angle a geographically restricted license is no less than a privately imposed tariff of prohibitive height. Let Congress decide whether the low foreign cost should be offset by tariff protection.

There is, however, another aspect to this question. If territorial limitations were impossible, self protection would prevent American corporations from licensing foreign cor-

porations that have lower costs. This would hardly be in the interests of anyone.

Even here there is a difference between a new development and an established product. In the latter case the burden of proof might rest on one who advocated geographical limitations. In the case of new inventions, however, it is obviously not in our national interest to insist that all licenses granted abroad be unrestricted, if lower foreign costs of production might result in the production of the new product being retarded or stopped in the United States. 

3. Production Abroad 

American corporations can engage in production abroad directly by wholly-owned subsidiaries or by partly owned foreign enterprise, or indirectly by licensing an enterprise wholly owned by foreigners.

The Webb Act dealt with a period in which American interests abroad were still predominantly export interests. 

With the worldwide trend towards trade restriction and tariff increases, as well as with the increase in the economic power and foreign assets of the United States, the acquisition or building up of subsidiaries of American corporations engaged in productive operations abroad became an increasingly typical case. It is not possible to make an estimate of how large a percentage of the total foreign business of American corporations took the form of production or processing abroad. The probabilities are that, prior to Lend-Lease, the latter were considerably more important than exports from this country. 

The American owner of a wholly-owned foreign subsidiary has been in an increasingly difficult position for

the last ten to fifteen years. The pressure to replace exports from the United States by local facilities was followed, whenever the local facilities were American owned, by a pressure to have the local facilities at least partly locally owned.

A wholly-owned American subsidiary is handicapped with respect to adaptation to local customs, tastes, sales methods. What is worse, it remains a "foreign" enterprise against which local interests can always stir prejudice. Examples abound of discriminatory action against such wholly-owned American subsidiaries abroad. 

Partnership with local groups and royalty agreements based on patent licensing are the two ways of forming a community of interests with someone inside the fort. These two methods are the only ways to stay in the foreign business and avoid discrimination directed against "foreign" enterprise. Short of withdrawal from business abroad, they are the only practicable alternatives to a policy of economic aggression from the outside by competitive exports, or to a policy of economic aggression from the inside by wholly-owned subsidiaries. 

Foreign nations have demonstrated that they will use every known means to resist these types of economic aggression. In such a fight the enterprise trying to invade foreign territory is always in a weak position. Especially in view of high American wage costs and of the important steps that have been taken in recent years to teach American technology to foreign nations, it is hard to believe that we shall be able to maintain a major position in foreign markets without cooperation with local interests.

Barring "trade" based on open or concealed subsidies, the only further alternative to economic aggression or economic cooperation is a withdrawal from business abroad. Such an ostrich policy of economic isolationism would not only be costly in terms of lost sales and assets, but it would eventually also show up in a slowing down of our technological and economic progress. 

Lack of agreement regarding export markets would not only not necessarily result in increased total exports, it might result in less. Small local enterprises would spring up behind each new tariff wall, the cost of production would be higher, and the price to the consumer more erratic. 

This in turn might mean a failure of the product to be developed properly and to reach the public in quantity, except after considerable delays.

The problem thus resolves itself into deciding what the proper range of agreements and cooperation with local interests should be, and what the effective practical significance of various provisions is on which foreign participants in such agreements usually insist. 

4. The Problem of the Future 

In a sense, all agreements between potential competitors are cartel agreements. The typical cartel agreement is, however, an understanding in a mature industry, regulating competition among fully developed enterprises. No useful purpose can be served by viewing agreements aimed at the development of new products in the same light as agreements aimed to raise the price of rubber or mercury to the consumer, or agreements aimed to prevent competitive bidding for government contracts.

With respect to the future it is apparent that cartels and other agreements represent the standard forms of organization of foreign business. There is no serious prospect that the Government of the United States will be able unilaterally to force American standards of competition on foreign countries. The latter have developed their own—and in their opinion adequate-methods of dealing with business organization, and they predominantly favor government-supervised cartelization of industry. 

The problem is to find a solution that will not force surrender of the foreign business of American corporations, that will afford American corporations a practical means of engaging in business in a cartelized world, and that will not offend the spirit of anti-trust legislation in the United States.
