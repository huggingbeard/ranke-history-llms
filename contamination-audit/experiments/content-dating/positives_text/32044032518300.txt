status The question has been asked a number of times, "'Do individuals in authority make decisions which vary as a function of their awareness of (a) the particular status - e.g., experimental or non-experimental of given youngsters, and/or as a reflection of the fact that they are (b) applying differing sets of standards or expectations to specified groups of individuals?" The further question has been asked, "What are the implications of any such differential decision-making relative to the study of comparative effectiveness of differing programs (e.g., Experimental and Control programs)?" A final question may be added: "Under what conditions and toward what ends - might differential decision-making be viewed as a rational and/or justified approach to the handling of given types of individuals, within specified types of programs?" 

This report will take up these questions with reference to the California 

Youth Authority's Community Treatment Project (CTP). 

Introduction And Definitions 

(2) As used in this report, differential decision-making will refer to both of the following, broad types of events (singly and in combination): (1) Each of two wards (one an Experimental, one a Control) commits a single, independent (i.e., unrelated) offense. By objective standards, the two offenses are essentially identical as to 'type' and as to degree of severity (e.g., they both consist of auto theft - unplanned). However, the parole agent who is responsible for the Experimental ward recommends that the ward be restored to parole, while the agent responsible for the Control ward recommends that his ward be removed from parole (revoked). 1 Subsequent to the above, differing recommendations, official action is taken by an individual or individuals who are responsible for the final disposition of the Experimental as well as the Control ward. (The Youth Authority Board possesses the final responsibility, in the present context.) The action in question may consist of either (a) restoration of the former (Experimental) ward and revocation of the latter (Control), or (b) revocation of the former and restoration of the latter, or (c) either restoration of each or revocation of each. In most instances, however, the final disposition (decision) is similar to that which had been recommended by the parole agent (whether Experimental or Control).

The Youth Authority Board 

Board actions which support the above recommendations generally appear to be based upon its belief, for the most part, that the possible risks involved in restoring wards in question will in some sense be outweighed by (a) the possible benefits which might eventually accrue from returning those wards to the given program, and by (b) the possible shorter and/or longer-term risks of revoking the given wards. The benefits in question appear to be judged most often in terms of what might be called the Board's perception of the overall, positive potential of the program to which the wards would be restored.

With reference to specific youths, the Board's assessment of the ultimate benefits which might accrue from the given program appears to be linked up with its own feeling of relative certainty or uncertainty as to the likelihood that behavioral control, as well as personal growth, will take place. This, in turn, is based in part upon its (a) assessment of the extent to which agents in question appear to have accurately understood and/or meaningfully related to the given youths, and its (b) familiarity with patterns of testing-out and acting-out which have been shown by similar types of youths within the given program.

Relevant, Rational, And information-Based Decision-Making

It becomes increasingly apparent that the concept of differential decision-making comprises but one significant part of a still broader issue - that of relevant decision-making and rational decision making. 

The former concept would refer to recommendations and/or final decisions which are intended to be made within the context of a specific, ultimate criterion - e.g., that of eventual rehabilitation (however defined). 

Relative to any such context, the main operations and research question would be, "Does the given recommendation/decision appear to be compatible with the given objective?" That is, does it appear to be of theoretical relevance to the stated criterion? As programs increase in level of sophistication, questions relating to the theoretical relevance of specific decisions and decision-making processes will, it would appear, themselves assume increasing significance.

Evaluation Of Program Effectiveness In the Presence Of Differential And Appropriate Decision-Making 

In terms of decisions which are made on the basis of current information concerning individual programs as well as individual wards, the above procedure can work in the direction of creating a meaningful balance between the factors of immediate risk and potential benefit. At the same time, however, an approach of this nature adds some complexity to the task of research evaluation. In this regard, the major implications of appropriate decision-making are found in the area of comprehensively assessing the comparative effectiveness of differing programs - some or all of which are regarded by decision-makers in particular, as being of a rather dissimilar nature from the standpoint of available resources and flexibility.

More specifically, the comparative effectiveness of any two or more programs can itself be very much a function of the areas of greatest and least sensitivity on the part of the particular index which has been selected as the indicator of effectiveness: The index of recidivism (revocation of parole; recommitment by the Juvenile or Criminal Courts) would perhaps be the prime example of a measure which is, e.g., particularly sensitive in the area of differential (albeit appropriate) decisionmaking. In any event, it is apparent that careful interpretation must be given to statements of comparative effectiveness concerning any two or more programs which appear to be very different in content and scope - particularly when the statements in question are based upon individual criterion measures which may tap very different processes within the respective programs. To be sure, scores or rates obtained in connection with any given criterion measure relative to any two programs are the product of at least somewhat differing factors, priorities, and resources present and operating within the respective programs. However, this is both inevitable and precisely as it should be - particularly when the primary research objective is that of determining which of two or more programs best accomplishes stated objectives.2

In the case of criterion measures which are particularly sensitive to the factor of differential decision-making, many of the difficulties and complexities discussed in the present report can be reduced when evaluative research is at a stage of investigating variations and possible refinements within individual programs. In this type of endeavor, research designs can be created in which all study subjects become part of a single, overall program - one which would have available roughly the same resources for all individuals. Here, some subjects would receive 'treatmentstrategy XI' while others would receive 'X2'; and, the research emphasis would primarily be upon comparisons among differing treatment-strategies rather than upon a comparison between programs which differed3 in some major way relative to available as well as perceived resources and overall flexibility. The operation and influence of differential decision-making would in such a case be likely to diminish, at least insofar as it would otherwise have been the product of actual and perceived differences in resources and flexibility at the program level. Insofar as it could be shown to have been a product of other factors as well, one would not expect it to be eliminated entirely. Finally, it would appear to make little sense to curtail appropriate - i.e., rational as well as relevant decision-making in this or any other type of treatment and research endeavor simply because of the methodological complexities which can be created by what is in any event the possibly inescapable presence of differential decision-making.

This applies not only to recidivism, but also to psychological test scores, favorable discharge, etc.

In endeavors of this nature, the important thing is to utilize more than a single measure of program-effectiveness whenever possible, even at the risk of apparent contradictions in terms of results.
