What role should the Government play in connection with broadcast programming? 

For nineteen days, beginning in December, 1959, the Federal Communications Commission heard more than ninety witnesses offer their views on this question and allied issues. On January 28, 1960 testifying for the

National Broadcasting Company, Chairman of the Board Robert W. Sarnoff presented his position, and a constructive proposal, in the statement that appears in the following pages.

HESE hearings have produced a voluminous record of testimony. I believe I can best introduce my own contribution with some observations on what you have already heard. 

Many of the witnesses have been sharply critical of television. Yet, even though they share a sense of grievance against the medium, what a variety of different panaceas, what a tangle of cross purposes, what a maze of administrative dilemmas have emerged from their testimony!

You have heard proposals for a staggering array of advisory councils, ranging from a national brain trust to a welter of local committees. People who say they abhor censorship have urged you to abolish or restrict some programs and to compel the production of others. Some witnesses-who would staunchly oppose Government intervention in the press, the theater or any other medium of public expression-have advocated making the Government not only a regulator of broadcasting but a broadcaster itself. Others want a system of station license renewal that would entail up to an average of six new renewal hearings every day. Still others have prescribed basic changes in the economics of the American system of broadcasting.

Even within groups presumably bound by the same interests, spokesmen have sometimes been at odds. The representative of one farmers' organization with 700,000 members says the networks "strangle" good programs, and the repre- 1

sentative of another farmers' organization with 

850,000 members thinks the networks are doing a good job. A professor from Harvard warns that advertising on the air is repelling the audience, and a professor from Columbia testifies that, according to his research, most people "love" commercials. A distinguished magazine editor urges that the government regulate broadcasting as a monopoly; a distinguished newspaper editor urges that broadcasters be permitted to solve their problems in freedom. And within the ranks of educators, you have heard a striking difference of opinion as to whether the depiction of violence may incite aggressions or release them vicariously.

Taken together, this testimony vividly illustrates an essential point about our central problem. On a small scale, within the confines of a single subject and even from a largely common point of departure, these hearings have reflected some of the diversity and endless conflict of opinion that are so characteristic of a free society-the same kind of multifaceted, often contradictory mass of views and desires that it is the mission of broadcasting to serve as well as it can.

I certainly do not challenge the sincerity of those who have testified, nor the fact that their criticism reflects the views, in varying degrees, of 2 large numbers of people of taste, intelligence and good will. I fully recognize that some of the criti-

cism also reflects genuine faults and shortcomings in television itself. Certainly it is a matter of real concern to us at NBC and, I am sure, to every conscientious broadcaster.

We do not presume to have attained perfection in twelve years; nor, for that matter do I think we can ever reach it. The question is not whether the television program service should or can be improved. Of course, it should; and it can. The question is how. I wish there were a simple answer without a catch to it. But the answer is not simple. 

It is complex and arduous; it is costly in time and patience, in trial and error, as well as in money. 

It is an answer we are dedicated to working out painstakingly in the spirit and traditions of a free society.

The Issues 

In my testimony today, I will seek to explore that answer in terms of what I believe to be the fundamental issues underlying the Commission's inquiry.

The basic purpose of this proceeding, as I understand it, is to make a searching reappraisal of what the Commission's role should be in connection with the programming that broadcasters present to the public.

This endeavor raises the most basic and sensitive questions of how a medium of public expression, operating in a competitive framework, and dependent for its survival on public acceptance, can

best function in a democracy.

It highlights the fact that the nation we serve is a multiple society, composed of many divergent elements, reflecting a vast range of difference in taste and interest.

It poses the problem, which is central in a democracy, of striking a balance between the rights of the majority and the rights of minorities. 

It demands scrutiny of the degree to which the 

Government can undertake to say what is "right" and "wrong" about programming, without arrogating authority over what public taste and interest should be; without entering into areas of judgment where it cannot effectively function; and without overstepping the bounds of Constitutional and statutory safeguards against censorship.

All these questions compel us to examine what constitutes the "public interest" for a medium designed to serve not one segment of the public, or even several segments, but the total public. This in turn requires understanding of the nature of the public to be served, and how it responds to the service it receives.

These questions lead to the ultimate questions in this proceeding: First, what is the responsibility of the broadcaster? And second, how can a Government in the democratic tradition most 

4 effectively assure the fulfillment of that responsibility?

In attempting to answer these questions, I will:

Outline what I believe to be the public-interest function of the mass medium of broadcasting; 

Indicate how NBC performs that function through its television network; 

Examine the criticism of the present broadcast service;

Consider the merits and consequences of proposals to license networks and apply the public-interest standard to their schedules;

And, finally, present our views on the role the Commission should play in relation to programming.

I will treat basically with the serious issues of public policy raised by these questions. They also raise controversial legal issues which are treated separately in a brief submitted for the record. 

The issues that concern us are not novel ones. 

They were born with our competitive free-enterprise system of broadcasting; they are ingrained in the relationship between the broadcaster, his audience, and the Government. They have been nourished through many cycles of debate since broadcasting began; and they have grown weightier over the last decade with the phenomenal expansion of television and its impact on our society.

Today particular attention has been drawn to these issues in the wake of the quiz show disclosures. I want to touch on these irregularities,

The Aftermath of the Quiz Show Disclosures We accept responsibility for what took place, in the sense that we assume responsibility to the public for everything presented on NBC facilities. 

This wrongdoing is not inherent in television nor peculiar to it. We have taken pains to prevent any repetition of these reprehensible incidents to the extent that it is humanly possible to guard against dishonesty. Going far beyond quiz shows, we have undertaken a comprehensive reappraisal of our operations to identify and eliminate any unethical or borderline practices that may have developed during the years in which a new art and a new industry have mushroomed around us. 

To do the job properly, we have created a new and permanent Department of Standards and Practices, which centralizes and expands activities we have conducted over the years. The task is not an overnight venture; we are pursuing it earnestly on a deliberate, systematic basis. practice, we will formalize rules of proper procedure and establish controls to enforce them.

The lesson to be drawn from these last months is that broadcasters are determined and able to exercise proper responsibility for what they offer the public. There is no question that the public's faith should not be abused by rigged shows or any other deceptive practices. The broader question of what sort of total broadcast service should be offered is not nearly as clear. That is the question. to which I now address myself.

The Public Interest Concept 

The legal standard for broadcasting—and the one universally embraced by American broadcasters -is the "public interest." But in examining exactly what it means, we find differences of interpretation and emphasis that vary as widely as the differences in taste and interest within the public itself. For in defining the public interest, each observer tends to project his own desires and interests as the standard for a medium serving the total public.

The primary public-interest role of broadcasting for the majority of viewers and listeners is to satisfy their needs for relaxation and diversion. 

I do not think it can be honestly disputed that most people want to be entertained by broadcasting most of the time. What interests the public is an important measure of the public interest. But that is only the beginning of the problem, for

within the entertainment area itself, there are wide divergencies in audience taste. These exist not only among the many varied forms of entertainment programs, but also among the varying levels of sophistication with which these forms are presented, ranging from the broadly popular to highly specialized appeal.

Apart from the entertainment field, there are smaller segments of the audience that equate the public interest chiefly with the presentation of information; with the sciences and humanities; with news and public affairs; with education; or with providing what they feel the majority audience ought to see, rather than what it wants to see.

These differences are not rigid, for the patterns of audience taste, like our society itself, are fluid and changing. Minority elements also enjoy popular entertainment to a degree; and to a degree, the majority can be attracted to material which offers new intellectual and cultural experience. 

Every mass medium has the problem of meeting a range of public tastes and interests. Broadcasting, however, and particularly television, has difficulties uniquely its own.

Elsewhere the process of public selection is taken for granted. For example, no national magazine is expected to match the tastes of every magazine 8 reader in the country; instead, the reader selects the magazines which meet his interests. He is not

outraged because Life magazine is not Harper's, or because Harper's is not the Hudson Review.

Television, however, undertakes to serve a total public. Each viewer takes a proprietary attitude toward the medium, because it comes to him on his own set and regularly shares the intimacy of his own home. He treats it as a kind of genie that awaits his bidding: at any time, he feels he has the right to click on the set and see something to his liking. What he really expects, though he may not think of it in this way, is a total service tailored to his own interests. When disappointed, as occasionally he must be, especially if his interests run with the minority, he feels a sense of personal affront.

It is the greatest over-simplification to describe the responsibility of a mass medium in terms of "giving the public what it wants," because there are many different publics and they want many. different things.

NBC's concept of a television service in the public interest is one that gives reasonable satisfaction to the varying interests of the main audience elements; which does so by proportioning its program structure in general to the relative weights of these varying elements; which does not allow majority tastes to suppress a fair reflection of minority interests and does not frustrate majority interests by converting a mass medium into a specialized one.

Beyond these balancing factors, we believe that broadcasters in a democracy have an obligation to lead the audience by providing information that will equip them for better citizenship, and by offering opportunities to enjoy the arts so as to broaden and cultivate taste. In leading the audience, however, the broadcaster cannot be so far ahead that his service is rejected by the majority, for that would defeat both the purpose of such leadership and the very nature of a mass medium.

Essentially, this is a doctrine of reasonable treatment of all segments of the public, so that each viewer, or listener, in varying degrees, can find something he values in the broadcast schedule. 

In a service based on these premises, popular entertainment, in a variety of forms, must predominate because it is responsive to majority audience desires. In addition, a responsible service will include entertainment in the more cultivated arts which have not yet established mass appeal; and it will give significant recognition to nonentertainment programs in the field of news, information, public affairs and education, even though these appeal to smaller audiences.

No one of these program types has any special claim to serving the public interest. It is the total proportioned blend of programming that serves the public interest, because only such a blend meets the varying and conflicting interests of the total

public served by broadcasting. The maintenance of such a service, to my mind, is the public-interest responsibility of the commercial television broadcaster.

Even in this respect, one must not generalize too broadly on how the public interest should be served by the individual broadcaster. The criterion of balanced programming I have just described does not really apply to educational television stations, which are specialists. Nor should it apply as a rule to the individual broadcaster in radio. Thanks in part to the far more numerous radio frequencies, the growth of FM and the lower costs of operation, radio has become a medium in which highly specialized services may be undertaken by individual broadcasters in the public interest where balance is served by a combination of other stations on the dial. Thus a fine music station can perform as a specialist and still serve the public interest, and so can a radio network concentrating on a basic news and information service.

In serving the public interest, television cannot serve all viewers equally. Consequently, the individual viewer must select out of the total service those programs that meet his own desires. 

The effort of selectivity is one that he makes constantly in his approach to any other medium of information or entertainment. He chooses to read 11 one newspaper over another-in those cities where

there is still more than one paper-and he thinks nothing of walking several blocks to buy his favorite paper if his regular newsstand is sold out. 

He makes a careful choice in buying a book or a magazine subscription. When he goes to the theater, or to a movie, his selection is anything but casual, and he must accommodate himself to the time when tickets are available and the hour when the performance begins.

Selecting a program from a television log involves less trouble and expense than most of those choices and as little as any; yet ironically, many who criticize television most are unwilling to assume this small burden of program selection to satisfy their own tastes.

I do not think it can be questioned that out of a total week's television offerings, there is a volume and variety of rewarding material for every significant element of the audience, if viewers will make the slight effort to seek it out. The issue thus becomes one of degree: Is the broadcaster providing enough of each type of material to meet properly the varying interests within the audience; and is he placing the various types of programming suitably in his schedule?

I am afraid there will never be unanimous answers to those questions. Viewers of specialized tastes will never be satisfied with what a mass medium 12 offers if they expect it to be fashioned primarily to their own interests. However, I believe an ob-

jective analysis of the program schedules will disclose a wealth of material for a wide range of specialized tastes.

We believe that a schedule of the variety and balance reflected in this brief account represents programming in the public interest. Still another example of the changing patterns of programming is a novel one that appeared only a week ago. The three television networks developed and adopted a plan to assure the public of at least three prime evening weekly hours of information, educational and cultural programming without any conflict or overlap. We at NBC are presenting far more of these programs, of course, but we welcome government permission to get together with the other networks on a scheduling pattern that will spread some of all the networks' programs in these areas across the weekly calendar. I need not go into further details of this plan here, for the Chairman of the Commission is an authority on the subject, having been responsible for the constructive suggestion that led to the plan. 

I mention these examples as indications that the composition and balance of the program schedule is never a static thing, but is constantly changing and growing. In its short dozen years, television programming has grown tremendously in scope, stature and technique. If you will review a typical network schedule of ten years ago, it will seem pale and primitive in comparison with the program structure of today.

sisted of variety shows-a kind of transplanted vaudeville. Since then, television has created a great diversity of forms in the entertainment field. Moreover, it has developed a high degree of craftsmanship to meet the audience tastes it has sharpened. As a result, many of today's programs make their predecessors of ten years ago seem childishly crude in production values, camera work, direction, script and performance.

One of the inventions of television's first decade has been the Special, which lavishes the budget, the pains and the creative zeal of a Broadway production on a single 60or 90-minute show.

Ten years ago, the Special was unheard of; this season NBC is presenting more than 200 of them, from Fred Astaire to Project Twenty's "Mark 

Twain's America." We have also greatly expanded the range of our sports coverage to an unprecedented 450 hours a year, and we have enlarged the scope and expertness of our news and public affairs operations. We have pioneered color television with a network schedule that now totals 20 hours a week of color. These efforts are being supported by many independently owned stations, which are demonstrating their willingness to invest their energy and resources to develop color as a new dimension of the television art-just as they did in the early pioneer- 

20 ing days of black-and-white television.

Though the steps I have described may have

seemed hardly perceptible from one season to the next, these have been giant strides for a period of only a decade. It may not be amiss to observe that this remarkable growth was launched by the willingness of the networks to risk many millions of dollars, and nourished by the support of the nation's advertisers, whose role in television has been called to account by so many witnesses in this room.

The striking growth in the vitality and achievement of television has been paralleled and dramatized by the phenomenon of expanding set usage. Television set ownership has been increasing at a faster rate than the growth of homes in the United States. Since 1955, for example, total homes have increased 8%, while television homes have increased 40%. And over the same period, there has also been a progressive increase in average hours of viewing, so that the combination of both factors has produced a 46% increase in home hours of viewing per day. This is not a reflection of failure to serve the interests of the public. A service based on voluntary acceptance could not grow-especially during a period of unprecedented competition for the leisure-time attention of Americans-by foisting on the public programs which did not actively engage their tastes and interests.

It is my conviction that in both scope and impact 21 on the public, the past growth of the television

service, under its own power, is the best token of this young medium's vitality and the best promise of its continued progress. 

Nevertheless, the criticism of television continues, and it is not hard to find targets among the shortcomings and defects of a medium that makes no pretense to perfection. But if one ranges through the catalogue of criticism, as I propose to do now, some of the complaints appear to rise from a basic misconception of the function of a mass medium. Some are irresponsible, and many are centered on artificial targets.

The Charge of Mediocrity 

One of the common criticisms against television programming is the charge of mediocrity. The following quotation from a well-known critic may strike a familiar note: "Anyone who has looked at fifty-four productions since the season started in August has sufficient reason to feel depressed. 

Last year was on the whole banal. This season, so far is worse. 99 That was written a few weeks ago about the 

Broadway theater by Brooks Atkinson of The 

New York Times. 

Is there a book critic in America who has not observed the overwhelming mediocrity of the 1,600 new works of fiction that are published annually? This is what Time Magazine wrote this 

22 month not about books that have fallen by the month-not wayside but about the current list of best sellers:

"It is a deplorable list. A reader who worked his way through all the books on it would find his sense of style outraged, his deepest emotions hardly touched, his stock of information increased but little and his understanding of his times barely improved."

The dictionary meaning of mediocre is "ordinary. "The outstanding or extraordinary play, book, movie or statesman is extraordinary only by comparison with the ordinary. 

Television is no exception. Indeed, in terms of the sheer volume of creative effort to fill more than the waking day, every day of the year, television undertakes a challenge unparalleled in the history of human expression. By the very nature of things, the public which demands this volume. of programming cannot have it all of uniform excellence.

No broadcaster tries to put on a poor show or even an ordinary show. Every broadcaster constantly seeks those programs that combine an effective concept with the most skillful writing, producing, directing and performing talent. When he has that happy combination, he has a hit-whether it is a Western or a documentary, a mystery or a classic drama. In spite of this dedication and its frequently successful results, the ordinary television show-like the ordinary play, book or movie - will always be with us.

The word "mediocre" is less precisely but widely 23

used in another sense as an epithet for a program that fails to match the observer's preferences, though it may be enjoyed by many millions of viewers. In this sense, it is applied less to a program's execution than to the very existence of certain program types. This criticism may be perfectly valid as a personal expression of individual taste. But it is not valid as a public yardstick for a mass medium which is obliged to serve the widest range of tastes.

This charge is often made in tandem with another the charge that television fails to provide responsible programming outside the entertainment field, and fails to serve as a medium of intellectual and cultural stimulation. To the extent that such criticism denies that television plays any role in these fields, it is simply untrue. Those who make such charges either do not look at television and are unaware of what it offers to satisfy their own interests; or they do not take the trouble as they would with any other medium to seek out and select what they would enjoy. To the extent that they quarrel with the amount of non-entertainment programming, they have an arguable point; but it is an argument that must also take into account the balance of interests in a medium serving the total public.

Westerns and Mysteries 

24 Then there is the criticism that there are too many Westerns on television, and too many mysteries.

Here again the argument hinges on a matter of degree. Westerns and mysteries were not invented by television. They are among the most popular entertainment forms, and as light reading matter they command a considerable following among educated and discriminating people; each of these two forms, as a matter of fact, has been known to provide one of the favorite relaxations of a different President of the United States. Zane Grey, Edgar Wallace and Erle Stanley Gardner may not be literary titans, but they are among the most popular storytellers of our time. 

Westerns and mysteries are popular with television viewers, too. In the light of their popularity, I do not think NBC presents too many of them.

In total hours of programming, Westerns come to 6% of the NBC television network schedule, and mysteries represent another 7%. We present more news and interview shows, more comedy-variety, and more audience participation shows than we do Westerns or mysteries. 

In the total output of television, Westerns and mysteries loom much larger for in addition to the offerings of any one network, there are those of the other two networks, plus the syndicated films used by stations, plus the feature movies. 

I believe that much of the criticism of network programming is based on impressions gained from this total service of which the network schedule is only a part. If a viewer chose to turn from station to 25

station for Westerns and mysteries, he could probably keep busy all evening watching them. But I do not know what standard of taste can be established and enforced to reduce the number of these programs in the total television output, short of inviting Big Brother to plan and coordinate all the television schedules in the land. 

The Problem of Violence 

Still another issue emerges from the popularity of these program types, and it has exercised many witnesses during these hearings. It is the issue of violence, notably in its effects upon children and particularly in its relationship to juvenile delinquency. I do not propose to make light of the genuine concern felt by many parents and teachers on this score; it is a concern that we at NBC share. 

But I think it is important to develop some perspective on these fears about the effects on children. Let me start by quoting an authority's observation on the subject of youth. Here it is: "They have bad manners, contempt for authority.

They show disrespect for elders and love chatter in place of exercise. Children are now tyrants, not the servants of their households. They no longer rise when elders enter the room. They contradict their parents, chatter before company, gobble up their food and tyrannize their teachers." 

That quotation was attributed to Socrates in the 

Fifth Century, B. C. Problem children and juvenile delinquents have been with us for a long, long 

---

time. Precisely how certain television programs may affect young viewers is a matter of dispute about which we have relatively little evidence. For example, as I indicated earlier, it has been testified here by an eminent educator that violence in drama may well purge viewers of their aggressive feelings. Others argue the contrary. 

The best evidence available suggests strongly that the fears about the effects of television on children have been exaggerated. In the early years of the medium, it was feared that television would harm children's eyesight, cut down on their sleep, lessen their outdoor activities, injure their schoolwork and reduce their reading. Recent studies, such as one published by the Boston University School of Education under the title "Television for Children," show us that these fears have proved unfounded. 99 Balanced against the possible adverse effects that television may have, says the same study, "is the stimulation children get from the new knowledge television provides, about distant places, and about things and events in the world which they would have very little chance of experiencing directly.' 

As the study puts it in another passage, "parents and educators have discovered that much of the television fare offered to children is of truly excellent quality, by moral, intellectual and esthetic standards." I would add the observation that responsible parents know how to guide their chil- 27

dren's viewing accordingly, and that conscientious teachers are increasingly learning to harness the potential and appeal of appropriate television programs to teach their classroom subjects more effectively. 

In our view at NBC, even the possibility that violence on the television screen may be harmful to children must be heeded in determining our programming course. It is the duty of every responsible broadcaster to be alert to this possibility. 

That does not mean we must or can abandon the element of conflict, which is an ancient ingredient of fiction that runs through all literature and drama, and for that matter, an ancient ingredient of life itself. Nor does it mean that we are compelled to program nothing but Pablum; it is manifestly impossible to present only programs suitable for children without frustrating the grownups whom we also exist to serve.

What it does mean is that we must be vigilant and conscientious, both in production and in our continuity-acceptance editing, to guard against violence and brutality for their own sake. NBC's

Continuity Acceptance Department, which scrutinizes all scripts and rough-cut films from the standpoint of taste and propriety, has done an effective job on our radio and television networks for many years, and with the instructions and support of NBC management, it is now taking an even stronger hand against anything that smacks

of sadistic indulgence or excessive violence.

Here again, we are unlikely to satisfy everyone, because we are dealing with matters of taste and judgment. But we are doing our best according to our lights. Apart from calling attention to that continuing, systematic effort, I believe I should point out that if it were not for the networks, the kind of program that is prone to violence would be far more in evidence on the television screen. 

Alone among all those who supply programs to the country's stations, it is the major networks that offer a balanced program service. The network offerings in news, public affairs, education and the cultural field thus provide elements of balance to all of television. 

The Charge of Advertiser Control 

Much of the criticism of television seems to issue from the double barreled charge that it is degraded by conformity to advertisers' objectives and by the worship of ratings. I believe that this charge stems from fundamental misconceptions. 

Like all influences on programming, the influence of advertisers is based on judgments of audience reaction. Here, too, the audience is the ultimate control. The typical advertiser of mass-produced package goods seeks a program that will appeal to the largest possible audience. This objective is consistent with one of the major public-interest rules of broadcasting. The largest possible audience is a natural goal and, indeed, a basic attri- 29

bute, of a mass medium. 

If this objective dictated the total program schedule, that would justify the argument of undue advertiser influence, because it would preclude programming of a more specialized kind. But that is not the case. Advertisers themselves have requirements other than size of circulation. They may seek a substantial audience, but they may also want to be identified with special quality and prestige in entertainment. Or they may seek the audience attracted by cultural or informational programming, such as the Bell Telephone science and music series, the Hallmark series of outstanding dramas, the Equitable Life Assurance Society's distinguished series dramatizing events in American history, and the wide range of sponsored Special programs and series in the documentary, public affairs and news fields. 

Once upon a time, a sustaining program was regarded as the only kind of program of special aspirations, quality or appeal. The phrase has been used by some in these hearings in just that sense. But that meaning of "sustaining program" is a casualty of the progress of broadcasting. Today whether a program is sponsored or sustaining has no necessary bearing on its subject matter, style or merit. 

Finally, apart from the considerations I have 30 cited, we plan and present programs needed for a balanced service whether we have a sponsor or

not. Often we commit a program to our schedule and then look for a sponsor. Sometimes we find him; sometimes we don't. On this basis, we provided comprehensive coverage of Premier Khrushchev's visit to the United States-which was not sponsored to Europe, which was. We have presented "Meet the Press" and Chet Huntley's Sunday reports each week with and without sponsorship. The decision to schedule "World Wide 60,” our major weekly series of public affairs programs in prime evening time, was made without sponsorship, and we hope to sell it. For many years, we have been presenting an outstanding series of operas in English without sponsorship and two weeks ago we obtained a sponsor for the remaining operas we have scheduled for this season. Our "Project 

Twenty" series of documentaries was originally developed without any sponsor commitment, but we have managed to sell many of them. and of President Eisenhower's trip

Advertiser objectives are taken into account in determining the composition of our schedule, but the final shape of the schedule remains our complete responsibility. In some cases, for example, we have declined programs offered to us by advertisers because they did not fit into the overall requirements of a balanced schedule, or failed to meet our standards of program quality. It is ironic that much of the criticism of television should 31 now tax the advertiser with controlling the net-

work, when until recently it taxed the network with controlling the advertiser, giving rise to proposals for eliminating the network's control of its program schedule. 

Just as we develop a balanced program structure to meet the range of audience interests and also to lead the audience, so we seek to meet the range of advertiser interests and also to lead the advertisers. And this effort has had substantial success in recent years in developing advertising support for public affairs programming, a trend which we believe will continue. But even in the unlikely event of its reversal, we will continue to program beyond the range of immediate advertiser interest. In any event, advertising objectives will not define the balance and composition of our total schedule.

In the largest sense, the influence of advertising on broadcasting is expansive and not restrictive, because it makes possible the resources for all we do, sponsored or unsponsored. For thirteen out of seventeen years nobody sponsored Arturo Toscanini and the NBC Symphony Orchestra, but advertising paid the bills, just as it enables us today to provide minority-interest programs that still do not attract the support of individual advertisers. In the last 10 years, the NBC Opera has presented more than 50 productions without a sponsor, but advertising paid the bills.

In the totality of the service we offer, we seek not

only to lead the audience and the advertisers, but also the stations. In the public affairs and cultural fields, we are offering more programming than many of our affiliates are prepared to accept. For example, "Meet the Press" is carried by a lineup of 80 stations, our weekly news-in-depth program is carried by 75 stations, our presentation of Beethoven's great opera "Fidelio" was carried by 100 stations, and a recent one-hour documentary on African nationalism, scheduled in prime evening time, was carried by 94 stations. These compare with the average 140-station lineup for our evening entertainment schedule, and a lineup of 184 stations for a top-rated program like "Wagon Train." Ratings

As for ratings, it has been my experience that the people who criticize them most understand them least. The various rating services, when properly used, serve three purposes: they provide a general body of information about the public's taste, viewing habits and program preferences; they measure audience behavior and composition in the case of individual programs; and they define the circulation a broadcaster sells. 

To find out as much as we can about audience reaction, we subscribe to a number of different rating services, because each makes a different type of contribution to our knowledge. Each of the services measures different things and that is 

33

why the results sometimes seem to laymen to be at odds. We employ a large full-time staff of experts to analyze and evaluate audience measurement data, because the raw material itself-the figures the public hears about-are not meaningful for our purposes.

In planning and evaluating programs, the two most important uses of rating information are to estimate audience trends and analyze the characteristics of program audiences. The first gives us information on how the audience is reacting to a program over a period of time, and the second indicates how the audience is made up as among men, women and children, various age groups, economic levels, family sizes and the like, so that we may schedule in keeping with the audience compositions available at various times of the broadcast day.

From rating data, we also learn which members of the family are responsible for the selection of given programs; and how enthusiastic people are about the programs they are watching. In addition to the regularly syndicated rating services, we undertake special qualitative audience research to determine what elements and personalities in particular programs are liked or disliked, and to pre-test programs before they are selected. 

The other principal function of rating informa- 34 tion-the measurement of how many and what 

kind of people the broadcaster is reaching — is

essential to show he is delivering circulation values competitive with other media. This is a business requirement of broadcasting, vital in attracting and justifying the advertising expenditures that support our system of broadcasting.

There are several complaints against ratings. Perhaps the most common is that not enough people are sampled to give an adequate basis for gauging the behavior of millions. According to the unanimous view of independent qualified experts in statistics and research, a remarkably small sample, properly chosen, does make it possible to draw national estimates within a small and predictable margin of statistical error. This very technique is used by the U. S. Census Bureau to check on changes in various types of population data during the years between each full census. 

The complaints that ratings dictate programming, driving it to the "lowest common denominator," do not square with the facts. Rating information is only one of the factors in program planning, and as our schedule makes clear, our program judgments and decisions involve considerations other than the size of audience alone.

There is a complaint about ratings, however, in which I am glad to join; indeed, the record will show that I was voicing it several years ago.

While they are highly useful as proper tools of research by those who know how to use them, 35

ratings can be and have been readily abused as a false yardstick of merit and prestige. When they are publicized as status symbols, they make it more difficult for broadcasters to gain proper support in trying to present programs whose contributions to diversity, balance and quality cannot be measured in ratings.

Perhaps the most flagrant myth arising from this abuse is that of the so-called "top ten." Many of the programs on the published lists of the top ten usually are separated only by fractions; yet the rating services themselves agree that they are not precise within fractions of a point and, in fact, any difference of less than two or three rating points is not significant. We have been trying to discourage the abuse of ratings by refusing to publicize them, and there are hopeful signs that this policy may help place ratings in their proper perspective. 

Complaints Against Commercials

The catalogue of criticism would not be complete without the charges against advertising content -that it is sometimes deceptive or distasteful, or both, and that there is just too much of it on the air.

As the Chairman of the Federal Trade Commis sion has observed, the issue of false or misleading advertising is common to all media-especially in 36 view of the fact that the same claims often appear on television, in newspapers and in magazines.

As the president of the National Association of

Better Business Bureaus testified here, the record of broadcasting in this respect can stand comparison with that of any other medium. Since the various advertising media complete with one another, it is only fair that all should be subject to equal treatment by the FTC.

But if broadcasters maintain, as we do, that ours is the most vivid and eloquent advertising medium, perhaps it is also fair that advertising deception on the air should have to reckon as well with the public-interest standard. This would require no new regulation. Any licensee who deliberately carried false advertising would clearly invite scrutiny of his character qualification under existing law.

The standard of taste in commercial content, length and frequency is, like any standard of taste, more difficult to define and apply. But the industry is conscientiously at work in this area, and as testimony here has already indicated, it is making genuine progress, through amendment, enforcement and wider application of its self-regulatory codes. 

Should Networks Be Licensed?

Amid these growing signs of self-regulation, there are, nevertheless, demands for more government regulation. One of them takes the form of a proposal that networks be licensed. This suggestion apparently stems from the theory that networks 37

share with advertisers and the rating system the chief blame for the fault some observers find in the total broadcast service; and that licensing networks will eliminate these faults.

This is a proposal directed against another artificial target. Even if it were feasible from a legal, Constitutional and administrative standpoint, it would be something like carrying coals to Newcastle, for ironically, as I have pointed out, it is the network program service today that serves as the major element of balance for all of television. Although I believe the reasons against licensing networks far outweigh any reasons for doing so, I have no doubt whatever that the NBC network schedule would win endorsement as a responsible and diversified program service under the public-interest standard.

The case for licensing networks has been reasoned in this way: the networks supply a substantial part of affiliated stations' programming; the networks assume a responsibility to serve the public interest; and the selection of programs offered by a network is made by the network. On these grounds, it has been argued, if it is proper for the Commission to exercise limited authority over the program performance of stations, it would seem no less proper for the Commission to apply the same authority to the program activi- 

38 ties of networks.

Opposed to such arguments are persuasive reasons

against the licensing of networks.

In broadcasting, the fundamental basis for licensing is the need of assigning frequencies to avoid destructive interference; since networks do not use frequencies, this basis for licensing them does not exist.

As far as programming is concerned, networks function as a program source for stations. Although the networks may be the most important suppliers of programs, licensing them for a review of programming at the source would raise questions as to whether the same review should not be applied to other program suppliers, such as packagers, agencies and film companies. Indeed, a network itself obtains most of the programs it offers from a variety of outside production sources. 

If the Commission's program review is to go behind the station, can it stop at the intermediate source represented by the network? Or would it have to go farther still to license the original program sources and apply a public-interest standard to their productions?

In any event, it is unnecessary to license networks for evaluation of the service they offer, because this service can reach the public only in one way -through stations that already operate under license. The total performance of these stations, consisting of programs obtained from networks, syndicators, feature film distributors and advertisers, as well as the station's own program 39

department—all this is already subject to Commission review.

The basic concept of Government action under our system is that the Government intervenes in private enterprise only when it is necessary to protect the public interest. No such situation justifies the addition of network licensing to the existing licensing of stations that include network programs in the total service under review. 

The Commission should also consider the confusion and inconsistencies that could arise if legal responsibility, now held by the stations under license, should be divided between a network which offers programs and a station which accepts them as part of total service.

Finally, the Commission should consider the practical results of imposing a licensee standard on networks for Government review of their program schedules. A finding that a network's offered schedule serves the public interest would be meaningless if it were not implemented to assure the broadcast of that schedule to the public. Presumably such a finding would take into account the diversity and balance of the network schedule, in terms of its entertainment, news, information, public affairs, cultural and educational programming. But if different parts of this balanced pattern were eliminated by various stations, the 40 schedule which had been found to serve the public interest would be only a paper schedule, and not

a broadcast schedule.

On this basis, to give effect to its review of a network's program service, the Commission would have to assure that the affiliated stations carried the full network schedule; or substituted a program of equivalent type for any network program they reject. This is an example of how ill-conceived, unnecessary regulation can beget still more regulation. Although networks have assumed a responsibility for the schedule they offer, a legal responsibility should not be imposed on an offered schedule. The public is concerned only with what is broadcast, and the public-interest standard already applies to that.

For all these reasons, I believe the licensing of networks is unnecessary, inappropriate, and inconsistent with the present system of licensing stations, and would lead to a duplication of the Commission's procedures in reviewing program performance of stations. The latter function is the final subject to which I will turn. 

The Commission's Role in Programming 

As I indicated earlier, the issue of what role the Commission should play in relation to programming is one of the most sensitive problems in Government.

It raises the ultimate questions of principle and practicality: First, should the Commission undertake to influence a broadcaster's program service through direct regulation? And second, if the 41

Commission does so, on what basis can it establish rules for programming and how can it enforce such rules?

Views on these questions have ranged between two extremes: on the one hand, advocacy of detailed program regulation, and on the other, the position that the Commission should not consider programming in any way in exercising its licensing authority.

Those who urge the Government to regulate programming argue that audience reaction is not a sufficient guide to serving the public interest. They contend that Government authority should set goals for the service the public should have; that to spell out the nature of these goals, the Government should create and enforce specific program standards; and that these standards should be designed to force an increase in the amount of the less popular types of programming. 

The opposing view holds that it is not the Government's job to influence programming; that its role is to provide a framework of competition within which the public can exercise its choice. According to this view, such a role is fulfilled by granting licenses to qualified applicants who will compete in meeting public needs, and then refraining from any interference with the programming developed in competition for public choice. The advo- 

42 cates of this view warn that the only program standards the Commission could fix would be sub-

jective ones reflecting the Commissioners' personal tastes; and that any real enforcement of such standards would be impossible, since it would require detailed review of thousands of hours of programming for each licensee.

As between these opposing positions, I believe that the one arguing against Government influence on programming has far more merit than the one advocating program regulation. It looks to competition among multiple units as the effective force leading to satisfaction of audience interests.

I recognize, however, that there is not a multiplicity of facilities in all cases, and that the Commission has some role in relation to programming. 

This role should be exercised with the utmost selfrestraint, and only when the Commission must consider programming to settle a licensing question. It is a role, moreover, which should be based on a programming standard broad enough to encompass the great variety of different broadcasting situations that come before the Commission. 

On these premises, NBC offers the following answers to the questions posed in the Commission's order on what standards it should apply and what procedures it should follow in the licensing process: 

1. Since the broadcaster serves a public of widely different tastes and interests, under different and changing conditions, the standard for program- 43 ming should be the "standard of reasonableness”

-reasonableness in recognizing the interests to be served and in making a contribution toward serving them.

I propose this broad standard because it best fits the diverse and dynamic nature of the broadcast medium. More detailed standards-for example, rules to fix a ratio of different program typeswould be too inflexible to embrace all the ways in which the public interest can be properly served.

If the Commission were to lay down rules on what kinds of programs should be offered in what proportions and where they should be placed in the schedule, it would mean acting as the ultimate Solomon-the mediator and the arbiter of the tastes of 180 million people, and, in effect, the dictator of what some of those tastes should be.

Moreover, it would mean taking on the impossible task of reviewing the specifics of three years of programming for each broadcaster to see if such detailed rules had been followed. 

By contrast, a standard of reasonableness can be applied flexibly to the different ways in which the public interest can be served. For example, in markets with numerous radio stations, the public interest may be reasonably served by a station specializing in fine music, by one specializing in foreign language broadcasts, by another special- 44 izing in news and sports-or even the top forty popular songs-and by one or more offering a gen-

erally diversified service. Here balance tends to develop within the framework of the community, as each competitive operation seeks its own audience. In such a community, the standard of reasonableness can embrace each of these different types of operation as a service in the public interest.

In television, the concept of diversified balance within each station's schedule takes on greater significance. This is true because, as a rule, there are not enough different stations in a single market to foster a sufficient variety of different specialized services; and also because television's cost levels do not generally permit stations to operate on a small-audience, specialized-service basis. 

Thus, in most television situations, natural competitive forces themselves are likely to produce a diversified service by each broadcaster-the kind of service that also meets a standard of reasonableness. 

2. The Commission's licensing procedures should place primary emphasis on self-regulation and self-development by the broadcasters, because that is the most effective way of serving the public interest.

The Commission is called upon to apply the public interest standard in granting licenses and in renewing them. In the original grant of a license, where the Commission must choose between com- 45 peting applicants, it may have to consider signifi-

cant differences in their program proposals. But such differences should be regarded with great caution, for as the Commission recognizes, these program proposals are necessarily speculative and in many respects almost abstract. 

The applicant is not in a position to predict possible changes in the community, or in the competitive services already on the air, or in the types of programming available to him in the years to come. At best, he can describe how he has evaluated the broadcast needs of the community and the goals he has set toward meeting them. Given these natural limitations, program proposals in comparative hearings may be more important for the light they throw on the character, imagination, and zeal of the applicants than for what they indicate of the actual programming to be furnished under future conditions.

A somewhat different situation applies in renewal proceedings, where the applicant has behind him a body of actual experience of seeking, through trial and error, to develop a service in the public interest. Here the Commission should operate on the basic premise that by attracting and maintaining an audience in competition with other broadcast services and other media, the licensee has met a public need. This premise should be questioned only when there is evidence that the licensee has operated unreasonably in the light of his particular situation.

That, of course, raises the question of how the Commission can determine a violation of the broad standard of reasonableness. 

What I propose is a procedure giving the licensee the affirmative responsibility of describing what he has done by way of serving the public and why he has done it. Under such a procedure the applicant for renewal would be called upon to describe how he has sought to determine the audience needs and interests in the community; to define the interests he is seeking to satisfy, whether specialized or diversified; and to outline, in a narrative account, the type of service he has provided and proposes to provide to meet those audience needs.

For example, an affiliated television station might show how acceptance of a network service itself represented a responsible choice of programming, even though the licensee did not participate in any detail in deciding the composition of that service. 

The station might show how this basic network service was supplemented by programs from syndication sources; and also how its own program production arose out of specific local needs and the availability of local resources. At the same time, a specialized radio station could show how its particular specialty met a specific need of a community with a range of varied broadcast services.

In each case, the Commission would review the 47

application to determine whether it reflected a service unreasonable on its face. Disregard by a licensee of his own program projections without reasonable justification could be considered a departure from the standard I have proposed. 

I recognize that a renewal application on this basis cannot be precise. It may have to be amended by the licensee to reflect changing conditions during the licensed period, and the licensee cannot be held to it in exact detail. These are the imperfections inherent in trying to forecast a service that must be changing and dynamic if it is to respond to changing audience situations. Yet with all these imperfections, I believe the kind of procedure I have suggested would be more meaningful than one based on assessment of performance in terms of standardized program categories fixed by the Commission.

These categories are necessarily arbitrary and vague, and they have no direct bearing on the different ways in which the public interest can be served under different competitive and community conditions. For example, agricultural broadcasts may be of key importance to one audience and a waste of time to another. Presumably where they are important to listeners or viewers they will be furnished, since the broadcaster's purpose is to create and maintain an audience. The same consid- 

48 erations hold true in varying degrees of the other program classifications, and most of them also lend

themselves to wide differences of interpretation. 

I realize that a statistical showing based on fixed program categories is a convenient processing device, but there is no reason to retain it if the end result of the process is mechanical rather than meaningful. I also recognize that under the present procedure, renewal applicants are permitted to present any additional material they desire. But, in my opinion, this does not place the emphasis where it belongs. 

The procedure I am recommending would place the emphasis and the responsibility on the licensee with regard to his own conception of his public interest duties in his particular environment and what he does to fulfill that conception.

It would recognize that the public-interest concept cannot be reduced to standardize uniformity.

And it would reflect the fact that a service in the public interest is a changing response to living needs that may differ from one community to another, from one time to another, and from one service to another. 

To encourage broadcasters in exercising their own responsibility, I also suggest that the Commission give official recognition to the Television Code and to the Standards of Good Practice of the NAB. 

These Codes embody the program and advertising standards created by broadcasters themselves for their own regulation. By officially expressing its 49 view that these are appropriate standards, the

Commission would not be interfering with the process of self-regulation, but would be strengthening it. And by giving its official blessing to these 

Codes, it would help make them more effective and universal in their application. 

At the outset of my testimony, I observed there was no simple answer to the question of how to do a better job of fulfilling the great potential of the television medium. In trying to assess the present situation and point out the hopeful trends that should be encouraged, I have put forward no drastic measures, no dramatic proposals. I recognize that the impatient and the critical may well view this presentation as a case for the "status quo"; but this would be a mistaken view. For what I have tried to present is the case for progress through evolution.

I wish it were possible to present a panacea for all the problems of broadcasting, or even major reforms which would not impose greater harm than benefits. It is simple enough to think of any number of revolutionary innovations-as some of the witnesses in this proceeding have amply demonstrated. But when you study their side effects, their implications and their ultimate impact, you find that they are administrative nightmares, they exact a heavy price of freedom or they cripple the fundamental structure of a system of broadcasting which, for all its faults, has given this

country the most varied and vital broadcasting service in the world.

The problems that occupy us in these hearings are enormously complex. But the central issue, to my mind, is quite plain. It is the old and ever new issue of freedom. The very nature of freedom does not always permit things to get done as quickly and efficiently as we would like—either in business or in government. It would seem, for example, that the Communist system of doing things may have given the Russians an edge in rockets and missiles. Americans are rightfully concerned about any lag in this field and are determined to overcome it. But, I have heard no one suggest that to achieve our goal we must abandon our American ways of freedom and adopt the Russian methods of compulsion. The virtues of freedom far outweigh its defects-and freedom itself, of course, is our ultimate prize in the missile race.

In a free society such as ours, the public would not be served by imposing federal regulation on the program process of broadcasting, any more than the public would be served by imposing regulation on how the press fills its columns. 

In this competitive private enterprise system, the role that Government can most fittingly and effectively play is the enlightened role of encouraging self-regulation by broadcasters and stimulating their self-development. In this role, the 51 government must safeguard both freedom of

expression and freedom of choice; because in the interplay of these freedoms between the broadcasters and the public, lies the surest hope for the best service this great medium can render to society. I join most earnestly in the observation of an earlier witness in this proceeding who remarked: "Freedom itself is an imperfect instrument, but we are, thank God, stuck with it."
