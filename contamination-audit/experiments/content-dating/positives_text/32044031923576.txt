FOREWORD 

This book is based on the writer's experience in Washington, D. C., in the investigation and adjustment of claims under automobile insurance policies, and in the trial of law suits, pro and con, arising from automobile accidents.

It is in no sense a law book. The law of negligence, as applied to automobile accidents, has been thoroughly covered in several books. Those books, however, require a certain amount of knowledge preparatory to understanding them. A function of this book is to give the explanations necessary in order that those texts may be used intelligently. Then, too, this book covers some legal matters which are important to the adjuster, but which are not properly a part of those texts. Except as noted, this book confines itself to the everyday work of the adjuster. 

This book attempts to indicate all of the steps which may be taken, and considerations which may be given to a claim. Of course, such full treatment will be called for in but a few of the cases handled by an adjuster each year. In the usual course of work all of these steps and considerations need not and indeed cannot be given to each claim; but an ad-

juster familiar with all steps and considerations is in a position to select those applicable to and expedient to use in connection with the claim at hand. Viewed in this light, the reader will understand that the suggestion that a certain thing should be done, or should not be done, is qualified by the judgment of the adjuster as to whether he deems it advisable to carry his investigation to a point at which the suggestion will apply.

Note should be made that in regard to a few matters, the treatment of the claim depends on company policy. These matters are so indicated. Any suggestion in the text in regard to them should be subordinated to company policy.

Chapter I THE ADJUSTER'S POSITION 

HE ADJUSTER OF. AUTOMOBILE 

accident claims has two basic objectives: to render to the policyholder his contract rights, and to protect the interests of the insuring company. 

He and the agent are the two contact points which the company has with the public. The agent has worked diligently to build up the list of policyholders. 

It is the duty of the adjuster to serve these policyholders. He must so treat them that they become welded to the company and become so well disposed towards the company that they will voluntarily aid the company to expand. 

The perfunctory adjustment of claims does not feed the vital forces which propel life. Adjustment work directed by intelligence and executed with understanding gives the glow of life which comes from constructive effort. So, never neglect an opportunity to boost the agent who wrote the policy, and the company back of the policy. Remember [1]

always that companies are but men plus money. The men are very important. All, as a unit, prosper as the company prospers. Company or unit success is their aim. They rely on your hearty cooperation to help build a structure so firm that it will prosper for their lifetime. 

You will contact in your work many people, not now policyholders, who need insurance protection. 

Don't fail to inform the agent that these particular people are prospects. If a policyholder seems dissatisfied with your work and cannot grasp your viewpoint, don't fail to so advise the agent, so that he may have an opportunity to save a policyholder. Cooperate to retain and attain.

In protecting the interests of the company, it is well not to dwell too heavily on money interest. The greatest asset of the company is good will. Much money and effort have been expended to build up the good will which the company now enjoys. If your dealings with the public are not fair, the small sums of money which you save will in no wise compensate for the good will which you destroy. Company competition, with the average policyholder, is not on the basis of company size, but is on the basis of good will, service, and pleasure in doing business with the com- [2]

pany representatives. You best protect and promote the company's interests by prompt, efficient, and courteous service to policyholders and claimants, and by fair treatment to all people whom you contact. 

In rendering service on claims, you cannot, of course, be everywhere at one time, nor can you spend large amounts of time on minor matters. Consequently, your claims must be divided into classes according to importance. Suggestion is offered that claims be divided into three classes as to promptness in handling: (a) Personal injury claims; immediate attention regardless of whether, or not, the first report indi cates liability. 

(b) Property damage claims; with substantial damage, prompt attention, regardless of liability. (c) Property damage claims; with minor damage, as prompt attention as other work warrants. 

The amount of time which it is practical to spend on a claim will vary with its monetary importance. 

Some claims will not require exhaustive investigation, while others will require the full survey outlined in the pages which follow. The adjuster must exercise sound judgment in the most effective use of his time, so that he neither wastes time on non-essen- [3]

tials regarding minor claims, nor overlooks any detail in his investigation of major claims.

There are three practices worth noting which are indulged in by some adjusters; practices which are costing the insurance companies a great amount of good will.

The first of these practices is failure to act promptly in acknowledging claims, letters, and telephone calls. These practices of evasion breed difficulties. If the adjuster is not ready to negotiate in regard to the claim, he can, at least, acknowledge receipt of the claim and communications in regard to it. When a claimant leaves his telephone number to be called, the adjuster should make the return call rather than ignore it, and thus force the claimant to phone until he catches the adjuster in the office. 

The second practice is to ignore, or refuse to pay, just claims in very small amounts. This practice is adopted on the theory that the loss is so small that the claimant will not take any legal action to force payment. Such theory is correct, but the claimant harbors resentment produced by this practice.

The third practice is to offer a claimant who has a valid property damage claim in which there is liability, a partial settlement, on the theory that if [4]

the claimant sues, he will have to pay his attorney one-third of the amount recovered, and he might just as well allow the company to deduct a third, as his net recovery will be the same in either event. 

This theory is so sound that it more often works than not.

The possible benefit of these three practices is that the adjuster may save the company a little money. 

This, however, is not the end of the matter. A claimant treated in these ways acquires a very poor opinion of the company making the adjustment. Often he does not know the name of the company making the adjustment. Whether he knows the name of the company, or not, he stores up resentment against the entire insurance business, and he becomes a "knocker" of insurance companies.

It is decidedly not fair for a few adjusters, or a few companies, by following these practices, to give the entire insurance business a bad name. So, do not be guilty of these practices unless the home office instructs you to handle claims in this way. If there is any question in your mind as to what policy the company wants followed in regard to these practices, you should make special inquiry. 

If a source of replacement of money unjustly [5]

retained through these practices is necessary before they are discontinued, it is suggested that the replacement be effected by contesting more property damage claims arising from accidents which occur at uncontrolled intersections, as more of these claims are paid than the claimants can successfully litigate. 

In dealing with policyholders, the adjuster must keep in mind the feelings of the policyholder; and although he may never have met him before, he must take a friendly attitude. To the policyholder, an accident most likely is a rare and upsetting episode. 

To him an accident is very personal. So, keep him posted as to what you are doing, and never fail to advise him what disposition you have made of the claim in which he is involved. 

It is unfortunately true that a few policyholders are calloused, and after an accident dump the affair into your lap, and take the attitude that now it is solely your business. If you meet any such people, encourage them to take an interest in the accident, as later their active participation may be required in resisting a suit.

The adjuster owes a duty to the company to settle claims on a fair basis, and not on a good-fellow basis. 

He works at a distance from any supervision, and is [6]

not checked closely on details of his settlements. 

Then, too, he does not handle dollars. He handles checks. The same size, color, and form of the check covers every size of claim payment. It shows, without expressing, dollars. For these reasons an adjuster finds it easy to be careless of dollars and cents. He fails to keep posted on low quoting garages; he favors certain garages; he fails to get maximum parts discounts; neglects to deduct depreciation where that is proper; and in many small ways consistently approves payments in sums a little larger than the minimum. The total wastage in the course of a year through these small extras will amaze him.

Claims coming for settlement from other offices are equally important with claims originating at the adjuster's office. The fact that any loss on account of the accident will form a part of the loss ratio of another office is no reason to neglect the investigation, or efforts to close it at the minimum sum possible.

In personal injury cases, an adjuster can often have the expense of X-rays avoided, or reduced; can pay correct wage losses, instead of taking claimant's word for rate of earnings; he can perhaps have the number of doctor's visits kept within the needs of [7]

the case; in compromising, he can get the claimant's demand nearer his own objective amount before moving his figure upward. A saving state of mind will start savings, and practice in effecting savings will show the way to increase them.

In the course of his work the adjuster will come in contact with policyholders who are bad risks on account of physical or sight defects, use of intoxicants, lack of nerve coordination, etc. These risks should not have been insured. In other instances, the adjuster will find that the policyholder is an entirely satisfactory risk, but that there are other people who make considerable use of the automobile, and that these people are bad risks, covered by the omnibus clause, and people of whom the agent knew nothing at the time the policy was issued. If the adjuster finds conditions which indicate to him that the risk is bad, he should report the facts to the home office for proper handling. It is presumed, of course, that the adjuster will not attempt to exercise censorship over the business on the books of the company. 

When, however, the adjuster becomes convinced that any risk is a bad one, he should not hesitate to report to the home office the conditions which have come to his attention. [8]

Chapter II PRE-INVESTIGATION WORK 

HE ADJUSTER WILL, PRIOR 

to investigating any accident, make himself thoroughly familiar with the policy form, the legal meaning of each paragraph in the policy, and the scope of the coverage. While the insurance world calls its forms "policies" and prints them in a distinctive style, yet they are in reality printed contracts whereby, in consideration of the premium paid by, and the promises made by the named assured, the company promises to do certain things after an accident occurs. 

On account of the fact that the company selected the wording used in this contract, the company is presumed to have inserted in the contract all of the matters which it wanted included, and to have included that matter in the form in which it wanted the matter included. Consequently, in case any of the terms or conditions of the policy are ambiguous, the interpretation of the terms and conditions most favorable to the assured is taken as the true meaning; [9]

in other words, the policy is construed against the company.

The contract expresses all of the terms and conditions to which the company and the assured agreed. 

The assured is entitled only to those things which the company, in the policy, agreed to provide, as limited by, and subject to, the restrictions and provisions set forth in the policy. 

If the agent represented that other promises or conditions, not contained in the policy, would be effective, these promises do not bind the company, as the assured is presumed to read the policy and to know its contents. If, on delivery, the policy terms differ from the terms as represented by the agent, or if the policy contains errors, the assured is obligated to return the policy for correction, or cancellation.

The adjuster's duty is to see that the provisions contained in the policy are carried out, and he has no right to vary from them without the consent of the home office. He has no right, duty, or authority to alter, add to, or subtract from the terms of the policy, or to waive any of the conditions of the policy. 

By knowing all of the details of the policy, the adjuster can find any flaws existing in the coverage, and can better convince people with whom he comes [10]

in contact that his acts are at all times in full conformity with the contract made between the company and the assured.

If certain terms of the policy are found to represent a mutual mistake by the agent and the assured, the assured can take proper steps after a loss to have the policy reformed to express correctly the agreement which the agent and the assured understood was to be put into effect. If the mistake was by one party only, the contract cannot be reformed. The adjuster has no authority to act on the policy as it is claimed it should be; but, until instructed to the contrary, must act on the policy as he finds it. 

An adjuster will occasionally have a claim which, on account of error, misrepresentation, or misunderstanding, does not come within the terms and conditions of the policy as written. In such a case, the adjuster should refer the whole matter to the company for a decision on whether the claim shall be accepted, in spite of its failure to be within the coverage of the policy.

It may happen that an accident is reported before any policy is written, and assertion is made that the risk was bound by the agent at a certain time. Should an assertion be made that a certain automobile was [11]

bound, and such assertion is admitted by an agent who writes exclusively for one company, then that company's standard policy is deemed to be in force, together with such standard riders as the agent had authority to issue, and did agree to issue. If, however, the risk was bound by an agent who is authorized to write policies for more than one company, a serious question arises as to which, if any, company represented by the agent is bound on the risk. Thorough investigation must be made to determine whether the company charged with the loss was actually bound on the risk.

After learning thoroughly the contents of the policy, the adjuster is prepared to proceed to function under it. The first step after receiving notice of an accident is, of course, to check the policy or the data sheet with the subject of the loss; such things as vehicle and engine number, ownership, etc. After this has been done, the adjuster can begin to gather the facts surrounding the accident. [12]

Chapter III SECURING AND ESTABLISHING FACTS 

AT 

T THE SCENE OF AN 

accident the passerby inquires, "What happened?" 

A casual answer usually suffices. 

The insurance company likewise inquires, "What happened?" This inquiry is directed to one man, the adjuster. The answer, in order to be of value, must be complete, and it must be accurate.

The passerby is satisfied to hear some person's conclusion. Not so the company. The company wants to know the facts on which a sound conclusion can be reached as to the cause of the accident, and its liability under its policy. 

So, the essential and first duty of the adjuster is to find the facts; the second duty, to report the facts clearly and accurately; and third, as to accidents within his authority to settle, to reach correctly a conclusion as to liability under the policy, and the amount of loss.

If the accident is serious, it is expedient and eco- [13]

nomical to make a prompt and diligent effort to secure all of the facts. The failure to secure one pertinent fact may mean that the company will pay a large claim when it should not make payment, or that it will refuse to pay a claim when it should make payment, with the result that costs of unnecessary law suits are added.

In your search for facts, you must train yourself to guard against two prevalent errors. The first is to accept as a fact a conclusion masquerading as a fact; and the second is to fail to recognize a multiple fact concealing within itself many individual facts. 

As to the first, we know that conclusions are easier to formulate than to substantiate. A conclusion, in theory, is the fulfillment of a sound reasoning process applied to definite facts. Without sound reasoning ability, conclusions must be faulty. Few people have any training in reasoning. Your reasoning on a given state of facts is more important, and more apt to be correct, than the reasoning of the witness. If you find a witness who states a conclusion, pry into the facts on which he bases his conclusion and, after you have his facts, discard his conclusion. For example, a statement may be made that "he was going too fast." This is obviously a conclusion, as ideas of what [14]

is fast vary. Ask where the automobile was when he first saw it; what was the speed at the time; where was the witness; did the automobile change speed; if so, where was it then; and what was speed at time of impact. Such a line of questioning may disclose that "too fast" referred to a time much prior to the accident, and that the speed at the time of the accident was reasonable. Such questioning may reveal that the witness has no recollection of the facts on which his conclusion was based, sufficiently precise to be of any value.

As an illustration of the importance of guarding against conclusions masquerading as facts, consider one obvious example. Who is the owner of the automobile involved? The automobile is registered in and title issued in a certain name. Such title is only prima facie evidence of ownership. Examination may show that the holder of the title has no real ownership in the automobile, or that he has only a part ownership. A release signed by the titleholder cannot be attacked by other true part owners, so it is safe and proper to pay damages to a titleholder. 

However, knowledge of the true owner, or owners, may be of great importance for purposes of defense, if suit results from the accident. [15]

As to the second error, we know that precision of expression is an ability enjoyed by few people. There is a tendency to group as one fact matters which examination will disclose comprise several facts. An instance of such a multiple fact may be that the claimant "came across the street. This statement is true, but conceals a whole group of facts. To get the individual facts you must learn: Did claimant walk, run, or trot? How did he start? What was his speed? Did speed change? Did he hesitate before starting? Did he hesitate at any time? Did he cross alone? Where were other people with relation to his position? Did he carry, push, or tug anything? 

Did he limp or stumble? Etc? The basic individual facts are what you seek.

Your investigating work will deal largely with time and distance. These are two elements which the average person cannot be relied upon to express accurately. Instead of taking an estimate as to distance, it is better to train yourself to be an expert at judging distance, and then, when occasion arises for asking a witness regarding distance, ask the witness to pick out some object about as far away as the distance he is trying to express. This observation will be approximately accurate. You, using your expert [16]

ability, will then estimate the distance in the proper footage. Likewise, in judging time. Some people will estimate an interval of half a minute as three minutes. You can check the witness's estimate of time, if small, by checking with your watch while the witness lets approximately the interval pass which he is trying to express.

In this day we deal with a public which is accident conscious. It is sensible for an adjuster to approach the investigation of any serious accident with the thought in mind that if the company does not settle the accident, a law suit will result. It is necessary, then, that the adjuster not only get the facts, but also that he get the means of establishing the facts by proper proof.

Some of the mechanics of doing this will be covered in detail later. The point to be noted now is that the adjuster is not a usable witness; he will not be called upon to testify to facts except under most unusual circumstances. Consequently, no matter how firmly he may be convinced regarding the cause of, or the liability for, or the facts concerning an accident, his conviction is of no value as legal evidence. If, then, he is not to be called to testify as to his conclusions and the facts on which he bases his conclusions, he [17]

must procure some person to give evidence of every fact which he used in reaching his conclusion. He may see a tell-tale skid mark. If so, he must secure a witness who will be able to testify regarding the mark. He may hear an admission against interest. 

If so, he must get a witness to procure the same admission.

If facts be considered as nails, then the adjuster must not only gather the nails, but he must also procure the hammer with which to drive them home. [18]

The adjuster needs to add a diagram, of his own making, accurately showing the location of fixed objects and measurements. If a wide discrepancy is found to exist between the diagram made from a witness's recollection and the one made by the adjuster, then the witness's diagram should be reviewed with the witness, as, making use of the accurate diagram, the witness may desire to make material alterations in his statement.

After a diagram has been completed, the adjuster can reduce to narrative form the statement of the witness, and have the witness sign the written statement. The witness should have an opportunity to [24]

read over the statement in its final form, and make any alteration or clarification. After this is done, a notation should be made that he has read the statement, and that the statement is true to the best of his knowledge. Then have the maker sign the statement below this acknowledgment.

The statement which you secure may some day have special value, for in case a witness at trial attempts to change his story, the statement may be used to show that he made a different statement when the facts were clear in his mind.

Very often statements are taken on company forms, or on paper which bears the company name or some printing which shows that the statement was taken by an insurance company. In those states which do not require compulsory insurance prior to registration of every automobile, every effort is made to keep from a jury knowledge that an insurance company is defending the case. Under these circumstances, a statement taken on paper which ties the statement to some insurance company, cannot be safely used or exposed to view, as it will show to the jury that there is an insurance company in the case. 

So, it is preferable to take signed statements on plain paper. [25]

Chapter V USE OF PHOTOGRAPHS 

SINCE 

INCE EARLIEST TIMES, the most efficient means of transmitting thoughts have been pictures and diagrams. 

How can we better convey the thought of what part of an automobile was struck than by a picture of the automobile? Many pictures will so show the damage done as to indicate clearly the nature of the collision which produced the damage. For instance, if assertion is made that one car streaked across in front of another, pictures should prove whether the assertion is true. If it is true, we shall find the front of one car stripped crosswise, and the side of the other car scraped lengthwise. If, however, the damage to one car is mashing in of the front, and to the other, mashing in of the side, we know and can prove that the accident did not occur as claimed. 

Then again, how can we better describe the conditions existing at the scene of an accident than by a photograph? [26]

Juries are as avid for pictures as children for the colored funny papers. 

If photographs are taken for future contingent use, it is important that they be taken by some one who can testify regarding them. The adjuster should not take them.

As photographs are taken after the accident has occurred, it is essential that some person be secured who is able to testify that the photograph represents conditions as they existed at the time of the accident. 

The assured, or some person familiar with the subject matter of the picture, is qualified to give this testimony. The assured is the preferable person to take the photographs and to testify regarding them. If the accident happens at night, in territory unfamiliar to the assured, or if the picture is taken a long time after the accident, some nearby resident should be located who is willing to testify that the photograph represents conditions existing at the time of the accident. 

In taking photographs of an automobile, the "shot" can usually be taken from a position to show the license number. As the owner can, without trouble, identify a photograph of his automobile, anyone can take the photograph of an automobile. [27]

If the photograph desired is of skid marks on the pavement, then someone who knows that a certain automobile made the marks must point them out to the photographer, and be able to testify that the marks photographed were the marks made by the automobile at the time of the accident. In case no person involved in the accident is able to testify that a certain car made the marks, then, as a substitute, someone may be found who can testify that the marks were not present just before the accident, but were present immediately after the accident.

On account of the distortion which occurs in taking photographs of flat surfaces, it is difficult to secure a usable photograph of marks left on the surface of a street if the "shot" is taken near the street level.

This distortion does not occur if the photograph is taken from above. So, if possible, in taking such photographs "shoot" down from an upper window of a nearby building, or from a tree. 

Photographs showing the automobile at the scene of the accident are good evidence if taken before the automobile is moved, but are not good if taken at a later time. Posed photographs and moving pictures are often of great value, but these should be taken only on special occasions. [28]

Photographs have this additional value: that when shown to a witness the witness has his recollection refreshed and clarified so that his statement is more definite and accurate than it otherwise would be. 

It is surprising that, in view of the negligible cost of snapshots, every adjuster does not carry a small camera for miscellaneous use, and have assureds and others snap views which will illumine his report. 

These may later be of great value. 

Each photograph should be marked for future reference, to show who took it, who, if anyone, identified the subject matter to the taker, the hour and date when taken, and the approximate spot from which it was taken. [29]

Chapter VI REAL EVIDENCE INVEST NVESTIGATION SHOULD 

disclose whether there is any real evidence which can be, or should be, preserved. By "real evidence" is meant specific objects which can be seen and judged. 

For instance, if the assured states that the accident was caused by a wheel coming off the automobile, then the wheel, axle, and nuts of the automobile should be examined to see whether there are stripped threads or the like, which show on examination to have been the cause of the accident. These parts should be preserved. If a stone is thrown at the car and causes the accident, preserve the stone. If metal parts are broken, they might well be preserved to show what force was necessary to break them. The assured can store such objects until the need for them is ended. Sometimes it is well to have the assured delay the repairs to his automobile, or the sale of the automobile, until after the need of the automobile as real evidence is ended. [30]

In those accidents occasioned by a pedestrian's walking into the side of an automobile, the real evidence consists of dusted portions of the automobile, and perhaps hand prints. A photograph taken promptly will preserve the evidence of dusted portions, and a wax impression of hand marks may show finger prints directly connecting the marks with the claimant.

Dirt which has fallen from an automobile is convincing real evidence of the point of collision. 

Objects knocked or broken from an automobile are valuable real evidence when some one, other than the adjuster, can testify as to the exact time and spot when they were discovered.

If any objects of real evidence cannot be preserved, a photograph of them should be taken.

The value of real evidence lies in the fact that it is permanent, truthful, and the statements of witnesses must square with it. [31]

Chapter VII CONVERSATIONS 

FEW 

EW ACCIDENTS HAPPEN 

without an aftermath of conversation in which the occupants of the automobiles take part. It is vitally important to find out what all parties said. 

Ordinarily each of the parties, when asked what was said, will voluntarily state only those bits of conversation which stand out in their minds as highlights. Then, too, persons may have made some damaging admissions which they regret having made, and which they wish to gloss over. So, there is more chance of securing a recital of a damaging statement, or admission, from one who heard it, rather than from the one who made it. If each witness is asked for an account of what was said, a fairly accurate account can be pieced together which will give definite color to the accident.

The claimant is almost certain to remember any damaging admission made by the assured, or the assured's operator.

The account as given by the [32]

claimant cannot be accepted at face value. Before being accepted as true, the alleged maker of a damaging statement should be given an opportunity to deny it, to clarify it, to correct it, or to explain it. 

The same holds true as to statements which are reported to have been made by the claimant. Check them in the same manner.

All damaging statements are of value in properly deciding the question of liability, and should the accident become the basis of a lawsuit, these statements may be important evidence in the trial of the case. 

Not all statements, however, are admissible as evidence in court. 

Statements made by the litigants are admissible because any person making a statement damaging to his interests should naturally be bound to stand on that statement. The person charged can, of course, deny having made the statement, and can, if he admits the statement, explain what he meant by the statement. Damaging statements are called admissions against interest.

In appraising the value to be placed on an admission against interest, a distinction must be made between admissions of fact and admission of conclusion. It is most difficult to explain away the [33]

damaging aspect of an admission of a fact. If, however, the admission is of a conclusion, the witness can explain the basis of his conclusion, and can often do this in such a way as to remove any damaging aspect of the admission. For example, the admission may be, "I was in the wrong." This is a conclusion.

The witness may explain that at the time he made the statement he thought that the other party had the right of way, but he has since discovered that there was a stop sign against the other party, and for that reason he now knows that the accident was not his fault. This explanation, if believed, entirely overcomes the apparent value of the admission. 

So many accidents occur when a person other than the assured is driving, that we must consider what use can be made of statements made by such operators. If a person is engaged in the work of another or is performing some duty for another, then that person is an agent in fact. In some other cases the operator of the automobile is, by operation of law, made the agent. Such a case is where a member of the family was operating the family automobile, and the claim is made against the head of the family. In this case, by operation of the family purpose doctrine, which is explained later, the member of the family [34]

operating the automobile is deemed to be the agent of the head of the family in operating the family automobile. 

Damaging statements made by agents are admissions against their own interests, and against the interests of the principal. They can be used against the agent, but they cannot be used in court against the principal. This must be true as the agency of the agent does not include the duty to make admissions against the interest of the principal. Such statements are entirely outside the authority of the agent.

In spite of what has just been written, the statements of agents are more often brought into a trial than not. The statement is offered and admitted, not to prove the facts of the statement, but to impeach the testimony of the agent. Suppose that the agent testifies that no conversation was held, or that only certain conversation was had. Then opponents can show that there was conversation, what it was, and give the witness' version of the details. While this testimony is admissible to prove that the agent lied, concealed, stated the conversation incorrectly, or has a deficient memory, yet the result is to get the damaging statement into the case, and the jury will not [35]

take the trouble to confine the statement to the purpose for which it was accepted in evidence. 

An admission against the interest of the principal made by an agent is admissible, and binds the principal if it was made regarding matters known to the principal, was made in the presence of the principal, and he was given an opportunity to repudiate it, but he did not repudiate it. Such an instance would arise in a case where a friend was operating the automobile while the owner was in it. If the friend admitted "We were speeding," and the owner did not dispute this statement, the statement would be admitted on the theory that the owner acquiesced in the truth of the admission. 

If your investigation reveals the fact that a damaging statement, or admission, was made you must anticipate the possibility that the maker of it will deny having made it. So, it is important that you secure from the witness and record the exact words used, and also the name of any person who heard, or may have heard, the statement, or admission.

There is one class of statements which require special mention. The statements considered above are strictly admissions against interest, which can be made at any time by the person charged. The state- [36]

ments now under consideration may be made by anyone and may be statements which are self-serving to the person making the statement. Technically they are spoken of as part of the res gestae, which means that they are so intimately connected with the accident that they are considered as a part of it. 

This class of statements includes statements made under such stress of emotion that mind operations do not consciously function in producing them, and, also, statements made by a person rendered unconscious by the accident, and made so promptly after recovering a degree of consciousness sufficient to enable them to speak, that they have no time in which to call upon the conscious mind to formulate the statement. In either instance, the statement, or fact, is considered as talking through the person, rather than the person talking about the fact. Such a statement or exclamation must be of a matter pertinent to the accident, and the statement, or exclamation, is evidence of the matter stated. 

These statements, when made before, or at the time of the accident, are spontaneous reactions to greatly excited emotions, and might be illustrated by supposing that a woman is standing on the sidewalk about to cross a street, and sees an automobile come [37]

rapidly to the crosswalk, and be about to strike a person crossing. She cries, "STOP!" Her exclamation becomes, under these circumstances, an important fact in the case, as it shows that she knew that the pedestrian was in, or about to be in, the path of the automobile, and the operator will find it hard to explain why he did not know the same thing and regulate his automobile accordingly. If the defense be that the person crossing unexpectedly stepped in front of the automobile, you will note how damaging this exclamation is to that defense.

If the statement is made after the accident, then it must be spontaneous, and must be made immediately upon regaining the senses, and, as stated, it must be made so promptly that the speaker has had no time to reflect and formulate a statement. Illustration of this form of statement might be, "You went too fast," or, "Didn't you see him?" Any such statement tends to show negligence on the part of the operator.

As a final comment on statements, let it be said that there is nothing quite so disconcerting to the company lawyer at the trial of an accident case as to have a previously unheard of admissible statement, or exclamation sprung as a surprise. If the conver- [38]

sation of the parties is disclosed in the investigation, such surprise, unless perjury, will not be possible, and if such statement, or exclamation, is offered as perjury, the attorney will have means at hand to meet it. 

An accident cannot be said to have been properly investigated unless the conversations and exclamations of all of the parties are disclosed and reported, or the fact established that there was no conversation or exclamation. [39]

Many more instances might be given as illustration of the proposition that investigation of the use of which the automobile was being put at the time of the accident will disclose many times that the use being made of the automobile relieves the owner and the company from liability. 

There are many circumstances regarding use which free the company from liability under the policy, such as the trip on which the accident happened may have been a joint enterprise, or the trip may have been made by a chauffeur in pursuit of his own interests, and not in the course of his employment. 

These instances are cited to illustrate the great importance of determining definitely what use was being made of the automobile at the time of his accident.

The principal service item is aiding the assured in collecting damages done to his property, or person. 

This form of service is an evil, apparently a necessary evil, which has developed on account of the desire of the agent to bind the assured to his agency through the gratuitous rendering by the agent of a favor in a field with which the agent is familiar, but with which the assured is unfamiliar. The performance of this service by the agent is but a natural and logical development of his desire to serve and sell. 

The company can hardly object if an agent elects to perform such service.

It followed naturally that the adjuster should be [44]

drawn in to cooperate with the agent in rendering this service, and, as the activities of the adjuster became so usual as to become expected, the agent tended to withdraw and leave the full burden of this service on the adjuster.

The rendering of this service has fundamental objections. In the first place, if the efforts to secure damages are carried beyond the time that the liability is disputed, the adjuster by attempting to secure settlement of a disputed claim, is practicing law without authority. In the second place, this service is a form of substitution for collision insurance, or personal accident insurance. The more success the adjuster has in collecting damages, the less need is felt, by the assured, to carry collision and personal accident insurance.

The effort of the agent or adjuster in behalf of the assured discloses to the claimant that there probably is insurance in force. The disclosure of this fact tends to encourage counterclaims and suits which would not otherwise arise.

The adjuster, armed with knowledge of the danger in attempting to render this service, can, if his company approves, cooperate with the agent in such a way as to avoid possible pitfalls. In order to avoid [45]

pitfalls, the adjuster should get the facts clearly before making a move. If the facts disclose freedom from negligence on the part of the assured, the adjuster can render aid up to the point where the other party disclaims liability, or refuses to act. If, however, the circumstances of the accident are such as to indicate the possibility of negligence by the assured, then the adjuster should refuse any request by the assured to render aid, and should frankly, but courteously, state to the assured that he cannot cooperate with the assured in making demands for damages for the reason that to do so might stir up a counterclaim, or a suit.

The adjuster can render valuable service to the assured who has suffered a property damage loss by arranging garage discount on parts and repairs. This service costs nothing, and is greatly appreciated. 

The adjuster can also render service by checking towing, storage, medical and other bills, and advising the assured whether the charges are reasonable. He can in many little ways render aid to the assured, but he should keep always in mind that his suggestions and aids are properly made available to the assured only up to the point of probability that the company's interests may become adversely affected. Chapter X SOME HUMAN TRAITS 

IT 

Ꭲ IS INTERESTING ΤΟ 

observe a small child with a watch try to determine what makes the watch tick. Eventually the child finds the reason.

It is equally interesting to observe what makes people tick. If an adjuster succeeds in learning the reason he will be a great success. Some of the processes are indicated here for the purpose of securing the interest of the adjuster in this subject so that he may observe the value of making a special study in this field.

The two prime motives behind human behavior are self-preservation and self-perpetuation. Continual exceptions to natural response to the first motive produce the majority of automobile accidents. However, it is still true that human traits, most of which are traceable to these prime motives, are dominant in the daily life of men and women. 

Let us consider a few human traits, and how the [47]

adjuster can adjust his conduct to them so as to aid him in reaching his objective of ascertaining the true facts. 

Humans dislike to admit error. 

The less excuse for the error, the more we dislike to admit it. Admitting error tends to damage the ego. We all like to think, and to think that others think, that we are at least ordinarily wise. An admission, in a particular case, that we were not wise is an admission of imperfection, and being imperfect and desiring to impress others that we are not as imperfect as we really are, we naturally act to make the best impression possible. The smaller the man, the harder it is to overcome this natural trait. With a realization of this truth, the adjuster adopts tactics to meet it. 

This is done by assuring the man that the worst isn't so bad as he thinks it is; that the error he made is common; that many people have done the same thing. Make him feel that he isn't a dunce, as compared with other people; that his act is no cause for shame, and he will loosen up and tell just what happened.

The trait of fear is one of the strongest. This trait enters into investigations of automobile accidents usually in one of two sets of circumstances. One is [48]

fear that an admission, no matter to whom made, will have a damaging effect if criminal proceedings grow out of the accident. The other is fear by wife, child, or employee that something unpleasant by reprimand or punishment will result if the truth becomes known by husband, parent, or employer. If the cause of the fear is removed, the truth probably will be revealed. If the adjuster senses that this element of fear is involved, it is good to get the witness away from the person he fears, and conduct the examination in a confidential manner. 

Another human trait is a tendency to forget promptly disagreeable facts. This trait enables us to retain a degree of sanity. You have known of people who had such vivid impressions of an accident that they were unable to talk coherently about it. A little later, you see them again, and the memory has become deadened so that they can talk coherently. 

Later still, the memory grows very dim, until the time comes when with many people it is impossible for them to give an account of the accident sufficiently accurate to be relied upon. This dying of memory impressions is an important fact, and is one good reason why investigations should be made promptly, and before the forgetting process sets in. [49]

The report which is made after the interview can be used at any later time to refresh the recollection, but if the facts are not in the report of course they will not be available to refresh the recollection. So the report should cover every important detail disclosed during the interview. 

It is human for an adult to try to place blame on some one else. As one man said, "That is the reason many men marry," to have some one to blame for everything that goes wrong. This trait is developed in childhood as self-protection, so it is really based on self-preservation. If a child were so treated that he had no reason to fear punishment, he would, as a matter of course, tell the truth; but the child quickly learns that if the event is not his fault, or if his parents think that it is not his fault, then they do not punish him. He learns to use this knowledge for his own protection. So many people carry this trait into adult life that it is quite commonly met with. If the witness starts to blame the other fellow, let him continue until he exhausts himself. If you avoid any suggestion that he was in the wrong until after he has exhausted his efforts to place the blame elsewhere, it is quite possible that he will be [50]

ready to discuss the facts in such a way as to prompt him to confess that he was perhaps in the wrong. 

Few people take any pleasure from being told that they did wrong. We may damn ourselves heartily, but we hate to have other people damn us. It is necessary for an adjuster to tell many a claimant that he was in the wrong, but, if possible, the person should be led to that conclusion before the conclusion is uttered.

Growing out of the trait to lay the blame elsewhere is the trait of sticking to a story. This developes in childhood after the child learns that as long as the parent is in doubt the feared punishment is delayed.

The child soon learns that it is best for his immediate interests to stick to his story. After he reaches a certain age, and becomes conscious that there is stigma attached to being a liar, he has another reason inducing him to stick to his story as by so doing he lessens the damage to his ego. Expressed otherwise, he feels that he is not a liar until he admits it, so while he knows that he lied, he believes that the world will not be sure that he lied until he admits the lie. 

A proposed treatment in overcoming this trait is covered in the next paragraph. 

Humans must save face. By this is meant that [51]

when a person must reverse position, suffer defeat, or back down, a suitable justification must be at hand in order that the result can be arrived at without damage to the ego. We call this "offering a way out." The person escapes being caught in an untenable position, and such a manner of escape is offered that the person feels as well after the escape as before. 

One excellent way to meet this situation is to offer new facts.

Suppose that a person takes a certain wrong position. If some new fact, or some twist of the facts is offered to him with the question, "Now if you had known that, wouldn't you have looked at the matter differently?" the means of escape with face is at hand, and he will probably accept the opportunity to get right.

This same trait must be considered in making settlements after excessive demands have been made by a claimant, after an accident which it is desired to settle amicably. If figures not used by the claimant are presented to him on items which go to make up his claim, it is apparent that the excessive demand is not his fault, but the fault of the persons who gave him his base figures. By offering more exact and equitable base figures, the claimant can compromise, [52]

and yet feel that he is thoroughly justified in accepting less.

Lying may be regarded as violation of a trait. 

Truth is inherent. The easiest thing to do, and the most natural, is to tell the truth. Lying is an acquired ability, which in some people is developed to an art. In telling the truth, memory works but the creative mind rests. A liar must call upon the creative mind to create the lie, and, at the same time, must examine the memory to ascertain which remembered facts he will use, and which he will replace with created facts.

After he has formulated the lie, usually a mixture of remembered and created facts, he must store in his memory the tale as told, which requires him to remember accurately which facts were true, and which facts he created. This process is complex and likely to be faulty. The weakness comes in the confusion in the memory in keeping the true story and the related tale, consisting of a mixture of true and created facts as distinct entities while at the same time keeping them properly co-related.

The true facts are more firmly fastened in the memory because they reached the memory through at least one of the senses, whereas the created facts. [53]

senses. were not impressed on the memory by any one of the On being requestioned, a liar has a double and difficult memory problem, so it is no wonder that he gets mixed up and caught in his lie. He is best caught by bringing to his attention some fact which escaped his notice at the time the lie was formulated. 

He must place this new fact in his tale without destroying the tale, and this requires him to associate it properly with the true facts and the created facts without destroying the formulated harmony of those facts as joined together in his false tale. This necessity of properly placing new facts explains why a liar answers questions slowly. 

Lying must not be confused with romancing.

There is the element of intent back of a lie, while intent has nothing to do with romancing. This trait, which is found in many people, seems to date from birth. These people have minds which work very rapidly, and they acquire a habit of letting the creative mind work without interruption, rather than interrupt the active mind and call on memory. 

These people do not want to be liars, but have so allowed the active mind to become habituated to working without the memory, or in loose contact with the memory, that they have become romancers. [54]

It is almost impossible to have any confidence in the statements of such persons, once they are classified. 

It is human to take sides. This trait is founded in the preservation motive, which is the basis of clans, etc. Couple this motive with the fact that man is a social animal, and we have a condition which the adjuster can turn to great advantage when dealing with witnesses to an accident.

The first person who personally contacts a witness calls out the clan spirit in the witness. The witness feels, although he may not be conscious of the feeling, that here is a man who seeks social contact with him, wants to deal with him on a clan basis, wants and perhaps needs his assistance, and is calling for his help. 

The stronger these feelings can be developed, the better the adjuster. A successful adjuster will capitalize these impulses with the result that, if the witness must state facts to support the claimant, he will not stress, or impress, the facts.

Often a preconceived opinion about the acts of the parties involved in the accident can be reversed. 

At other times it will be so weakened as to lose most of its force, and in most other cases any venom the witness might have felt against the assured will be removed. So, a witness who otherwise would be hos- [55]

tile can be changed, at least to the point where he will conclude that while he must tell the truth, he will do so in a manner such as to cause as little damage as possible.

An instance to illustrate this point is that of a witness to a pedestrian accident who said that he saw the pedestrian hurled through the air, and demonstrated the flight with a sweeping wave of his arm.

Such a statement and gesture, had that accident reached trial, might well have added a thousand dollars to the damages. This statement had to be toned down to a description of the pedestrian stumbling before falling, and omitting the sweeping gesture.

Had an attorney for the claimant reached this witness first, he would likely have so thoroughly fastened in the mind of the witness the flight and the gesture, that no outside influence could remove it.

The point is that if the adjuster gets there first, he gets the most and best the witness can give his side; if the claimant gets to the witness first, the claimant gets the best of it, and the adjuster takes what is left. The adjuster should strive to get there first.

It is human to favor the underdog. Great masses of people resent any person getting above them in [56]

social, or economic standing. This is a ready explanation of why leaders in the economic world are so severely and unjustly criticized. The underdog complex exists in most people to some degree. Few of us are free from it, with the result that when a witness learns that an insured person is involved, or a person with money, he tends to favor the other side. 

When a pedestrian is struck, the tendency is for witnesses to favor the pedestrian, partly on the supposition that the automobile owner is better off economically than the pedestrian. The same applies to a nice big automobile striking an old automobile which is about to fall apart. The public seems to think that ability to pay damages should be a factor in determining who will pay.

There is nothing vicious about this trait. Right or wrong, this impulse must be met and mastered. 

If the impulse is met before a statement is secured, so much the better, as any necessity for change of front will be avoided. There are various ways to meet it. One is suggested.

Appeal to the desire of all normal people to see justice done. If the facts at hand indicate absence of liability, you can suggest that while the claimant appears to be in the wrong, yet you want to do what [57]

you can for him. If, from the facts at hand, it appears that there is liability, tell the witness that you feel sorry for the claimant, but want to do all that you can for him, and the witness can best help you to accomplish this by telling exactly what he knows. 

If the witness gets the idea that the company is trying to evade a responsibility, a hostile attitude can be expected. If, however, the witness is with sincerity led to the thought that the company wants to do the right thing, and needs the facts to decide what is right, then the attitude of the witness is likely to be friendly.

This matter is doubly important if the witness to a personal injury is a woman. The adjuster must anticipate that she is sympathetic towards the one injured. It will aid greatly if this witness is made to feel that the adjuster shares her sympathy. By getting on the same footing with the witness, the adjuster can neutralize the sympathy element and get a fair statement.

A lawyer, given the choice of a hostile male witness, or a hostile female witness, will choose the male witness, because a jury is swayed more by the woman, and he has a most difficult task to break down, or alter her story without, at the same time, incurring [58]

the hostility of the jury. It is well worth while to get the ladies in a favorable frame of mind, then, even though they may testify against your side, the swaying emotional element will be largely eliminated. 

Never, if possible, allow a woman witness to retain a hostile attitude. As Kipling said, "The female of the species is more deadly than the male."

There exists a prejudice against taxicab, bus, and truck drivers which must be met. If a witness shows a tendency to color his statement against one of these, it will help to have the employer visit the witness and get the story anew. If the employer will make it clear that he wants to know the facts at first hand so that he can discharge the driver, if that appears advisable, the witness will be inclined to tell the truth, rather than have a man discharged through his exaggeration.

Considerable space has been given to the subject of human traits, and several of them have been pointed out. These, however, are not all of them by any means, but enough ground has been covered to indicate the breadth and importance of the field of human behavior, or why we do what we do. [59]

The official in the claim department at the home office examines the file on the case, and after reading these conflicting statements must decide what to do with the claim. If the witnesses' statements are not accompanied by a notation to show the degree of credibility to give to each statement, the home office official is not sufficiently supplied with the facts, where statements regarding the facts are in conflict, to make a sound decision. It must be borne in mind that the number of witnesses agreeing on the facts is not the deciding factor. One good, reliable, and substantial witness may be so worthy of belief as to overweigh several unreliable witnesses. In order to appraise the claim properly, the home office must have at hand an accurate appraisal of the weight to be given the statement of each witness. In other [60]

words, it must be informed as to the credibility, or worthiness of belief, of the witness. Some few witnesses will be deliberate and premeditated liars, others will be romancers, but the great majority will tell the facts as they know the facts.

Suppose that we weed out the deliberate liars first. 

There are three observable factors involved in telling a lie. These are words, tone, and muscle reaction. 

Of these three it is easiest to lie by word, harder to lie by tone, and hardest to lie by muscle reaction. 

We are all good liars at times. Were this not true, there would be much more agony in the world than there is. However, our ability to lie with any success is limited in scope to the little things about which we find it necessary, or courteous, to lie. When a normal person attempts to lie about the facts connected with an accident he is outside his usual field of lying, and lacks the aid of experience. In telling a lie, while the words are being uttered, the mind rechecks with a resultant slowing of the rate of speech. Under continued questioning, the witness may think that the lie is detected, and in order to cover up, will consciously increase the rate of speed of speech, and in attempting to increase the speed of speech to normal, will err and speak faster than nor- [61]

mal. Respiration and circulation will be thrown out of normal, the witness will tend to perspire, and become generally uncertain in the flow of words, and in the voice tone.

The tone of voice, when a lie is told, will be a head tone because the thought being voiced is the product of intellect. The tone of truth, while relating an observed fact, will be more of a chest tone. 

The muscles of the eyes are almost impossible to control when telling a conscious lie; and were they subject to control, it is doubtful if many witnesses would think to control them. Few people can keep their eyes focused on you and tell you a lie, for the reason that almost no one can concentrate vision and create ideas at the same time. The conscious effort is to create the lie, in the mind, in such a way that the lie will stick, and this concentration of mind is so great and exclusive that the eyes are left to care for themselves, and automatically they go out of sharp focus and rest at some point in more or less of a stare.

As to muscle movements, we all retain some of the instinctive animal reactions. In case a fight seems imminent, we brace our shoulder muscles; in case we want to scent, we elevate the nose and sniff. The [62]

connection between thought and muscular action is in some people so strong that they cannot talk without making gestures at the same time. This gesturemaking impulse survives in most people to the extent, at least, that the muscle reaction takes place although they may have acquired such control that they have habituated themselves to control the impulse. Under strain, the force of habit is not strong enough, and they gesticulate. If a witness is reliving mentally the events of an accident, his emotions should be in play to some extent, and the reaction of the muscles should take place, although the reaction may be checked so as to show no more than the start of the response.

If we see this start of the response, the result of natural reaction, then we can see that the witness is in earnest. We say that this person is speaking the truth from the heart. If, however, there is not this rudimentary muscle reaction, we can expect that the witness is not reliving events, but is portraying a mental picture which is the product of his conscious mind.

As to words, tone, eye and muscle reaction we must bear in mind that each time the false story is told, the reactions become less and less pronounced, and if the story is repeated often enough, these indications [63]

of lying will be missing. The stage has been reached which calls for the comment so often heard, "He has told that story so often that he believes it himself." 

The romantic type of liar calls on his memory so little, and is so used to lying that his reactions, along the lines noted above, will be markedly less than in normal people.

His voice, tone, etc., remain normal because it is normal for him to lie; to make up facts with the conscious part of his mind, rather than trouble to call on the memory. This type of person will use head tones. He is not a dangerous type because he is readily spotted by giving him an opportunity to romance over a wide field. It is seldom that this type is a sufficiently convincing speaker to do great damage.

Not much can be done with these people, except to work on them long enough to get them to replace the fancied story with the real story, so far as the real story goes, and let their fancy roam in making up unimportant embellishments. 

Now we reach for consideration the average man and woman, and as to them let us use this key thought; a witness relates recorded facts as those facts are recorded in his memory. The thought contained [64]

in the last sentence is so important that it is worth memorizing for daily review. Let us repeat it:

A WITNESS RELATES RECORDED FACTS AS THOSE FACTS ARE RECORDED IN HIS MEMORY. 

The facts related must have reached the memory through at least one of the senses, and they must be retained by the memory. 

The variation between people in ability to receive impressions and the ability to remember impressions is very great.

For instance, you have a key with which to open a tumbler type of lock. How many tumblers does its shape indicate that the lock has? Most probably you do not know although you have carried the key for years. Yet a locksmith would know. The reason for this is that you have never had enough interest in keys to observe them. The locksmith is interested in keys, and, consequently, he has greater ability to receive and retain impressions about keys. Let us use another instance: 

You see a street car go by, and if, later, asked details to identify it, you could recall, perhaps, only the color. A street car designer might, and probably would, recall minute details regarding the car.

So we receive dull impressions of things in which [65]

we are not interested, and sharp impressions of things in which we are interested. An onlooker to an accident tends to focus attention on learning whether anyone was hurt. He is interested in that, but in the other details of the accident he has but a passing interest. The persons in danger, as an accident evolves, are keenly interested in their own safety; other details are secondary. In a bad smashup, the self-preservation motive will be so great that details of what happened are likely to be jumbled together to such an extent in the mind of all participants, that no one of them can give a reliable account of what occurred after they realized their great danger. 

Impressions come into the memory in great volume, and at a rapid rate. In self-protection, the memory rejects, or refuses to retain, the vast majority of impressions, and retains the few selected impressions which for one reason or another are accepted and retained. You can demonstrate this by trying to recall the details of the shifting scenes viewed on a trip through the countryside. Of the vast number of impressions received by eye, ear, and nose you will be able to recall very few.

As interest in the subject matter increases, the ability to remember increases. As between bystand- [66]

ers, passengers, and operators, the interest in the facts connected with an accident follows in that order.

The rapidity with which people can use their senses varies. For instance, crooked card deals are based on the fact that the hand is quicker than the eye. The motion-picture industry is based on the inability of the eye to register everything which occurs. The telegrapher can read the ticker, but the hearing of the average person is not sufficiently discriminating to distinguish between telegraphic dots and dashes. 

So, too, we have people in whom the sense of taste, or smell, is so keen that they are able to earn a living through the use of one sense. Each sense is developed in each person to a different degree. 

It is true that the more senses which convey any fact to the memory, the greater the impression made, and the more probable that the fact will be remembered. So, if a fact is written, read, and spoken, four senses are involved, and these senses coordinate in fastening the fact in the memory. One witness may have used more senses in recording a fact than another witness, and so can retain the fact longer and more accurately. 

Then, too, impressions are received while we are paying attention to other matters, or, as we say, while [67]

our minds are on something else. This inattention limits the strength of the impression, and may cause the impression to be so slight as to fail to register. 

How often during a sermon do we day-dream, and, while our ears have functioned, we cannot recall a word delivered in the sermon.

The memory at the time an impression is received is not empty. It is, rather, full of a great mixture of facts, beliefs, etc., so that when the new impression is received it must take its place with the matter already in the memory, and if we have become habituated to react in certain ways to certain impressions, we admit the new impression and steer it into memory through existing channels. Such cases often occur in conversation, so that one person will say, "Don't call me that," and the other will say, “I did not call you that." Who is wrong, the speaker or the hearer? One or the other must be. It is probable that the hearer was wrong, because as the words came into his memory, they got mixed up with what, on account of a complex, he expected to hear and he received the impression along the complex rut, rather than through a fresh channel. As we receive impressions under varying circumstances and into a [68]

mass of mind matter, prejudices, complexes, etc., it is to be expected that we shall err in reception.

The result of the fact that people vary in ability to perceive and classify impressions, is that facts are impressed in the memory of each witness in a manner different from each other witness, so there is little reason to hope that the impressions as related by the different witnesses will be the same. The variation in the ability of witnesses to register impressions in a clear and orderly manner is accompanied by variation in ability to get impressions out of the mind in a clear and orderly manner.

The account which the adjuster wishes to get from the memory of the witness lies in the memory of the witness much like a cross-section map of mountainous country. Left to himself, the usual witness will recall and recount the peaks. The adjuster wants the valleys and slopes as well. He can aid the witness in recalling them by discussing facts in sequence, or in association with other facts. If the witness is prompted to recall the facts of the accident in the order in which he received impressions of it, his task in recalling the facts will be made easy. So, have him recall the events step by step. The shorter the steps, the better. The other aid to recollection [69]

is to place one fact in association with another fact, or facts. For instance, if a witness cannot recall the width of a street, he can perhaps recall how many traffic lanes are in the street, and by the association of traffic lanes he can recall the width of the street. 

Often the memory refuses to recall facts which the witness knows that he knows. We say of this oddity, "It slipped my mind." Memory is often stubborn. For this reason, if the witness should be able to give a better account of an accident than he has given, it is well to see him again to ask whether anything new regarding the accident has occurred to him, or to go over his statement to see whether reading the statement, after a mental rest, will call to his memory any missing facts. If the witness is dullwitted, it may pay to go over the statement several times with him.

The credibility of a witness or, to phrase it differently, the worth of a witness as the possessor of remembered facts, his ability to recount them, and his veracity and earnestness in recounting these facts, varies from time to time. Events subsequent to the interview may make the witness alter his account, his memory may become dulled, etc., but if the statement secured from him is signed, he will be in no [70]

position to alter his account, and if the statement contains all of the details, his memory as to events may be refreshed.

It is a rule with most adjusters, and a good one, to read over to a witness the written statement, but too often this reading is perfunctory. This rereading may be done in a manner extremely helpful to the adjuster. By reading the statement forcefully, and placing the emphasis at the right spots, the witness relives the scene and the facts in the statement are impressed in his memory. If the adjuster thinks that there are missing facts, he may interpolate these facts as he believes them to be into his reading, and see whether the witness in reliving the episode agrees that these interpolated facts are true. 

In claims of a major nature it is essential to go a step further and express an opinion of how much credit a jury, with limited opportunity for observation, would probably give to the testimony of each witness when weighing all of the testimony regarding the accident. In other words, express an opinion whether the witness is strong, convincing, reliable, weak, uncertain, etc. True testimony may be given in such an indifferent, hesitant and apparently uncertain manner as to carry no weight and be worthy of [71]

little credit, while lies may be uttered so forcefully and with such certainty as to be convincing, and be given full credit. So, a distinction must be made between real credibility in determining true facts and apparent credibility in proving those facts. 

If the interview has been skillfully handled, the adjuster will know what true facts the liar will testify to, what lies he will testify to, and perhaps the reason the witness lies about these facts. He will know what facts the romancer will testify to, and which of these facts he has created. He will know the degree of credibility, or worthiness of belief, of each witness. In short, he will know the value to attach to the statement of each witness, both in arriving at the truth and in attempting to prove the truth.

If the adjuster will report on the factors which he observed which bear on credibility of witnesses, he will enable the home office to appraise the value of each statement, and to give it proper weight in determining the disposition of the claim. [72]

Chapter XII DEALING WITH CLAIMANTS 

HERE IS A CHOICE BETWEEN 

two ways of handling possible, or probable, claimants; to go to them, or let them come to you. The company, as a matter of policy, decides which course of action is to be followed.

If possible, or probable, claimants are ignored until they present a claim, quite a few small claims, and an occasional fair sized one will never be presented, and the company will be saved the amount of money which would have been paid to settle these claims. 

The amount of the savings is so indefinite that it cannot be estimated. Should this course be pursued, a company cannot safely close such a possible claim until the Statute of Limitation has run against the claim.

If possible, or probable, claimants are seen, a fairly accurate loss reserve can be set up at once, and the company's balance sheet will more truly represent its financial position. We must bear in mind that a [73]

claim can be prosecuted at any time within the Statute of Limitations and that, as to claims for damages to minors, the statute does not begin to run until the minor reaches legal age. Unscrupulous people may wait until the Statute of Limitations has nearly run before asserting a claim, in the hope that the company will not have a record of witnesses, etc., to enable it to fight the claim. Considering the probability of error, or deception, by the assured in making his report, if the persons who may have suffered a loss are not seen, valid claims may be expected to pop up at any time.

It is not safe to take the word of the assured that no one was hurt in the accident. He may be only guessing. In one case, the assured reported no one injured, and accounted for the manner in which each person involved acted after the accident, and that they had told the police that they were not injured. 

There was no reason to anticipate any claim for personal injuries arising from this accident. Possible claimants were not seen by the adjuster. Several months after the accident, two perfectly legitimate claims were presented, one of which was serious. 

The fact that the report of an accident indicates only a small property damage is no excuse for passing [74]

by the possibility of a personal injury claim. There was, in one instance, a minor property damage claim which arose from a slight collision of two automobiles. Both operators told the police there was no injury, and there was no reason to anticipate a claim for personal injuries; yet a claim was made for miscarriage, and this claim had to be met. 

If it be the policy of your company to have the adjuster see each possible claimant, then the sooner the possible claimant is seen, the better. Possible claimants and their families, as soon as bills begin to be incurred, start a serious course of worry as to how the accumulating bills will be met. They are anxious to be relieved of this worry, and it is worth a great deal to them to be relieved of this worry. If the adjuster is in contact with them at this time, conditions favor a fair settlement. After plans have been made to meet bills, or after the injured person has recovered, or the bills are a thing of the past, there is no worry to be relieved, the claimant is in a position to take time in settling, and many items of expense are added which would not be added had the claim been settled promptly.

There is another angle to this matter which must not be overlooked. If the claimant is visited [75]

promptly, the adjuster can secure a full statement of the facts which the claimant knows about the accident. This statement may show contributory negligence, or other facts constituting a defense to any claim presented. This statement will inform the company, for use in case a suit is later brought, just what specific acts of negligence will be advanced by the claimant, as the basis of liability. 

It is often extremely helpful in personal injury cases to have the operator of the assured's automobile visit the claimant. A natural resentment develops when the operator pays no attention to the person injured, and leaves the dealings regarding settlement exclusively with the adjuster, as all people resent the idea that some one can injure them, and then think that dollars will even the score. If the assured, or the operator of his automobile, even where there is no liability, will act human, and visit the claimant, an atmosphere favorable to amicable settlement will be created.

It will sometimes happen that such relations will be established between the assured and the injured person that settlement can be effected through the assured without the presence of insurance being disclosed to the injured person. The factors, in such a [76]

situation, in aid of a fair and reasonable settlement should not be overlooked.

Some accidents involve more than two possible wrongdoers. Each believes, in the initial stages, that the other operator caused the accident. The innocent sufferer of their wrongdoing may have difficulty in determining whether one or both of them was negligent. Such a situation favors an alliance between the innocent person and one of the operators against the third party. Such an alliance does not just happen. 

It is procured. Why not be the procurer, and so strengthen your hand?

It appears, then, that some little harm may be done if each possible claimant is interviewed, prior to his filing claim, and that some great advantages may be obtained if each possible claimant is seen as promptly as possible. As stated previously, this is a matter of company policy regarding which you should be advised. [77]

Let us dispassionately analyze these claims, and find a way to meet them.

When the policy was sold, the assured was informed regarding this guest coverage. Perhaps a prime motive prompting the purchase of the policy was a desire, by the buyer, to have his friends protected. If the guest is injured, under such circumstances as to make the coverage applicable, the assured is right in insisting that the company perform its obligations as expressed in the policy. The assured has a right to insist on the same fair treatment to the guest as to any other claimant. He has more interest in seeing such a claim cleared, because the claim is apt to make a barrier between him and his guest. [78]

The guest perhaps knew, or, at least, expected, that the host had a policy which protects a guest in case a guest suffers damage by reason of negligent acts of the host.* Knowledge that the operator is, through his policy, financially able to take care of possible damages may have been a reason, behind the guest's acceptance of the invitation, to ride in the host's automobile. When, before an accident, a guest thinks of insurance against possible damage, he thinks of his monetary damage and protection against that damage. His attitude is much like that of a person who buys a trip travel policy. He seldom knows exactly what coverage the policy provides, but he does know that it provides for monetary losses, and these are the losses against which he desires protection. 

Insurance companies, speaking of them as a whole, do not have to write the guest hazard coverage. They do so from choice, because they know that the assured demands this coverage. They make a fair charge for this coverage. For these reasons a company is in no position to justify treating guest claims on a different basis from other claims. 

The term "collusion," meaning "a conspiracy to defraud," is not applicable to a desire by the assured

[79]

and by the guest, in cases where there is liability, that the claim be treated fairly and settled promptly. The term "collusion" is properly applied to a conspiracy between the assured and his guest to falsify the facts surrounding the accident so as to show liability when the true facts would show non-liability. If the facts are truly reported, the after relations of host and guest cannot be termed collusion. Such acts, if detrimental to settlement, would be classed as failure to cooperate with the company.

Collusion is a defense which the company can use in a suit brought by the assured to force the company to pay a judgment recovered against the assured. 

Unless there is clear proof offered to support this defense, the company will lose the suit. The probability of the company successfully defending such a suit is so remote that it is wiser for the adjuster to make collusion impossible, rather than to permit it to develop, and then try to fight it. 

If the facts in a case justify the adjuster in concluding that there probably is collusion, he must be cautious in expressing his opinion. If he says that in his opinion there is collusion, that is true. If, however, he, in an unguarded moment, tells a party, other [80]

than the assured, that there is collusion, he has laid his company open to a suit for slander.

If the injury to the guest is of such nature that the host and guest have an immediate opportunity to conspire, then collusion may occur. If the injury is of a painful or nauseating nature, the guest will be in no humor to conspire. If there are several people in the automobile, there is a hesitation to initiate collusion. If the injury is slight, there is absent any strong financial motive prompting collusion. 

That suspected collusion which is feared arises in major accidents. In these cases the guest is usually rushed to a hospital before any opportunity exists for collusion. At the hospital, the guest is not, for some time, in a mood to collude. In the meantime, the alert adjuster will have visited the assured and procured his statement. He will go to the hospital and get the guest's statement before the parties have any opportunity to collude. By some variation of this procedure to fit the circumstances, the adjuster can avoid collusion.

In the interview with the assured, the adjuster will call to his attention the clause in the policy which requires the assured to cooperate in settling the claim, and to do nothing which will [81]

prejudice settlement. He will tell the assured that guest claims cause a good deal of ill will, but that by working together they can avoid that happening. 

When he leaves the assured, the assured will know his rights and duties, and can be expected to abide by them. If possible, the assured should be sold on the idea of standing by, ready to cooperate with the adjuster in amicably and properly disposing of the claim. 

If the adjuster will reach the guest promptly, he will find that the guest is not vindictive, that he is still friendly towards the assured, and that he has no tendency to place undue blame on the assured. The statement of the guest, secured at this time, will tend to minimize any fault of the assured, rather than to exaggerate it. Further, if the guest, at this time, thinks that the host will be charged, criminally, with an infraction of law, he will be cautious in placing the blame. If the assured is present when this statement is taken, the guest will be rather certain to give a true statement.

When interviewing the guest, the adjuster will bear in mind that the guest is usually vitally interested in knowing who will pay the accumulating bills. As soon as possible, determine the question of [82]

liability, and start negotiations for a settlement. If there is liability, an agreement may be made to pay all reasonable bills, plus a stated sum, as full satisfaction for the claim. Such an agreement is binding as soon as you do any act under the agreement. 

Release can be handled later. If there is probable liability, make the same effort. If there is possible liability you know that the claim has nuisance value. 

If it can be purchased at a fair price, buy it. If there is no liability, hustle to get all of the facts which will clinch your conclusion. Even though there is no liability, the claim may have nuisance value, and, if there is no third party involved, the guest will probably see the correctness of your conclusion and agree, for a small sum, to execute a release. 

A goodly number of those accidents in which you conclude that there is no probable guest hazard liability will be caused by the negligence of a third party. In such case, work for an alliance as suggested in the next preceding chapter. Offer to cooperate with the guest in gathering and giving to him proof of the negligence of the third party in exchange for his undertaking not to make claim against the assured. The guest should not make a formal release at this time, as a release of one wrong- [83]

doer, where more than one is responsible for the injury, will release all of the wrongdoers.

In case the law of the state where the accident happened, requires that the guest establish gross negligence in a suit against the host, you might explain this to the guest, and make him realize how hard it is to prove gross negligence, as compared with the ease of proving an act of ordinary negligence committed by the third party.

It is safe to say that most of the troubles which arise in handling guest claims are attributable to the adjuster, in that he allows their birth and development through his prejudice against these claims. 

This prejudice, unless he guards against it, prompt him to be dilatory and unsympathetic in his dealings with the assured and the guest, and will prompt him to offer to do less in settlement of the claim than his good judgment tells him is fair.

When the assured and the guest realize that the claim is not being treated diligently and fairly, the adjuster's task in disposing of it becomes difficult, and the costs mount upward. Bear in mind that there is a limit to the restraint which the policy puts upon the assured. He does not need to remain passive after the adjuster has shown that he does not intend [84]

to be considerate, or fair; nor need he remain quiet while the adjuster neglects the claim, or attempts to sell an unjust settlement to the guest. The policy, by implication, requires that the company be reasonably fair and diligent in carrying out its part of the contract; so if the company breaches the contract in this respect, the assured is released from his obligation not to interfere with settlement of the claim. 

Bear in mind that after such a claim is open an undue length of time without decision by the company on the question of liability, there is strain and embarrassment on the assured, and he has to stand for many derogatory remarks regarding his poor judgment in selecting his insurance company. The longer the claim remains open, the worse the situation grows; and as time goes on many people hear complaints, and the effect on good will of these complaints is not removed when settlement is finally made.

Guest claims should not be hard to handle, and will not be, if the adjuster will act with diligence and sympathy, and will secure and retain the active, interested cooperation of the assured. [85]

Chapter XIV ACCIDENTITIS AND HYSTERIA 

"ACCID 

CCIDENTITIS" IS OFTEN 

mentioned in discussion of a personal injury claim, and, generally it is ridiculed. When so treated, it may be fairly assumed that the speaker is confusing "accidentitis" with malingering. The two are entirely distinct. 

The malingerer knows that he is not injured as claimed; he knows that his claim is fradulent. His acts are intentional and, as he thinks, well planned.

He selects his claimed injury in that class of injuries hard to prove, or disprove, the favorites being convulsions, paralysis, and injuries to the back. Unless he has competent medical advice, he will, in all probability, forget, or be unacquainted with some one symptom which will expose his fraud. 

The claimant who suffers from "accidentitis” does not intentionally acquire or fabricate his ills.

His ills have their root in his state of mind. They do not appear to him as the result of a state of mind, but [86]

do appear to him as natural and logical results of the injury he received.

The power of mind is tremendous; so much so that people escape their death bed merely through the will to live. If we realize the power of the mind to be great when exercised positively, we can realize how great the damage may be and become, if no power of mind is exerted to aid the healing processes, or if a negative attitude is taken.

Many people exist in a neutral state in which they are not greatly interested whether they continue to live, or not. They carry through the troubles of the world day after day with the realization that they are getting nowhere; life seems to them to be a treadmill; their will power is about exhausted in fighting the daily economic and physical battle. You will realize that any slight additional trouble may be enough to induce these people to give up the struggle. A personal injury to them is a misfortune just too great to bear, so they give up the fight and quit. 

They are in a state of mental depression. The injury from which they suffer does not properly respond to treatment; the usual recovery period passes by but they are far from a recovered state; ills previously suppressed now manifest themselves and are [87]

charged to the accident, and the whole picture becomes very drab. These people have "accidentitis.'

It is a real ailment. Its manifestations are physical, and the physician attempts to treat the manifestations; but, as previously stated, the basis is mental and can be treated only through treating the mind. 

The conditions existing, unless a change of mental attitude is brought about, may become permanent. 

There is no doubt that a prompt settlement in such cases tends to arrest the development of "accidentitis," as the claimant, after settlement, has no reward for yielding to his ailment, and is prompted to get back into the daily routine. 

In one pronounced case of "accidentitis" a woman had received a blow on the shoulder. According to her sworn testimony, the injury to the shoulder brought on arthritis, which, in turn, deranged her nervous system, which caused nervous indigestion, which led to ulcers of the stomach and gastritis, which produced gas pressure under the heart and brought on heart trouble. Such a chain of developments would be hard to understand if considered as physical disabilities only, but when the mind, as a producing factor, is considered, it is less difficult to understand how this woman was able, to make [88]

honestly, under oath, claims to such a course of physical events.

Hysteria is an artificial condition, existing by force of mind, but having the aspects and symptoms of a real condition. The ailment might be as simple as a sore throat, or as serious as a case of heart trouble.

No element of sham, pretense, or intent is involved in cases of hysteria. The person, to all intents and purposes, has a true case of the ailment. Yet in reality the ailment is self imposed by some mental state.

An accident case in point is that of a man who could not lift the lid of his left eye, and could not bear any pressure on one shoulder, without suffering great pain. No injury to the nerves, or muscles of the eye, eye-lid, or shoulder could be found. After being under the care of physicians for some time, it was evident that this was an unusual case, and a test of his condition was made while he was hypnotized. 

While in the hypnotic state, he could lift the eye-lid, and felt no pain when struck on the shoulder. This test proved that the ailments were hysterical, and that the condition called for mental reeducation. 

A recent study of the case histories of 5,000 persons suffering from exophthalmic goiter, a peculiar dis- [89]

ease of the goiter, disclosed that shock and terror incident to accidents and narrow escapes from accidents, served as the exciting cause in 35 per cent of these cases.

In an accident which recently resulted in a law suit, a man stubbed his toe on an elevation, negligently allowed to exist in a concrete sidewalk. There was a resultant bruise which should have been the only disability received, but the man lost the use of his foot, and the failure to use the muscles of the foot caused atrophy to set in. A most careful diagnosis failed to reveal any physical condition which would account for the loss of use of the foot. The loss of use was entirely functional, that is, it was in the operation of the mind, rather than in the physical condition of the foot. The claimant rested his claim on testimony that his disability was of psychogenic, or mental, origin which had been precipitated by the accident. The inducing cause was found to be stubbing the toe, but substantial damages were awarded to compensate for the entire disability found to exist. 

Although the disability arising from an injury may be hysterical, the damages accruing while this condition exists are proper damages, and unless the disability is accurately diagnosed and properly treated, [90]

the damages, as finally determined, may be all out of proportion to the damages anticipated at the time of the accident.

The few instances cited above will indicate the necessity of keeping in contact with injured persons so that if the injuries do not respond to ordinary medical treatment, the claimant may be put under the care of a psychiatrist in the hope of bringing about a mental condition which will place the mind force in cooperation with the healing process. 

If an adjuster meets a case where he suspects, before a medical examination has been made, that malingering, "accidentitis," or hysteria is present he should notify the company doctor to be particularly watchful for indications of such condition. If, after a medical examination has been had, the adjuster suspects that such condition has arisen, he should ask for the privilege of having another examination made. 

In any case where this condition is disclosed, the adjuster should take steps to have the producing causes corrected. [91]

Chapter XV THE ART OF SAYING "NO" 

"N. 

O" IS A TINY WORD. It is a very important word; almost as important as the word "if." It is a word which makes friends, and a word which makes enemies. It is dynamite when handled by the unskilled. 

Practice in saying "No," and observation of others as they say "No" will lead to skill in the use of the word. The art of saying "No" is best exemplified by successful bankers. In order to learn the art, study these artists. The best of them can refuse a loan and make the refusal seem like a favor. You leave the bank in an elevated frame of mind, but you do not have the loan. You are convinced that the bank is truly sorry the loan cannot be granted, and the bank has let you know that you are a fine fellow, but some little thing must be done, or can't be done, and, with regret that the loan cannot be made, the banker makes you believe that a man as efficient as you are will work out the problem without a loan, or [92]

some good friend will step up with the money. In any event, you are sure that the banker is your friend. 

A stupid banker would have turned you down so quickly as to make you feel hatred for him and his bank.

A large proportion of claimants expect the adjuster to say "No"; they would be greatly surprised to hear "Yes." When the claimant knows that the adjuster should say "No," nothing is gained by the adjuster saying "Yes"; in fact the claimant is likely to think the company sort of stupid to pay his claim, and there is a resulting loss of respect. When the claimant expects to receive a "Yes" and gets a "No," he is not hateful about it. We all get a blow now and then, and expect to get blows in the future, but not all of the blows we receive leave scars. So in saying "No," it is not the word which produces resentment, it is the way the "No" is delivered.

By mastering the banker's art, the adjuster many times makes a friend for his company, even though he says "No." [93]

Chapter XVI STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS 

AMAGES ARISING FROM 

an automobile accident may be claimed, at any time, by the person damaged, from the person who did the damage. If the damage is not paid, the person damaged has legal recourse to the judicial system, by filing a law suit in which he demands that his damage be assessed as a judgment against the wrongdoer. In this law suit, the damaged party is called the plaintiff, and the party from whom damage is claimed is called the defendant.

It is obviously unfair to allow the claimant to wait so long before entering his suit that the defendant cannot gather his necessary evidence, and it is unfair to allow a claimant to hold a claim as a threat over the head of the other party for an indefinite time. 

Accordingly, each state has adopted a statute which specifies how long a time after the happening of an accident the claimant is allowed in which to file a [94]

suit. This statute is called the Statute of Limitations. 

The time limit varies in different states. 

The courts interpret the meaning of statutes, and in interpreting the Statute of Limitation, some few state courts hold that the meaning is that if the suit is not filed within the specified time, the court has no authority, or jurisdiction, to hear it. Other states, and the majority of them, hold that the statute is an available plea, which the defendant may use if he wishes to or neglect to use, and, so, no attention is paid by the court to whether the time set by the statute has expired, or not. If, however, the defendant wishes to call the fact to the attention of the court, he may do so by filing a plea that the statutory time has run, and the action barred.

Regardless of which interpretation is used in your state, the effect is that in all law suits which arise from an accident which happened so far back as to exceed the time limit set by the statute, the defendant does not have to defend the suit, unless he chooses to do so. [95]

ence in the market value of the automobile before, and after, the accident; or, (b) the fair and reasonable cost of repairs made necessary by the accident, to restore the automobile to the condition it was in before the accident. "Standard" (b) is the one now generally used. Fair and reasonable value is a matter of opinion, and, as opinion varies, it is almost impossible to prove that a repair bill is unreasonable. The opinions of various repairmen are expectedly different, because they do not have the same rent, labor, and overhead charges.

If the damage to an automobile is severe, the amount as determined by (a) should be greater than the amount as determined by (b) because an automobile which is known to have been severely damaged has a lower market value than a like automobile which has not been damaged and repaired. It is also quite probable that the repairs, although intended to be complete, will not restore every item of damage. 

Claimants usually prefer to have repairs made at the garage of the dealer from whom the automobile was purchased. This is a reasonable preference, and, in fact, there is no necessity for the claimant to do otherwise than take his automobile to that garage, order the repairs made, and stand on the bill as ren- [97]

dered. This, however, is not the usual course for the reason that claimants prefer to know who will pay before they order repairs made.

The adjuster desires, in order to save money, that he be permitted to have a couple of repair shops, which specialize in accident repair work, render estimates. After getting a satisfactory estimate, the adjuster is in a position to pay the claim, and allow the claimant to get the work done in any shop which suits his fancy. In practice, however, the repair shops which make estimates for adjusters must receive work in some volume to compensate for the time consumed in making free estimates. For this reason, adjusters must cause a flow of work to these shops sufficient to cover these free estimates, or, otherwise, be prepared to pay about $3 per about $3 per estimate. 

Claimants also fail to realize that they are entitled only to have their automobile restored to the condition it was in immediately before the accident, and injured parts need be replaced only by parts in similar condition. So, if a tire is ruined which was 60 per cent used, claimants are entitled only to 40 per cent of the cost of a new tire. If a battery is ruined, they are entitled to the remaining unused value of the battery at the time of the loss. If a fender had been [98]

previously injured, the claimant is entitled to the cost of repairing the new damage, and if a used fender is available, as good as his old one was, he cannot demand a new fender. Claimants quickly see the fairness of this adjustment, although when first mentioned the knowledge comes as a distinct and unwelcome surprise.

Occasionally the claimant will be able to make repairs himself, or itself, if claimant is a corporation. 

For example, an automobile might be owned by a garage, the claimant may have enough repair work to operate his own shop; traction companies repair their own cars and busses; and municipalities have their own repair crews. These claimants, who make their own repairs, cannot charge, in estimating the loss, any overhead, unless it can be shown that the overhead charge was necessitated directly by the accident. Such claimants can charge for labor and parts at cost; they cannot charge rent, or any addition on actual wages paid, or any profit. They are limited to actual expense.

If the damage be to a building, the claimant is entitled to the difference between the value of the building before and after the accident, or the cost of restoration according to the original plans. [99]

In no case is the claimant entiled to more than the reasonable value of repairs, because ordinary property has no sentimental value. In case the amount shown by the estimate of repairs exceeds the value of the automobile immediately before the accident, this value should be determined and the automobile should be bought for that price, and sold to a junk yard for salvage value, or the salvage value should be deducted from the value of the automobile as determined, and the claimant paid the net amount. 

If the automobile damaged be very old, the claimant may not desire repairs to be made. He need not have them made, but he is entitled to an amount of money which fairly represents his damage. In most cases these owners will take less than the cost of repairs, and feel happy about the settlement. 

In shops operated by dealers, the repairmen are very often paid on a commission basis, and receive a percentage on all new parts sold. As a consequence, they are tempted to replace parts, rather than repair them, and so often do so that the adjuster must be prepared to prove that replacement was not necessary, and was more expensive than repair to the part. 

Shops which cater to insurance companies are familiar with the objectives of the adjuster, and will repair [100]

parts if that can be done satisfactorily, rather than replace them.

Claimant can be advised that few except the largest dealers' garages do any body work, as their prime function is to keep automobiles in proper mechanical, or operative, condition, and that when these garages contract for body work, they subcontract it, and add about 15 per cent to the price of the subcontractor. 

When the facts are explained to the claimant, he will either authorize the repair shop selected by the adjuster to do the work, or accept the estimate of that shop as the fair amount of his damages. 

After the work has been ordered, the adjuster can leave a release, properly filled in, with the repair man who will have the claimant sign the release at the time he accepts his repaired automobile. [101]

Chapter XVIII MEDICAL EXPENSES 

NE OF THE TROUBLESOME 

factors in adjusting public liability claims is the apparent excess in medical charges which appear in connection with many claims. It is most difficult to keep these charges down to the line of reasonable necessity. 

Very wide latitude is allowed a physician in the treatment of his patient. He has a legal right to perform any service, or have any service rendered which his judgment dictates as proper in the circumstances of the particular case under treatment; and he has a right, if not a duty, to use, or call to his aid precautions to guide his treatment. The only requirement which is placed on him, is that he have and exercise that degree of skill ordinarily displayed by physicians in his area, do the things usually done by careful practitioners in that area, and charge rates considered fair and reasonable in that area. The word "reasonable" covers considerable ground, and it is most difficult to prove that a thing done was unreasonable. 

Consequently, frontal attack on medical expenses usually results in failure. [102]

An apparent basis for attack on high medical charges is to attempt to show that certain expenses, such as cost of x-rays, urine analysis, nurses, specialists, etc., would not have accrued, had the diagnosis been correct. This line of attack presupposes that a physician has a duty to make a correct diagnosis, and a companion duty to effect a prompt cure. Such is not the case. The physician is in no sense an insurer that the diagnosis is correct, or that the treatment is correct, or that a cure will be effected. As stated, he must exercise ordinary care and skill, as such is determined in his area, and must act in good faith. It would be most difficult to prove his lapse in either respect.

Even when the adjuster feels certain that the physician, through apparent fault caused, or allowed, unnecessary expense, he must realize the difficulty of securing medical testimony to prove that the bill is excessive. So a direct attack on the nature of treatment and expenses incurred is generally futile, Another apparent reason for attack on medical charges is that too many visits were made by the attending physician; visits which did the patient no good, and served no good purpose. The physician is entitled to use his own judgment as to how often he should visit his patient. This right to exercise judg- [103]

ment gives wide latitude. If the physician is a busy practitioner, his visits will tend to be in harmony with necessity, but if he has a great deal of time available, he will likely visit the patient more often than necessary. 

Sometimes the apparent excess medical expenses are due to deliberate intent to run up the bill. In case the attending physician knows that a reliable corporation will be charged with the bill, a temptation exists to spare no expense. At other times, the excess results from mere neglect of the physician to consider that someone will have to pay. At other times, the excess is due to overcaution on the physician's part. At other times, the excess will accrue in the performance of service which the physician honestly believes to have been necessary under the circumstances.

There are many kinds of kickbacks available to physicians, if they care to arrange for them, such as percentage returns from drug stores, hospitals, x-ray, and other laboratories, consultants and even from undertakers; yet the attending physician has legal authority to use his own judgment, as to the necessity for hospitalization, x-rays, etc.

The medical profession offers a livelihood to many [104]

thousands of men and women, and, like every other profession and occupation, it has in it all manner of men and women. Some take the professional attitude and minister to the suffering without much regard to their own welfare, while others enter the profession with a business view-point, and work it for all they can get out of it. Some are men of honor, some are not. The profession attempts to keep its ranks clean, but it can never be certain that its ranks are clean. The motives actuating physicians' conduct are so varied that an adjuster cannot afford to jump at the conclusion that a charge is excessive. He should get the physician's view-point, and then examine the evidence of excess charge in the light of the view-point expressed by the physician.

If the adjuster, on sound facts, concludes that the medical charges are too high, he can frankly so state, but, otherwise, he should act on the assumption that the charges are, to the physician, fair and reasonable and honestly incurred. It can then be pointed out to the physician that the itemized charges total an amount which the company feels it cannot pay, and request the physician to give a new bill in less amount, made up, not of many items, but one lump sum to cover the case. [105]

Another approach, often applicable, is to explain to the physician that a settlement can be effected at a certain amount which is less than the total of all items making up the claimant's damages, both actual expenses and intangible items, and that, in order to effect a settlement, all parties must cooperate by scaling the items down. Physicians are reasonable men, and will cooperate in this way if properly approached. 

Usually the adjuster does not contact the physician and hospital, but receives the medical bills from the claimant. Any apparent overcharge in the bills can be pointed out to the claimant, and the claimant can be sold on the idea of accepting the adjuster's estimate of what the bill, or bills, should be. The claimant then can try to settle for these bills on a reduced basis. He is in a better position to adjust these bills satisfactorily than anyone else.

In cases where there is real or probable liability, and where a considerable disability period is expected, or where complications may set in, such as pneumonia after fractures, or where it appears that the claimant is not responding properly to treatment, the adjuster may aid in reducing the final bill for medical expenses by offering the attending physician the privilege of calling the company physician, or a qualified [106]

specialist selected by the company, into consultation on the case. This consultant may be able to offer suggestions which will materially reduce the disability period, and perhaps avoid complications entering the case. If improper, or insufficient, treatment is being given, this condition can be corrected, and if the service of a man in an allied line, such as an osteopath, or psychiatrist, appears necessary he can be called into the case. Such a suggestion to the attending physician is merely an acknowledgment that two heads are better than one, and there will seldom be any feeling on the part of the attending physician other than pleasure in receiving this cooperation. 

The adjuster does not waive any rights of the company if he consults and cooperates with the claimant to avoid excess charges, just so long as he does not take control of matters into his own hands. 

The adjuster, in his dealings with physicians, must avoid creating the slightest enmity, as physicians are in a position to retaliate when called as witnesses, by testifying in such a way as to exaggerate the severity of the claimant's injuries. Always seek to cultivate the good will of the medical fraternity. [107]

Chapter XIX PHYSICIAN'S REPORTS PHYSICIAN'S REPORTS ARE 

secured by the company in injury cases as a check on the extent and reality of the injuries claimed. The usual practice is to have the company's physician call the claimant's physician, and arrange a time for this examination. The claimant is entitled to have his physician present at the time this examination is made. The company's physician is not present to aid the patient or to intrude in any way in the relationship existing between the claimant and his physician. 

He is there simply to get the facts as accurately as possible. A copy of his report is usually supplied to claimant's representative. The claimant may make the giving of a copy of the report a condition to his consent.

It is the usual thing to order a medical examination as promptly as possible, and if this is done, complications will not arise. It is very seldom that a claimant, hopeful of damages, will refuse to permit a medical examination. If, however, the claimant gets the [108]

idea that the examination is not to be made to favor him, but is to be made to help the company fight him, he may refuse to permit an examination to be made. If such a claimant enters suit, it may not be possible to secure a court order requiring the claimant to submit to a physical examination. 

In those states in which physical examination cannot be ordered by the court,* it is vital that, if possible need exists for such a report, the adjuster have the examination made while the claimant is in a favorable frame of mind.

The doctor will have no trouble in receiving permission to look over the hospital records. A hospital has no right to permit anyone to look at the hospital records without the permission of the patient, or his doctor, and at times it is difficult for the adjuster to get permission to look at these reports. So, in case the adjuster has not, for any reason, been able to examine these reports, the doctor should be requested to look over and summarize the findings and the treatment at the hospital.

X-rays are regarded as so technical as to require special training in the reading of them, so x-ray laboratories have a trained expert read the x-rays, and * See page 226. [109]

reduce his reading to the form of a report. In any case where an x-ray reads positive, the doctor should examine the x-ray, check it against the reader's report, and report to the company what the reader's report is, and whether he agrees with it.

On account of the fact that it is usual to give to the claimant, or his representative, a copy of the report made by the company's doctor, the adjuster need not complain if this report is incomplete, provided he can secure in a supplemental report all of the facts which will complete the report to his satisfaction.

Liability, in cases of personal injury, extends to any subsequent disease, or infirmity, which would occur as the natural result of the injury, where it is not shown that any other independent cause existed of which it might have been the result. This liability is so broad that a complete medical examination should be made to preclude having other and subsequent ailments improperly charged to the accident. 

If possible, the medical examination should be made in the office of the company's doctor, as he has better facilities there for conducting an examination, and can make a much more detailed and accurate examination. [110]

Suggestion is made that in addition to the examining physician's report as submitted, showing his findings in regard to the injury, the adjuster secure from him a supplemental report, either orally, or by letter, giving his opinion as to observed conditions, such as mental attitude of claimant towards recovery, any psychic factors in the case, ability of claimant to describe his suffering, surrounding home conditions, effect on the claimant of any concurrent ailments, any factors affecting residuals, any comments he may care to make on the quality of services rendered by the attending physician, including diagnosis and treatment, and any other pertinent facts which came under his observation.

Physician's reports, like other papers which may be put to use at a trial, should not reveal that the report was made by order of, or for the account of, the company. The report should be ordered in the name of the assured; it should be addressed to the assured; and it should be billed to the assured. [111]

Chapter XX DEALING WITH CLAIMANT'S LAWYER

O MANY SETTLEMENTS 

for damages are made with lawyers that it is valuable to the adjuster to know the lawyer's viewpoint, and his interest in a claim.

Lawyers are so numerous that in their ranks will be found all manner of men. Some are ethical, others are not. Some are well-bred and intelligent, others are not. Some are honest, and some dishonest. The variation in lawyers is so great that they cannot be placed in one class, or considered as a type. So, the mere fact that a man is a lawyer leads to no conclusion as to what manner of man he is. He must first be known, or interviewed.

The law is a very broad field; so broad, in fact, that a lawyer can have little hope of being a specialist in all of its branches. Any honest lawyer will admit that there are branches of the law with which he has had very little acquaintance. If, in the course of your work, you contact men familiar with the law [112]

of negligence, you can reach a decision on disposition of the claim expeditiously.

Perhaps not one half of the lawyers you will meet know as much as you do about automobile negligence law, and will attempt to fill the place where knowledge should be with bluff and quotations from the

Latin. Under these circumstances, there is no need for the adjuster to feel any inferiority complex. 

Many people have no occasion to hire a lawyer until they have an accident, and if some one does not direct them to a lawyer friend, they are likely to recall the name of some lawyer about whom they have read in the newspapers. This man will most likely be a criminal lawyer, as crime makes the front page. The lawyer selected in this manner is probably not much interested in, or up on, negligence law, but nevertheless, he takes the claim.

In our large cities, so it is said, certain lawyers who seek and specialize in negligence cases go so far as to finance their clients until trial of the case is reached. Outside of our large cities ambulance chasing is not resorted to with any diligence, but as matters now stand, injury claims somehow, and almost without fail, find their way to a lawyer's office. 

Whether there is probable negligence, or not, if the [113]

injury is serious, a claimant can be expected to get into some law office.

The first interest, and sometimes the sole initial interest, which some lawyers have in a claim is to determine whether the person who caused the injury is responsible. If that party is responsible on account of an insurance policy, interest is immediate, as a good many lawyers look upon insurance companies as a pool of money into which to dip. The number of these lawyers is increasing. 

Some specialists in negligence law hire investigators to dig up the facts and witnesses to the facts. The expression "dig up" applies actually in some cases. 

These investigators often outdo the adjuster in getting witnesses to certain facts. This may be attributed to the fact that the investigator knows what he wants to get, and must get, and he also knows that if he succeeds, a law suit will follow which depends for its success on his work. Often the investigator will share in the amount of damages recovered. The adjuster, on the other hand, does not work with the same direct objective. His work may be just enough to enable him and his company to reach a decision on the question of liability. He does not have as an objective reaching the very bottom of the case, or the [114]

ultimate end of possible search in preparation for a defense which may or may not be necessary.

The different objectives of the adjuster and the claimant's investigator may be stated briefly in this manner: the adjuster desires to secure facts on which to appraise fairly the liability and damage, while the claimant's investigator primarily desires to secure enough evidence of negligence to make out a prima facie case, and, secondarily, desires to secure evidence which will win a case. If this primary objective is attained, there is established a real nuisance value. 

The adjuster, in his preliminary work, might get no witnesses, and yet, at the trial, the claimant will produce so many witnesses as to cause a comment like that which was made the time a judge remarked that it was peculiar that the accidents about which Attorney tried cases were always witnessed by so many people.

The cases are few and far between when claimant's lawyer receives a retaining fee for handling a claim arising from an automobile accident. Under conditions existing today, a retaining fee is expected only in those cases where the claimant is willing to gamble on the outcome of the case, but the lawyer believes that, except for a lucky break, the case will be lost. [115]

However, it is usual, except in some instances in the largest cities, for the claimant to pay court costs as they accrue.

The contingent fee in accident cases is usually onethird of the amount recovered. If the case is exceptionally strong, and the claim very large, the fee may be one-quarter and if the case is weak, the fee may be one-half.

Every company knows that it costs money to go to court, the cost ranging from $20, in real small cases, to several hundred dollars in the important cases. 

Claims in the middle bracket, say where the damages demanded are $3,000 to $10,000, will cost an estimated minimum of $250 to defend. If either side should appeal to a higher court; or, if there should be a new trial granted, the costs would be higher in order to cover these items.

Claimant's lawyer knows of these prospective expenses which the company must bear, even if it wins. the case, and a little experience teaches him that a company will probably prefer to pay some sum near the estimated defense costs by way of settlement in order to clear the file of the claim. This money which will be paid to clear the file is spoken of as the purchase price of nuisance value. The practice of buy- [116]

ing nuisance claims enables a lawyer to sell to the company an unenforceable claim for its threat value. 

It requires good judgment on the part of the adjuster to know the nuisance value, and to know what the claimant's lawyer regards as the nuisance value, as well as to know the degree of positiveness felt at the home office that the figure which the home office sets as maximum is the real maximum.

If the company which the adjuster represents has a reputation for making fair offers where there is a possibility of recovery, but fights, rather than pay, claims having no basis in merit, and that it often succeeds in defeating such cases, the adjuster's hand is strengthened. However, if the company has a reputation for being an easy settler and a buyer of unenforceable claims, or has taken a couple of bad beatings in court, then the adjuster will not find his work easy. 

He must take what advantage he can out of the particular circumstances existing. 

It is unfortunate that many claimants and their lawyers view an offer of nuisance value as a confession of weakness in ability to defend against the claim, and are thereby stimulated to press for a settlement based on liability.

As you know, there are 12 men or women on a [117]

jury, and their verdict must be unanimous. Lawyers with fancy ideas of the value of doubtful claims can often be brought to reason by reminding them that they must convince 12 men that their view of the case is right, whereas the company need convince only one juryman that its view is right.

If the first notice of a claim comes through a lawyer, this fact is an indication that the claim is so weak that the claimant is hiding behind the lawyer in the hope that he can make something of it. If the claimant hasn't the fairness, or sufficient feeling of justification, to make the initial demand on the assured, suspicion is justified that he has no confidence in his claim.

You will meet many lawyers who desire to be fair with you, while at the same time they discharge faithfully their full duty to the claimant. In such cases, the adjuster must meet the lawyer half way. As the claimant will receive only about two-thirds of the amount paid for the claim, the sum set for a settlement figure might be unacceptable to him, whereas, were he to get the full amount of the settlement, he would be satisfied. This means that the charge made by the lawyer for his services is the amount remaining to be adjusted. The adjuster must be prepared to [118]

pay more through the lawyer than he would if dealing directly with the claimant. 

This additional outlay should not be looked upon as wastage, because while it may be a real cause for dissatisfaction in some few cases, the work of the lawyer must be looked upon as a whole. He can, and will, in a great many instances bring about fair adjustments which the adjuster cannot secure in dealing directly with the claimant, and this for the reason that the adjuster is looked upon by the claimant as a party in interest against him, so that, no matter how fair the adjuster may try to be, instances will arise where he cannot get the claimant to see the fairness and the advisability of accepting the offer made. This same claimant, after going over the accident with his lawyer, and being advised that a certain settlement is wise, will take the lawyers' advice because he has confidence that the lawyer is taking his view-point and giving it full consideration. If, after understanding the claimant's position, the lawyer advises a certain settlement, the claimant has a feeling that he is being treated properly, and he will settle on a satisfactory basis. The company, then, gets real benefit from the fact that a lawyer is in the case. Looked at from another angle, if the company had to bring in [119]

a third party to effect a settlement, a charge for services would have to be met. The lawyer takes the place of such third party, so, it is reasonable to make an allowance for his services.

It is, then, not correct to view a lawyer as an interloper, or a meddlesome complication in the settlement of a claim, or as a stirrer-up of trouble, unless such is found actually to be the case. yer In dealing with the claimant's lawyer, an effort should be made to determine what authority the lawhas from the claimant. In some few cases, lawyers secure a contract from the client giving them, the lawyers, absolute control of the claim. A client under such a contract may be a restive client, and the lawyer may have difficulty keeping him under control. Contracts, giving the lawyer control, are likely to be found in large cities, and also in smaller cities in those cases where the client is a stranger to the lawyer. More generally, there is an understanding between lawyer and claimant that they will keep in touch with each other, and jointly work out offers and decisions. The nature of a lawyer's control over a claim can often be determined by suggesting that he go over the situation again with his client. His reply [120]

to this suggestion will generally reveal the information desired.

In this connection it is well for the adjuster to keep in mind the type of person he found the claimant to be, as some claimants want recognition, and a fair measure of compensation, whereas others want a finish fight for all that can be obtained. Some people will not hesitate to sue, others do not like to sue, but will do so if necessary, while others will not sue under any circumstances. Women, busy men, prominent men, especially prominent politicians, claimants who have lost a previous law suit, and corporations, or business people who depend greatly on local good will, all desire to stay out of court, and will go a long way to effect an amicable settlement. If you know what manner of claimant the lawyer is representing, you will have some guide as to how much cooperation from the claimant the lawyer will receive in insisting on a large settlement figure, and how low a figure the claimant will require his lawyer to accept. 

A lawyer, in attempting to settle an injury claim, is in reality acting as a sales agent for the claimant. 

His mere presence does not enhance the value of the claim. The sale price of the claim is indeterminate and may have no connection with the real value, in [121]

the same way that the sale price of art objects may have no connection with value. A shrewd seller of art objects may secure a high price from a gullible buyer, or a shrewd buyer may purchase at a low price from an ignorant seller. So, in claim settlements there is no necessary connection between purchase price and value. Lawyers generally appraise a claim at too high a value and often cause their clients to lose money by their inability to accept a price in line with value.

In those claims which are represented by lawyers, several facts exist which are decidedly in your favor. 

One is that, in other than exceptional cases, the lawyer receives nothing for his work until the claim is finally disposed of. If he has little practice, this fact will prompt him to work with the object of getting the file cleared. If he is a busy practitioner, he already will have enough litigation on hand, and will desire to close your claim if that can be done on a satisfactory basis.

Another fact in your favor is that lawyers, like other people, do not seek the hardest way out. A lawyer does not crave the exacting work of preparing a case for trial, and the nerve exhausting stress of a trial, if an amicable settlement can be effected. [122]

Another factor prompting a lawyer towards a settlement is that he knows that the best case, that is looking at the case as it appears before trial, can be lost; that the defense may have a surprise; that his witnesses may not testify as they have led him to believe they would; that his witnesses may not be believed; that his witnesses may not be available at the time of trial; that the defendant may die before trial is reached; etc., and he knows, as stated before, that owing to these uncertain factors, a bad settlement is better than a good law suit. These matters, though intangible, all tend to make an experienced lawyer agreeable to deal with. 

At one time it was considered by adjusters as smart to get back of the lawyer and effect a settlement direct with the claimant, before the claim was formally entered as a law suit. This practice, as well as others of an even shadier nature, resulted in adjusters being looked upon as scalpers and the reputation of the adjusting fraternity suffered severely. Such practices are not viewed as smart today, and adjustment work, with some exceptions, is carried on on a high plane; but it will take a long time for the old reputation to become replaced with a real respect for the honest service which adjusters perform. If the acts [123]

of the adjuster, in any community, are not on a high plane this fact soon becomes common knowledge among the lawyers of the community, and the company suffers in inability to secure proper cooperation in settling claims, and, also, the lawyers are tempted to start suits first, and talk later, so that a situation is created adverse to the interests of all parties concerned.

Adjusters look and work for the day when their efforts will be accepted with appreciation by the public, and when they will be looked upon as fair minded men who do honest and painstaking work rather than enemies of justice seeking to trick the public. 

On account of multiple claims arising from the same automobile accident, and the tendency of claimants to hang together in securing redress, lawyers largely unwittingly often get themselves into a position which is not strictly ethical. The ethical rule is that a lawyer should not represent two people who have conflicting interests. An adjuster might be glad to pay a pedestrian damages for cuts received by flying glass, but at the same time resist paying for damages to the claimant's automobile, as there appears to be a good defense to any property damage claim. 

Or, to use another illustration, he may be willing to [124]

pay one claimant to whom the assured owed a duty to use ordinary negligence, but he may not care to settle with another claimant involved in the same accident to whom the assured owed only a duty to refrain from acts of gross negligence. In such cases, the claimants' interests are different. The lawyer who represents both claimants insists that both claims must be settled, or neither. This makes it difficult for the adjuster to handle the claim justly. Diplomacy and considerable thought is required to work a way around this situation. It might prove helpful, in such cases to indicate to the lawyer that you feel that he is remiss in his duty to the claimant with whom you desire to settle and that he cannot justify his position. It might further be intimated that if the claimant is not advised of the proposed settlement, which should be satisfactory to him, that you will see that word of the proposed settlement gets to this claimant.

Perhaps nothing annoys people more than to be cheated of just rewards. Some claimants are so dishonest and unscrupulous that they will, if opportunity is offered, cheat the lawyer of his fee. You can secure the good will of the legal fraternity by cooperating with lawyers to reduce the possibility of [125]

their client cheating them. This cooperation is to make certain that the lawyer's name is on any check drawn to pay the claim. It is a personal responsibility of the adjuster to see that the home office is advised of what names to put on the check. Checks may be made in two ways to protect the lawyer. The first is to make the check payable to the lawyer as attorney for the claimant. As the lawyer is the duly authorized agent of the claimant, his acceptance of the check is a satisfaction of the claim just as if the check had been drawn to the claimant. As, however, the proper amount of the proceeds might not get to the claimant, the company may have some further bother about the payment even though the company cannot be forced to make an additional payment. On account of this weakness, unless peculiar circumstances exist, large checks should be made in the second suggested manner, i.e., make the check to the lawyer and the claimant as joint payees. If this is done the claimant will have to endorse the check, and will know the correct settlement figure; will know that the claim is paid, and will see that he gets his portion of it, and at the same time the lawyer will be protected. [126]

If the claim arises from a really serious accident, and it appears, at once, to the adjuster that it will cause considerable trouble and result in large claims, which claims will be pushed, if for no other reason that they are large, it is advisable to start working with the company's lawyer at once, so that proof will be secured, definitely covering the vital points. 

After a suit has been filed and served, unless he has previously done so, the adjuster turns his file over to the company's lawyer. At this time the adjuster should advise the lawyer of any details and bits of information which he may have in regard to the claim, but which are not a part of the record. 

At this point, many adjusters think that they are through, and recede into the background. The adjuster is not through until this, like other claims, is definitely closed. [127]

The adjuster should see that the lawyer reviews the file carefully and promptly, to pick up loose ends, to review witness's statements for weak spots, interview any witness whose story needs to be clarified, or checked. Key witnesses should be brought to the lawyer's office so that he can revalue their testimony. 

After this is done, the adjuster should get an appraisal from the lawyer of the probable limits of recovery, and so aid the company to keep the loss reserve adequate to cover the loss.

This appraised amount of probable recovery is an approximation only. Even though the estimated amount of probable recovery is soundly based, there exists the possibility that some unanticipated event may interpose itself and materially increase the recovery. This possibility must be guarded against. 

On account of the increasing tendency of courts to hold insurance companies liable for the amount of any judgment in excess of the policy limit in those cases which could have been settled for less than the policy limit, and in which there is any neglect on the part of the company to protect the assured against contingent liability, care must be exercised, if the reasonably possible recovery exceeds the policy limit, [128]

that the company is protected against the contingency of having to pay any excess recovered. 

If there appears to be a possibility that the recovery will exceed the policy limit, the adjuster should have the company's lawyer write a letter to the assured pointing out the importance of the case, the amount sued for, the policy limit, the possibility of the recovery exceeding the policy limit and offering to the assured an opportunity to engage a lawyer to represent his interest in protecting against the contingent liability, and offering to accept the cooperation of this lawyer in preparing and trying the case. 

Occasionally a collision will occur and each operator will demand damages from the other. In such a case the right of the company to settle with the claimant is granted by the policy. This right must not be exercised arbitrarily, because if the company by the settlement prejudices adversely the rights of the assured to collect for his damages the assured may make a claim against the company for his loss thereby. 

Instances also arise wherein the assured insists that the claimant shall not be paid a claim which the company desires decidedly to settle. In instances of this nature it is well to go over the facts with the com- [129]

pany's lawyer, or the home office, before taking any decisive action.

Some cases may arise in which the difference between the amount offered by the company in settlement, and the amount demanded by the claimant are not far apart, but neither party will make a new offer. In such case it is fair to apprize the assured of the existing situation and show him the fairness of the company in fixing the amount of its offer. He may, after understanding the situation, prefer to make up the difference personally, rather than be bothered with a trial of the case. There is no fundamental objection to an assured's making a voluntary contribution in order to dispose of the nuisance of a claim. An opportunity to close a case in this way should not be overlooked.

At a reasonable time before a trial, the adjuster should check the present location of witnesses to see if any are missing, and he should, where possible, get the employment address to facilitate service of subpoenas. Friendly witnesses should be kept advised as to the probable trial date, so that they can arrange to get time off from work. Any payment, above the regular witness fee, should be arranged for at this time. Doctors' charges for testifying should be fixed [130]

before trial. Arrangements for transporting witnesses to the court house should be made. Any disclosed change in status of a witness should be conveyed to the company's lawyer.

If all of the men who work on accident claims were graded according to their proficiency as investigators and their proficiency as adjusters, no doubt the great majority would receive a better grade as adjusters than as investigators. This may be due to a preponderence of affability, charm, and persuasion over imagination, reflection, correlation, and precision. 

It will aid in bringing about more equality in grading if adjusters will cooperate wholeheartedly with the company's lawyer in preparing and marshalling defenses as, in this way, the adjuster will gain knowledge by observing what facts are vital in the defense of cases arising under a variety of circumstances. [131]

Chapter XXII RELEASES 

RELEASE IS SUPPOSED TO 

be a final disposition of a claim; all parties to it are supposed to be satisfied with the terms of settlement forming the basis of the release, and the release is exchanged for the check to close the claim. 

The release is not necessarily the termination of the claim. At a later date, the claimant may develop a feeling of dissatisfaction, and demand a larger settlement. Before he can become legally entitled to more, he must attack the release, and have it set aside. 

He can have this done, provided he can show that the release is inequitable.

The reasons for which a release may be set aside are, in brief: (a) That although he acted as a reasonably prudent person would in making the release; yet, it represents a mistake on his part, or on the part of both parties, regarding a material fact, which [132]

fact was beyond his knowledge at the time, and not reasonably to be anticipated under the circumstances, and the release gives to the company such an advantage as to shock the conscience of a reasonable person. This ground is most difficult to establish. The reason stated below is the one most commonly advanced; (b) That there was gross inadequacy of consideration paid for the release. In this case, after gross inadequacy has been shown, the company must come forward with explanation to show that the release was secured under circumstances free from false, deceitful, or unfair means calculated to take advantage of the claimant; or this third ground; (c) That in addition to gross inadequacy of consideration, paid for the release, the claimant executed the release through weakness of mind, that is, unsound, disordered mind, but not necessarily insanity, or that the claimant was forced to make the release under dire pecuniary distress, or by fraud, duress, or while so under the influence of intoxicants, or drugs that he was not mentally responsible for his acts. [133]

A mere mistake on the part of the claimant in estimating his anticipated disability period, or subsequently developed physical complications arising from the injury, does not constitute sufficient ground to have the release set aside. The fact that the claimant made a bad bargain is not sufficient cause to set aside the release.

All of the reasons for which a release can be set aside may be summed up thus: If the release is secured by other than fair means, it is subject to and may be attacked. To defend a release against attack will be expensive. Act so as to make releases which claimants cannot, in good faith, attack.

It often is necessary to secure releases of claims by minors. A release is, in fact, a contract, and a minor is under disability to make a contract. His claim, unless properly disposed of at a prior date, accrues, or comes into effect, in his own right, on his twentyfirst birthday, and he has until he becomes 21 years of age, plus the time specified in the Statute of Limitations of his state, to sue on his claim. His right to claim for his damages after he becomes 21 years of age is not barred because someone has executed a release for him, and accepted money for the release, even though he secured benefits on account of the [134]

release. It is a sad fact, but a true one, that parents sometimes sell out their children's interest in order to get some ready cash. The law does not give to third parties any vested interest in claims by minors for damages, regardless of the relationship of the minor to the third party. An orderly and legal means has been provided for minors to secure their rights through court action, and under the scrutiny of the court.

The courts are open to minors, and they are entitled to their day in court just as an adult is. The laws of the state specify that suits by minors may be brought through some adult as a representative of the minor, and the law specifies who these adults can be. Ordinarily, the father, as the natural guardian of the child, has the first right to represent his child; then comes the mother, if the father be dead; then come next friends of the minor. If the parents are divorced, the parent who was given custory of the child has the first right to represent the child.

In presenting small claims on behalf of the minor, the minor's representative usually includes all items of expense incurred as a result of the injury. This is practical, but is not strictly accurate, as the parent of the minor is responsible for medical expenses. In [135]

the case of divorced parents, the decree of divorce will probably specify which parent is responsible for medical expenses. These items, then, are not a proper element of damage payable to the minor, but in small claims, especially those settled on an expense basis, if these medical expenses were omitted there would be little, or nothing, left worth a settlement. A release should be obtained from the parent responsible for the medical expenses, so as to dispose of any claim by him, or her.

If it be desired to settle a claim of a minor, which involves any substantial sum of money, it is the better practice to have the company's attorney prepare a friendly suit against the assured, and then have judgment entered by confession, and then have the judgment satisfied. This method will close the claim of the minor and, with the release of the legal representative, will close the claim.

It is usual to pay small judgments of this sort direct to the representative of the child, using the same designation on the check that the representative used in filing the suit, as Charles H. Jones, father and natural guardian of Charles H. Jones, Jr., A Minor.

If the amount of the judgment includes a substantial sum for personal injuries, a question may arise [136]

at a future date whether the money was applied for the account of the minor, and whether the company had a duty to see that this money was properly expended, or saved, for the benefit of the child. In such cases, it is worth while to insist that a legal guardian of the estate of the minor be appointed by the court, and payment to this guardian withheld until he has qualified. The guardian is then responsible to the court for his administration of the estate of the minor. 

Similar precautions should be followed in securing releases from any person who is incompetent to contract. In such cases, if the incompetent has a guardian, or trustee, duly appointed and qualified, this person will present the settlement to the court for ratification, and after this approval is secured, payment may be made to this representative, but the check should be to him in his representative capacity, as John H. Jones, Trustee of Edward R. Smith, An Incompetent.

It is the usual practice to refuse to settle one claim arising from an accident, unless all claims arising from that accident can be closed at the same time. This practice works very well in the settlement of property damage claims, or small injury claims. How- [137]

ever, one important exception should be made. In a case where a personal injury claim, which threatens to be substantial, can be settled for nuisance, or threat value, it should be disposed of, even though the property damage claim remains open. Such action will not prejudice the defense of the property damage claim, because in the release non-liability is expressly stated, and the claimant is precluded from using such settlement as proof at the trial of the property damage case.

Not infrequently accidents are caused by the concurrent negligence of two, or more, wrongdoers. In such an event the person who suffered the damage has a right of action against all, or each, of the wrongdoers, regardless of the proportion which the negligence of each wrongdoer contributed to the accident. 

If the claimant, in such a case, releases one wrongdoer, this release releases all of the wrongdoers. 

These comments on releases will make the adjuster aware of the necessity of making certain that the release actually does release the assured. [138]

Chapter XXIII FINAL APPRAISAL 

FTER THE ADJUSTER'S 

report is complete, and he reaches the welcome conclusion that all of the facts are in, and that he has these facts classified and appraised so that he and others can properly weigh the liability, or lack of liability, he may rest in mellow content that he has the perfect defense to any claim arising out of the accident, and he is ready to give an emphatic "No" to any claimant, and stick to his "No" until the bitter end. 

Then a disquieting thought enters his mind: "Must people necessarily agree with my conclusions?" which in turn is followed by the weaker query, "Will people agree with my conclusions?" Of course, you cannot answer these questions conclusively, because the answer must come through the operation of the minds of others. One cannot compel other minds to operate in certain ways, or to reach a certain conclusion. 

Many times your conclusions will be agreed with, [139]

but if the claim be a major one, some lawyer will no doubt get it, and give a different slant to the cause of the accident than you have taken. He visualizes the final arbiter of questions of fact; twelve men in a jury box, and the pertinent, immediate question in the lawyer's mind is, "Can I get this case to the jury for deliberation, sympathetic acceptance and verdict?"

Bear in mind that the facts which you have gathered are now viewed for effectiveness as matters of defense. This must be so, because the claimant has taken the offensive. These facts gathered by you, which in your opinion constitute a perfect defense, are not presented to the jury until the facts favorable to the claimant are presented. The facts, presented by the claimant, were dug up by someone who had the offensive point of view, and this investigator may have dug deep and well enough to have facts which appear rather favorable to the claimant. When all of the claimant's witnesses have been heard, claimant rests his case. At this point two questions are presented which the judge must decide. These questions are: (a) Do the facts, as presented, constitute a prima facie case; that is, giving full credit to the evidence, [140]

and assuming that no defense were interposed, is this evidence sufficient to warrant the jury in finding negligence on the part of the defendant, and damage to the claimant? (b) Do the facts, as presented, establish, as a matter of law, that the claimant was guilty of contributory negligence? (In some few states this question (b) will be: Do the facts, as presented, establish that the claimant was free from contributory negligence?) 

You will note that little evidence of negligence, whether true, or not, suffices to warrant an affirmative answer to question (a). A negative answer may be secured to question (b), but the chances are against a negative answer, because the witnesses were selected from those available to the claimant, and a witness who would give, unless inadvertently, evidence of contributory negligence would not be called and used. The claimant can be counted upon to protect his case, if possible, from exposing contributory negligence. 

If the judge answers question (a) "Yes" and question (b) "No," your defense must be presented. [141]

(In the few states excepted, the answer to the second question, as framed above in brackets, will be "Yes.")

At the conclusion of all the evidence by both parties, these two questions are asked and answered again. 

If the same answers, as before, are received to the questions, the jury will weigh the evidence and arrive at a verdict.

At this point, note this vital fact. A jury can reach any verdict it wants to reach, just so long as there is evidence in the case to justify the verdict. 

Note also that a jury can disregard the entire testimony of any witness it believes to have lied about any material fact. The jury is the final arbiter of facts.

The claimant need not offer evidence which is conclusive, nor need he prove his case beyond a reasonable doubt. His obligation is to prove his case by a fair preponderance of the evidence. What constitutes a fair preponderance of the evidence is a matter exclusively for the jury to decide. Preponderance of evidence is not determined by the number of witnesses nor the length of the evidence. For that reason one excellent witness may overweigh half a dozen weak witnesses. It is in this connection that credibility of witnesses is so important. Further, a jury [142]

never offers explanation for its verdict. The one requisite is that there must be evidence to support the verdict, and there will be such evidence, otherwise question (a) above would have been answered in the negative, and the judge would have directed the jury to find a verdict for the defendant. You will note that this extreme latitude allowed to a jury places a jury in a position to almost arbitrarily dispose of a case.

The amount of damages determined by a jury is not the finding of facts. So, if the amount of damages is grossly excessive, or clearly inadequate, the amount as determined by a jury may be reduced, or a new trial ordered. But as to the finding by the jury that the evidence satisfies them that the operator was, or was not, negligent, the finding of the jury is final, and cannot be set aside, unless it is clearly shown that the finding was reached owing to passion, prejudice, or some other emotion so strong as to sway the reason of the jury. In negligence cases, such an attitude on the part of the jury is most difficult to establish. This dominant position of the jury, as to finding the facts, is guaranteed by the seventh amendment to the United States Constitution.

While, as stated, it is most difficult to establish that [143]

the emotions of the jurors were so aroused as to rob them of reason, yet everybody who has had experience with juries, knows that they are swayed by emotion, although this swaying is seldom enough to rob the jury of reason. Every jury is swayed by the parties, the evidence, the lawyers, and various episodes which take place during a trial. This swaying is natural.

If the claimant be an attractive woman, a child, or a person attracting sympathy, or if the assured looks like a person who would be negligent, if defense witnesses seem to lie, or are disreputable looking people, if the negligence seems to be aggravated, etc., all of these matters, while not evidence according to the record in the case, are evidence which sways the jury one way or another.

As a consequence, when the evidence is all in, that perfect defense, so carefully built by your hard work, may be ineffectual to prevent a verdict against the defendant. You now know that people do not necessarily have to agree with your conclusion, and that this jury in particular did not agree with your conclusion.

Such an experience is most depressing. It is sad [144]

to learn that feelings rule, rather than reason, but situations must be dealt with as we find them. 

The comments above have been directed to the unfavorable aspect. Now look at the favorable aspect. If the defendant is a beauty, or if other factors are favorable to the creation of emotional sway toward the defendant, your case will be greatly aided.

It is a poor rule that does not work both ways. You should get half of the breaks if you rely on luck, and more than half of the breaks if you analyze the emotional reaction to the intangible elements connected with the claim, and dispose of, by way of settlement, those claims where the emotional elements will probably override factual elements necessary to defend successfully in court.

The emotional elements must be considered also in determining probable damages, if any, which will be awarded. Even though a jury may decide that it must find the facts against you, if the emotional element favors the defendant, the damages will be low, whereas if the emotional element is against the defendant, the damages will be high. In other instances, of course, the damages will tend to be as fair as is humanly possible. The presence of the emotional element, for, or against, accounts in large measure [145]

for the fact that the amount of damages awarded in different cases for approximately the same injury, varies so greatly. It also accounts, in part, for the fact that experienced people, trying from a distance to appraise the value of a certain injury, will be so far wrong in their estimate. The more accurately the adjuster reports noted emotional elements, the closer the home office can come to determining the real value of a claim.

The above has been written to give an understanding of the real basis, in fact, of the expressions so often heard, "A bad settlement is better than a good law suit," and "The best case can be lost. 

Personal injury cases can be, and are, successfully defended, and perhaps more of them should be defended; but excellent judgment is required to select those claims which can be allowed to go to trial, and those which should be settled out of court. [146]

Chapter XXV 

WAIVER AND NON-WAIVER

EACH 

ACH POLICY CONTAINS 

a provision that no change in the terms and conditions shall be binding on the company, unless in writing, and signed by one of certain officers of the company. This provision applies to conditions before loss, and not to the requirements after loss occurs.

For instance, if the automobile covered by the policy, is specified as a pleasure automobile, the agent and the adjuster cannot change the policy to cover this vehicle as a commercial automobile, but if the policy requires immediate notice of a personal injury, the adjuster can waive this, and accept the notice at some later date. So the company, through the acts, or statements of the adjuster, or other employee may waive requirements such as reports, cooperation, interfering with settlement, or other requirement subsequent to loss.

The basis for this is that, if the assured through the acts or statements of the adjuster, is led to believe [153]

that nothing more is required of him, or that some wrong done by him has been excused, he is justified in assuming that all is satisfactory to the company, and the company is no longer in a position to claim a violation of the policy. So, if an informal report of the accident is made, and the adjuster goes to work without requiring the assured to make a formal report, the company cannot later claim forfeiture because of failure to make the formal report.

Some policies contain a non-waiver stipulation which allows the company, through the adjuster, to do certain acts without those acts being acts of waiver. These stipulations do not prevent the adjuster from making a waiver of the non-waiver stipulation by his independent acts and statements. 

If denial of liability is made on ground other than non-performance of policy terms; or if liability is admitted, then all requirements, as to future acts to be done by the insured, are waived.

The right to assert a forfeiture might accrue at the beginning of the claim, and be based on undue delay in reporting, or failure to report, the accident. 

If there appears to have been undue delay, check must be made to determine the time and person who first received notice. Then check should be made [154]

to determine whether the delay was waived, or acquiesced in, by any authorized person. 

Likewise, as the investigation proceeds, other reasons for forfeiture may appear. A reason discovered in the course of the investigation may have existed prior to the loss, but the company had no prior notice of it. The event allowing for forfeiture may occur at any time, or be discovered at any time before the loss is paid, but the company is not bound to act in regard to it until the company acquires knowledge of it.

The circumstances surrounding the reason for forfeiture may be such that the company does not care to, or is not ready to, assert non-liability under the policy due to the breach by the assured of his contract, and, of course, the assured is desirous that the company should delay definite adverse action in the hope that the definite action will be not to insist on the forfeiture.

In such case, the adjuster should explain to the assured the reasons which he finds to exist which permit the company to call for a forfeiture of the policy, and, that under the circumstances, he cannot proceed further with his investigation, or efforts to [155]

effect an adjustment, until he receives from the assured a non-forfeiture agreement.

Such an agreement sets out clearly the claimed reasons for forfeiture, and provides that the assured authorizes the company to proceed to take all steps advisable to investigate and adjust the claim without waiving the claimed breach of the terms of the policy specified in the non-waiver agreement. In this way, the rights of both parties as to the claimed ground for forfeiture, are maintained in the same status as of the date the non-waiver agreement is signed. 

In the event that the assured refuses to sign a nonwaiver agreement, and you feel that the company will not desire to call a forfeiture, then write a letter to the assured stating the ground of forfeiture, and that, after a certain date, you will proceed with your investigation without waiving any of the rights of the company, unless, before that date he instructs you that you are to do nothing further about the claim. This is a practical manner of handling what might become a delicate situation, as there is great probability that the claim will be disposed of, and the point at issue, regarding ground for forfeiture, will be automatically disposed of.

A non-waiver agreement may itself be waived, so [156]

the adjuster needs to be careful, after effecting the non-waiver, not to make any commitment which will lead the assured to believe that the non-waiver agreement is no longer in effect.

If the ground of forfeiture is such that the adjuster desires to call a forfeiture, he should call a forfeiture in a letter which notifies the assured definitely that the company calls for a forfeiture, and he should state definitely the ground on which the forfeiture is based. State clearly all of the reasons for forfeiture relied upon, as, at a later date, the company may be estopped from asserting other reasons which were known at the time the forfeiture was called.

Courts and companies look with disfavor on forfeitures, unless it clearly appears that the interests of the company have been substantially injured by the failure of the assured to perform the contract terms. The value of a non-waiver agreement lies, not so much in its use to preserve to the company some right which the company will not care to exercise except in a flagrant case, as in its use as a pawn in trading with a claimant who demands an unreasonable settlement of a claim against an assured who has little, if any, financial responsibility. 

As there are no contract relations between the [157]

claimant and the company, the company is not bound to the claimant by any acts of the adjuster in regard to investigation, appraisal of damages, etc., but the company may be bound by definite commitments of the adjuster. 

NOTE: In fire and theft losses, the contract is direct with the assured, so if the adjuster leads the assured to believe that the claim will be paid on the securing of certain data, and, as a result of this belief, the assured undertakes labor, or expense in securing this data, the act of the adjuster, in requesting this data, may be considered as a waiver of ground for forfeiture. 

Avoid this possibility. [158]

Chapter XXVI SUBROGATION 

IT 

F THE ASSURED, under a collision policy, has an accident with a third party due to negligence of the third party, and if the assured does not collect his damages from the third party, he has a right, under his policy, to collect his damage from the company, as provided for in his policy. When he receives payment from the company, his rights against the third party automatically transfer to the company. The right of subrogation is stated in the policy more to advise the assured regarding it, than for any other purpose, as the right exists, whether, or not, it is expressed in the policy. 

If the assured receives compensation for a loss from the third party, who is responsible for the loss, the insurance company is relieved from liability to the extent of the payment received. If this payment is received by the assured in full satisfaction of his loss, the company is fully released from liability under the policy. This must be so, as it is inequitable [159]

that an assured should be compensated by the company and by the third person, as, then, a profit would accrue to the assured, and such profit is against both the theory and practice of insurance.

The assured has a choice to look to the company, or to the third party, for compensation to cover the loss. If the third party refuses to pay the loss, or if the assured elects to ignore the third party, the company must pay the loss. It would be inequitable for the assured, by election, to collect from the company, and thereby release the wrongdoer, and leave the company without a right to reimbursement. So, equity has established a rule that if the company makes payment to the assured under the policy, it becomes subrogated to the rights of the assured to the extent of the amount paid, and may assert a claim against the third party, and recover for itself the amount paid on the loss.

The rule of the common law, as stated above, has been, in some states, restated in a statute, while in New York the operation of this rule has been limited to the rights actually expressed in the policy. 

At times it is necessary that suit be instituted to recover from the wrongdoer. In such event, it is practical for the company to assign back to the [160]

assured its subrogated rights, and have the assured sue, as better results are likely if an individual, rather than the company, appears as the plaintiff. The costs of suits should be pro rated between the company and the assured in proportion to the amount of the claim of each against the wrongdoer.

A complex situation may, at times, result from subrogation. One accident might bring into play a large number of policies. This duty varies with the circumstances surrounding each accident, and consequently the degree of negligence varies as the duty varies. The degrees of negligence are spoken of as slight, or as ordinary, or as gross, or as wilful and wanton. If negligence is looked upon as a variable term, classified under these four heads, it is rather difficult to get a basic understanding of each class. It is much easier, and more accurate to view negligence as a stable term, and as being negligence, no matter how characterized, and as being the failure to exercise DUE care under the circumstances. What constitutes "due care” is variable, according to circumstances. 

To a passenger riding for hire, the operator owes a duty to exercise a degree of care described variously as "high," "highest," "utmost," and defined as the [164]

highest degree of care which an operator of ordinary prudence would exercise under similar circumstances.

This degree of care is restraint from acts of slight negligence.

To a person riding in, or on, an automobile without the knowledge of the operator, the operator owes no duty.

To an uninvited person riding in, or on, the automobile with the knowledge of the operator, who endures his presence, the operator owes, in a few states, the same degree of care as is owed to a guest; but in most of the states, the operator owes a duty to exercise but slight care. This slight measure of duty is defined as a duty to refrain from wilfully, or wantonly, bringing injury to such person. The violation of this degree of duty is called wilful and wanton negligence. "Wilful negligence" is a misnomer, because an act cannot be wilful, that is by intent, and negligent at the same time. "Wanton negligence" is unrestrained conduct amounting to an abandonment of care.

To a person, or to property, outside the automobile, the operator owes a higher degree of duty than to the person described above. This must be so, because the person, or owner, of the property has certain [165]

rights in the enjoyment of the public highways which are shared by all. The care which is owed to these persons, or this property, is defined as a "duty to use ordinary care to protect them from injury." The violation of this duty is negligence; and this negligence, because it is the most usual form, is called "ordinary negligence," or negligence without qualification. A true definition of this negligence is "the failure of a responsible person, either by omission, or by action, to exercise that degree of care, vigilence, and forethought, which, in the discharge of the duty then resting on him, a person of ordinary caution and prudence ought to exercise under the particular circumstances." 

You will notice that the person must be a responsible person. So, an idiot cannot be charged with negligence. Nor can a man suddenly overcome by a heart attack, or a woman with a fainting spell, be charged with negligence, provided they had no reason to anticipate the disability.

The degree of care required towards infants depends upon the age and knowledge of the infant. 

A child may be so young as to be incapable of comprehending danger. Such a child cannot under any circumstances act negligently, and the operator is [166]

held to a high degree of care. There is no set age at which ability to apprehend comes." This is a matter of fact to be determined in each instance. As the ability to apprehend increases, the duty of a child to use care increases, but at no set pace. As age increases, the ability to use care increases, and, correspondingly, the degree of care required of the operator decreases.

The caution which an ordinary man ought to exercise is dependent on the circumstances. So, to drive fifty miles per hour on the open road may not be negligent, whereas driving at five miles per hour in a parade crowd might be negligent. If a child is apparently coming into the path of an automobile, a much greater degree of care must be exercised than if a grown man is involved. The degree of care varies according to each case, and yet, each act is the breach of the same general duty, and is charged as ordinary negligence.

To a gratuitous guest riding in an automobile, the operator owes a different degree of duty, depending upon the law of the state in which the accident This difference came about because some states decided that applicable common law directed occurs. * See page 225. [167]

This duty has been defined in this way: "that the gratuitous undertaker shall be mindful of the life and limb of his guest, and shall not unreasonably expose him to additional perils." *

The courts of two states, Massachusetts and Virginia, took the other view: that the operator owed to his guest a less degree of care than to the public. 

This degree of care is defined as "a duty to refrain from acts of gross negligence."

On account of the fact that it appeared unjust and inequitable to impose upon the host a duty to his guest of the same degree as that imposed upon him towards the general public, the legislatures of many states have passed statutes, changing the required degree of care so that in these states the host is liable to his gratuitous guest only when he is guilty of wilful, wanton, or gross negligence. It is quite probable that such legislation will be passed in other states. 

As some standard is necessary by which to meas- * See page 226. [168]

ure gross negligence, this definition from Massachusetts is given: "Gross negligence is substantially and appreciably higher in magnitude than ordinary negligence. It is materially more want of care than constitutes simple inadvertance. It is an act or omission respecting legal duty of an aggravated character, as distinguished from a mere failure to exercise ordinary care. It is very great negligence, or the absence of slight diligence, or the want of even scant care. It amounts to indifference to present legal duty, and to utter forgetfulness of legal obligation so far as other persons may be effected. It is a heedless and palpable violation of legal duty respecting the rights of others. 

The element of culpability which characterizes all negligence is in gross negligence magnified to a high degree, as compared with that present in ordinary negligence. Gross negligence is manifestly a smaller amount of watchfulness than the circumstances require of a person of ordinary prudence. But it is something less that the wilful, wanton, and reckless conduct which renders a defendant in rightful possession of real estate liable to a trespasser he has injured. It falls short of being such reckless disregard of probable consequences as is equivalent to a [169]

wilful and intentional wrong. Ordinary and gross negligence differ in degree of inattention, while both differ in kind from wilful and intentional conduct which is, or ought to be, known to have a tendency to injure." *

When the nature of the claim is such that gross negligence must be proven, the claimant, or the claimant's lawyer, is apt to base his claim of gross negligence on acts of the operator which constitute reckless driving, and, as evidence of reckless driving, will refer to certain sections of the road rules of the state regarding reckless driving. If these road rules specify that the doing of the act shall be prima facie evidence of reckless driving, that is as far as the road rule can be used. Other road rules, however, specify that any person doing certain acts, naming them, shall be, prima facie, guilty of reckless driving.

There is a great distinction between these two forms of rules. The first mentioned is a rule of evidence, and applies to civil claims, and so must be considered by you. The other is meant to be used only in the prosecution of the operator on traffic charges. 

Such a road rule has no application in civil cases, and need not be considered in regard to a claim as having * See page 226. [170]

any weight in establishing gross negligence in a civil case.* The one has to do with proof, while the other has to do with guilt.

Many statutes, regulations and ordinances have been passed to regulate automobiles. These statutes, or road rules, specify that the operator shall, or shall not, do certain acts. These road rules are made for the benefit of all, and in the interests of all. They impose duties on anyone operating an automobile. 

To so operate an automobile as to violate one of these rules is negligence. Whether this negligence is ordinary, wanton, or gross, depends on the circumstances involved in the violation. It is ordinary negligence in any event, and may be wanton or gross.

In your work, you have noticed that the great majority of accidents concern the violation of some road rule, and, consequently, it is necessary that you be thoroughly familiar with the road rules of your state. In this connection, it may be informative to state that while the legislative authority makes the rules of the road, the courts interpret these rules. As a sample of this judicial interpretation, this illustration will suffice. Consider that a road rule specifies in definite words that the automobile coming from * See page 226. [171]

the right at any uncontrolled intersection shall have the right of way. This seems definite, but when looked at by the court, the court will say that this road rule does not confer on the automobile coming from the right, any absolute right of way; that this automobile operator retains a duty to keep a lookout for other automobiles, and control his automobile with relation to other automobiles near that intersection. The court has thus limited the meaning to this road rule in a way which is not to be found in the rule, but, still, the meaning as found by the court is sound in carrying out the general intent of the statute, which is to make for safer use of the streets and highways.

So, in addition to knowing the road rules, it is necessary for you to know how the courts of your state construe these road rules. 

It is obvious to anyone reading statutes enacting road rules, that the doing of many of the prohibited acts is also the doing of an act of negligence under the common law, so that the road rule is simply a statement to operators of applicable common law.

If, however, there is a conflict between the common law and the road rule statute, then the statute holds good over the common law. [172]

The courts, in civil actions, in making rulings regarding the effect to be given to the violation of these road rules, were confronted with this question: 

Should the violation of the road rules be of itself negligence? or, as expressed technically, should the violation be negligence, per se. By this is meant that proof of the violation is conclusive proof of negligence, meaning ordinary negligence. The answer in most states was "Yes."

Above, we have considered road rules which specify that the operator shall, or shall not, do certain acts. 

There is contained in most road rules statutes another group of rules which do not order, or forbid, certain acts, but do specify that the doing, or not doing, of these acts shall be prima facie evidence of negligence.

An example of this sort of rule is one stating that an operator leaving the scene of an accident without making his identity known, shall be, prima facie, guilty of negligence. This means that the act of leaving the scene shall be presumed to be admission of negligence. The presumption lasts only until the operator gives a satisfactory explanation of his act, or failure to act. If the explanation is convincing that he had a sufficient reason for leaving the scene, this road rule has been overcome and need be no [173]

longer considered. But if the explanation is not satisfactory, the charge of negligence stands.

While on the subject of road rules, or traffic regulations, another angle must be considered. The violation of any of them draws the punishment specified, regardless of whether, or not, anyone suffered any damage on account of the violation. It does not follow that because of a violation in which some person, or property, is injured, the operator is liable for the damages ensuing.

The acts of the operator out of which grow charges of violation of a road rule may have no real, or immediate, bearing on the cause of the happening of the accident. In order that liability can flow from the act of negligence charged against the operator, it is necessary that the claimant go further than mere proof of the act of negligence. He must, in addition, show that the act of negligence was the immediate and proximate cause of the accident. For instance, the act of negligence may be excessive speed.

If the operator's automobile is on its right side of the road, and traveling at excessive speed, and is sideswiped by an automobile approaching in the center of the road, the unlawful speed of the operator's automobile was not a cause of the accident. As an- [174]

other example, the operator may be negligent in driving in the center of the road, and into an intersection where his automobile is struck by another automobile approaching from his left which passes a stop sign without stopping. Here the fact that the operator was in the center of the road, in violation of a road rule, is not a factor, as the violation had no part in causing the accident. 

This matter of producing, or proximate, cause must be kept in mind when the assured is convicted in a criminal proceeding, of a violation of the laws of the road, and a claim arises from an accident produced at the time the operator committed the violation. Such a conviction is not proof of liability. 

The conviction establishes only that the operator was guilty of a violation of law. It does not establish, or tend to establish, the real cause of the accident, as the violation may have no bearing on the real cause of the accident. The accident may have been caused by a distinct act of negligence committed by the claimant, and the conviction of the operator, in a criminal proceeding, need not necessarily bar a civil claim by the operator.

Very many charges of violation of road rules are made as a form of duress to force the operator, under [175]

the stress of possible punishment, to pay for damages for which he is not legally liable. The operator is likely, in such case, to look to the adjuster to settle the accident, and so free him from the possibility of conviction. Now, the operator and the adjuster are fully aware of the fact that the assured's policy does not undertake to defend the operator in case criminal charges are preferred, or to pay company money to clear the assured of such charges. So, an adjuster must make his position perfectly clear, and not undertake, through sympathy, to pay damages which he should not pay. To do that is, in essence, to pay the fine of the operator.

In this connection though, it must be said that when an assured faces traffic charges, the adjuster must cooperate as best he can to avoid a conviction. 

The adjuster should strive, wherever possible, to avoid the operator's being put to embarrassment due to his lack of extra diligence in investigating the claim. 

If the adjuster will go ahead rapidly with his investigation, he can promptly learn whether the violation charged was the proximate cause of the accident, and whether he has an adequate defense to the claim. 

If he finds that liability rests on the operator he can settle for property damage in short order, and, as [176]

an incident, free the operator from his difficulty with the police authorities. If the accident produced serious personal injuries, he will have to take steps to negotiate with the claimant, and secure from the claimant cooperation in having charges dismissed. If the adjuster finds that there is contributory negligence, he can deny liability, and let the operator take care of the traffic charges.

The police and traffic authorities are becoming used to the insurance companies' point of view, and the fair manner in which the companies take care of accident claims; so that, in case the operator is insured, the authorities are not now prone to use the threat of prosecution as duress to force payments which may not be legally justified. When the authorities learn that an insurance company is in the matter, to discharge fairly any legal obligation to the claimant, their interest in prosecution recedes, and they are inclined to let the parties, or the civil courts, dispose of the accident. So, when the operator is likely to be charged with violation of regulations, the adjuster, by properly performing his duties, can very often avoid charges being made, or, if charges are on file, have them dismissed, and leave the parties to civil remedies. [177]

The fact that the policy does not provide for the defense of the assured by the company when the assured is on trial for road rule violation, leads some adjusters to the hasty conclusion that the company is not interested in the defense against the charge. That is generally true, but watch must be kept for the exceptional cases. If, in a major claim, the negligence of the assured is a matter of doubt, the claim can be settled at a low amount, so long as the doubt continues. If this doubt be converted into a belief that the claimant can possibly, or probably, establish negligence, the claimant will be likely to stand out for a settlement based on liability, or probable liability. 

The adjuster has a real interest in preventing, if possible, this doubt from being converted into a belief. He has a real interest in increasing the degree of doubt. If he permits the assured to be convicted of a violation, he allows the doubt to be converted into a belief. If conviction is not secured, the doubt is raised in degree and may be raised to such a degree that the claimant will feel that he has no basis for a suit, and he will accordingly be open to an amicable settlement.

If such a case as this arises, the adjuster should make certain that an able attorney represents the assured, [178]

and that all of the facts are within the grasp of this attorney, so that the charge against the assured may be dismissed, rather than that the charge be proven, and the claimant encouraged by the knowledge that he has a prima facie case of negligence against the assured.

Negligence is often concurrent; that is the negligence of two, or more, wrongdoers concurs to produce the accident which results in the damage to a passenger in one of the automobiles, or to some other innocent person. In such case, the person damaged has a right of action against all, or any one, of the wrongdoers, regardless of the extent to which the negligence of each wrongdoer contributed to the accident, and the resulting damage. So, one operator may have contributed only 1 per cent of the concurrent negligence, and yet be chargeable for the entire damage. [179]

N THE PRECEDING CHAPTER, mention was made that the common law and the statute law of two states might be, and at times are, different in the one state from that applicable in the other.

For example, the duty due to a guest of an operator involved in an accident in Virginia is to refrain from acts of gross negligence, whereas in the District of Columbia the duty is to refrain from acts of ordinary negligence. Very often an accident will occur in Virginia, and the resulting case be tried in the District of Columbia. The question arises then, what degree of negligence must be proven.

As the accident happened in Virginia, the cause forming the basis of the claim accrued there, and there is no basis for a claim, except as the basis lies in the laws of Virginia. And so the laws of Virginia must determine whether, or not, the claimant has a lawful claim. Virginia is called the locus, and the [180]

laws of the locus are spoken of technically as "lex locii." 

A claim arising from an automobile accident is ambulatory; that is, it can be carried from one place to another. Our Federal Constitution provides that each state shall give full faith and credit to the laws of other states. So, if the claimant can properly bring his lawsuit in another state, the state in which the suit is brought will try the case according to the law of the locus. The state where the trial is held is technically called the forum. These provisions of law result in the peculiar fact that a claim can be asserted in a court arising from a foreign accident, which could not be entertained if the accident happened within the state.

The effect of this situation is not now as important as formerly, because, under the financial responsibility bills and other laws of the various states, suits arising from automobile accidents in all states except 

Colorado, Georgia, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, Utah, and West Virginia, may be instituted in the state where the accident happened, and service had on the non-resident operator through some proper official of the state. The tendency of this provision is to have [181]

the suit arising from the accident, tried at the place where the accident occurred.

While each state must give full faith and credit to the laws of another state, there is no requirement that one state must follow the rules of evidence made by another state. In fact, one state will not accept rules of evidence made by another state, if in conflict with its own. So, as previously stated, those road rules which state that the existence of certain facts shall be prima facie evidence of negligence are of no effect outside the state which enacted them.

The Statute of Limitations of the state where the accident happened determines the life of a claim. 

For instance, the limitation on automobile accident claims in Virginia is one year, whereas in the District of Columbia it is three years. If suit is filed in the 

District of Columbia for damages arising from an accident which happened in Virginia, it must be brought within one year, for the reason that the right of action, accruing under the law of Virginia, dies at the end of one year. [182]

Chapter XXX CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE CONTRIBUTO ONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE 

is no different from direct negligence, previously covered. The special name is used only to distinguish the person charged with the negligence. When the negligence is charged against the operator by the claimant, the operator's negligence is spoken of simply as negligence, but when the operator charges that the claimant was also negligent, this negligence, charged against the claimant, is spoken of as "contributory negligence."

The claimant owes a duty to the operator to refrain from acts of negligence which may bring injury to himself. If the claimant fails in this duty, he is guilty of contributory negligence.

Under the common law, contributory negligence, regardless of degree, is a complete defense. This rule is generally applicable throughout the United States.

It appears best to discuss contributory negligence according to this general rule, and at the end of this chapter, to point out the exceptions to this rule. [183]

Contributory negligence is the defense most often used in automobile accident cases, and, as it is so effective, the adjuster must be constantly on the alert to discover whether convincing evidence is present on which to base this defense. 

Just as the negligence of the operator must be shown to be the producing, or proximate, cause of the accident, in order for claimant's claim to stand, so the negligence of the claimant, or his contributory negligence, must be shown to be a contributing cause to the accident, or a cause in the absence of which there would have been no accident. 

The operator, by asserting contributory negligence, sets up an affirmative defense. Just as the claimant has the duty to establish negligence by a fair preponderence of the evidence, so the operator has the burden of establishing contributory negligence.

There is, though, this important distinction in the nature of the proofs required:

The claimant must prove negligence by facts, as negligence is never presumed, and is never left to conjecture. The operator need not prove contributory negligence by direct facts. While contributory negligence cannot be presumed, yet it may be proven by deduction, and by circumstantial evidence. So, [184]

many times the proof of contributory negligence does not stand out as something to be clearly seen, but, instead, hovers like a cloud obscuring vision, and making the cause of the accident as asserted by the claimant seem doubtful and improbable. It is 

It is very much easier to satisfy a jury that a claimant was guilty of contributory negligence than to prove that the operator was negligent.

As the claimant has the duty of establishing his claim on the whole evidence by a fair preponderence of the evidence, it is clear that the stronger the circumstances point to the probability of contributory negligence, the weaker the probability of claimant's being able to prove that the accident was due solely to the negligence of the operator. A powerful doubt established in the minds of a few jurors will likely result in a failure to agree on a finding, or in a verdict for the operator.

In a few states, notably New York, there is a requirement that the claimant assert and prove freedom from negligence. The general rule, however, is that contributory negligence is a matter of defense to which the claimant need pay no attention until the operator raises it as a matter of defense. The distinction is a matter to which the adjuster need pay [185]

no attention, because, regardless of differences in the legal requirements of pleading and proof, the proposition is of universal application, except as noted below, that if the claimant was guilty of any negligence which contributed to the accident, he cannot recover damages.

In property damage claims, the amount of damage is definite, so, if a claim is denied, and this claim results in a suit, the only additional loss possible will be the costs of trial. One successful defense may save enough money to pay the expense of defending several suits. It must be remembered that the element of sympathy for a claimant enters but slightly into property damage claims. So, as to property damage claims, particularly where they arise from intersection collisions, where contributory negligence appears probable, the adjuster can deny liability without fear of great loss coming from the claim, due to a mistake in his judgment in deciding not to pay the claim.

The situation is, of course, different in regard to personal injury claims, because these do involve the element of sympathy, and because some of the elements of damages are intangible. [186]

There are several exceptions to the general rule regarding contributory negligence. 

If the negligence of the operator is wilful—that is, if the operator intentionally inflicted injury-contributory negligence is no defense.

Since the enactment of statutes in many states requiring that a guest must prove gross negligence in order to recover damages from the host, there have been many decisions on the effect of a defense of contributory negligence in cases where the negligence of the host has been reckless, or otherwise gross, and it is broadly held that the defense of contributory negligence will not bar recovery by the guest unless his negligence was also gross.

Some courts have expanded this exception to include constructive wilful negligence. By this expansion, it is not necessary to prove intent to inflict injury. All that is necessary is to prove those elements of gross negligence involving intent to violate a legal obligation, or a wanton abandonment of consideration of the consequences of the act.

These states have specifically ruled that contributory negligence is no defense against wilful and wanton negligence: Arizona, Georgia, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, [187]

Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, Utah, and Michigan.

These states have held the same way, but expressly exclude gross negligence not based on wilful and wanton negligence: Alabama, California, Florida, and Nebraska.

From the above lists of states, it will be observed that the exception, as extended to include constructive wilful and wanton negligence, has general application. 

If the contributory negligence is gross, then such contributory negligence can be set up as a defense. 

In Nebraska, the rule is that if claimant's negligence is slight, in comparison to gross negligence of the operator, contributory negligence is not a defense, but if claimant's negligence is more than slight, or that of the operator is not gross contributory negligence is a defense. 

Mississippi has a statute* providing for proportional damages in personal injury cases only. 

Wisconsin has a similar statute * applying to both personal injuries and property damage.

The idea of comparative negligence, as applied to automobile accident damages, is limited to these three * See page 225. [188]

states, and there seems to be no likelihood of its receiving any wide application.

Other states have statutes limiting the application of the defense of contributory negligence in cases of accidents to employees, and in railroad accidents, but these are outside the scope of this book. 

Another exception to the general rule is that if claimant places himself in a position of peril, in order to save another person from death, or injury, contributory negligence is not a defense, unless the act was rash, or reckless. 

Another exception is in cases involving last clear chance. This matter is discussed in Chapter XXXV. 

One more matter needs to be considered in regard to contributory negligence. There is no semblance of equality in opportunity for personal safety between a pedestrian and the operator of an automobile. 

The operator sits in safety behind the steering wheel. 

The pedestrian has no armor to protect him from the mass and power of an automobile. The courts are giving grave consideration to this inequality, not from the view that an automobile is a dangerous instrumentality, but from the view that the defense of the pedestrian against injury is inadequate to meet his needs. [189]

The mass and power of an automobile make it easy for the operator to rob the pedestrian of his rights, so the courts tend to throw a protecting arm over the pedestrian. This protection takes the form of a tendency to increase the degree of care due on the part of the operator, and to apply rigorously the obligation to obey road rules in favor of the pedestrian, and to construe failure on the part of the operator to exercise the higher degree of care and observation, as gross or wilful negligence against which contributory negligence is no defense. In other words, the courts are tending to extend the scope of gross and wilful negligence, and thereby limiting defenses. The adjuster must keep abreast of this trend. [190]

Chapter XXXI IMPUTED NEGLIGENCE IMPUTED MPUTED NEGLIGENCE IS 

strictly a matter of defense against a claimant who was riding in another automobile, and was not operating it. Imputed negligence can exist only in connection with a claim of contributory negligence on the part of the person operating the automobile in which the claimant was riding.

It is an assertion that, because of peculiar relations existing between the claimant and the operator of the automobile in which he was riding, that any contributory negligence of this operator is chargeable against, or imputed to the claimant, as effectively as though he were operating the automobile himself. 

In order for contributory negligence to be imputable to the claimant, one of three conditions must exist: (a) Claimant must have authorized the conduct resulting in the accident; or [191]

(b) He must have participated in it; or (c) He must have had a right, or power, to control the conduct of the person operating the automobile. 

One such case is that of joint enterprise. To make a case of joint enterprise, the claimant and the operator of his automobile must have a joint interest in the object of the trip, and equal right in directing the automobile on the trip.

If an agent is operating the automobile, his negligence is imputable to the owner, if the owner is riding with him; but if the owner is operating, his negligence is not imputable to the agent riding with him. 

Keep in mind that imputed negligence can arise only where there is an assertion of contributory negligence. You, in handling the claim against the assured, charge that there was contributory negligence, and then carry this negligence one step further by saying that this contributory negligence, on account of the relations between the parties, becomes the contributory negligence of the claimant by imputation, and, so, he is not entitled to damages.

A peculiar case of imputed negligence, which may be overlooked, is that a parent is chargeable with the negligence and wrongful acts of the person to whom [192]

he entrusts the custody and care of his minor child, and the parent cannot recover damages if the custodian was negligent in permitting the child to be in a position of danger. This defense does not bar recovery on behalf of the child for damages to the child, but does bar an action by the parent.

In those states which recognize the property of husband and wife as community property, if the injury to the husband or wife is contributed to by the other mate, this contributory negligence is attributable to the injured mate. [193]

Note that the automobile must be a family automobile. If the head of the family has the automobile for his own use, the lending of it on some occasion to a member of the family does not bring the use under the doctrine.

This doctrine applies in most of the states, but some states have rejected it, and insist that before the head of the family can be held liable, there must be proof that the operator was actually the agent of the family head, and operating within the scope of his authority as an agent.

Efforts have been made, and will be made, to have this doctrine applied in a breadth entirely unwarranted. It is to be strictly applied, and cannot be extended to permit the member of the family for whose operation the head of the family is responsible to, in turn, permit operation of the automobile by another person and charge the owner with liability under the doctrine. [195]

Chapter XXXIII ASSUMPTION OF RISK CLOSELY LOSELY AKIN TO CONTRIBUTORY 

negligence, is another defense called "assumption of risk." This means simply that if a person knowingly places, or remains, in circumstances which a reasonably prudent person would realize to be dangerous, he assumes any risk involved. You will note that this is being negligent as to the safety of one's person, and is a violation of the duty everyone owes himself to preserve his own safety and life. If a person cares so little about himself as unnecessarily to run risks, there seems to be no sound reason why the law should allow him reimbursement for injuries which he could have prevented by exercising respect for his own life and limb. 

A few examples will make this matter clear. If a claimant rides with an intoxicated operator, he assumes the risk of acts which the operator does on account of drink. Likewise, if a guest has reason to believe that the operator is sleepy, he has a duty to [196]

stay awake, and help keep a lookout. If he goes to sleep, he cannot blame the operator for doing the same. So, if the operator is driving at such an excessive rate of speed as to be reckless, the guest assumes the risk of accident. 

If the guest does not know of dangerous circumstances upon entering the automobile, but later discovers them, he must make some efforts to have the automobile properly operated, and if his efforts are of no avail, he must ask to be allowed to leave the automobile. 

In all guest cases, the circumstances should be probed to determine whether the negligence of the operator, or his inability to operate properly, were within the knowledge of the guest, and assumed by him. This defense has an added attraction when it is considered that juries are not friendly to guest hazard cases. 

If this principle of assumption of risk is made clear to the claimant, he may see that he was at fault in being in the automobile, and, as he will realize that this fault is much to his disadvantage, he may be glad to effect a settlement on a minimum basis. [197]

Chapter XXXIV INTERVENING EFFICIENT CAUSE THE ADJUSTER WILL SOMETIMES 

come across instances in which damage was done outside of the immediate accident. As was pointed out, if an act of negligence sets in motion a sequence of events, the damage from all of these events is chargeable to the initial act of negligence. There comes a time, however, when this chain of events comes to a dead stop. At this point, liability to be charged to the initial event, ceases. There may be, however, additional damages before the effects, or results, of the accident are over. The cause of such subsequent damage is spoken of as an "intervening efficient cause." Such a claim must be denied.

The usual type of such damage is that caused by moving the vehicles from the position in which they came to rest after the accident. If the claimant, through nervousness produced by the accident, undertakes to move his vehicle, and does any damage, this damage is due to his negligence in operating an [198]

automobile when not in proper condition to do so with safety. Such an accident cannot be charged to the initial act of negligence. 

You will note that the intervening efficient cause, and the previously discussed proximate cause essentially the same. The distinction is that the claimant asserts that a certain proximate cause is the basis for liability. You assert that the effect of that cause had ceased, and that another cause intervened, and that this cause was the proximate cause of the accident. [199]

Chapter XXXV LAST CLEAR CHANCE 

HE DOCTRINE OF "LAST 

clear chance" is known, also, as "the humanitarian rule" and as "the rule of discovered peril." It is the most difficult to grasp, and to apply, of all matters involved in the law of negligence. Because the claims which arise under it are generally large, the adjuster must have a clear working knowledge of the doctrine. 

The doctrine dates from 1843, and arose in a case where an owner negligently left an ass tethered on a highway, and the ass was struck by a passing cart.

According to the general rule that contributory negligence bars recovery, the owner of the ass would not be entitled to damages. It was obvious, the application of the general rule to these facts would not be just. The driver of the cart could have, and should have, driven in such a way as to avoid injury to the ass. He had the last clear chance to avoid the injury.

So the court made this case an exception to the general rule, and started the doctrine of "last clear [200]

chance," which has since been amplified to cover situations of a similar nature.

There are several conditions which must exist before the doctrine can apply. These are: 

1. The claimant must be in a position of actual peril, or about to come into such position; 

2. The claimant must be in such position because of his negligence; 

3. The operator must see the claimant in this position; or 

4. The operator must have a duty to see claimant in this position; 

5. The operator must have a chance, with the facilities at hand, to save the claimant from injury after he sees, or should see, the claimant in this position.

Any acts of negligence by the operator prior to the time that he discovers, or should discover, the claimant in a position of peril, have no bearing on this doctrine. If the operator is unable to avoid the injury, after he sees, or should see, the claimant in a position of peril, due to the operator's antecedent negligence, such as excessive speed, or faulty brakes, recovery of damages may be had on account of the [201]

antecedent negligence being the primary and proximate cause of the injury.

It will facilitate understanding of the application of this doctrine, if the conduct of the parties be divided under four heads and discussed by groups. 

The operator may be in such position that-- (a) He saw the claimant in a position of peril, or (b) He had a duty to see the claimant; he could, with the exercise of ordinary care, have seen the claimant; and, therefore, should have seen him in, or about to be in, a position of peril. 

The claimant may be in such position that, while the operator is in such circumstances that he has a chance to avoid injury, he (the claimant) is (c) Unable to escape from his position; (d) Able to escape from his position. 

If these four divisions are kept in mind, and each accident is classified according to the appropriate combination of letters, it will be easier to apply the doctrine to the particular accident at hand. 

If the combination (ac) exists, it is universally recognized that the operator is liable. 

Liability under this combination may well exist, regardless of the doctrine of "last clear chance." If claimant's position of peril was discovered in time to [202]

avoid the injury, and the operator was negligent subsequently in any way in producing the injury, he would be liable on the ground of wilful and wanton negligence to which contributory negligence is not a defense. He might, also, be liable on the ground that his negligence was the proximate producing cause of the injury.

In applying this doctrine, the negligence, if any, of the operator is to be determined by his acts upon, and after, discovering the peril of the claimant, and these acts must be considered in relation to the surrounding circumstances existing at the time. These acts must be the proximate cause of the injury. 

The obligation attaches to the operator as to persons, or property, in the danger zone, or, to his knowledge, about to enter the danger zone. It is immaterial whether the claimant's negligence is continuing, or has ceased, so long as his negligence has placed him, or will place him, in a position of peril from which he is unable to extricate himself. 

It is sufficient if this inability to escape was due to claimant's obliviousness to his peril.

If the combination (ad) exists, the operator is aware of the presence of the claimant, and the claimant is able to extricate himself, but does not do so. [203]

He is in a position where, by the exercise of due care, he could have, and should have, discovered his peril and escaped. The question, then, is whether his neglect to discover his peril will bar recovery. His neglect may be due to obliviousness of his danger, failure to realize his peril after seeing the operator's automobile, faith that the operator will not strike him, or simply neglect to act.

There is some distinction drawn, in considering a case under this combination, between active and passive negligence. Passive negligence is a mere continuance of the original negligence, whereas active negligence is an act of negligence different in kind from the original negligence.

The general rule is that if the negligence of the claimant is continuous and concurrent with the negligence of the operator, the doctrine does not apply, and there can be no recovery. It is not safe to apply this rule to accidents which occur under this combination, where the negligence of the claimant is passive, nor where claimant's negligence is active, until the circumstances of the accident have been thoroughly studied, and definitely provable facts are available showing active negligence by the claimant. 

It is not important whether the negligence of the 

[204]

claimant has terminated because the operator sees what the claimant is doing, and should operate accordingly. 

Obliviousness to danger is passive negligence, and the rule is that this obliviousness is equivalent to inability to escape, and stands in the place of actual inability to escape. Specific assent has been given to this application in Alabama, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Delaware, District of Columbia, Florida, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New York, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Rhode 

Island, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, Washington, and 

West Virginia.

The claimant, upon becoming aware of the presence of the operator, is obligated to act as a reasonably prudent man would act under the circumstances then existing. The ability to escape must exist prior to, or down to, a time after which it is impossible for the operator to avoid the injury. Whether, or not, the claimant acted as a reasonably prudent man would under the circumstances, is a question of fact. 

This question of fact can be expected to be decided against the operator, unless definite and convincing [205]

proof is offered that the claimant's acts were not those of an ordinary man.

If, from the proof submitted by the claimant and by the operator, it appears that the claimant was guilty of continuing negligence after he actually discovered his peril, and should have realized his peril, this doctrine does not apply, and claimant is not entitled to recover.

A distinction must be considered between the collision of two automobiles, and the collision of an automobile with a pedestrian. If two automobiles are involved, conditions are favorable to strict application of the law, and a denial of the application of this doctrine. If an automobile and a pedestrian be involved, the conclusion reached by people in general, and so by juries, is that the operator observing all of the acts of the claimant should have realized that the claimant was not acting to avoid injury, should have realized that claimant was unaware of his peril, should have realized that claimant was depending on the care and skill of the operator. The operator has a horn to sound, and a flexible vehicle to maneuver. Under all such circumstances, the conclusion is generally drawn that the operator could have, and so should have, avoided the injury. [206]

If the claimant fails to escape, due to fright, or to fainting, the situation becomes one ruled by the combination (ac). 

If the acts of the operator are wilful and wanton, there is no need to apply this doctrine, as contributory negligence is not a defense. Likewise, if the acts of the claimant are wilful and wanton, the doctrine does not apply.

If the combination (bc) exists, we find some conflict between the courts of various states as to whether the doctrine will apply.

In the states of Alabama, Arkansas, California, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, New York, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Dakota, Tennessee, and Washington, the courts in unqualified manner state that the doctrine does not apply unless the claimant's peril was discovered; that is, the breach of duty to discover the claimant is not sufficient. 

It must be noted that the cases, which establish the law of "discovered peril," are cases of railroad accidents. The danger zone in railroad accidents is limited, harm can ordinarily come but from only two directions, the location of the danger zone is marked by warning signs, a train makes considerable noise in its approach, the duty to keep a lookout is limited. [207]

By contrast, the danger zone of an automobile may be the entire roadway, and from any direction; the approach is not necessarily accompanied by noise. 

On account of these differences, one must exercise great care in accepting a railroad case as a precedent for an automobile case.

Statutes and ordinances have placed responsibility, in addition to common law responsibility, on the operator, as to his duty to keep a lookout, and have his automobile under emergency control.

The established law in many states is that if the operator has a duty to discover the claimant, the breach of this duty is sufficient ground to apply this doctrine where the negligence of the claimant has ceased, or culminated in placing him in a position of peril from which position he is unable to escape, and this negligence is, therefore, not continuing and concurring, while it was possible for the operator to have avoided the injury if he had performed his duty to discover the claimant. Alabama, Connecticut, District of Columbia, Florida, Georgia, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Maine, Maryland, Missouri, Montana, North Carolina, Ohio, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, and Washington.

In some states, the courts have conceived the idea [208]

of constructive knowledge, which means that the circumstances are such that the operator must have seen the claimant, and become obligated accordingly. 

Under this view, responsibility attaches as in combination (ac). 

There must, in all of these cases, be something by way of statute, or circumstances, to put the operator on notice of the necessity for taking precautions to see the claimant. There must be a duty to discover the claimant.

Obliviousness to danger by the claimant is here not accepted as equivalent to inability to escape. 

Instances will arise in which this doctrine will not be applied, because if the operator had performed his duty, and had discovered the claimant, he would not have had reason to conclude that claimant would not enter the danger zone or would move out of it. 

The difference in application of law in various states as to this combination may be attributed to different views as to the duty of the operator and the duty of the claimant. If the claimant be a pedestrian, he will receive every favorable consideration possible, and some line of reasoning will be found to arrive at a conclusion favorable to him. 

No great reliance can be placed on the courts to [209]

apply the doctrine of "discovered peril" to automobile accidents involving injury to pedestrians, but, rather, one must expect every opportunity to be taken by the courts to evade applying this strict rule. 

It is believed that the doctrine of "last clear chance" will be applied by the courts in substantially this way, regardless of what reasoning may be used to reach this end; and the adjuster may be guided accordingly:

If the negligence of the claimant had terminated, leaving him in a position from which he was, in the exercise of due care, unable to extricate himself, and at the time his negligence ceased, the operator, had he performed his duty and had discovered the claimant's peril, would have been able to avert the injury, then this doctrine will apply; but

If the negligence of the claimant had not terminated, but was continuous, and concurring with the negligence of the operator, that is, claimant's ability, but for his own negligence and inattention, to escape, continued to a time when it would have been impossible for the operator to avoid injury, this doctrine will not apply.

If the combination (bd) exists, the general rule is that the doctrine does not apply. It has been so ruled [210]

in California, Florida, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Louisiant, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, New York, North 

Carolina, Ohio, Oregon, Tennessee, Utah, Vermont,

Washington, and West Virginia. The contrary has been ruled in Connecticut and Missouri under what is called the "humanitarian rule.”

The cases which have established the law in those states first named above, involve, with the exception of Kansas, railroad accidents.

In these cases, there is continuing and concurring negligence by the claimant and the operator, and, in most of them, the negligence of the claimant has been inattention to his danger.

This combination assumes a violation of duty on the part of both claimant and operator. The duty of the operator may be made so great, by future statutes, towards pedestrians, under certain circumstances, that failure to see by the operator will be barred as the basis of a defense, thus throwing such accidents under the combination (ac). The adjuster must be watchful for such changes.

In the explanation above, note was made that courts of certain states had established certain law. 

It is well to know how this comes about. Only the trial court hears testimony. In minor suits, the judge [211]

acts as judge and jury. In other suits, there is both judge and jury. If the suit involves more than twenty dollars, the parties have a constitutional right to a trial by jury. This right may be waived.

The party who loses in the trial court, may desire to appeal to an appellate court. The ruling of the highest court of the state, or the court of last resort, becomes the established law of the state. 

Appellate courts do not review the whole trial.

The appealing party, called the appellant, must set forth the errors which he claims were committed during the trial, and the appellate court will rule on these claimed errors. These errors will be, in the main, errors of law committed by the trial judge in admitting, or excluding, certain evidence, errors by the judge in wrongly instructing the jury on the applicable law, or error, by the judge in denying a motion for a directed verdict for the defendant (operator) on the evidence. Such a motion, in effect, is an assertion by defendant that, giving to the evidence that aspect most favorable to the plaintiff (claimant), no legal basis exists to support a verdict for the plaintiff.

The finding of the jury as to the facts, cannot be attacked on appeal, unless it be shown that the jury [212]

acted from passion and prejudice. If the jury acted with reasonable fairness, and there was evidence to support its verdict, the verdict will not be set aside unless, as stated above, the trial judge committed some specific error which materially affected the verdict. 

When the appellate court writes its opinion, it may neglect to pass on the precise errors at issue or may decide the appeal from an entirely new angle. The decision which the court reaches is applicable only to the facts as they appear in the specific case before it, and the court is not bound, in future appeals, by statements of law not applicable to the facts of the particular case under consideration.

In applying decisions forming a part of the law of a state, the adjuster is likely to assume that because the facts in his case, as he finds them, appear to be the same as the facts in some case decided in the highest court, the conclusion reached in that case must apply to the case which he has at hand. Such need not, and may not, be the case, because on exactly the same evidence, the jury, due to credibility of witnesses, etc., may properly reach a finding of fact contrary to that found in the decided case.

You noted in reading the above explanation of the doctrine of "last clear chance," that it is a much [213]

involved matter, and, perhaps, in order to grasp it, you had to reread parts of it more than once. 

Is it reasonable to suppose that a jury, after hearing the trial judge state the law, as applicable to the various possible findings of fact, will be able to grasp intelligently and remember the distinctions, and apply the law accurately to the facts as found? 

Is it not more reasonable to suppose that a jury will decide the case before it in a practical way, in disregard of such fine technical distinctions as exist in the application of the doctrine of "last clear chance"?

Is it not probable that a jury will proceed in some such way as this?— (a) In accidents involving the collision of two, or more, automobiles, to disregard the doctrine of "last clear chance," and determine the proximate cause of the accident, and reach a verdict according to the general law of negligence and contributory negligence. (b) In pedestrian injury cases, to determine the duties of the pedestrian and the operator in relation to the time and place of the accident, that is, to consider duty on open roads, at intersecting rural roads, and whether this intersection is controlled by warning signs, in cities between intersections, at uncontrolled cross walks, at controlled cross walks, near [214]

schoolhouses, in congested areas, in daylight, at nighttime, in fair weather, etc. (c) To consider the testimony in regard to performance, or failure, of the operator, to perform his duty to exercise due care. (d) To disbelieve a denial by the operator that he saw the claimant, if under the circumstances it is probable that he did see the claimant. (e) To weigh the general appearance and demeanor of the operator, and judge whether he is a person who probably would be careful, or careless, or a person who would disregard the rights of a pedestrian. (f) To disbelieve a denial by the claimant that he saw the operator's automobile, if, under the circumstances, it is probable that he did see it. (g) To weigh the general appearance and demeanor of the claimant, and judge whether he is a person who probably would be careful, or careless, and whether he would take chances, or would selfishly thrust an undue burden on the operator to avoid injury. If the claimant is a woman, ordinarily allow a presumption that she did not heedlessly, or selfishly, rush into danger. (h) To determine whether the operator did have [215]

a chance to avoid injury, and whether the claimant should have acted to avoid injury. (i) To consider the opportunity for, and probable effect of, omitted warning signals. (j) To work the details down to a basis of proximate cause, and decide the case on the basis of nonperformance of duty by either party, rather than on some technical distinction of the doctrine of "last clear chance." (k) If the blame lies squarely on the operator, to find ample damage to cover the intangible elements of damage. If the blame probably lies on the operator, and emotional elements are absent, to assess actual damages and make a reasonable allowance for intangible elements. If blame lies on the claimant, to give a verdict for the operator.

It will be noted that in the above manner of reaching a verdict, the doctrine of "last clear chance" may not be the reason for the verdict, but may be a justification for it.

If an adjuster will gather all of the facts, he can reason on the facts in the same manner as it is suggested that a jury would, and he should be able to reach a sound decision on any claim which arises under the doctrine of "last clear chance." [216]

Chapter XXXVI RES IPSA LOQUITUR 

N ORDER THAT A CLAIMANT 

be entitled to recover damages arising from an automobile accident, negligence must be proven on the part of the person charged. It sometimes happens that all of the occupants of the automobile, including the operator, are killed under such circumstances that there is no living witness to the cause of the accident, and no way of proving the cause to have been an act of negligence by the operator.

The representatives of the occupants cannot recover damages, because there is no proof of negligence. 

Negligence is never presumed.

There are, however, accidents which occur under such circumstances that evidence of the probable cause exists, but this evidence is not available to the claimant; so, if the possessor of the evidence is permitted to keep quiet, the claimant cannot recover damages. To permit evidence to be withheld, and so defeat the claimant, would be inequitable, and so, the courts developed the rule of Res Ipsa Loquitur. [217]

These Latin words mean that the thing speaks for itself. An adjuster needs to know what in the legal sense is meant by this expression, and in what cases it applies.

In a legal sense, Res Ipsa Loquitur is a rule of evidence. It is not evidence, but stands in the place of evidence.

This rule received application, according to the bare meaning of the words, in railroad accident suits brought by passengers against railroads for damages resulting from accidents. It is obvious that a railroad passenger is in a weak position to secure evidence of negligence, and is often totally unable to secure any evidence of negligence on the part of the railroad; yet the accident is of such nature as not to be expected in the ordinary course of train operation, and, if knowledge of the cause is available, it is available to the railroad, rather than to the passenger.

This rule, Res Ipsa Loquitur, may be stated as: "Where the thing involved in the accident is shown to be under the management of the defendant, or his agents, and the accident is such as in the ordinary course of things does not happen if those who have the management use proper care, the happening of the accident affords reasonable evidence, in the ab- [218]

sence of explanation by the defendant, that the accident arose from want of care.”

It will be noted that this rule does not arise from the abstract fact of an accident to a passenger, but arises from a consideration of the nature and quality of the accident. It is a rule of necessity. Note also that the rule is grounded upon the fact that the evidence of the cause of the accident, whether culpable, or innocent, is accessible to the defendant, but inaccessible to the plaintiff.

The facts of the accident warrant an inference of negligence; they do not compel it. Decision of the question whether the rule applies in a given case, is made by the judge. He considers all of the facts surrounding the particular case, and takes judicial notice, as a matter of common knowledge and experience, whether, or not, the accident could have occurred, except for negligence on the part of the defendant. If he holds that the rule applies to the plaintiff's case, then the defendant must explain as best he can why the accident happened. The jury then decides whether the explanation is sufficient to overcome the inference of negligence.

It is obvious that in many railroad accident cases, the passenger knows, or has available, no facts regard- [219]

ing the cause of the accident, and must rest his case on the bare happening of the accident. 

In automobile accident cases, such a situation, even in an accident involving a carrier, would be extraordinary. In automobile accident cases, the cause of the accident is seldom peculiarly within the knowledge of the defendant and his agents, and inaccessible to the plaintiff. The plaintiff, if awake, knows considerable about the circumstances surrounding the accident. The plaintiff must produce all the available evidence surrounding the accident, so as to show a necessity for the application of the rule, and, as there are a great many possible causes for an automobile accident, other than negligence by the operator, it must be shown that, in view of all of the circumstances, the accident would not have happened except for negligence on the part of the defendant.

Restricted in this way, the rule cannot apply in automobile accident cases on the mere showing of the happening of an accident. In fact, the rule can be applied to relatively few automobile accidents.

If the plaintiff offers any evidence of acts of negligence by the defendant, this rule cannot have application, as the rule applies only when there is no evidence of negligence available to the claimant. [220]

In accidents involving the collision of two automobiles, if the plaintiff can show absence of negligence by the operator of the automobile in which he was riding, and shows that the only other automobile, or moving vehicle, in the vicinity was the one which collided with the rear of the automobile in which he was riding, then the rule will apply against the operator of the automobile in the rear. In other two-car collision cases, no possibility appears for application of the rule by an occupant of either automobile as facts tending to prove, or disprove, negligence of the defendant, are available.

The rule cannot apply where there are two, or more, possible wrongdoers.

Except where a pedestrian is hit from the rear, it is impossible to think of a pedestrian case where the rule would apply.

The rule is readily applicable when a parked automobile is struck by a moving automobile, and where an unoccupied automobile rolls down a hill. 

The degree of care which the defendant owes to the plaintiff has a distinct bearing on the force of this rule. As the rule requires the defendant to come forward with evidence to overcome the presumption of want of due care, it is obvious that the higher the [221]

degree of care owed to the plaintiff by the defendant, the harder it is for the defendant to show performance of his duty. So, public carriers who owe passengers the highest degree of care have difficulty in overcoming the presumption.

This rule does not apply where the claimant must prove gross, or wilful negligence.

This rule of Res Ipsa Loquitur applies, as to automobile accident cases, in all states. 

Many of the states have recognized that persons damaged as a result of automobile accidents are often unable to secure application of the rule of Res Ipsa Loquitur, and so are unable to effect recovery of damages. To aid the person damaged, these states have included in their road rules, provisions that the doing of certain acts, such as colliding with the rear of an automobile, shall be prima facie evidence of negligence. Such road rules have the same effect as the application of the rule of Res Ipsa Loquitur, in that after the damaged person shows the happening of the event prohibited by the road rule, the operator must satisfactorily explain the happenings of the event.

Such road rules must be literally applied. If a rule specifies that the act shall be prima facie evidence of [222]

negligence, this negligence is limited to ordinary negligence. A road rule cannot be used as prima facie evidence of gross or wanton negligence unless the rule so specifies.

Claimants and their lawyers have a tendency to assert that this rule means that the happening of the accident establishes a prima facie case of negligence, and warrants recovery of damages. An adjuster who possesses an understanding of this rule and its application can disregard such an assertion, unless the surrounding facts indicate that the rule correctly applies to the accident, or a road rule acts as a substitute for it.

If the rule would apply in court, then the adjuster should give it application in his daily work.
