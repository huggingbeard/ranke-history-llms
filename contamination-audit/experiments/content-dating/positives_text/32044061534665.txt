INTRODUCTION 

This report is a summary of the tentative findings of several months of investigation concerning the nature and magnitude of the impediments to American private foreign investment and of possible methods and means of removing or decreasing those impediments. This investigation was undertaken by the Department of Commerce, in consultation with other U. S. Government agencies, the International 

Bank for Reconstruction and Development, and with private business groups, pursuant to section 516 (c) of the Mutual Security Act of 

1951, as amended. The study has been made in three parts:

(1) Factors in Foreign Countries. This is a survey of impediments in twenty-six selected countries, together with summaries of impediments in the four principal areas of the free world. The countries were selected as representing the following categories: Countries in which significant opportunities exist but to which American capital is not moving in appreciable quantities; countries most typical of various stages of economic development; countries in which accumulated U. S. direct capital investment is greatest; countries to which the rate of flow of private U. S. investment in the postwar period has been greatest; and countries which have received the greatest amount of U. S. economic aid.

While the form and treatment vary among the country studies, in each country analysis consideration has been given to the more significant basic factors peculiar to that country. Examination is made of the effects on foreign investments of political, social, and cultural factors, the nature of the economy, financial resources and facilities, the role of the government, and government regulations, In view of the nature of the study directed by section 516 (c), the emphasis in this report is necessarily upon impediments rather than upon the positive factors which attract foreign investments. Furthermore, since the investigation was directed toward impediments in foreign countries, it does not include a full examination of deterring factors in the United States and the strong counterattraction of investment within the United States which also limit the flow of U. S. capital abroad. 

The study, as a whole, and in particular the section on specific foreign countries may, therefore, appear to present a more negative picture than if full consideration were given to all factors affecting foreign investments. An attempt has been made, however, to evaluate impediments in the light of the underlying factors and the general climate for foreign investments in each selected country.

-2- 

(2) Survey of Business Opinion. The survey of business opinion on impediments to private foreign investment was conducted through personal interviews by Department of Commerce personnel (mostly through Field Offices) with representatives of well over 300 U. S. firms and leading business groups. 

The purpose of the survey was to ascertain the actual experience, opinions, and recommendations (a) of business firms that have invested abroad; (b) of firms that have tried but have not succeeded in making foreign investments; (c) of those firms which, though qualified by virtue of capital resources and technical know-how, have shown no interest in foreign investment; and (d) of a number of banks, investment houses, and trade organizations. The final report will include business comments and opinions on the following subjects: Company policies affecting foreign investment; reasons why American firms invest abroad; influence of various impediments on the activities and actions of U. S. firms abroad; safeguards or conditions abroad that are desired by U. S. firms; U. S. Government actions as they affect foreign investment; recommendations as to means of removing or reducing impediments; and views on prospects for increased investment. (3) General Analysis of Basic Factors. This analysis is in terms of the basic factors which are the underlying causes of impediments and will afford a comparative review of conditions abroad. It presents a general background of the history and characteristics of U. S. private foreign investments and the relationship between trade and investments, followed by discussion of the basic economic factors abroad; political, social, and cultural factors; policy and attitude of foreign governments; and specific regulations of foreign governments affecting private foreign investment.

It has been necessary because of the complexity of the subject and the great number of factors affecting foreign investment to omit in this study a number of subjects which need further treatment. 

The many factors in the U. S. economy and our laws and regulations which affect foreign investment are mentioned only briefly -- some of these have already been thoroughly treated in other studies and others are the subject of current investigation.

The discussion is mainly in terms of direct investment, which in this study is defined as investments in foreign enterprises in which American investors or parent companies have a controlling interest, consisting of an ownership of 25 percent or more of the voting stock of foreign subsidiary companies, or unincorporated foreign branches. 

Only brief consideration has been given to portfolio investment, which is defined as investment in foreign bonds or stocks other than those of direct investment companies, plus long-term bank loans, notes, real estate, and the like. Most portfolio investment is of a creditor, or fixed interest, nature rather than equity investment. It includes,

however, minority equity investment where the total U. S. investment in an enterprise represents less than 25 percent of the total voting stock.

The whole study has concentrated on factors affecting American private investments, although many of these factors also affect the foreign investment of other countries and local private investment. 

It has not attempted to evaluate the role of private investments, both local and foreign, or the achievements of U. S. programs for the purpose of increasing the participation of private enterprise in foreign economic development.

It must be borne in mind that this is an analysis of general situations and that all the factors discussed do not affect all types of investments or do not affect them in the same way. Thus while local market limitations may deter manufacturing enterprises, they are of little importance for extractive industries; and, in general, the relative importance of various factors varies considerably, depending on whether the investment is direct or in combination with local capital. It is also recognized that, even in those countries where the overall impediments are mumerous and formidable, there may be some investment opportunities which, by reason of special factors present in the particular case, prove highly attractive to particular American investors. Moreover, experience shows that a realistic appreciation of the advantages of foreign investments to both the host country and the foreign investor has led to satisfactory working arrangements which have mitigated the severity of formal restrictions. 

Obviously a study of foreign investment problems must be a continuous one evaluating rapidly changing situations. Nevertheless, this three-part study provides a background of knowledge and understanding from which recommendations for action can be drawn. Although the study is still in progress and will not be completed for some time, its main outlines are now clear enough to permit both a broad summary of its findings and a preliminary listing of the principal recommendations for action. It must be pointed out, however, that the summary of findings appearing on pages. 5 to 22 of this report is of necessity a very brief presentation, which does not bring out sufficiently individual problems either by country or by types of impediments. 

These will appear in sharper focus in the full study. The first part of the study containing the country surveys has been completed and is now available.

This report may disappoint those who are looking to a substantial increase in private foreign investment as an immediate solution to the dollar problem or as a means of releasing in the next few years the

1425 creative energies of private enterprise toward the building of modern economies in the underdeveloped countries. On the other hand, the report shows that positive action can be taken both here and abroad for the immediate removal of certain impediments and for a long-range attack on the difficulties which confront private foreign investments. 

It points out the possibilities of taking practical measures to bring about a closer relationship between business and government in the locating of investment opportunities and the solution of individual investment problems, and of negotiating with other governments for the creation of an improved investment climate acceptable both to U. S. business and foreign countries. 

Other U. S. Government agencies and the International Bank for 

Reconstruction and Development have been very helpful in furnishing information and in making comments and suggestions on various parts of the study. Individual acknowledgments will not be made, but appreciation should be expressed for the contributions of the Office of Business Economics (Department of Commerce), the Department of State, the Mutual Security Agency, the Technical Cooperation Administration, the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, the Department of Labor, the Export-Import Bank, the Federal Reserve Board, the 

Treasury Department, and the Securities and Exchange Commission.

Members of the business community have shown great interest and have given their time generously in contributing to the business survey.

THE FINDINGS IN BRIEF 1. Survey of Impediments by Area 

The study shows that the principal obstacles to private foreign investment are: (1) Imbalance and dislocation in trade and currency relationships, leading to controls over the amounts of and purposes for which capital may be invested and the rate at which earnings and capital may be repatriated; (2) economic nationalism, accompanied by unfavorable reactions on the part of the public and domestic business and by an increasing number and stringency of controls by governments over the entry and the conduct of foreign investments; (3) insecure and unstable conditions which cause uncertainties and lack of confidence on the part of investors; (4) low levels of economic development in many countries limiting opportunities for private investments.

The study takes into account previous investigations and confirms some of their findings. It goes beyond most other investigations, however, by attempting to point out basic conditions which limit foreign investment in different countries and which affect different types of investment. In this section, summaries of the findings are presented by area.1/ a.

American Republics. At the end of 1950, about 40 percent of all U. S. direct investments abroad were in Latin America. Latin 

America has attracted more private direct investments from the United States than has any other part of the world. The volume of future U. S. private investment in this area depends chiefly upon the success with which the desires of the countries to develop more self-sufficient, diversified economies and to broaden national participation can be reconciled with the legitimate expectations of U. S. investors.

Currency and exchange difficulties are important deterrents in about half the Latin American countries, but because of the relative continuity of the demand for Latin American raw materials and foodstuffs in the United States this problem is less difficult than in 1/ Canada has not been covered in this study because of the unique investment relationship between Canada and the United States.

most other areas. The instability of internal political regimes is a serious deterrent to investment in a few countries, but it is a reality with which U. S. investors have long been familiar and to which they have learned to adjust. 

Latin America has long been accustomed to a considerable measure of government intervention in economic affairs, although before 1950 there was comparative freedom of movement of peoples, goods, and capital. During the depression years of the 1930's, various measures originally introduced in many Latin American countries on an emergency basis were gradually merged into more self-conscious national policies. The governments sought greater local control of the economies, either by the extension of the activities of official agencies or by increased local ownership and management of enterprises, at times in substitution for foreign-owned enterprises. 

In some countries there were also widespread defaults on foreignheld bond issues.

The coming of World War II and the continuing international tension after the war reinforced these tendencies. In some countries some existing foreign interests were purchased or expropriated and in other cases were subjected to discriminatory treatment in the application of labor and tax laws, as well as in the administration of exchange control and other measures. In some countries certain fields of activity have been reserved exclusively to nationals. There has been wide variation in the manner in which these restrictions have been imposed, and in some countries and in some fields of activity the treatment of foreign investors has been favorable. In view of these divergencies, it is difficult to generalize both as regards the problems and as regards their solution. The desire for hastening economic development is strong in Latin America and since there is general recognition of the important contribution to be made by foreign capital and techniques, there is a basis for action which can bring mutual benefit to the investors and to the capital-receiving countries. b. Western Europe. At the end of 1950, U. S. private direct investments in Western Europe (including the United Kingdom) accounted for about 15 percent of total American private direct investments abroad. More than half of these investments were in manufacturing enterprises. Of the total direct investment made during the years 

1946 through 1951 in this area, amounting to 851 million, about two-thirds represent retained profits of subsidiaries. 

As long as the world political situation remains precarious the volume of new American investment in Europe will be limited

unless risks can be offset by commensurately high yields. Prospective yields for new American investments in Europe at the present time do not seem generally attractive, particularly in relation to the risks involved, either for direct or portfolio investments.

The range of opportunities for American investment in this area will remain narrow as long as impediments resulting from balance-of-payment difficulties as well as those imposed by government controls and administrative policies, such as restrictive investment screening, persist. However, in some countries tne balance-of-payment situation may materially ease and an improved attitude of various governments and peoples toward foreign investment may reduce or eliminate some of the major obstacles. 

Even in the interim, while foreign exchange difficulties keep out many American products, an increasing number of American firms may be interested in establishing assembly or manufacturing plants to protect or serve markets in the sterling and European currency areas at present limited by dollar import restrictions. 

In some countries the range of investment opportunities is narrowed by the fact that whole industries or large segments of industries are nationalized or cartelized. This, however, does not materially affect investment opportunities in other industries within the same countries. 

American investors may see potentialities for profitable operation of new ventures in Europe through the introduction of efficient production and distribution techniques which would lower costs and prices but they may be precluded from entry in some instances by government controls and planning policies or by the opposition of established business interests. In many 

European countries business practice tends to favor high profits per unit rather than the creation of mass markets by competitive pricing. There is a discernible resistance in some countries, on the part of both government and business, to the establishment of new enterprises who se competition with the established business concerns might result in industrial and labor dislocation. 

An increase in tne dollar earnings of European countries, which would be stimulated by a rise in U. S. imports from that area, would permit a relaxation of restrictions on capital movements. Some European countries are already taking steps to promote a greater flow of U. S. capital. 

It is difficult to assess investment prospects for Western Europe as a whole. In many ways the area is a promising field for investment because of its position and experience in world trade,

its reserve of industrial workers, progress in technology, and the similarity of its cultural and legal traditions to those of the United States. It might, therefore, be expected that the elimination or reduction of the impediments mentioned above would stimulate à greater flow of capital to that area than is moving at the present time. Unfortunately, some of the factors, including the uncertainty of the world political situation, restrictive business practices, instability of cost-price relationships, and the memory of unfortunate investment experiences in the past, also present problems whose solution may require many years.

C. 

Near East and Africa.l/ At the end of 1950, U. S. private direct investments in the Near East and Africa accounted for about 5 percent of total American private investments abroad. 

Roughly 80 percent of these investments were in petroleum, mainly in the Near East.

A large increase of private investments from the United States to this area cannot be expected in the immediate future. In certain regions, especially in Africa, which possess basic resources of interest to American industry, U. S. investments have been retarded by the lack of basic facilities and by an inadequate labor supply. In the manufacturing field, most of the area is not sufficiently developed and the aggregate purchasing power is not large enough to support large investments. 

Governmental restrictions and either nationalist opposition, as in most of the Near East, or the concern of metropolitan powers to maintain their position in their overseas territories, have tended to limit American investments in some countries. Moreover, unsettled conditions in the Near East, nationalist strife in many African colonies, and racial tensions such as in the Union of 

South Africa have been impediments.

Except where American enterprise has been attracted by substantial resources, such as in the case of oil in the Near East, the unfamiliarity of American investors with actual conditions and the way of life of some of the countries in this area has also deterred the flow of investments. 

1/ In a summary of this area which contains such dissimilar economies as those of Turkey, the Arab States, Israel, Ethopia, Liberia, the Union of South Africa, and the dependent areas of Africa, it is difficult to describe briefly the conditions in the area as a whole. It is believed, however, that the general remarks in this summary are true for most of the countries concerned.

Long-run prospects for an increased flow of private U. S. investments to this area are somewhat more promising. Most of the governments in the area are conscious of the need for rapid development and are making progress in this respect, with the aid of international loan funds. These developments will not only provide basic facilities for extractive industries but also purchasing power and markets for manufacturing enterprises. The growing strength of the economies of these countries is likely to contribute to their stability and their desire to utilize foreign capital for further development. 

In this context technical assistance programs, IBRD or Export-Import Bank loans, diplomatic encouragement, and adequate dissemination of information can play a great role. d. The Far East. Only a small part of the total American investment abroad has been made in the Far East, which is defined here as the area from Afghanistan eastward to Indonesia and Japan, excluding Australia and New Zealand. China and Korea, though part of this area, are also excluded from the discussion for obvious reasons. Most of the 

American investments in the Far East have been made in the Philippines, with which the United States has had special relations, and in Indonesia and India. Investments in other Far Eastern countries were confined largely to direct investments in petroleum and commercial enterprises. 

Portfolio investments were of considerable importance in Japan in the prewar period. While they have been increasing somewhat in recent years, the greatest area of investment is in conjunction with patent licensing and private technical assistance.

American investments in the Far East have been low mainly because of the close ties between many of the Far Eastern countries and specific European countries, the relative unfamiliarity of many American businessmen with the area as compared with areas geographically and culturally closer to the United States, and the relatively low stage of development in some Far Eastern countries which limited opportunities for diversified investments. 

The variety of conditions within the area and the rapid changes which it has been undergoing in recent years make it difficult to arrive at accurate and complete generalizations with regard to current impediments to greater U. S. investments. While most of the countries have only recently acquired complete independence and are only beginning to industrialize and otherwise develop their economies, Japan even in the prewar period was an important world power with a well-developed industrial base, and with capital available for export. 

A number of countries are densely populated, while others have extensive undeveloped land and other resources. 

Outstanding among the risks and difficulties in some areas of the Far East are the threat of invasion and internal disturbance, the

newness of the governments and the average investor's doubts as to their ability to maintain stable and efficient administrations; the inadequacy of information on the technical and economic feasibility of projects; the existence of rigid controls; and the generally undeveloped stage of the economies, particularly the inadequacy of basic industries and facilities and of skilled, experienced personnel, and the low level of average incomes. 

Government policy statements and regulations as well as uncertainties regarding public attitudes toward foreign investment have, in some cases, been discouraging to American investors. 

Faced in some countries with socialistic and nationalistic trends and with limitations on the entry, use, and repatriation of capital, and on management policies and personnel, the potential investor has often looked to more familiar areas where the risks and limitations may be great but where he has more familiarity with the means of meeting them.

The profound political and economic changes through which many of the Far Eastern countries are passing, and the present difficulties with which new investments are faced, need not prevent increased American investments in the area. In many ways, the changes taking place may provide increased opportunities for American investments. The new governments are exerting great efforts to accelerate the rate of economic development of their countries. Some of the countries have shown that they are aware of the value of foreign capital and foreign technology. Improvement in the general investment climate in the Far East will take some time, in view of the general unfamiliarity of U. S. investors with the area and the nature of the problems with which the area is faced; but there are increasing opportunities for investments based on practical adjustments between the investor's business objectives and the aspirations of the country concerned.

2. General Analysis of Basic Factors a. History and Characteristics of U. S. Private Foreign 

Investment. In order to assess the direction and volume of 

U. S. private foreign investment in the future it is helpful to know its main characteristics in the past. From the study. of the recent history of U. S. foreign investment, two principal characteristics emerge: (1) A preference for equity investment rather than investments of loan capital with fixed interest; and (2) a large proportion of investment in the 

Western Hemisphere. 

The extent to which American private investment has been concentrated upon direct investments of American firms is shown by the figures on outstanding U. S. foreign investment in 1951. 

Of the total of $21.2 billion, $13.2 billion was in direct investment, $4.5 billion in foreign stocks and bonds, and $3.5 billion in miscellaneous long and short-term claims on foreigners. 

Of the $4.5 billion in stocks and bonds, 80 percent was invested either in Canadian securities or in bonds of the International Bank. U. S. private portfolio investment has never been sizable except in the 1920's.

The heavy proportion of direct investment by large American companies is due chiefly to the active interest of those companies in the product to be produced. It is probably fair to say that large foreign investments abroad have been motivated more by a desire to supplement supplies of materials or to expand markets than the generous returns that might be expected, although the latter, of course, are a necessary element. As these large companies have both the capital and the technical skills in their particular field, they prefer to retain managerial responsibility in their operations. Moreover, very few direct foreign investments have been made in an enterprise by those not directly involved in that particular line of business. 

By contrast, foreign portfolio investment has declined. Fol- lowing the widespread default of the 1930's, there was a loss of confidence on the part of the public in foreign securities and a tendency to prefer investment in savings institutions (insurance, banks, pension funds, etc.) over investment in foreign securities.

These, in turn, by reason of either policy or law, usually do not undertake the greater risks inherent in foreign portfolio investments. There is every likelihood that private foreign investment will continue to follow, in general, the pattern outlined unless

some means are found to provide greater incentive and security for portfolio investment.

In the 6 years from 1946 through 1951, American investors added $6.5 billion to their direct investments abroad. Part of this does not represent an inflow of new U. S. capital funds because included in the total is a substantial amount of retained profits, which may be permanently invested abroad or held as liquid assets. This figure includes $2.9 billion of profits retained by subsidiaries incorporated abroad and a large but indeterminate amount of retained profits of foreign branches of American firms. However, the use of these earnings by American investors to enlarge existing productive enterprises abroad is as much a contribution as an equivalent amount of capital from the United States.

A striking characteristic of U. S. private investment abroad in the postwar years, especially in the years 1947-49, was the heavy emphasis on the development of foreign sources of petroleum. Of the $6.5 billion net additions to direct investments abroad through capital outflows and retained profits in the period 1946-51, $3 billion was invested in petroleum. 

Manufacturing investments abroad have been growing steadily, largely by the reinvestment of earnings. In 1951, for instance, net capital outflows in manufacturing were $141 million, a postwar high, and to this was added a record $365 million of retained earnings. Foreign investment in manufacturing tends to follow the shifts in domestic production, that is, as new industries come to the fore in the United States they are soon extended abroad. Thus sizable foreign investments are now being made in antibiotics, synthetic fibers, chemical products including plastics, and building materials.

Another field of foreign investment with a great potential for growth is mining and smelting. Although only $400 million, net, was invested in this industry in 1946-51, about $150 million was invested in 1951 alone and this amount is probably more representative of the annual rate for the near future. Large investments are now in process or are contemplated in the iron ore of Canada, Venezuela, and Liberia, the bauxite, titanium, and other metals of Canada and the West Indies, the copper of Peru and 

Chile, the manganese of Brazil, and many others.

Any serious consideration of the problem of increasing U. S. foreign investments must take account of the wide-held fears by both investors and foreign countries as to the ability of the

countries to service additional dollar investments. In this comection mention is frequently made of the outstanding volume af debt and investment requiring dollar servicing. At the end of 1951, for instance, the U. S. Government had about $10.2 billion of credits outstanding. Scheduled principal repayments until 1961 average about $260 million annually, and interest collections amount to another $180 million annually over the period. 

About 80 percent of these credits were extended to Western Europe, so that repayments from that area will be about $350 million annually. To these amounts

To these amounts must be added the large income remittances from private dollar investments abroad, and the service requirements of the dollar loans of the International 

Bank for Reconstruction and Development.

Whether the servicing of these credits will be so heavy a burden on foreign countries that they will discourage additional private investment depends in part on the amounts of new capital flowing abroad from the United States. But it depends primarily upon an improvement in the trade position of debtor countries with the United States. b.

Relationship between Foreign Trade and Foreign Investment. Much of the outflow of capital from this country is directed toward the development or expansion of facilities abroad for the production of goods which the United States imports on a substantial scale. 

In addition, the desire of U. S. manufacturers to protect existing markets, or to expand them, has led to the establishment of plants abroad.

Many investments in activities other than the extractive and related industries have resulted directly from the familiarity with conditions abroad gained by U. S. exporters in the process of developing their foreign markets. The knowledge of specific af local needs and of local sources of materials and labor has often led to investments in assembly or manufacturing plants abroad. 

Investment in manufacturing and assembling operations has also been considerably stimulated in the postwar years by the existence of trade and exchange barriers which make it difficult for U. S. producers to retain certain foreign markets for direct shipments of finished merchandise from the United States. 

The volume of exports directly linked to private direct foreign investments is sizable. A large part of our direct foreign investment takes the form of machinery and equipment shipped to foreign installations by the American investing firms. 

Moreover, the bulk of the funds provided through other types of investment is also used for the purchase of such goods from U. S. producers, and dollar disbursements for local expenditures provide

dollar exchange for the purchase of other U. S. goods.

Whatever the impetus behind the establishment of assembly or manufacturing plants abroad, it has a tendency to alter the composition of U. S. exports. Parts and semifinished materials are substituted for finished manufactures. In the main, the products of these plants are sold locally or elsewhere in the world market rather than in the United States.

On the other hand, the establishment of American installations abroad gives rise to an interchange of industrial and marketing techniques as well as familiarity with American products, all of which promotes the general expansion of trade in both directions. 

The even closer link between U. S. imports and U. S. foreign investment is shown by the large volume of investments in the extractive industries which supply raw materials to meet U. S. industrial requirements. Products derived from the operations of American direct investment installations abroad are estimated to have comprised roughly one-fourth of the value of all U. S. imports in recent years.

During at least the past decade or two, the rapid expansion of industrial output in the United States, together with the limited availability or rising costs of exploiting domestic reserves of many raw materials, has brought a greater demand for imported supplies of these materials. In particular, the rapid expansion of industrial production in the United States since World War II has greatly increased the U. S. demand for such products as nonferrous metals.

These characteristics of the postwar U. S. economy have provided strong incentives for the investment abroad of private capital--both domestic and foreign--in facilities to provide the needed raw materials. If the U. S. demand for imported raw materials continues to increase, such incentives will be further strengthened. This interdependence between the United States, with its needs for industrial raw materials, and foreign countries needing capital equipment to develop their basic natural resources is a base of mutual interest upon which to build an increasing volume of private foreign investment. 

An increase of U. S. imports relative to exports would have a significant effect on U. S. foreign investment. Through reducing

the dollar gap it would contribute to a greater degree of convertibility of currencies and ease the problem of the remittance of investment income. The importance of this factor is underlined by the fact that in the postwar period (1946 through 1952) U. S. exports of goods and services amounted to $123 billion as against imports of only $78 billion. At the present time convertibility problems are considered by U. S. businessmen as one of the major deter rents to U. S. private investments abroad.

An expansion of U. S. imports relative to exports would also have important direct and indirect effects on business conditions and economic stability abroad. Increased sales to the United States by underdeveloped areas would add to their dollar exchange resources and permit larger purchases of the plant and equipment needed to accelerate development programs. 

The increased productive employment generated would expand purchasing power, help raise living standards, and broaden the market for manufactured goods. Business conditions in industrialized countries would also benefit from higher sales to the United States, through the impact of these sales on the level of employment and investment.

The interdependence of trade and investment in this respect is particularly important because, by and large, they complement each other in contributing to the improvement of some of the basic economic conditions which at present limit the flow of private American investments.

C. Basic Economic Factors. Among the principal impediments which have deterred all types of private foreign investment are the interrelated problems of economic instability; balance-ofpayment difficulties and exchange shortages; the underdeveloped nature of some economies as reflected in inadequate basic facilities, shortages of trained labor, lack of allied industries, and shortages of venture capital; and, in certain countries or industries, limited market demand.

Many American businessmen are reluctant to undertake longterm capital commitments in countries where economic conditions are unstable. In the early 1930's instability resulted from the downward spiral. In recent years instability has stemmed from inflationary forces in a number of countries. Severe or prolonged inflation or deflation distorts price-cost relationships, and carries a threat of currency revaluation and the imposition of government controls over prices and distribution. 

To many of the less developed countries whose economies are heavily

dependent on the production and marketing of a few primary raw materials, changes in world demand and prices for these key commodities bring fluctuations in their general business conditions, in their budget positions, and in foreign exchange earnings.

The shortage of dollar exchange, which prevails in the majority of the nations throughout the world, is of particular importance to the investor because it has resulted in the imposition of severe government controls over the entry and servicing of American investment. Underlying this dollar shortage is a complex of well-known factors. Even in cases where the policies with respect to capital investments are liberal, the existence of exchange controls acts as a psychological deterrent to investors, who fear that a future deterioration in the country's balance-of-payment position may result in an alteration of these policies.

In many countries investment opportunities are limited by the lack of facilities basic to economic development. Some countries have rich natural resources which cannot be utilized until transportation, power, port and other needed facilities are available. 

While the introduction of these facilities might, in itself, afford investment opportunities, the tendency toward national ownership and operation of transportation and public utilities often precludes their development by private foreign capital. 

In most underdeveloped countries there is a serious lack of trained manpower. This shortage not only constitutes a direct major obstacle to investment, but is one of the factors indirectly contributing to restrictions on investment by these countries. 

The shortage of private local capital available for investment is a deterrent to certain types of foreign investment. 

In part, this shortage of capital reflects the low level of incomes and private savings; to some extent, however, it reflects more the diversion of savings to idle currency hoards, precious metals, or to traditional investments in real estate, agricultural lands, family enterprises, or specialized trading operations. In some cases it is due to the lack of financial institutions for the channeling of savings into other forms of investment. As a result, the development of basic facilities and industries is retarded. 

Local capital may also be difficult to procure for foreign investors who desire to enter into licensing arrangements or who wish to have local capital participation or to obtain operating credit. In such cases the need to meet local costs with dollar expenditures is a deterrent.

In many underdeveloped countries public funds, both local and international, are playing the dominant role in development. 

This reflects a lack of private venture capital in some cases, a desire for rapid economic development in basic fields which are not attractive to private capital, and, in certain instances, an ideological preference for the use of public funds. 

In some countries immediate market possibilities for the sale of many manufactured or processed goods are very limited. 

This situation, of course, is not static; potential investors are concerned with future market prospects. In some countries even though American investors may see potentialities for profitable operation of new ventures through the introduction of more efficient production and distribution techniques, they may not be permitted to enter because of the opposition of already established business interests. Market limitations also have an effect upon investment in agricultural and extractive enterprises. Demand in world markets for many primary foodstuffs and raw materials grows only slowly and in certain cases productive facilities already in existence are adequate to supply the current markets for these items. In some cases, new investment opportunities are limited by the development of synthetic substitutes, although the latter are becoming increasingly important themselves as fields for foreign private investments in other countries.

The level of costs of production in a particular country abroad in relation to profits and as compared with costs and profits in this country or other countries is an important factor in the investor's decision. He may decide to develop substitutes or alternative sources of raw materials in new areas. 

He may decide to forego a market in one country in favor of another or to concentrate on the nome market. 

Most foreign investments involve greater risks than at nome which must be compensated by higher returns or other advantages. 

Since the most commonly enumerated risks are those beyond the investor's control, such as economic and political instability, sudden shifts in policies and regulations, and nationalization, in many cases he feels that he must have a differential profit as insurance against hazards. This is particularly so because the time, energy, and capital needed to initiate and develop many foreign investments do not produce returns in the first few years. In addition, foreign investors are often faced with a difficult supply situation and with a labor and technical force needing extensive training before efficient production can be attained; these types of delays tend to increase the length of time that elapses before profits are received.

Since comparative raw material costs, labor costs, combined U. S.-foreign tax costs, transportation and other cost factors have to be evaluated in the light of each foreign investment situation, no attempt is made in this summary to generalize with regard to cost factors in relation to returns. The relative importance of the basic economic factors affecting foreign investment varies considerably by the type of investment, whetner direct, in combination with local capital, or portfolio; or by major field of enterprise--agricultural, extractive, and special lines of manufacturing.

Most of the basic economic factors limiting foreign investment cannot be removed quickly or easily. An increased flow of capital itself would assist in their removal. d. Political, Social, and Cultural Factors. This study shows that differences in the political, social, and cultural traditions of the investor and the host country contribute to the reluctance of U. S. business to invest abroad. 

This kind of problem is most apparent where a country's traditions differ vastly from those of the investors, but it is of substantial importance even in cases where many common traditions make the difference less readily discernible. 

Virtually every country has repeatedly expressed its determination to maintain its personality and, consequently, to carry on its development according to its traditions and aspirations. 

Because of this, many countries, particularly those which have gained their independence recently, have looked upon the introduction of foreign capital and enterprise as a challenge if not a threat to their individual way of life.

Although usually characterized as nationalist opposition, this sentiment has a much wider basis. Large-scale foreign investments entail far-reaching changes which affeet all aspects of a country's social structure. They involve such matters as religious and moral values, concepts of law and equity, the relationship of the state and the community, family and social relations, the status of people employed by a new enterprise, and the direction of the country's development. 

The significance of this problem is perhaps better revealed by the wide range of attitudes toward free enterprise than by the more obvious differences in religion, language, and social customs. 

Very few countries nave tne American's devotion to the free enterprise system. Even some of the Western European countries which

traditionally favor free enterprise tolerate a greater degree of government responsibility in determining the course of national development than is practiced in the United States. 

In some underdeveloped countries, the people have traditionally relied on governments to take the lead in economic development. 

Similarly, attitudes toward competition and methods of production are variable, especially with regard to foreign competition. American competition meets resistance not only on nationalist grounds but more importantly because its mass production technique clashes even in Western Europe with the traditional preference for personalized, nign-cost production for steady smaller markets. In many instances associations of manufacturers abroad nave a voice in deciding whether a given industry is "saturated" or has room for new competitors. over, influential individuals or groups in underdeveloped areas and highly organized cartels in industrialized countries have often exercised effective pressure to ward off U. S. competition. 

In their desire for rapid economic development, many countries take too little account of the resource base on which such development must rest. Thus, some countries, which should give attention to the improvement of agriculture and other primary resource industries fail to do so and instead mistakenly direct their efforts to attracting investment into heavy industries inappropriate to their stage of development. 

Because of the impact of American investments on both traditions and the acquired position of several groups, a wide variety of forces in each country have brought their combined pressure to bear on their governments to regulate and control the flow of foreign investments.

This opposition is less pronounced with regard to portfolio investments because in that case the direction of the change is under broad national control. But for precisely the same reason, the American investor is reluctant to trust his capital to foreign management.

More generally, many potential American investors have been reluctant to venture into countries where they encounter restraints which they do not consider economically justifiable or where attitudes seem likely to bring about such restraints. Moreover, they are also discouraged by the insecurity arising from the East-West tension, actual conditions of warfare, political instability, and general uncertainty inherent in the inexperienced administrations of some countries.

The positions of the American investor and the host country have been reconciled in an increasing number of instances, thus indicating that the problem is one of realistic adjustment of the requirements of both parties. 

However, the preceding factors are enduring features of today's nation-states and will continue to require that, instead of being financially geared only to international market needs, foreign capital will need to take account of national aspirations as well, as indeed it frequently endeavors to do at present. e.

Policy and Attitude of Foreign Governments Toward American Private Investment. In most countries of the free world American private investors are faced with restrictive government policies and attitudes toward foreign private investment and terms rigidly prescribed by foreign governments. In only a few countries is there genuine effort to promote and encourage the in-flow of American private capital. Few governments have by their actions fully recognized their own responsibility for the creation of an investment climate, based on confidence, equality of opportunity and the prospect of reasonable profits, which will induce foreign capital to emigrate to their countries. On the contrary, governments of some countries which most urgently need foreign capital investment in their economic development all too frequently have taken actions which create uncertainty in the mind of the foreign investor or otherwise undermine his confidence.

In theory most governments of the free world favor private enterprise. In practice, private initiative and private enterprise are allowed to operate in many countries only within varying degrees of stringency of government planning, regulation, and control and in certain fields are faced with active government participation in the ownership and operation of enterprises which private capital might otherwise develop. 

Uncertainties with respect to local government encouragment and support, to changing government policies, and even to possible nationalization of his enterprise are formidable barriers in the mind of the potential American investor. Recently, for example, the U. S. investment community was vocal in its criticism of the failure of most countries to join the United States in voting against the one-sided presentation of the problem of national development of resources embodied in the U. N. resolution on "Exploitation of 

Natural Resources."

Many countries have from time to time made statements regarding the contribution which foreign private investment, along with its

technical know-how and managerial skills, can make. Too frequently, however, these policy statements have not been followed up by effective government action both at the legislative and the administrative levels. Moreover, what is lacking is actual promotion and encouragement of foreign investments. 

Nearly all governments limit foreign capital investment, whether by field of investment or extent of capital participation. 

In most countries, permissible fields of investment are defined only in very broad terms, leaving the potential foreign investor much in doubt as to whether or not his project would be welcome. f. Specific Regulations of Foreign Governments Affecting Private 

Foreign Investment. Major regulations affecting American private investors and often deterring them from making an investment are those regulating the entry of capital, the remittance of earnings and repatriation of capital, and the operations of business. 

The entry of foreign capital into most countries of the free world is controlled by some type of screening. Screening of investments represents a greater degree of national determination of the direction of economic development than the U. S. investor encounters at home and is, to that extent, an impediment to the flow of capital. The investor is, nevertheless, more likely to object to the manner in which the screening operation is administered than to the principle of screening itself. Relatively few countries have defined clearly the terms and conditions under which foreign investment can be made. Some countries have not even clearly and publicly designated the agency of government which is empowered to make the screening decision. Often the applications of foreign investors are dealt with on a case-by-case basis, with the only guide line a vague policy of considering the contemplated investment "on its merit."

A considerable number of foreign governments have discouraged investment by restrictive limitations on ownership and management, ranging from prohibitions on foreign control of key industries to across-the-board restrictions on all industries. The most general practice is to limit foreign participation, not by regulations or decrees, but by administrative decision in individual cases. Investment in mining industries is especially restricted by laws confining concession rights to nationals or to companies incorporated under the laws of the country concerned, or by redtape, indecision, or antipathy encountered in the licensing process. 

Restrictions on the remittance of profits and repatriation

of capital not only hamper enterprises once the investment is made but also are among the greatest deterrents to private investment abroad. Very few countries are today free of exchange controls. A number of countries, however, have explicitly encouraged foreign investment by liberal policies on the remittance and repatriation of capital but usually only for those investments which have met the rigid screening criteria. In general, restrictions vary in accordance with the severity of the balanceof payment problem in individual countries. Investors protest most when the stringency of controls does not appear justified by the balance-of-payment position. 

Once his investment has been admitted, the American investor is in many countries beset by numerous types of controls affecting his operation which he does not face in the United States. Chief among these are import controls, difficulties of converting his profits into dollars and of making remittances to the United States, unfavorable exchange rates, discriminatory and costly labor regulations, restrictions on staffing and management, discriminatory tax treatment, and unduly complicated tax legislation. Excessively high taxes constitute a deterrent in some countries; others have offered attractive incentives in the form of tax concessions to new industries. Since such a large proportion of American private investment is concentrated in the field of resource development, the deterring effects of export taxes, multiple exchange rates, and other measures impeding the export of raw materials are worthy of separate mention.

Most American investors have grown accustomed to operating in a world where, at least for the present, business risks include uncertainties with respect to convertibility, remittance of profits, import controls, and the like. Most recognize and understand the position of foreign governments in imposing such regulations for balance-of-payment reasons or for purposes of economic development. 

If they know in advance what is involved in their cost of operations, many still move ahead with their plans, provided the returns in the form of raw materials, the maintenance and expansion of markets or profits are sufficient to outweigh the difficulties. 

Often, however, it is the extra and often unnecessary deterrents over and above the basic ones which discourage investment -- changing government policies and regulations, arbitrary official actions, new taxes or other charges not expected when the investment was made, favoritism shown local groups, redtape, and minor irritations. Apart from any solution of major problems of balance-of-payment and basic economic and political uncertainties, there is, therefore, much that foreign governments can do to make the climate more stable and more attractive to the American investor. Some countries have already made determined efforts to improve the conditions of investment in their policy statements, in their regulations, and in general government practices.

CONCLUSION 

1. A greatly increased flow of private investment from this country cannot be expected in the next few years. The capacity of the United 

States to supply private capital is great, and most of veteran U. S. private investors abroad at present face the future with restrained optimism. But the obstacles to private foreign investment are also sizable and stubborn. Essentially they consist of chronic difficulties in the flow of trade and in exchange relationships; in political instability within and between nations; and in the manifold differences between ourselves and other countries regarding the "conditions" of investment. Moreover, for many potential investors, these impediments deter investment in many countries because of the relatively attractive opportunities still offered in the United States and some other countries in this Hemisphere. Without major changes in these conditions, it is not likely that the rate, nature, and direction of U. S. private foreign investment will be substantially changed in the next few years. 2. 

Although the basic impediments to investment are found in most countries, the variations in the way they affect investment in different countries and areas and in different industries are so great that means of overcoming them must be worked out to fit indivi dual situations. This is a slow process and will require give and take on the part of foreign business and governments as well as 

American investors. 

3. In some parts of the world, and especially in countries most in need of U. S. capital for development, political and economic condi tions are highly unattractive to U. S. investors; but at the same time, many of these countries have material resources needed by U. S. industry, and they in turn need the capital goods and the skills United States industry can supply them to advance their own economic development. This common interest is a base upon which practical understanding and mutually satisfactory adjustments can be built. 

This constructive effort will require time and patience. Above all, it will require respect on our part for aims and methods which are radically different from our own but which other sovereign nations have chosen as their way of achieving what they want. And it will require similar respect on their part for our own system of free enterprise. In his Inaugural Address, President Eisenhower said: "Honoring the identity and the special heritage of each nation in the world, we shall never use our strength to try to impress upon another people our own cherished political and economic institutions."

4. The American investor will decide for himself whether or not to invest abroad. The U. S. and foreign governments can influence his decision by making the conditions of investment more attractive. 

Positive and effective steps can be taken by both the United States and other governments for the immediate removal of certain impediments and for a long-range program designed to remove the more basic impediments.

RECOMMENDATIONS 1. Actions Within the Power of the U. S. Government. 

a. Trade Programs 

Efforts should be made to increase the earning capacity of other countries through progressive and careful revisions af U. S. tariff and import policies and support of international efforts to remove barriers to trade. (1) U. S. trade and investment policies and programs are interdependent, and in all policy decisions affecting foreign investment, this inter-relationship must be an important consideration. In negotiations for the lowering of tariffs, careful thought should be given to impediments to the international flow of capital, such as import and export controls, quotas and other regulations imposed by both the capital exporting and capital importing countries. U. S. private foreign investment can increase the dollar earning potentialities of foreign countries; at the same time increasing investment creates new claims against their dollar earnings in the form of remittances of earnings. Particular attention should be given to the lowering of tariffs and other trade restrictions which would encourage manufacturing and processing industries in other countries. Progress toward currency convertibility must be one of the important aims of all U. S. trade policy. (2) Serious considerations should be given to the problem of introducing greater stability in the demand for certain basic raw materials. In the past, violent fluctuations in the market for raw materials have caused serious disruptions in the economies and national budgets of some countries that are most in need of U. S. capital. 

Since U. S. trade policy is being re-examined in other studies, no detailed recommendations are felt necessary in this report. 

b. Public Aid Programs 

Since the end of World War II, the U. S. Government has made largescale assistance available to other countries in order to assist them in their reconstruction and defense programs. These basically political undertakings are not susceptible of the type of analysis underlying this report. Accordingly, the recommendations which follow should not be interpreted as having application to this type of assistance. However,

to the maximum extent possible private trade and private financial facilities should be utilized in the administration of such aid which, in addition, should be so administered as to facilitate the steadily increasing participation by private enterprise in marshaling the resources for the purposes contemplated by the aid program. 

The Government should continue to assist less developed countries in their efforts to develop their basic economy as a long-range program for improving their ability to attract private investments. (1) The Government should, when requested by foreign governments and in full cooperation with U. S. business, help to provide directly or indirectly technical assistance to improve the economic institutions, public administration, and health and educational conditions of less developed countries in order to increase their ability to attract private foreign investment. The Government should also support and encourage the flow of free voluntary assistance from private U. S. institutions, foundations, and individuals. (2) Loans by U. S. Government agencies can, if properly administered, help to establish the conditions abroad which will be conducive af increased private investment. If public loans are granted for the purpose of facilitating the growth of such basic facilities as transportation, power, irrigation, and the like, they can help in establishing the economic base for development through private channels. 

Loans by U. S. Government agencies for economic development purposes should be granted only after a careful check has been made to determine that private finance is not available or not interested, and after determination that the undertaking should not be financed by the IBRD.

Such loans, of course, should be extended only if there is a reasonable expectation that they will be repaid and that the interest of the United States in the undertaking is such as to warrant the use of public funds. (3) When circumstances necessitate special economic assistance on a grant basis, grants should, in general, concentrate on or be related to those projects which would contribute to the development of the basic resources of the country, the amelioration of public services, the improvement of its credit-worthiness and thus provide the framework for increased private investment. 

C. Other Legislative and Administrative Action 

(1) Taxation

The findings of this study indicate the desirability of reviewing various aspects of U. S. taxation to provide additional incentive to increased U. S. private investments abroad. It is felt, however, that the matter requires careful study by specialists in taxation, and evaluation

in terms of economic policy objectives. Various proposals, and justifications for each, should be worked out by specialists in the Government agencies concerned and in industry for presentation to Congress as soon as possible.

Proposals for tax revision have been made on various occasions by government agencies and private groups and these proposals are being carefully studied by the U. S. Treasury Department as part of its general survey of the U. S. tax system. The business survey undertaken for the present study indicates that tax incentives hold considerable appeal to present and potential investors in the foreign field. While it is difficult to gauge in advance the effectiveness of tax incentives in increasing the flow of private foreign investments, there is ample justification for a review of certain aspects of U. S. taxation. 

Special consideration should be given to a revision of the tax credit system in such a way that, in no event, will tax concessions given by a foreign government to attract American investments merely result in smaller U. S. tax credits for the investor.

Other proposals which have been made by such groups include: 

Broadening the definition of creditable foreign taxes; elimination of the so-called "overall limitation" on the foreign tax credit; extension of the tax advantages offered by Western Hemisphere Trade Corporations to the whole world; extension to foreign branches of U. S. firms of the privilege now enjoyed by foreign subsidiaries of U. S. firms to defer tax on incomes earned abroad until it is repatriated; special tax incentives to encourage the purchase of foreign securities by individual and institutional American investors; provisions for accelerated amortization of plant and property abroad; and complete or partial exemption from U. S. taxes of income earned abroad -- either unilaterally or subject to reciprocal advantages given by foreign countries through tax treaties. 

(2) Guaranties and Risk Insurance. 

No recommendations for action on guaranties is made in this study. 

It is felt, however, that a specific study should be made of the effectiveness of the present program, of various proposals for its modification, and the possibility of replacing the program by private insurance backed by government reinsurance. 

The proposals for modification include: (a) Extending the guaranties to old as well as new investments. (b) Extending the range of risk to cover international and civil disturbances.

(c) Providing guaranties for portfolio as well as direct investments. (d) Reducing the cost of guaranties. (e) Reducing the extent of government scrutiny of applications for guaranties. (f) Eliminating in whole or in part the requirement for agreement with the host government as a prerequisite for issuing a U. S. guaranty.

Inability to convert earnings into dollars, the possibility of expropriation, and threats of international or civil disturbance are among the most important obstacles to increased private foreign investment. 

There is no clear evidence emerging from this study that the present guaranty system, under which guaranties are negotiated by the Mutual Security Agency and administered by the Export-Import Bank to insure new investors against the risks of inconvertibility and expropriation, has resulted in an increased volume of investment. Moreover, business opinion on the effectiveness of guaranties is far from unanimous. Some businessmen are definitely opposed to the guaranty principle on the grounds (1) that the foreign government, not the United States, should make the guaranties and pay any liabilities, (2) that guaranties tend to perpetuate the very risks they are designed to compensate and (3) that the existence of a government guaranty system leads to wider government interference in private business. Others object to the cost of the guaranties, the methods of negotiating the contracts, the necessity of disclosing business affairs to the Government, and the limitation of guaranties to new investments. Others believe, however, that the guaranty principle is sound as a means of giving confidence to business in the face of the extraordinary risks encountered under present world conditions. (3) Antitrust Laws and Regulations.

From the findings of this study, it is evident that there should be an examination by the appropriate agencies, in consultation with private business, of the effects, good and bad, ar antitrust laws and their administration on foreign investment.

U. S. corporations and firms are subject to the antitrust laws in their foreign operations. Generally speaking, activities in foreign camerce are subject to possible antitrust prosecution if they affect trade in this country. Under certain circumstances, many typical forms of private foreign economic agreements are illegal or at least suspect in the eyes of the U. S. law. These include price-fixing arrangements

and the allocation of markets; and there may also be a question as to the propriety of certain exchanges of know-how. Concession contracts or other types of exclusive agreements may likewise be made the basis of prosecution if they tend to limit ca petition. 

Same business representatives maintain that the antitrust laws constitute a deterrent to private foreign investment and that business should be free to adjust to monopolistic arrangements abroad developed by foreign firms in accord with foreign legislation. As an alternative to relaxing our laws, some business opinion favors at least a clarification of their scope and applicability so that businessmen would be better informed as to permissible actions in their foreign operations. 

This might be accomplished by statutory declaration or administrative interpretation. Complaints from business suggest that more certainty in this regard, even without change, might provide some stimulant to private foreign investment.

On the other hand, it is arguable with equal or perhaps greater weight that the enactment in other countries of antitrust laws is a more desirable way to promote foreign development. Cartels may deter development because they limit access to resources and to markets, and they foster unhealthy concentration of economic power. The existence of local antitrust protection in a foreign country might, therefore, make it more rather than less attractive to private investment. (4) Securities and Exchange

Study should be made of the laws administered by the Securities and Exchange Commission and the regulations, forms, and especially the procedures which the Commission has adopted, in order to determine whether any of them should be modified to facilitate the sale of foreign securities in the United States.

The Securities Act of 1933 requires the registration with the Commission of any securities issued by foreign as well as domestic business enterprises which seek to sell those securities to the United States public. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 requires the registration with the Commission of all securities, including foreign securities, listed on the United States securities exchanges. In both cases the purpose of registration is to make available to American investors adequate and up-to-date information regarding the affairs of the companies whose securities are so registered. While no detailed study of this subject has yet been made, it is recognized that there are substantial differences between American and foreign standards concerning the kind and amount of information about business affairs that is required by law or custom to be disclosed to investors. 

---

U. S. accounting and auditing standards aim to give investors a more revealing picture of a company's financial affairs than is customarily given in many foreign countries. The same is true with regard to disclosures required under the Securities Act and the Securities Exchange Act of the interests of the management and controlling stockholders. In some foreign countries, there are business secrecy laws which restrict the amount of information that can be disclosed to potential private investors. In short, many foreign business firms would find it difficult, or would be reluctant, for various reasons, to comply with the requirements of the Securities Act of 1933 and the rules thereunder before offering their securities for public sale in the United States.

The Public Utility Holding Act of 1935 and the Investment Company 

Act of 1940, which are administered by the Securities and Exchange Commission, also have a bearing on certain types of U. S. investments abroad, and may create further impediments. 

It should be understood that these statutes are considered proper and necessary for the protection of American investors. Every effort should be made, however, to assist foreign business firms and governments to sell their securities in the United States by (1) simplifying S.E.C. regulations and procedures, and (2) helping foreign firms and governments to improve their methods of financial administration so as to conform to S.E.C. requirements.

2. Program to Achieve Understanding with Foreign Governments as to

Mutually Acceptable Conditions for Foreign Investment. 

The economic development of the countries of the free world is to the advantage of both the United States and these countries. Private American investment in sound enterprises abroad can contribute substantially to this development. The United States may, therefore, properly attempt to bring about an improvement in the investment climate abroad by promoting mutual understanding between the United States and other countries in this respect. This effort must be carried out without any implication of paternalism or imperialism. 

Many foreign countries are in fact coming to realize that there are great advantages to them in the importation of American capital particularly where it is accompanied by American know-how. Where this condition exists, the United States may appropriately assist these foreign governments in a series of changes in their laws and regulations and in adopting consistent and reliable policies calculated to attract American private investment. 

The method by which this work might be carried on will vary according to the circumstances, but it is an important and significant part of the pursuit of American foreign economic policy. There are no ready-made set of rules that can be published and urged upon the United States friends abroad. Thus, our efforts in this direction should be carried out on a sustained basis through highly competent professional personnel whose activities must be carefully coordinated with the pursuit of other aspects of our foreign policy. 

Negotiation of Treaties a.

A vigorous diplomatic effort should be made to achieve better understanding with foreign governments with regard to mutually acceptable conditions for foreign investment. Treaties should be negotiated as formal recognition of closer relations. 

(1) Treaties of Friendship, Commerce and Navigation. Efforts should be continued to negotiate FCN treaties with foreign governments. They should be pushed and signed only when cônditions are satisfactory enough that the treaties amount to an endorsement of existing or anticipated relations. It may be necessary to modify the terms of these treaties to meet the requirements of the U. S. investor and the foreign country in the light of the situation existing in a particular country.

(2) Special Investment Treaties. If agreement on all provi sions of the standard FCN Treaty cannot be reached at this time, it might be advisable to arrive at agreement on certain conditions related to the investment field. In some cases, however, such an abbreviated treaty would be equally difficult to negotiate. The advisability of special investment treaties would have to be carefully considered by this Government, acting in consultation with the business community, in the light of conditions in the country concerned. In some cases, it might be preferable to wait for successful conclusion of FCN treaties; in others, the abbreviated treaty might be an acceptable interim measure to establish mutual agreement on specific issues. (3) Taxation Treaties. Efforts should be continued to negotiate taxation treaties. If, in the future, legislative concessions are provided for in U. S. taxes on income earned abroad, consideration should be given to the possibility of making them dependent upon reciprocal advantages from foreign countries. b. Program to Encourage Foreign Governments to Take Specific 

Actions to Attract Private American Investment.

The . S. Government should encourage foreign governments to take positive steps to attract American capital. 

Many such steps to improve the climate for U. S. investment abroad have already been taken by some countries. In others, there have been relaxations of some restrictions for the purpose of attracting increased investment in those fields considered important for national development. The U. S. Government should point out the mutual advantages derived from such measures and encourage further steps in this direction. (1) Clarification of Attitude. Foreign governments should be encouraged to consider the advantages of stating as clearly as possible their attitude and policy toward private foreign capital. These statements might include: (a) Definitions of fields in which foreign capital may operate. 

Foreign governments should consider the advantages of long-range economic investments that are not now permitted because they are not immediate dollar earners or dollar savers or for other reasons. (b) Assurances--through general policy statements, bilateral treaties, or in specific agreements with individual firms--that

investments will not be nationalized or expropriated without adequate, prompt, and effective compensation; that U. S. investors will receive treatment no less favorable than given local or other foreign investors; that discriminatory measures will not be taken; and that in no event will measures which retroactively harm the investor's position be taken. (2) Controls Affecting Investment. In cases where such controls exist, foreign governments can be encouraged to be more consistent and more efficient in their screening of proposed foreign investments and in the administration of their import and exchange controls. (a) Where the screening of proposed new investments is considered necessary, the screening authority should be clearly designated and decisions should be quick, clear, and consistent. 

Whenever possible, arrangements should be made for prescreening by foreign officials located in the United States. When approval of a proposed new investment is given, it should carry automatic approval of import and exchange licenses. (b) Where import controls exist, they should be coordinated with exchange controls on the entry of capital, and import licenses should automatically insure the allocations of exchange. While it is recognized that in administering exchange controls, countries will continue to give priority to the most essential imports, remittances of profits should be given priority over nonessential imports. (c) It is highly important that foreign governments earn a reputation for consistency in making decisions affecting foreign investment, so that the investor knows where he stands. 

(3) Special Inducements. The U. S. Government can suggest to other governments the value of special inducements in attracting increased foreign investment in enterprises conducive to sound economic development. (a) Duty-free entry of equipment needed for new investment during the early years of operation should be considered for its incentive value. (b) Liberal tax treatment in the host country can be an incentive to new investment. Such treatment might include: Accelerated amortization for the easing of corporate income taxes;

reduction of tax rates for the earlier years of a new investment; and refraining from special taxes which in effect confiscate profits. (The current study of the U. S. tax system should consider the extent to which modification of the tax credit system could give greater strength to this kind of incentive.) (c) New foreign investments in need of local capital can be assisted by the private and public financial institutions of the host countries. (d) New enterprises can be assisted in obtaining land, power facilities, etc., at reasonable cost. (e) The terms of leases, the size of concessions, and special fees on mining concessions can be liberalized to attract investors in extractive enterprises and assure them that they will be able to recover the high costs of development and earn reasonable profits. (f) The terms of entry and the tax treatment of technical personnel can be liberalized. (g) Adjustment can be made in the requirement for local participation in capital, ownership, and management. (4) Information on Investment Opportunities. One of the most effective ways in which foreign governments can attract American capital is to undertake a vigorous campaign, in cooperation with their own businessmen, to publicize the conditions of investment in their countries and to bring investment opportunities to the attention of potential investors. 

(5) Dependent Overseas Territories. The U. S. Government should continue to work with metropolitan countries and with their dependent overseas territories to encourage by practical measures greater participation of American capital in the overseas territories.

3. Program to Support, Advise, and Inform U. S. Business. 

U. S. business, in its foreign investment activities, should be given the strongest possible support by the Government principally by means of a strengthened Foreign Service which will promote the Interests of business and help business to locate investment opportunities. The Government should also improve its investment information service, especially for small business. In addition, it should take positive action to encourage an increase in private portfolio Investment. a.

Strengthening the Role of U. S. Missions Abroad. 

U. S. Missions abroad should be adequately staffed with competent economic officers for the purpose of maintaining a constant flow of information on economic conditions and investment opportunities, and for the purpose of helping individual firms work out their foreign investment problems with local governments. 

The Missions should strive for closer cooperation with American business groups and for more effective and vigorous support of the legitimate rights and interests of American investors. U. S. 

U. S. repre sentatives abroad should work with local government and business groups to improve the investment climate by pointing out the advantages of development through private enterprise and American investments. If called upon, they should also provide investment counseling for businessmen of other countries who are seeking American capital or partners, or who need assistance in preparing investment projects for effective presentation in the United States.

This strengthening of the U. S. Missions abroad will require: (1) More intensive training of new economic officers in the Department of Commerce and in field work. (2) Specialization in commercial and investment affairs. (3) Specialists in investment work assigned for such period as necessary to a few countries where the opportunities for U. S. investment are great, but where there are numerous investment problems. (4) Greater emphasis in Foreign Service reporting on economic conditions, commodities, industries, and government regulations affecting investment.

(5) Closer coordination with the work of U. S. Missions of the efforts of representatives of U. S. foreign economic and technical assistance agencies who, by the nature of their work, are involved in closely related activities. b. The U. S. Government here and abroad should support and encourage private firms and associations in their efforts to improve relations with local governments and local private groups abroad. 

American businessmen must bear the main responsibility as "missionaries" for private enterprise and American methods. Through the conduct of their own business, their labor-management practices, their contributions to the development of the community, and in their associations with local trade and business groups, U. S. businessmen abroad are in every sense ambassadors of the United States. As such, they should be carefully chosen and trained by their firms in their responsibility of representing this country in all of their activities abroad. This kind of selection and training is already being provided by a number of U. S. firms. American businessmen might also take more initiative to insure that there is better understanding abroad of the contribution which foreign capital has made and can make to the advancement of underdeveloped countries. In particular, concrete examples of beneficial foreign investment might be given greater publicity. 

Above all, there must be close, frank working relations between U. S. Government representatives and business firms and trade associations who desire Government support in working out their foreign investment problems.

C. The Government must step up its investment information activities in this country.

Investment services should be centralized in the Department of Commerce and its field offices. Well-trained personnel should work more closely with economic and commercial officers of foreign embassies and with businessmen from this country and abroad. There should be considerable expansion of frequent, regular, and prompt dissemination, both by general publication and by specific communi cation with selected potential investors, of investment opportunities, basic economic information, foreign government regulations, etc. 

This assumes that related activities of U. S. Missions abroad will also be expanded.

This centralized investment service should include a means of providing closer relations between Government and portfolio investors for the exchange of information in this field and for studies of the problems and means of facilitating a greater flow of American capital into other than direct investments. d. Special attention should be given to the potentialities of greater participation by smaller U. S. business firms in foreign investment.

Particular attention to small and medium-sized firms who might invest abroad is needed for several reasons. A number of foreign countries offer investment opportunities--in canneries, leather industries, service industries, etc.,--which are not attractive to large U. S. investors because of the small size of the investment and return or because of limited markets and resources. Though needing and desiring such investment, many of these countries do not know how to present these more modest investment opportunities effectively. On the other hand, there are in this country many small and medium-sized corporations or individuals who might be interested in overseas operations if they had sufficient knowledge of the facts of the investment opportunities and economic conditions abroad. Many cannot afford the heavy expenses of exploratory investigations. 

By assisting in disseminating information on investment opportunities, the Government can perform a useful function which may lead not to a great amount of new capital flow but certainly to an effective means of aiding the economic development of foreign countries with the know-how and technical assistance of American "small business" as well as large. As at present, all such Government information services should, of course, be equally available to all segments of the business community. e. Special attention should also be given to the expansion of portfolio investment by American firms, savings institutions, and individuals in foreign enterprises. 

This study has merely touched the fringes of this aspect of the foreign investment situation. Much more needs to be done in the field of portfolio investment, which in some countries is the kind of capital most needed or desired. The main obstacles (other than legal restraints) in the way of increased portfolio holdings abroad are the lack of confidence by American investors in the stability and credit-worthiness of foreign governments and private firms, and their fear of the unavailability of dollar exchange for the payment

of interest and principal. The following recommendations are ways and means of increasing confidence among American investors as a basis of expanded holdings of foreign stocks and bonds: (1) The Government should continue to give its encouragement and support to the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, as a means of building confidence among American investors, which will lead to an increased volume of portfolio investment. Much of the foreign portfolio investment by Americans in the past few years (disregarding the purchase of Canadian securities) has been made indirectly through the purchase of the bonds of the IBRD, which in turn has invested the proceeds in foreign undertakings--mostly public projects. In addition, private investors have bought from the IBRD--with or without its guarantee--an increasing volume of the bonds of foreign governments and firms received by the Bank in connection with its loans. This indirect process has been useful in maintaining a flow of portfolio investment and in building confidence that may lead to a greater flow of private portfolio investment in the future. The proponents of the proposed 

International Finance Corporation urge that it would provide a useful additional facility for serving the same purpose. This study, however, has not attempted to evaluate the proposal. (2) The U. S. Government should put additional emphasis on helping to secure technical assistance for the purpose of improving financial methods and institutions abroad. 

The Government should coordinate its facilities to fill requests of foreign governments and firms for assistance of this kind. This would make it possible to supply foreign governments with names of those who can help them, not only with problems of financial administration and control, but also with the development of capital markets, investment banks, the floating of securities issues, and similar matters. Assistance of this kind can contribute greatly to increased confidence among potential buyers of foreign stocks and bonds. (3) With the increase of confidence in the credit-worthiness of foreign governments and firms, careful study should be given to the possibility of relaxing State and ederal laws and regulations governing investments by . S. savings institutions and insurance companies in foreign undertakings. With the profound change that has taken place in the patterns of saving in the United States, any sizable increase in U. S. portfolio

investment abroad will depend largely upon the ability of savings institutions and insurance companies to lend their funds to foreign borrowers. In its early years of operation, bond sales of the IBRD were restricted by State laws and regulations in many parts of the United States. All but a few States now permit these sales, and it would be desirable to relax similarly provisions of State laws with regard to direct sales of foreign stocks and bonds to such institutions and to individual fiduciaries. (4) The U. S. Government, in close cooperation with financial institutions, should explore ways of encouraging foreign investments through investment trusts as a means of spreading risks and increasing confidence among small investors and institutions The greater difficulty of making preliminary investigations of foreign securities, as compared with domestic securities, and the details involved in the subsequent management of foreign security holdings, may prevent many individual investors from taking an interest in foreign stocks and bonds. An investment trust, however, would ordinarily be interested in larger blocks of each individual security so that these burdens would not be excessive in relation to the amounts of money involved. f. Government officials must work more closely with business groups in this country-in securing their advice on matters affecting both trade and investment.

This would include such matters as advice on proposals for. public loans which might conflict with investments being undertaken by the private sector, on trade and investment treaties, and on aspects of the technical assistance programs which affect private trade and investment. The surveys undertaken in this study should be followed by a continuing program to maintain closer working relations between Government and business in the investment field.
