WHY CONSTITUTION IS GENERALLY CONSIDERED DRY AND UNINTERESTING The provisions of the Constitu tion fall into two classes: 

(1) The machinery provisions. 

These have to do with the mechanics of government, such as the manner in which the President and members of Congress shall be elected, how 

Congress shall be convened, the powers and prerogatives of the differ ent departments of the Government, and so forth. 

(2) The charter provisions. They are the great rocks on which is founded all human happiness-the human and property rights guaranteed every individual.

It is the technical nature of the machinery provisions of the Constitution that is responsible for the general, erroneous impression that it is a dry, ponderous, and uninteresting document.

FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF CONSTITUTION 

The three fundamental principles of the Constitution are: (1) The guarantee of liberty by the limitation of the powers of government. 

(2) Representative government. 

(3) Division of the powers of the government.

1. GUARANTEE OF LIBERTY BY LIMITATION OF POWERS OF GOVERNMENT 

The most fundamental of the basic principles of the Constitution is the GUARANTEE OF LIBERTY BY THE LIMITATION OF THE POWERS OF GOVERNMENT. 

In the American system of gov ernment the people are absolutely and solely the source of all power. 

The only power the government has is that which has been granted it by the people and which is stated definitely in the Constitution.

The people may at any time, through the amendment of their 

Constitution, increase or decrease this power.

It is through this limitation of power that the people protect themselves from abuse and tyranny on the part of those whom they select to govern them. From the beginning of time "man born of the loin of woman" has, because of greed, self-interest or personal ambition, been given to oppress his fellow-beings through abuse of power. "Love of power" is as inherent and deeply rooted in mortal man as are the emotions of love, hatred and jealousy.

Again and again in American history, as in the history of all other nations, groups or factions for the time being in power have wished to enact laws the desire for which grew out of the passions of the hour, or the element of self-interest-but they have always run up against, and been stopped by, that great principle in our Constitution which limits the powers of Congress. 

And thus we have remained a free people, retaining our liberty. 

The blessings of liberty can be preserved to ourselves and our posterity only if we understand and support the Constitution.

You naturally ask, "If at any time those in power should willfully or ignorantly refuse to be governed by the limitations of power prescribed by the Constitution, what will stop them from doing what they wish?

The answer is, the Supreme Court of the United States and the lower Federal Courts which have the power to render ineffective all legislation which may be in conflict with the Constitution. (See, "The 

Supreme Court," page 15.)

III. DIVISION OF POWERS OF GOVERNMENT 

The third fundamental principle of the Constitution is the Division of the Powers of the Government, which is accomplished through the separation of the Government into three branches or departments: (1)

The Executive (the President), (2) 

the Legislative (Congress), and (3) 

the Judicial (the Supreme Court and the lower Federal Courts).

Under this plan our National laws are made by Congress, interpreted by the Federal Courts and executed by the President.

While these different authorities are closely inter-related, each per forms independently its own func tions, in its own sphere, under the Constitution, acting as a check on the other two, thus giving an effective system of "checks and balances", which is one of the most admirable features of our Constitution.

The basic purpose of this separation of powers of government is to safeguard the people against too much power being in the hands of any one man or group of men.

HOW THE DIFFERENT BRANCHES CHECK ONE ANOTHER EXECUTIVE BRANCH 

The Executive branch checks the 

Judicial one through the President's power of pardon and authority to appoint the judges of the courts. 

It checks the Legislative branch through the power of the President to veto the acts of Congress. 

JUDICIAL BRANCH 

The Judicial branch checks both the Executive and Legislative ones through its power to pass upon the constitutionality of their acts, thus keeping them within the bounds of the Constitution.

LEGISLATIVE BRANCH 

The Legislative branch checks the 

Executive and the Judicial branches through its control over appropriations of money to support their activities; also, through the power of the House of Representatives to impeach and of the Senate to try the impeachment of any civil officer. 

Furthermore, the appointment by 

President of public officers, includ ing judges, must be confirmed, and all treaties which he negotiates must be ratified, by the Senate.

RESULT 

This system of checks and balances, which is one of the outstanding features of the Constitution, has worked admirably. It has been a most effective preventive against any one branch of the Government usurping power at the expense of either or both of the other two. 

It has given us "a government of laws," and not "a government of The Constitution of the United States is the beacon light of all who love freedom.

THE SUPREME COURT The Supreme Court of the United States, created by Section 1, Article III of the Constitution (see page 

31), is rightly called "The balance wheel of the Constitution"; "The high guardian of the Constitution itself"; "The pillar and supporter of the National power and supremacy."

Of the Supreme Court, DANIEL WEBSTER, said: 

"The Constitution without it would be no constitution, the 

Government, no government."

Certain provisions of the Con stitution are intended to make the 

Chief Justice of the United States, the Associate Justices and the 

Judges of the lower Federal Courts, all of whom are appointed for life, independent of Congress, the Presi dent, and the voters, so that in all their decisions they may be fearless and impartial.

The Supreme Court is the final authority upon the constitutionality of all laws passed by Congress and by State legislatures as well as upon the acts of all executive officers. 

Any American citizen, however humble, who feels that some law has been passed or some act committed which is in violation of his rights under the Constitution, may have the matter decided by a Federal

Court, and if the decision is unfavorable to him, he may appeal to the Supreme Court of the United 

States, whose decision will be final.

The independence of the Federal 

Judiciary is one of the outstanding features of the American Govern ment. It inspires the people with the utmost confidence in the vigor ous and fearless enforcement of our 

Federal laws.

HUMAN AND PROPERTY RIGHTS 

The individual rights guaranteed by the Constitution are of two kinds: Human rights and property rights. (See page 9.)

We have of late been hearing a great deal about human and property rights, and two opposite schools of thought have arisen regarding their relative value and importance. 

As the author sees it, they are so interwoven and interdependent that it is impossible to determine which are the more important. In his judgment, they are of equal value, the same as oxygen and hydrogen are of equal value in the composi tion of water, which, as we all know, consists of two parts of hydrogen and one of oxygen.

If we want water, we must have both hydrogen and oxygen. So, likewise, if we are to realize the ideal of "Life, Liberty and the Pur suit of Happiness" embodied in the

AMENDING TH 

(See, "Amendments to the Constitution," page 32)

OUR forefathers who framed the Constitution foresaw that in order to meet the future needs of a great and growing nation, changes would have to be made from time to time, and they therefore made proper provision for this in Article V (page 31), which provides two procedures for amending the Constitution. 

1. Proposition originating with Congress. 

1. Congress, by two-thirds vote of both houses, may propose amendments to the State Legislatures to be ratified either by the legislatures, themselves, or by conventions to be called by the legislatures, according to the method of ratification designated by Congress. 

2. Upon ratification by three-fourths of the legislatures or the conventions, the amendment shall be declared in force and become a part of the Constitution. 

II. Proposition originating with State legislatures. 

1. On application of two-thirds of the State legislatures, Congress shall call a convention for proposing amendments. 

2. Upon ratification by three-fourths of the legislatures or conventions called by the legislatures, the proposed amendment shall become a part of the Constitution. 

AMENDMENTS TO BE MADE IN PRESCRIBED WAY 

Because of this power of amendment the Constitution is a perfectly flexible instrument which, in accordance with the will of the people as expressed through their chosen representatives, can always be modified, in a regular and prescribed way, to meet any requirements made necessary by new conditions-social, political, economic or other. 

GREATEST CAUTION-PROFOUND DELIBERATION 

However, when the following facts are considered reason as well as self-interest tells us that the Constitution should be amended, especially as regards human and property rights, only with the greatest care, after profound consideration and serious deliberation: 

1. Guards liberties of 125,000,000 people. One of the greatest heritages that a people can possess, the Constitution is a document that guards the personal liberties of 125,000,000 human beings. Playing a vitally important part in our daily lives in our happiness and contentment-the human and property rights which it guarantees every man, women and child, are the greatest treasure we possess. 

2. Cost of our Constitutional rights. Mankind has not always had the rights which are guaranteed American citizens by the Constitution. It has taken hundreds of years of strife and struggle, suffering and sacrifice, involving many wars, at tremendous cost of blood and treasure, to get and hold these rights. 

3. Impairment of Constitution a calamity. Any amendment which might in any way destroy or impair any of the rights guaranteed us by the Constitu tion would, indeed, be a calamity for which not only we of today would pay dearly, but posterity would also be penalized. 

4. Caution Care—Deliberation. Common sense, and our welfare and well-being tell us to go slowly-very slowly; cautiously-very cautiously; deliberately-very deliberately, in changing a document, which, while not per fect, has as a whole, worked so remarkably well. (See, "Proof of Pudding

RIGHT TO AMEND UNQUESTIONED 

That the American people have a right to amend their Constitution any way and to any extent they see fit, and that at times amendments are necessary and should be made, no one can question. In fact, the provision in the Constitution for amendments contemplates that, when necessary, changes shall be made to meet changed conditions. 

But the spirit of the Constitution—its fundamental conceptions-contemplate that amendments shall be made only when necessary, and most cautiously, deliberately and wisely, only after serious, thoughtful and intelligent consideration.

And sanity as well as the happiness and well-being of the American people demand that in our deliberations we shall not allow ourselves to be swayed by the passions of the hour; nor by the spell-binding oratory of politicians; nor the sophistry of demagogues; nor should we allow ourselves to be controlled by temporary fear, apprehension, or tribulation due to transient economic depression or any other abnormal condition.

Writ of Habeas Corpus-Bills of Attainder-Ex Post Facto Laws SECTION 9. The privilege of the writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when, in cases of rebellion or invasion, the public safety may require it. 

The purpose of the "Writ of habeas corpus" is to prevent wrongful imprisonmen, protect citizens against the exercise of unwarranted authority and help to provide speedy trial of the accused. "Writ" means "order"; "habeas" is the Latin "to have"; "corpus" means "body." "Writ of habeas corpus" there fore means "An order to have (to produce) the body"—that is, to produce the body in court.

Any person who is imprisoned and feels that he is being illegally deprived of his freedom, may apply to the proper court of law for a writ of habeas corpus, which will order the jailer or sheriff in whose custody he is, to produce (have) his body in the presence of the court, who will determine whether the accused is being illegally deprived of his freedom-in other words, whether his personal rights under the Constitution are being violated. If the Court finds that there is no right to hold the prisoner, he is set free. 

No bill of attainder, or ex post facto law shall be passed.

It used to be that any person sen tenced to death, or convicted of treason or felony, was considered so "tainted" that not only did he forfeit all prop erty and civil rights, but his progeny, too, forfeited all right of inheritance.

The Constitution made this impossible in America. "Ex-post facto" means "after the fact,' after accomplishment." An expost facto law is, therefore, one that is retroactive-in other words, that would apply to acts committed before the enactment of the law. The enactment of such a law is prohibited only where crime is concerned.

AMENDMENTS TO THE CONSTITUTION 

(All provisions regarding human and property rights are in bold face type and underscored) (See, "Amending the Constitution," pages 18-19) 

Since the first ten amendments, which may almost be called a part of the original Constitution, were adopted in 1791-almost a century and a half ago -the Constitution has been amended only eleven (11) times-an average of once every thirteen years. 

And none of these eleven amend ments affected any basic principle of the Constitution, or modified in any way the Constitution's fundamental conception of the relationship of government to freedom and human happiness. Could there be better evidence of the adequacy, permanency soundness of the doctrines of government embodied in the Constitution?

Could anything show more eloquently and emphatically what a wonderful heritage the American people possess in this Great Document? 

Many Constitutions of governments that came into being after the adoption of the Constitution of the United States have become absolete or been discarded. 

However, the American Constitution, because of its power of amendment which makes it a perfectly flexible instrument, is just as live and adequate today as it was at the time of its adoption. 

"BILL OF RIGHTS" The first ten amendments to the Con stitution, adopted December 15, 1791, and known as the "Bill of Rights,' contain nearly all the guarantees of personal liberties embodied in the Constitution.

So jealous were our forefathers of the rights of American citizens and so sure did they want to be that none of them would be invaded that, in order to "make sure doubly sure," soon after the adoption of the Constitution ten amend ments safeguarding these rights were submitted to the people and adopted as promptly as possible. They were intended to set at ease the minds of citizens who knew of the ageold sins of government. They were a kind of "safety first" precaution in connection with the new government which the Constitution had created.

An established religion is one that is made the religion of the State and is supported by taxation. 

To choose one's own religion and to practice that religion according to one's conscience is probably man's most cher ished right.

The right of freedom of speech and of the press enables a person to say, write, or print anything he pleases, provided it is not libelous or slanderous, or subversive of public morals. A citizen may criticize the Government as freely as he chooses, if his criticism is not made for the purpose of inviting insurrection or treason. The principle on which is based freedom of speech and of the press was splendidly expressed by the illustrious 

Voltaire: “I wholly disapprove of what you say, but will defend to the death your right to say it.'

No assembly can be prevented by the authorities if there is reasonable assur ance that the meeting will be peaceable.

However, the Constitution does not sanc tion disorderly assemblies, those having an unlawful purpose, or those that violate the rights of others.

The right which the Constitution guarantees to petition the Government for a redress of grievances is one that is highly prized by all free people. Any one may petition the Government at Washington or the government of any State.

ARTICLE II Right of People to Bear Arms not to be Infringed

A well regulated militia being necessary to the security of a free state, the right of the people to keep and bear arms shall not be infringed by law. 

The right to bear arms prevents an arbitrary government from seizing all weapons in a certain State or district in order to render the people defenseless. 

However, this provision of the Constitu tion does not mean that a State can not regulate the sale, display and and carry. ing of firearms.

ARTICLE III Quartering of Troops 

No soldier shall, in time of peace, be quartered in any house, without the consent of the owner; nor in time of war, but in a manner to be prescribed by law. "Quartering" means the giving of food and lodging. The quartering of soldiers in homes was one of the causes of the Revolutionary War. 

ARTICLE IV 

Persons and Houses to be Secure from Unreasonable Searches and Seizures

The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated; and no warrants shall issue but upon probable cause, supported by oath or affirmations, and particularly describing the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. 

This provision guarantees the people security in their persons, houses, papers and effects against unreasonable searches and seizures, which was one of the causes of the Revolutionary War. This guarantee confirms the principle that 'every man's house is his castle," not to be entered under ordinary circumstances without his consent. Before the War of the Revolution it was customary for the British authorities to issue. "Writs of Assistance" to aid their officers in searching for goods alleged to have been smuggled. Such writs could be issued to allow the search of all the houses on a certain street or district, and, naturally, this led to all kinds of abuse. Now no one can be searched nor property seized unless a court has first issued a warrant, based on probable cause, sup ported by oath or affirmation, and describing specifically the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized.

By speedy trial is meant trial without unreasonable delay. Public trial is for the benefit of the accused, so that the public, especially those who may have an interest in the case, may see that the accused is given a fair and legal trial.
