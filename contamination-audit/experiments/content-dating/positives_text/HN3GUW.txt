According to the ancient biblical proverb, the claim that bread eaten in secret is sweet, is only the harlot's beguiling sales talk for fools. This throbbing love story poses a modern case of a secret love that for a time brought a rare happiness. The two lovers find in each other a sweetness to compensate for the wretchedness of their unhappy married lives. For a few months in a lonely seaside cabin they enjoy an interlude of untroubled love. Then the storm of an outraged wife breaks upon them. 

The man, Palmerston, is a great criminal lawyer with a firm sense of honor. The girl,

Nana, is his secretary. Palmerston's wife is a sour, sanctified social climber. She is too moral to give him love and not moral enough to give him justice. Their marriage. is an empty pretense. Nana's husband is a miserable consumptive weakling. She cares for him as she would for a sick child, generously and without complaint, even though she is faithless to him. Palmerston would not pursue his love furtively but fought to give Nana the social privileges and advantages that his love entitled her to. 

Can this adulterous love endure through intense suffering when Nana is framed as a prostitute and railroaded to jail? Can it find a way out when an enraged woman uses the vilest means to smash it? Are all such clandestine affairs bound to run amok, and must the lovers pay for secret happiness with misery? The author shows these problems at their maximum intensity in the lives of two strong and charming characters. 222

THIS is the story of a wife who was too moral to give her husband love—and not moral enough to give him justice.

The other woman inevitably entered his life. He could have eaten his bread in secret, as so many husbands do, but he made a desperate fight to give the other woman the social privileges and advantages that his love entitled her to. 

In the struggle between the wife and the sweetheart, it is the "moral" woman who resorts to baseness, and the "immoral" one who rises to nobility. The frameup by detectives, the criminal proceedings, the trial, are powerfully done and afford a startling exposé of how the processes of law can be worked into devious channels. "Bread Eaten In Secret" is the most vigorous of Moroso's novels, and brings into full play his knowledge of police methods, and his remarkable narrative gifts.

NANA became the beloved of Howard Palmer ston on her twenty-fourth birthday anniversary.

The many clerks, stenographers, file girls, investigators and office boys of the law firm of Palmerston, Wallace and Snowden had chipped in and had presented her with a huge bouquet of roses and a desk set. They all worshipped Nana. They were proud of her ability and her beauty and were dependent on her to a great extent, for she was the liaison between them and the silent thoughtful tranquil man who was the head of the firm. "What's all this?" Mr. Palmerston asked, as he came in upon the presentation. "Nan's birthday!" shouted the merry group about her. "Ah!" His face, dark and generally cast with shadows, broke into one of his rare smiles. "And you didn't invite me to share in the party?" he asked. 

Nana Wilkinson's smooth cheeks rivalled the pink buds on her desk and delighted appreciation danced in her soft brown eyes. Her well-rounded bosom

rose and fell under its filmy covering of ivory silk; her shapely hands sought her temples and played nervously with the crisp black curls, moist and suddenly enticing to the handsome man of law standing before her. I suppose we should all have a holiday. But how can we?" "The Stillwell case at ten o'clock, Part I. Supreme

Court," she briskly reminded him, tapping his brief case lying on her desk. "It's all here." His own features were not unlike these, thought having brought deep creases from the spandrils of the nose to the chin, his blue eyes alive with intelligence, the nose sharp, the smooth lips somewhat thin, suggesting asceticism.

II 

FOR three days the twelve men in the jury box had simmered in the poorly ventilated, evil-smelling court room, the majority of them half asleep, especially in the afternoon, for they invariably ate heartily during the noon recess at the expense of the county. Each depended on some other to refresh his memory when the time came to deliberate on the verdict, many of them being mentally incapable of strict attention because of thoughts of their own affairs. Their little shops, their little jobs, their complaining wives, their sick children called to them constantly. Compared with the least of their responsibilities at home or at work, the responsibility of deciding whether William Stillwell should live or die was secondary. Not only that but a bore by the time the fourth and last day of the trial began.

The assistant district attorney representing the People was young and dull but noisy, a politician of limited claptrap. The jurors had hoped that the famous Mr. Palmerston would reward them with a display of his brilliance, with sallies of wit, with an 16

occasional burst of oratory. But Mr. Palmerston's plan of conduct for the defense was to have the prosecuting officer lose the sympathy of the jurors. 

Adroitly he allowed and aided the unskilled prosecutor to appear as a hound of the law tearing at the flesh of the defendant, eager to bay in triumph over his dead body, his reward political advancement.

Stillwell, a young man, had defaulted as cashier of a commercial firm and had served two years in Sing Sing. He had stolen for his old mother, his wife and baby. On his release he found that his child was in a public institution and his wife living with a man he had believed his friend, the one who had promised to help her while he served his sentence. 

His mother, neglected, had died.

The double unfaithfulness had brought him to murder.

Palmerston produced as a witness a psychiatrist friend who went through the rigmarole of brain storm insanity as a matter of charity and did it as thoroughly as if he were being paid five thousand dollars a day. There had been the usual minor traces of neuroses in the family of the defendant. The case was finished at the morning session of the fourth day. A reasonable doubt as to the mental responsi- 17

bility of the defendant at the time of the slaying was all that was necessary to bring him acquittal. During the recess the assistant district attorney sought Palmerston with the suggestion that if he would withdraw the plea of not guilty and enter a plea of guilty to murder in the second degree he would bring about a mistrial and his client's life would be safe. "I will think it over during lunch." Palmerston and Nana found a secluded table in the best of the downtown restaurants, where special attention is paid to privacy for such couples from the business sanctums of the successful. "Well?" she asked. "I will get him free." He laughed. It was the first time she had heard him laugh in that way, a happy sound, free of cynicism or care. "Are you sure?" "I can't fail, Nana. I have you with me and if 

I but touch you casually as I make my argument my eloquence will stir the soul of the dullest man in the jury box, make him love passionately once more, as this poor fool Stillwell did, and as I do now, and make him believe beyond hesitation that his act was justifiable. There may be only the faintest doubt 18

raised in their minds by the psychiatric evidence but if I can drive into the hearts of these men the thrill of love again, the echo of the voice of vanished youth, the remembrance of long ago passion, the feel of clinging lips and the beat of heart against heart, they will, in sympathy for this poor fellow, grasp at this straw of temporary insanity and convince themselves that it is a raft of heavy lumber." 

Nana buried a flaming face in her hands. A lover, a mighty lover, her lover, was speaking. Nothing else mattered. Three years of matrimonial fidelity in a tiny flat, three years of drudgery before she became Palmerston's secretary, dropped from her like hard-worn heavy garments, stained, ravelled. 

The three pleasant and profitable years working with him remained and now there was beginning a heaven on earth, its first day wonderful with his kisses, the feel of his fine strong hands, the magnificent enthusiasm that had come upon him so swiftly because of their troth. "Oh, Nana," he sighed as he pressed her hand across the table, "you have brought life back to me. 

I was dead, a piece of machinery. I had forgotten all about poetry and music and the entrancing beauty of the sky by night and by day. Now I want to sit 19

and listen to a stringed orchestra in a garden and, forgetting everything of the past, regain the old treasures that only love can give."

Again and again their waiter came to the table for their order but was ignored. He was one of the most discreet and discerning of the staff of the Ann Street place, known and trusted by bankers, brokers, manufacturers, lawyers devoted to their office wives. He had seen many times before this final stage of business romance, two flushed faces, hands under the table, the torrent of quietly spoken words, toe touching toe beneath the damask, the eyes of men approaching elderliness filled with the divine light. "The jurors are going back to court, Mr. Palmerston," their waiter whispered, "and you haven't had as much as a cup of coffee." "Thank you, Dan." Palmerston tipped splendidly. "We'll come back later perhaps." 

William Stillwell, a shabby, pale specimen of clerical life, timid, humble, colorless, had told his own story on the stand, as commonplace a narrative as ever a jury hearkened to. He had started as office boy, had gone to night school, had advanced with his firm, had fallen in love with a girl clerk, had married her. He had his mother to support in addi- 20

tion to her. His nose was at the grindstone. His mother was an invalid. The doctors took all his little savings and earnings. He was tempted. He had stolen for the two women he loved, had never spent a penny upon himself, had lined his leaky shoes with pasteboard even when there was as much as a hundred dollars of his firm's money in his pocket. He had served his time to come out and find his mother dead and his wife living with the man he thought his friend. The familiar old excuse of everything going black followed. The man who had betrayed him lay dead.

But that was not the way that Palmerston gave the story in his argument. Into the drab life of this defendant had come love, timidly but surely, the one recompense for living and working, the one blessing in life, the only thing that could wipe away the curse of birth in poverty and take the sting from death. 

He had sacrificed himself to retain it, had gone to prison for it. He had hoped to find it awaiting him on his release. With it he could have started again the unequal fight of the poorly equipped warrior and come up again after every stroke of foul fortune, come up with a smile. But with this only thing worth while in human existence stolen from him, leaving 21

his very soul empty, he was sent crashing to the earth, the blow being more terrible, more cruel than if this man who had posed as his friend had used a bar of iron, bringing it down upon his head time and time again.

The judge, who had hoped for a little nap in his silken gown as he scribbled curlicues on a sheet of paper before him in pretense of making notes, half closed his eyes and listened to the old, old song, the canticle of canticles. The jurors forgot their domestic and business worries. Palmerston was too fine a lawyer to let a single phrase escape him that might bring objection to and therefore an interruption of his panegyric of the little god of the courtship days of the two city strugglers. Nana managed to press her knee against the calf of his leg and the contact seemed to cause his whole slender body to tremble with passion for the cause he was defending so sincerely. 

William Stillwell, seated on the other side of his advocate, had not the power of evocation to tell the twelve men what that love for one woman had meant to him and what its loss had meant to him. 

If he had tried to tell what was in his heart he would have said, in all probability, that from the first night 22

they had slept together he had felt a part of his woman and that she had become a part of him, that they had flowed into each other, becoming one person, and that in being betrayed he had been torn asunder. Palmerston told it all for him, the physical part of the one experience of man that makes him. stranger to despair, the mental part of it, the psychical part of it.

To Nana the argument was the pleading of her lover, not to the court but to her. It was a continuation of the morning troth in the office, the pleading during the lunch hour.

The jury was briefly charged and sent to its deliberation.

Nana was once more on the job, getting the chief clerk at the office on the telephone. The Stillwell case was in the hands of the jury. Have Mr. Palmerston's valet pack his things for a short trip out of the city and his chauffeur bring his runabout to the court building.

III 

IT T was when she was passing through the first years of office work and saw promises of advancement ahead that Nana and James Wilkinson were married. They had courted like two sparrows, in the niches of downtown doorways, a short tunnel under old Brooklyn Bridge at William Street, dark subway station corners; holding hands, squeezing close together with eyes furtively ranging for the cops or the killjoys, kissing, kneading each other's bodies almost violently in attempted palliation of fierce desire, hall-bedroom lovers without a trysting place, victims of increasing anguish, especially after the cold of winter had passed and in the parks the bulbs began to break through the sod to blossom, accept the kiss of sun and rain, and propagate. 

To give up her job and get married on Jim's wages-twenty-five dollars a week as a junior bank clerk, his father, the last of his family, senile and palsied with age, dependent upon him-was out of the question. Her wages had started with eighteen, risen to twenty and at the time when they thought 25

they were madly in love had reached twenty-five. 

It put Jim at a disadvantage. But they wanted each other so during that lovely springtime that Jim was in danger of losing his job from inattention and 

Nana went to work each morning a wreck from restless nights.

That summer they managed to get their vacations together, both tired as dogs, worn to the bone and the heart threads. They couldn't afford any of the attractive resorts. Ten or twelve dollars a week each was their limit. "If Pop had only been a Mason or Odd Fellow or something," he complained, "there would have been a home for him during the last years. But, Nana, all his life was spent in the same bank that employs me and God knows a married teller or assistant teller has nothing over after he pays his rent." 

They found a boarding place down Barnegat way -out on the Island, the long white strand where the sea beats up the shore royally; Scandinavian fisherfolk, clumps of myrtle, a few ancient and gnarled cedars. At night the dunes were soft and warm and when the wind blew in from the sea, heavy with salt that made the flesh tingle pleasantly, bringing the sweet song of breaking waves upon the many hard 26

reefs, the stars crowding the inverted bowl and no paths to watch for intruders, the lovesick sparrows from lower Broadway and Nassau street were, at last, as free as the gulls mating near them.

The landlady, her man a fisherman, smiled upon them and knew their secret so well that she unlocked and left open the door between their rooms. Nana asked her, when they were together in the kitchen one day, if she looked that sort of girl. "What sort of girl?" A pair of fine blue Swedish eyes looked deep into Nana's brown eyes. "A fast girl." "Fast?" The kitchen rang with her merriment. "You're slow, my dear. But I didn't mean to offend you, child. I understand. So long as it isn't for money it's all right with me. This is a respectable house you're in and Oscar and me are Godfearing people. 

We had to do the same thing. Maybe some day you'll have money enough to get married."

Nana's mother, a milliner, had lived long enough to give her two years in high school and a year in business college, to see her begin to earn her own living. The girl knew little of her father, having no memory of him whatever. Her mother said that he was an Englishman of good family, outcast because 27

of his marriage to an attic trimmer of bonnets in Paris. He had brought her to New York where Nana was born, got a job as a travelling salesman and was killed in a railroad accident. The steadiness of the

English strain, mingled with the industry of the French and a strong trace of Gallic charm and piquancy, made for her advancement as a stenographer. 

With the end of the Barnegat honeymoon, Nana and Jim, loath to retire to their separate city cells, both uneasy over the possibility of fruition of their mating, and both ignorant of preventive measures, decided to marry. Hardly more than boy and girl, it seemed to them that it would be impossible to have enough of each other. They would pool their resources, share the work of a little flat, do their duty by Jim's miserable and complaining old father, and trust to God to take care of the uncertain future. 

There was no seduction, no tears, no reproaches, no feeling of guilt in their surrender. But there might be a baby to have registered and bastard was not a pretty word. Gamely and decently they went to City Hall during lunch hour and Nana and Jim swore in the name of God and the State to love, 28

honor and obey until death came to their succor should unhappiness follow their pledge.

There was no baby. The wedding ring was put away. Men at work with women do not like to be reminded of matrimony. 

Bravely Nana fought the fight, doing frequently the ugliest tasks of a trained nurse for the old man after her work in the law office. There was little time for love. The doctor's bills ate up their money, the rent kept rising, the advance in prices of food and clothing never paused and half promises of an increase in pay at the bank were never kept. Jim's place could be filled at a moment's notice from thousands of young men equipped with college education, with handsome regular features, pleasing personalities, neat clothes and limited brains and funds. 

But Nana had brains and not only brains but limitless capacity for work, besides beauty and charm. The opportunity made for her by Palmerston and the increase in salary lifted her from the down-pulling demands of the burden she had married. But it compelled Jim to give all his home time to his father and the kitchen, even to mending his clothes. When 

Nana came home after classes at Columbia it was frequently midnight. Jim had to sleep in the one 29

sizable bedroom with the invalid. The few hours of rest she could command were spent on a loungebed in the sitting room.

Chapters of her own story flashed through her mind as Palmerston drove the machine farther and farther away from the monoxid-laden air of the city, nearer and nearer the sea, symbol of heaven and earth at their sweetest to those who have first loved close to its lacey edge. The night wind brought them the perfume of the pines as they passed through Lakewood with its lighted shop windows and many hotels. He made her lay her head upon his shoulder. 

The Lincoln highway was as smooth as the floor of a ball room. When she awakened he was kissing her and lifting her from the car and when he threw back his head with a laugh of delight she seemed to feel the radiance of the countless stars above his windtossed black and silver hair, so clear and sweet was the night. 

What was it that made her heart suddenly leap with pain that was half pleasure and bring to her a feeling of complete surrender? She looked about and listened as he put her down at the edge of a little cedar grove. It was the music of the breakers, the pleasant sting of salt upon her cheeks, the damp- 30

ness in her hair, the white mounds looming through the shadows of scrub myrtle, the quick memory of that first night three years before.

In a few moments the bungalow they entered was snugly aglow with the soft light from a wroughtiron oil lamp, showing shelves of books evidently not for work, an open fireplace, comfortable chairs, fishing rods, nets and creels, a man's refuge, open doors giving glimpses of kitchen and bedrooms, all finished in fragrant cedar.

It was not too late to turn back, for Palmerston was, she knew, the soul of honor. But she had so quickly accepted him for the night, the entire night, she had so completely succumbed to him in the office, his triumph in court had so thoroughly won her heart and mind that the words which Conscience was trying to force to her lips would not shape. Nor did she have opportunity to plead for release, for all the poetry of his being, long submerged and all but driven out by routine of domestic and professional life, broke upon her in a wild passionate rush, the flight of a soul from its suddenly opened cage of convention.

This was not the same thing as the office affairs she knew so well after six years of downtown New 31

York as stenographer and secretary, no aftermath of 

Ann Street feasting and drinking, a Broadway show, gifts of money and jewels, tearing of clothes in taxis and the old lie at a hotel of baggage coming later from station or pier. Perhaps it had been better for 

Nana had it been merely the casual "good time," to be forgotten in a day, what she gave to be measured by what she received in excitement and perhaps added salary or a stock market investment. 32

IV 

IN an important case before the Supreme Court of the United States it had been necessary for Nana to spend a night in Washington and on another occasion she was away for two nights in the same city, her employer appearing before a Congressional committee as legal representative of a great industrial combine. 

The excuse for the Chicago trip, therefore, caused Jim no worry and, in fact, he met her indefinite absence with that feeling of relief a business woman's husband experiences. For a little while he would be the master of the house; the furniture, all of which she had bought, was his; the janitor would take orders from him. He liked canned salmon, which 

Nana detested. He could live on canned salmon. His dog, the one thing upon which he lavished all the love his frail body could hold, a wheezy, waddling Boston bull terrier, gray at the snout with the years, worn places on his screw tail, could have the one easy chair with the cushion and if he should wet on the floor there would be no outcry. And as long as there 33

was a piece of silver in the bowl in the kitchen cupboard his old man could have what he wanted. "All right, Pop! On the fire! Coming up!" Probably sugar brought some internal heat to his father's all but dead vascular system for, when exacting Nana was away, he pleaded like a child for cream puffs.

The soft filling would trickle down his bristled jaws, down his stringy throat to the bedclothes. At times some of it, if his palsied hands were not guided carefully, would stick in his eyes. He would whimper for help.

For three years Jim had been telling Nana that soon he would be gone. If sweets brought some little comfort to his last days he should have them.

It was eight o'clock when Nana called up, and he had finished his supper, had fed his father and the dog and was preparing to wash the dishes. When she was not home by seven it was understood that she would eat at a restaurant. Pete, in his dog way, realized that a holiday was in order when his master hung up the telephone; he lumbered to his hide-out under the kitchen table to fetch his collar to the beloved boss.

The flat was the top floor of a faded residence facing little Mount Morris Park, just south of 34

Harlem's Great White Way, One hundred and twenty-fifth Street, at the ragged end of Fifth

Avenue with its tenements of Negroes, Spaniards and poor Russian Jews. So near to this motley with its jabber of Iberian and Slavic tongues, mixed with the soft voices of the colored natives and their laughter,

Mount Morris Park still has a certain dignity in its houses of brick and brownstone, not yet entirely crowded out by apartment buildings. The window signs, "Vacancies," meant furnished rooms and light housekeeping apartments, tired landladies and hard working men and women occupants, but the architecture and the trees and the remoteness from the underground and overhead roar of subway and elevated traffic and the absence of clanging trolleys made it a pleasant corner to live in.

The park, a mass of high-jutting rock, capped by a wooden observatory and flagpole, its alternate patches of grass and bald earth railed in and guarded by large signs warning the people to "Keep Off," when the twilight deepened, was the meeting place of dog-husbands, each dutifully taking out the family pet for its airing while wife rested and renewed the make-up for the evening movie. Old mothers and fathers, waiting for the scythe, would 35

sit silently on the benches, and occasionally, late at night, a man or woman, sometimes a child, would seek the shadows of clumps of weeds or neglected ornamental bushes and bury a pet that had died, digging small graves with a table knife; a beloved dog, kittens, goldfish, an Easter bunny that could not survive.

Jim and Pete trudged slowly up the stone paths to the top of the Mount, seeking to peer over the high wall of their prison. Cops and Tammany park attendants would not climb that high on a warm night, the breezes of speakeasy electric fans being more easily and comfortably attained. ALMERSTON was not the sort of man to use his office or his home as a place for assignation. 

Although his dignity might have been at times a bit oppressive to the rather cynical Wallace or a little annoying to the ever jovial Snowden, the head of the firm was far above the petty vanity of posing. He was merely a left-over of the day and generation of gentility. His partners, because of their lack of years, had tasted the so-called romance of a war that had led a world to licentiousness. He had been of service to his country-in the State department in Washington, helping a man much of his type, Mr. Wilson. 

Not that Palmerston wanted to appear superior in his personal conduct nor was it that he lacked flexibility. His decency was so deeply ingrained that the tearing down of the old inhibitions in the infancy of a new day for mankind left him somewhat alone.

Without children, who would have taken the place of the happiness of his own childhood and youth; with, therefore, a vain love for his wife, which had grown formal and then cold and meaningless because 75

of its purposelessness, he was, until Nana came, an empty-hearted man. One could imagine a spring in the woods, a spring upon which a circle of fronds depended for their thrill of life, gradually drying up and ceasing its flow, whereupon the once green wreath at its border shrivelled and was almost at the moment of death when once again the water began to pour. That was Palmerston. When the magic of this half-French girl made his heart warm again, the charm and perfume of her youth slowly, through three years, encompassing him, the dam of convention that had been built in generations back of him broke and he became human again, young again, a poet again and, without fee, a successful supplicant for the life of a penniless man who had loved and hated. 

The enthusiasms of his younger partners had never stirred him. They had experienced, of course, something that he had not, the realization of the soldier that the brevity of life can be measured only between the moment of the shell's explosion and the striking of the shrapnel and that, away from the field of battle, a moment of passion may become an eternity of delight. In Washington, he could, and did, from his desk, behold war as a senseless and cruel 76

thing. For him, as he was situated, there could be no recompensing thrill, no song in billets, no casting away of empty bottles, no thewing with wantons in village stables or attics, no pinning on of medals made of the same stuff that disembowelled youth. In his New York office, when it was all over, he had placed in passepartout under his portrait of Voltaire these few lines of the philosopher's comment on war: "Twenty years are required to bring man from the state of a plant, in which he exists in the womb of his mother, and from the state of an animal, which is his condition in infancy, to a state in which the maturity of reason begins to make itself felt. Thirty centuries are necessary in which to discover even a little of his structure. An eternity would be required to know anything of his soul. But one moment suffices in which to kill him.” 

Wallace had read this aloud to Snowden one day in the presence of Palmerston. He had been in artillery, Snowden in aviation. "What's the answer, Snowden?" he asked. Never had he dreamed of having such a summer as this, never had he thought it possible for him to feel so deeply for any woman as he felt for Nana, for he had gone through life utterly without romance and without taste of passion, striding dignifiedly through the years upon the solid ground of respectability, his intellectual achievements serving to cover the losses of the heart.

It had just so happened. It was Fate, Destiny, at play with them, drawing them closer and closer together during three years, balancing them mentally in that time and finally, now, physically.

A woman nearer his age that he could have loved would not have afforded him the quiet amusement and the joy that Nana afforded him. THE end of the winter had come when the district attorney's investigators, aided by those of Palmerston, Wallace and Snowden, secured the final bit of evidence that would warrant indictments. 

The messenger who delivered the decoy note to Nana was found and furnished a description of Mockerle, who was promptly arrested. The telephone records of the apartment house were searched and showed the second call to the Nevada ranch.

The operator, under questioning, broke down and admitted taking a bribe to permit Mockerle to listen in on the first call, identifying him positively. Witnesses identified him as sitting at table in the cabaret with the magistrate who sent Nana to prison and other witnesses who saw Mockerle in the company of the forger known to the underworld as "Educated Harry."

The register of the Queen's Arms showed Nana's handwriting and the entry "Mrs. Alfred Smythe," under which she was convicted. Mrs. Palmerston's letter to Nana under that name forged the link that 247

connected her with Mockerle, the magistrate, the forger. 

Mockerle provided bail and was released. He could have given a hundred thousand dollars for bond under the circumstances. Another magistrate was in peril. On his disappearance depended the fate of that corrupt judge's backers, the politicians who sold him his seat and also the fate of the wealthy and unscrupulous law firm entangled in the conspiracy.

Myra Palmerston suddenly found herself facing disgrace and prison. With the haste of frightened rabbits before a brush fire her lawyers deserted her. 

Mockerle was gone, his pockets well lined. If there had been any forgery Mrs. Palmerston had provided the means for it and had directed the conspiracy herself.

The rich woman was called to the office of the district attorney. The shadow of the law was heavy upon her. Her social prestige, her church standing, her money, her boast of heraldry, availed her nothing. She sat a long time in an outer office of the county's criminal prosecutor in the dingy old Criminal Courts building on Centre Street, tied to the Tombs by the Bridge of Sighs. There were many 248

Anxious, fearful, trembling many of them were.

It was necessary for Mrs. Palmerston to be questioned at the same time that Nana was questioned -the one who wrote the cruel note and the one who had received it-neither with the comforting presence of counsel, for as yet no charge had been entered and the case was not yet in the hands of the grand jury for indictment.
