Bills and Notes. 

COMMERCIAL PAPER. 

This subject, affectionately (?) known amongst

Law Students as Bolts and Nuts than which hardware, it is no more digestible, embraces a description of those media used in place of money by business men in their trade with one another. 

These indicia of dough are called promissory notes, bills of exchange (inland and foreign), drafts, checks, banker's bills, trade acceptances, etc., but due-bills, commonly known as IOU's, don't qualify since they merely constitute representations of temporary, but definite, insolvency. 

The idea seems to be that money is too valuable and too scarce to be risked in ordinary business transactions and so the party of the first part gives the party of the second part a slip of paper on which is written a memorandum that someone will pay or is directed to pay someone else a certain sum of money at a certain time and place. 

This memorandum is sometimes passed around from one party to another for awhile, and whoever holds it last is entitled to cash it for the stated number of fish or quantity of lettuce according to its terms. Sometimes, however, all the holder gets is the Horse Laff with the observation that it's just paper and he (the holder) should know what he can do with it. And so he takes it to a lawyer, and our little memo becomes exhibit "A" forever after.

Character of Instrument. 

Commercial paper, meaning what the boys use instead of cash (see above), was a device adopted 684711

long ago by merchants and bankers to transfer credits between distant points, and is distinguished from other species of written obligations by its negotiability, by which is meant the fact that it is transferable from hand to hand and that a person taking such an instrument in good faith acquires rights of collection against parties whose names appear theron and is not affected by arguments amongst these parties.

Although originally the principal instruments of negotiability were called promissory notes and bills of exchange, today the check has become the almost universal means of transfering credit, and is usually considered a bill of exchange. In contradistinction, the promissory note is today the principal means of obtaining credit. 

In all this confusion, though, the quality and respectability of commercial paper is still determined by just one thing-namely, the reception that is accorded the instrument, whatever its name, on Due Day. If the holder thereof knocks on the door of the party named in the instrument as the one who is to pay off (be he maker, drawee or indorser), but is given the brush-off with the explanation that "Annie doesn't live here anymore", the instrument is N. G. and the holder has been robbed. On the other hand, if the cash is laid on the line on Due Day, then it really doesn't matter that what one person would call a draft, another would call a check, since the folding money has the same feel in either case. 

To recapitulate, then, all commercial paper is divided into two classes, promissory notes and bills of exchange.

A promissory note is a written promise to pay so much wampum to the payee at a stated time. and place. It's what you sign when you buy a car or a refrigerator or a diamond ring on the instalment plan.

A bill of exchange is a written order by the party of the second part directed to some third party to pay the party of the first part a stated number of shills at a stated time and place. 

The most common form of bill of exchange is the ordinary bank check. Your check is just an order to your bank to pay to the order, say, of 

Simeon Swivelchair sixteen sawbucks on June 7th. 

Other names for the bill of exchange are:

Draft-which is usually drawn on a commercial institution other than a bank. 

Trade Acceptance-A draft drawn by the seller on the purchaser of goods sold, and accepted by such purchaser, as indicated by the purchaser's signature on the face.

Banker's Acceptance-A draft accepted by a bank.

Certified Check-another name for a banker's acceptance.

As between the terms "draft" and "bill of exchange", "draft" is customarily used in domestic trade and "bill of exchange" in foreign trade, and if all this isn't clear as a bell, kindly remember that its just paper in any case and if the dough isn't there when the slip is put in the window, its classification is synonymous with that other kind of paper usually found behind the bathroom door.

Execution and Delivery. 

The bill or note must be in writing and must be definite as to what is to be paid to whom and when. 

However, it becomes effective, and draws its first breath of legal air, on delivery, and if it is delivered signed in blank (Heaven forbid), the holder may fill in the blanks according to his authorization. If he exceeds his authorization and relies on his imagination putting in $100 instead of $10, the joke is on the maker, but if he fills in the name of the maker as well as the amount, the joke is on him (if he gets caught) and the judge will call it forgery. Acceptance.

This is what makes the bill of exchange respectable in society. The drawee writes "accepted" across the face and thereby assumes the obligation before all the world. It is what a bank does when it certifies a check. The accepted bill, or certified check, is used as a method of payment, found convenient because of distance or trade conditions, whereby the seller takes the acceptance or the certification in exchange for his goods, instead of relying merely on the buyer's unbuttressed word. 

As it says in the proverbs "He who selleth his ass for a promise, deserveth bunions for his pains”. 

Or was it the Proverbs? Consideration.

This is what the maker or promisor gets for undertaking the obligation in the first place, and although its usually money, it may be other things such as personal services. But if Nellie G. String parts with her guitar, she better collect in coin of

the realm, and in advance, since a sharp in the wrong flat won't get consideration from the local dicks nohow. And love may be La Grande Passion but as Nellie and Lulu Belle have long since found out, the Judges say it's not legal and valid consideration for a promise to pay. And though such lack of appreciation is hardly deserved, the Judges in their infinite wisdom continue to think only of the safety and sanctity of the home.

Be all that as it may, once the bill or note has been endorsed over to a third party who paid his dough in good faith because the instrument looked O. K. on its face, how the original party was paid, or if he was, is no longer a matter of concern. 

Which brings us to the next point, namely, the transferability of these instruments. Negotiability.

Negotiability means the capability of being transferred by indorsement and delivery, or just delivery, so as to pass to the next holder the right to sue in his own name and to take the instrument free of defences that might have existed as against the payee. This is a rather broad statement but sufficiently true for such a stinking subject. In other words, if the instrument looks all right on its face, the transferee or buyer, gets what he thinks he is getting. 

The instrument to be negotiable must: 

1. Be payable to the order of someone, or to bearer. The magic word is "order". If it is payable "to Whitney-Whistlestop-period'

6 all Whistlestop has is an obligation which he can assign but not negotiate, and the party who gets it from Whistlestop, gets Whistlestop's headaches too. 

2. Be payable in money, by which is meant dollars, be they paper, rubber or plain boloney, but not kopecks, lires, francs, marks, or clam shells. 

3. Be payable unconditionally, by which is meant that the promise to pay cannot be hitched to an agreement to do something else, nor made to depend on the happening of some event. A promise to pay, or to marry the girl, is as unnegotiable as a promise to pay when she reaches the age of 25. After all everyone knows that an old chicken like Lulu 

Belle is way past a mere 25. 

4. Be payable within a reasonable time, by which is meant a period of years even up to 

99. But a promise to pay "when the cows came home" is N. G. That, my friend, is too long. 

5. Be payable at a definite time and in a definite amount, which means just what it says. 

For once, you have to omit the ands, ifs and buts and be specific. Negotiation.

Having finally put together an instrument that is negotiable (see above) and is, therefore, synonomous with folding money except that it lacks a picture of Lincoln or Washington and the peculiar diluted color so characteristic of our long green these days, you negotiate it, which means you pass

it on to the next guy. If it is payable to a specific person (or order), it must be indorsed by this person before delivery, but if it is indorsed in blank or payable to bearer, title passes on mere delivery. Protest.

This is what the holder of the instrument does when Due Day leaves his basket empty. Its yelling bloody murder in a business-like way. It consists of a certificate of a notary public that the instrument was duly presented for payment or acceptance, that same was demanded but refused all of which was protested (and why not). This certificate is mailed to the parties secondarily liable, namely indorsers and drawers, who thereupon become liable for the obligation. It's now their turn to yell bloody murder.

Rights and Liabilities of Indorsers. 

Indorsers are primie facie liable in the order in which they signed their names. This leaves the maker or acceptee as Number One man. But if on 

Due Day, it developes that the Number One man is visiting relatives in China and is not expected back before stopping off to see other relatives in 

Africa, the holder can start at the top of the list of indorsers and work his way down. And if he collects from anyone on the list, that one can go after any of his predecessors, a right that would appear to be of little value, except as a means of killing a dull afternoon.

But where a third person indorses an instrument before delivery to the payee, he is liable as a joint maker and becomes a Number One man on his own.

An indorsement "for collection" gives the holder authority only to collect for the indorser. 

An indorsement "without recourse" passes title to the indorsee, but eliminates the indorser from the chain of responsibility.

The absence or inadequacy of consideration for negotiable paper between the original or prior parties is no defense as against a bona fide indorsee. Thus the fact that all that Phineas Phizzlestick got from his tomato, Lulu Belle, for his pains (and promissory note) was goo-goo eyes, wouldn't have any bearing on his liability to pay the note once Lulu Belle passed it on to her number two man, Whitney Whistlestop. Such is life. 

Whistlestop is a holder in due course, Lulu Belle is a gal who knows the value of the written word and Phizzlestick is just a lug who should've stood in bed.

The result would be the same if Lulu Belle got the note from Phizzlestick through fraud, and it certainly wouldn't make much difference to Phizzlestick. Whistlestop is a holder in due course again, Lulu Belle is the same old gal and Phizzlestick this time is the guy who didn't even get googoo eyes. Checks.

Despite all the talk in the books about promissory notes and bills of exchange, drafts and acceptances, and their use by the merchants and longbeards of 1828, the fact is that today, 1946, just about ninety-five per cent of all business deals are settled with the good (I hope) old American check. "Get out your pen, Daddy, that man's here

again" is the maxim that should be framed and hung over the U. S. mantelpiece.

Greenbacks may have become debased to the extent that they are referred to in vulgar parlance as sawbucks, fish, shills, or clams, but the check (as such) is still viewed with respectful awe unless and until its fair name is tainted with the foul smell of rubber. But if it once developes a stretch like a pre-war panty waist, then the consequences will be decided under the provisions of the "Bad Check Law", or, as the statute is decorously referred to in some states, the "Not Good Check 

Law". Anyway you look at it, if a check bounces back at you when you try to put it through the little window, that ain't good, brother; no, that's bad!

Nevertheless, the check is technically a bill of exchange and subject to the rules and regulations governing negotiable instruments. 

It is drawn on a bank whose obligation to the payee arises only on its certification.

It is usually payable on demand which means within a reasonable time from its date. 

Although a depositor's own bank is usually his agent for collection, by virtue of an arrangement between them, actual or implied, yet the bank's transferee will be a holder in due course, if it had no knowledge of the depositary's limited authority and if it paid a valuable consideration for the check. This is the situation where the depositor's bank transfers the check to another bank which makes the actual collection. The question of who has title to the check at a particular time becomes

of great importance if one of the banks fails, and the usual unseemly scramble ensues. Of course this sort of thing could be easily avoided by the depositor indorsing his check "for collection".

The truth is that in our society checks are a kind of money. Thus, the Government prints promissory notes in the form of paper money which Phizzlestick, Whistlestop and their pal, Simeon Swivelchair, accumulate when, as and if they can, and which for safe keeping and the convenience of doing business on credit, they deposit in the Three 

Ball Bank and Trust Company. Next they issue what amounts to their own money, by writing checks drawn against these deposits. If they occasionally write one check too many and the Bank doesn't have any more of their Government money left to pay it with, that's bad. In fact its against the law. But, then, Governments sometimes issue their kind of money without the means of paying off, whether it be gold or silver (which used to be the basis of all money) or the sweat and labor of the common people (which is the modern Governments' basis of money), and this is bad, too. In fact when Governments fail to pay off it frequently results in a change of management and this sort of thing can be very bad.

Money, or the love of it, still seems to be the root of all evil, and the always changing speed with which it moves from hand to hand inevitably deposits too much in one place and too little in another. 

It's like Phizzlestick, Whistlestop and Swivelchair playing musical chairs with Lulu Belle and 

Nellie G. String. Someone always gets left out.
