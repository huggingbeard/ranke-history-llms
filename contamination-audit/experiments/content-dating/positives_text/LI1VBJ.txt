Effect of Total Digestible Nutrient Intake on Growth and Reproductive Development of Dairy Bulls The Pennsylvania State College SCHOOL OF AGRICULTURE Agricultural Experiment Station State College, Pennsylvania

Effect of Total Digestible Nutrient Intake on Growth and Reproductive Development of Dairy Bulls* R. J. FLIPSE, J. W. SNYDER, D. L. THACKER AND J. O. ALMQUIST† DEVE 

EVELOPMENT of the artificial breeding program has resulted in a change in methods of handling dairy sires: instead of being housed on individual farms they are now centralized in groups of 30 to 80 for convenience in the operation of artificial breeding associations. With scores of bulls under the same roof, bull feeding and management practices receive much more attention than formerly. 

Longevity and factors affecting it have become very important; whereas dairymen ordinarily use bulls only two or three years before disposing of them, artificial breeding associations are interested in using good sires as long as they live, and in means of extending their life spans.

Essentially all of the experimental work involving relationships between nutrition and reproduction has been done with laboratory animals. Nutritional requirements for reproduction vary so much from one species to another that a diet adequate for reproduction in the rat will not support reproduction in the dog (18), thus the results from small animal experimentation can be considered as little more than suggestive with regard to applications to cattle. 

It is generally acknowledged that inanition adversely affects the reproductive efficiency of man and animals (4, 7, 14). Not so well established, even in laboratory animals, is whether the reproductive failures ascribed to underfeeding are due to lack of calories or to a deficiency of one or more specific nutrients. The effect of plane of nutrition (different levels of feed intake, and thus different levels of energy, protein, minerals, etc.) has been investigated both in heifers and in bulls. Steensberg (24), who placed heifers on three planes of nutrition, was interested primarily in requirements for growth, but noted that heifers on the low plane calved about two months later than heifers on normal or high planes even though an attempt was made to

With these indications that nutrition has a definite role in bovine reproduction, it is evident that further research is needed to determine what nutrients are involved. Inasmuch as specific dietary deficiencies are often accompanied by anorexia and thus a reduction in energy intake, it was essential that the effect of energy intake upon reproductive performance be determined before specific nutritional deficiencies could be evaluated properly. Thus an investigation of the effect of level of energy intake, with adequate consumption of specific nutrients, upon the growth, reproductive development and sexual capacity of bulls was undertaken. A report of results to date, covering only the 18 bulls that have reached two years (96 weeks) of age, is presented herein. 

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE 

In order to study the effects of energy intake upon the growth, reproductive development and subsequent reproductive capacity of dairy bulls, different levels of energy intake (total digestible nutrients) were tested for their effects upon these characteristics. The quantities fed were based upon the recommended allowances for growing dairy animals as formulated by the National Research Council (12).

Two classes of Holstein bull calves were used in the trial. Class 

A calves included registered stock from selected matings. These were either owned or leased by the artificial breeding cooperatives of the State. The matings from which these calves came were planned with the hope that the male offspring would be satisfactory for future use as sires in the artificial breeding program. These bull calves were loaned to the College for use in the total digestible nutrient (TDN) study. Class B calves were ordinary high grade or registered animals, not of sufficiently high quality to be considered as sires in the artificial breeding associations. Animals of both classes were used for studies of blood constituents, body growth and development, reproductive development and laboratory semen evaluation tests. In addition to these criteria, it was planned that semen quality of class A bulls would be evaluated further by fertility testing through the artificial breeding cooperatives.

Four groups of class B calves were used to test four levels of TDN intake: groups I, II (control), III and IV, which received 70, 100, 115 and 130 per cent, respectively, of the recommended allowances for TDN. Due to the anticipated restricted availability of 1

class A animals, these calves were used in groups I, II and IV only. 

The present report includes only those animals that have reached 96 weeks of age. Thus three replications of class B calves and two replications of class A calves are included. 

The statistical procedure used in evaluating results is outlined by Snedecor (22, p. 273). Inasmuch as it is logical to assume that there is a level of energy intake above which there is no additional response, the regression is based upon the reciprocals of the levels of energy intake. 

Feeding and management.-Calves were obtained and placed in the experiment at the earliest possible age. In most cases it was possible to start calves in the experiment before reaching two weeks of age, although certain class A bulls were as much as six weeks old before they were received for use. Upon arrival at the Dairy Cattle 

Breeding Research Center, each calf was placed in an individual 5½by-8-foot pen and randomly assigned to one of the groups. All animals were housed in a tightly constructed, mechanically ventilated barn.

Consumption of dry feeds at an early age was encouraged by following a limited milk feeding program. Group I calves received 

190 pounds; group II, 230 pounds; group III, 275 pounds; and group

IV, 295 pounds of milk during the first six weeks. No milk was fed after the sixth week. Good quality legume hay and the appropriate starting ration were fed free choice until consumption reached the desired level. 12 months of age.

Starting at 24 weeks of age calves were tested for the development of sexual interest by presenting them to a heifer of appropriate size in a breeding rack. The testing interval was limited to 10 minutes or three mountings, whichever was shorter.

Thereafter body weight was determined weekly and the other measurements at four-week intervals. All measurements were made between seven and eight a.m., prior to the morning feeding. Body weight, height at withers and chest girth for each group are presented in figures 1, 2 and 3, respectively.

DISCUSSION 

Two rather divergent opinions have been advanced with regard to the role of nutrition in reproductive functions. These views may be represented on the one hand by Walton, who states (25) that there are no critical substances required for spermatogenesis and that so long as body health is maintained the needs for sperm production will be met adequately. Such a view is logical, for sperm production amounts to only a few grams weekly, no critical substances are involved, and the energy content is small. On the other hand, Jones (9) has stressed the importance of nutrition with his report of the delay in sexual maturity in young bulls grown on alfalfa hay. This delay he attributed to reduced energy intake, but since the alfalfa hay-fed bulls were only some 15 per cent below the supplemented bulls in TDN 10

consumption, other factors undoubtedly were involved in producing the differences in reproductive performance. 

The differences in the age at which sexual maturity was reached in this experiment certainly do not approach the differences reported by Jones (9), which should be the case inasmuch as energy intake was essentially the only variable in this trial. 

It remains to be determined to what extent the differences in semen quality observed in these young growing bulls will be carried over into adulthood. The more liberally fed groups should have the advantage, for adequate nutrition should permit them to complete their growth at an earlier age and thus be able to devote more of their energy to reproduction and less to growth requirements. Yet this rapid rate of development may not be advantageous in the end, for if continued it may result in a shorter life span (15, 16). Short-time studies with mature bulls (2) indicate that fertility is not influenced by increasing the TDN intake as much as 40 per cent above requirements. Asdell (1) has pointed out that short-time studies have their limitations since even a diet which causes a sudden break in spermatogenesis will not have its full effect upon semen quality for several weeks. Perhaps maximum benefit will be obtained from bulls by rapid development during early life, followed by restricted energy intake to prolong useful life; however, the value of such a program must await evaluation by further experimentation. 

By far the most obvious effect of energy limitation was the reduced gain in body weight, for at 96 weeks the group I mean weight was only little more than half the group IV mean. In contrast the mean height at withers differed by only about 10 per cent while differences in chest girth were intermediate between those for weight and height at withers. This indicates that the correlation usually found between chest girth and body weight does not apply well under extreme conditions. The lack of significant differences in skeletal growth among groups was not unexpected, for adequate protein and mineral intakes were provided for skeletal development. These results emphasize not only the importance of energy for body weight gains but also the minor role of energy in bone growth. With the skeleton so well developed, there appears little doubt that the bull deficient in energy during early life will develop into a normal individual when energy intake is increased in later life. Whether semen quality will improve with such treatment remains to be determined, but it is being investigated as a part of the study. 11

SUMMARY 

1. Neonatal Holstein bull calves were assigned to four levels of TDN intake, with groups I, II, III and IV receiving 70, 100, 115 and 130 per cent of the recommended allowances for growing dairy calves, respectively. Protein and minerals were provided on a body weight basis and were nearly equal in all groups. Hay was fed at the daily rate of 1 pound per 100 pounds of body weight. 

2. Sexual maturity, the age at which the first viable spermatozoa were obtained, was delayed 12 to 14 weeks in group I as compared to the other three groups. Tests of semen quality between 72 and 96 weeks of age indicated that group I was significantly lower than the other groups in concentration of spermatozoa, per cent initially motile sperm, rate of motility, and motility after 5 and 10 days of storage. Other semen characteristics not materially affected by treatment included volume, per cent live (unstained) spermatozoa, fructose content and pH. 

3. At 96 weeks, relative body weights were 62, 100, 111 and 

119 and relative heights at withers were 93, 100, 101 and 102 for groups I, II, III and IV, respectively. Group I was most efficient in feed utilization, followed in order by groups II, III and IV. 

4. Periodic analysis of the blood for hemoglobin and glucose, and the blood plasma for calcium, inorganic phosphorus, protein and carotene indicated that there were only minor differences among groups in the concentrations of these components. All mean values were within the normal range. 

5. The investigation is being continued to study the effects upon the bulls as they grow older, and to determine the affects upon fertility.
