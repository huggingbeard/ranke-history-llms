Special mention and thanks are due the residents of the three Pennsylvania rural communities who submitted to the rigors of a long and personal schedule form. These people belonged to the following types of formal organizations: church, grange, extension, cooperative, patriotic, civic, fraternal, recreational, and professional. 

The field work and analysis of the data, together with the preparation of this report, was done by the De partment of Agricultural Economics and Rural Sociology of The Pennsylvania State College.

WHO TAKE PART IN RURAL ORGANIZATIONS?* EMORY J. BROWN† 

This is a story of the forces in everyday community living which influence people to take part in organizations in three Pennsylvania rural communities. 

Hence, the graphs that follow portray some differences between people who belong to and take an active part in organizations and those who do not belong or take little part in organizations, which elect officers and meet at designated times on a face-to-face basis. Approximately one-half of the 624 people interviewed were classified as "active" participants, and the other half "'inactive." 

These people were between the ages of 20 and 65 and were selected so that one-half were males and one-half females. The data were collected in 1948 by a schedule of questions in personal interviews. Opposite each chart is a short statement explaining the material on the chart. 

These findings are important to leaders who are concerned about the effectiveness of their organizations. In addition, this publication is seen as being useful to rural discussion groups interested in better working organizations as well as in developing more effective democratic communities.

LOCATION OF COMMUNITIES 

STUDIED Three Pennsylvania rural communities were chosen as being fairly representative of areas with a predominantly dairy type of farming and little or no industry in the community centers. The usual trades and services were available in all communities which were relatively isolated from large urban centers. Approximately 200 people, one-half of them being the most active in organizations and the other half the least active, were interviewed in each community. These people belonged to 45 different organizations in Fredonia, 50 in Millheim, and 53 in Newville. These three communities were fairly typical of communities in the dairy region of Pennsylvania, but no attempt has been made to generalize beyond the three communities studied.

AGES OF ACTIVES AND INACTIVES 

The high and low participants did not differ to any great extent except in the age group 20-29 years where the inactives had more females than the actives. Thirty per cent of the female inactives and 12 per cent of the female actives were in that group. Women of this age group often have babies to care for at home. About 42 per cent of the inactive females and 58 per cent of the active females were 30-49 years of age.

The male actives and inactives did not differ significantly in age distribution.

SIZE OF FAMILY 

More of the actives than inactives were from families composed of only man and wife or man and wife with one or two children. Hence, the inactives had larger families than the actives. A little over seven out of ten actives and six out of ten inactives were from families having two to four members. 

About four out of ten inactives and less than three out of ten actives were from families having five or more members.

INCOME OF FAMILY 

The actives had higher incomes than the inactives. A little over four-tenths of the actives and one-tenth of the inactives reported family incomes of over $3,000 a year. A little over one-fifth of the actives and one-half of the inactives had family incomes of less than $2,000. 

Thus, four times more actives than inactives were from the upper income group.

GRADES COMPLETED IN SCHOOL 

The actives had more formal schooling than the inactives. While about three-fourths of the active participants had completed more than eight grades of school, a little over one-third of the inactives had had that much schooling. It seems that education supplies certain skills which are useful in organizational activity; in addition, the community expects the more educated people to be active in voluntary organizations.

OCCUPATION OF FAMILY HEAD 

The white collar worker families usually were represented among the active participants; the blue collar worker families (skilled, semi-skilled and unskilled laborers) usually were found among the inactives. The inactives included a slightly larger percentage of farmers than the actives. More than 40 per cent of the actives and only 7 per cent of the inactives were professional, business men or clerical workers. A little over one-half of the inactives and one-fifth of the actives belonged to blue collar worker families. Since only

COMMUNICATION FACILITIES 

The automobile and the telephone have been instrumental in minimizing the space which separates people in rural communities. More actives than inactives had telephones and automobiles. In addition, the wives among the actives more often drove automobiles. A little over seven-tenths of the active praticipants and two-tenths of the inactive participants had telephones. Slightly over one-half of the actives and one-fourth of the inactives had automobiles which the wives drove. Hence, the actives more often had communication facilities as an aid in taking part in organizations.

GEOGRAPHICAL LOCATION 

The actives lived closer than the inactives to the place where most organizations met. A little over one-half of the actives and one-third of the inactives resided less than a mile from or very close to the meeting place of organizations. 

There was a tendency for the high participants to shop locally more than the low participants, but the difference was small. Over half of all respondents traveled more than 3 miles to their usual shopping centers. There was evidence that many of the community residents shopped at centers beyond their community.

FEELING A PART OF THE COMMUNITY 

Both actives and inactives defined the area of their community in much the same way, but the high participants expressed a greater degree of identification with their community. 

A little over 40 per cent of the actives and 20 per cent of the inactives felt very much a part of their community. About 18 per cent of the inactives and 8 per cent of the actives felt not very much a part of their community. Officers in organizations expressed a greater degree of identification with their community than non-officers.

SATISFACTION WITH THE COMMUNITY 

The inactives were better satisfied than the actives with the educational, recreational, and health facilities in their community. However, the active participants were more satisfied with the religious facilities. About one-half of the actives and one-third of the inactives were not satisfied with the educational and recreational facilities. About one-third of the actives and one-fourth of the inactives were not satisfied with the health facilities. The low participants evidently did not feel a need for better educational, recreational and health facilities as much as the high participants.

GROUP ADJUSTMENT 

The high participants felt more at ease in organization situations than the low participants. 

While a little over one-fourth of the actives rated themselves as being very much at ease in participation situations requiring spontaneous response or a formal speech, only about one-tenth of the inactives felt that well adjusted to such group situations. Over one-third of the inactives and one-tenth of the actives rated themselves as being not at all at ease in situations faced in taking part in organizations.

SELF-RATINGS 

As compared with the low participants, the high participants saw themselves as belonging to more organizations, taking a more active part in organizations, and being called upon more often for help and opinions. While no inactive rated him or herself among that group in the community who belong to the most organizations, a little over one-fourth of the actives rated themselves in that category. Approximately the same difference was evident in self-ratings on degree of organizational activity and degree of informal leadership. The extent to which these people actually took part in formal and informal groups showed high agreement with their self-judgments.

BENEFITS FROM ORGANIZATIONS 

Both men and women designated "friends" as being the greatest benefit from taking part in organizations. The social interaction involved in organizational activities evidently serves as a worth-while objective for many people. The inactive females. expressed "spiritual" benefits as being the most important. These women belonged to few organizations, other than the church. The inactive men reported "financial" benefit more than anything else while the women did not rate this very high. A smaller percentage mentioned "education" and "personal satisfaction" as benefits from taking part in organizations.

SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION 

This study shows that people who are active in rural organizations generally occupy higher positions in their community than people who take no or little part in organizations. In comparison with the inactives, the actives have more formal education, higher incomes, and higher prestige occupations. The actives also have smaller families. Age does not seem to differentiate high and low participants except in the early years of married life where young children would be a factor in preventing the mother from taking part in organizations outside the home. High participants live closer to where organizations meet than low participants. 

People generally behave in accordance with what the "self" means to them—that is, how they see themselves. In this study, the actives perceived themselves as being more at ease than inactives in situations common to taking part in organizations; in addition, the actives rated themselves as being in more organizations and taking a more active part. The high participants were better satisfied with life in general but were less satisfied than the inactives with recreation, schools, and health facilities in the community.

Leaders in rural organizations are faced with the challenge of involving more people in activities of their organization. New techniques and programs that appeal to the needs and interests of inactives are required to integrate them into existing organizations.
