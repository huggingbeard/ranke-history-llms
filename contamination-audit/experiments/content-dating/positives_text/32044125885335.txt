ORGANIZATION AND FUNCTIONING OF COURTS OF RECORD REPORT OF THE VIRGINIA ADVISORY LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL To: 

RICHMOND, VIRGINIA, OCTOBER 25, 1955. 

HONORABLE THOS. B. STANLEY, Governor of Virginia and

THE GENERAL ASSEMBLY OF VIRGINIA. 

During the session of the General Assembly of 1954 bills were offered to create five additional judicial circuits and to provide, in addition to existing circuit judges, additional circuit judges to the number of three. 

Also, many amendments were offered to the Committee considering these bills to increase the number or circuits above those provided for in the bills referred to. None of these bills were adopted, but the General Assembly, in order to provide for a proper reallocation of the work load among the judges of the courts of record throughout the State, directed the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council to make a study of the organization and functioning of the courts of record and recommend such changes therein as appeared proper. The resolution directing this study follows: 

SENATE JOINT RESOLUTION NO. 27 

Directing the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council to study the organization of the courts of record throughout the State. 

Whereas, the General Assembly of nineteen hundred fifty-two adopted a statute creating a position designed to result in equalizing the work of the courts of record throughout the State; and

Whereas, this present session of the General Assembly is confronted with the greatest number of bills for additional judges of courts of record and the establishment of new circuits therefor, as well as the establishment of corporation courts, which any session of the General Assembly in the preceding twenty years has had; and

Whereas, an exhaustive study is required as to the functioning of all courts of record and the machinery for equalizing the workloads therein; now, therefore, be it Resolved by the Senate of Virginia, the House of Delegates concurring, That the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council is hereby directed to make a study and report upon the organization and functioning of the several courts of record, exclusive of the Supreme Court of Appeals of the State of Virginia. The Council shall consider the jurisdiction of such courts, their respective workloads, and what changes, if any, should be made therein. 

It shall also consider the effectiveness of the machinery devised for equalizing the workload of such courts and to what extent the same has functioned. All agencies of the State shall assist the Council in its study. The 

Council shall complete its study and make its report to the Governor and

General Assembly not later than November fifteen, nineteen hundred fifty-five.

Following the session of 1954 the Council met and assigned this study to Mosby G. Perrow, Jr., member of the State Senate and of the Council, as Chairman of a Committee to make the preliminary investigation and report. The following served with Senator Perrow on this Committee: 

J. Bradie Allman, member of the House of Delegates, Franklin County; 

Edward L. Breeden, Jr., member, State Senate, Norfolk City; Russell M. 

Carneal, member, House of Delegates, Williamsburg; George Damm, former member, House of Delegates, Arlington; Howard C. Gilmer, Jr., Attorney at Law, Pulaski; Douglas A. Robertson, Attorney at Law, Lynchburg; 

Clifton A. Woodrum, Jr., Attorney at Law, Roanoke City, and Lewis F. 

Powell, Jr., Attorney at Law, City of Richmond. Mr. Gilmer was elected

Vice-Chairman of the Committee. John B. Boatwright, Jr. and G. M. 

Lapsley served as Secretary and Recording Secretary, respectively, to the Committee.

Following the passage of the resolution by the General Assembly, Judge Edward Meeks, Judge of the Twenty-ninth Judicial Circuit died. In pursuance of Code § 17-122, the Supreme Court of Appeals made an investigation of the business of the circuit and reported to Governor Stanley that the circuit should be abolished and the counties in the circuit transferred to other circuits. The Committee took this matter under consideration. 

The Committee examined the bills and amendments which were submitted to the General Assembly of 1954 and discussed the changes which they would have made. In addition, it obtained information from Hubert 

D. Bennett, Executive Secretary, Supreme Court of Appeals, on the business of the courts. This was carefully studied by the members of the Committee. After widespread publicity and due notice, a public hearing was held on August 29 in Richmond.

At the hearing representatives of several circuits appeared and presented their views. The Legislative delegation from the Twenty-ninth 

Circuit as well as members of the Bar therefrom, recommended to the Committee that the recommendations of the Supreme Court be overruled and that the circuit be continued as presently constituted. 

Various representatives from the Fourth Circuit appeared and recommended that Chesterfield County be established as a separate circuit due to the pressure of business. This would leave the remaining counties and city in the Circuit as the Fourth Circuit. 

A delegation appeared from the Seventh Circuit and recommended that Pittsylvania County be established as a separate circuit with the present Seventh Circuit to consist of the counties of Patrick and Henry and the City of Martinsville. A great increase in business was said to be overburdening the judge and delaying the trial of cases. Some opinion was expressed that an additional judgeship for the circuit would remedy the situation.

Representatives of the Twelfth Circuit recommended that the County of Essex be transferred from the Twelfth to the Thirteenth Circuit. 

Representatives of the Fourteenth Circuit pointed out to the Committee that the area is a fast growing one and will soon require some relief either by way of an additional judge or by the creation of an additional circuit or corporation court.

Representatives of the Sixteenth Circuit appeared and stated that they desire either an additional circuit judge or creation of a Court of Law and

Chancery for Fairfax County in order to handle the rapidly increasing litigation in that area. The area was also said to be a fast growing one. 

Representatives of the Twentieth Circuit recommended that this be divided into two circuits, one composed of the City of Roanoke and the County of Roanoke and the other composed of Floyd and Montgomery Counties and the City of Radford. They stated that the work of the circuit has increased greatly and to such an extent that it cannot be handled by the existing judiciary even with the allocation temporarily of judges from other circuits. The judge of the present circuit has been overburdened to such an extent that his health has been impaired. 

Representatives of the Thirtieth Circuit, consisting of the County of Franklin appeared, and stated while they are satisfied with the circuit as presently constituted, they would not oppose the county's being joined in a circuit with some other county or counties. 

Representatives of the Thirty-fifth Circuit stated that an additional judge is required to serve the increasing judicial work load in Arlington County. They further recommended increases in the salaries of the judiciary, pointing out the difficulty of getting able lawyers to serve as judges. 

Following the public hearings, additional evidence was received concerning conditions in the Fourteenth Circuit and the Twenty-eighth Circuit indicating a need for immediate relief in those areas. 

Following the adoption of the resolution by the General Assembly, the Judicial Council appointed a committee of judges of courts of record to consider the problem and to advise with the committee of the Council in connection with this study. This committee was originally composed of the following judges: Richard B. Spindle, Frederick L. Hoback, John D. 

Hooker, Thomas L. Hutton and William D. Medley. Following the death of

Judge Spindle, Judge J. Jordan Temple was appointed to this committee and served as its Chairman. Judge Temple sat with the Committee at its public hearing and the Committee subsequently conferred with him and the committee of judges.

The Executive Secretary of the Supreme Court of Appeals, at the request of the Committee, prepared detailed charts showing the work load of the several judges in the circuits to which reference was above made. 

After examination of this and other material before it, and after conference with the committee of judges, the Committee submitted its report to the Council. The Council, having reviewed the report of the Committee now submits the following recommendations:

RECOMMENDATIONS 1. The recommendation of the Supreme Court of Appeals relative to abolishing the Twenty-ninth Judicial Circuit and the transfer of the counties therein to neighboring circuits should be followed by the General 

Assembly of 1956. 

2. In order to lighten the work in the Fourth Circuit to the extent needed, Powhatan County, should be taken from that Circuit and added to the Ninth Circuit to avoid the creation of an additional circuit. 

3. Pittsylvania County should be taken from the Seventh Circuit and added to the Thirtieth Circuit, which would consist of that county and Franklin County. The Seventh Circuit should then consist of the counties of Henry and Patrick and the City of Martinsville. 

4. An additional judge should be provided for the Fourteenth Circuit which consists of the counties of Charles City, James City, New Kent, York

and the cities of Warwick and Williamsburg. The senior judge of the 

Circuit should exercise the powers and duties conferred by statute upon circuit judges. 

5. An additional judge should be provided for the Sixteenth Circuit which consists of the counties of Fairfax and Prince William and the city of Alexandria with the senior judge in the Circuit to retain the powers and duties presently conferred upon him by statute. 

6. The Twentieth Circuit should consist only of the county of Roanoke and the city of Roanoke. A new circuit should be created to consist of the counties of Floyd and Montgomery and the city of Radford. 

7. An additional judge should be provided for the Twenty-eight Circuit which consists of the counties of Isle of Wight and Princess Anne and the city of Portsmouth, the senior judge continuing to exercise the statutory powers and duties. 

8. An additional judge should be provided for the Thirty-fifth Circuit, which consists of Arlington County, with the powers and duties of the senior judge being continued as presently provided for by statute. 

9. Continuing study should be made of all areas of the State where rapid population growth and industrial development result in such congestion of the work of the courts as may require additional judges in the system of circuit courts and the judges thereof. 

THE GENERAL PROBLEM 

The Constitution provides for a circuit court in each county and in each city of the first class; in each such city there may be in addition to the circuit court a corporation court. Some cities of the second class have city courts of record and some do not. The counties and cities having circuit courts are divided into circuits which are based upon the amount of litigation, the number of inhabitants, and, where cities are involved, the existence of other courts of record. In all political subdivisions there are courts not of record having jurisdiction of smaller matters. 

One of the essentials to the proper functioning of our society is the prompt and impartial administration of justice. We have been fortunate in the judges chosen to interpret and apply the laws enacted by the General Assembly. Virginia has been marked by an absence of judicial excesses. 

Impartiality has been the rule. 

Litigants can be denied justice by being forced to a long delay in having a trial on the merits just as much as they can by the absence of judicial impartiality. Most civil cases involve substantial rights and the parties deserve a prompt and speedy trial in these as well as in criminal trials. 

If this cannot be had, the parties suffer. For example, a person who has incurred injuries in an automobile accident is frequently forced to accept a sum as damages far below what he properly should receive, if because of a crowded docket it may be a year or more before he can get a trial and his creditors cannot wait.

For many years the Supreme Court of Appeals has assigned judges from one circuit to another to aid in the expedition of business in courts which are congested with litigation, either by reason of the volume of business or the illness or other disability to serve of the judge of the court being aided. In addition, in recent years the General Assembly has created additional judgeships for established circuits to aid in expediting the business of the courts instead of creating additional circuits with their consequent expense.

While the citizenry is entitled to prompt and impartial justice, it should not be required to pay the expense of unneeded judicial machinery. 

To provide for the elimination of circuits which are not required for the prompt administration of justice, the General Assembly has enacted a statute directing the Supreme Court of Appeals, whenever a vacancy arises in the office of judge of a circuit court, to investigate the business of the circuit and to report to the Governor on whether the vacancy should be filled, and if not, how the localities within the circuit should be assigned to other circuits. The Legislature is not of course bound by the findings of the Supreme Court. 

If the cost of government is to be reduced or even held in line, the recommendations of the Supreme Court in this field should be followed. 

Every time a vacancy occurs in a circuit the cry of "do not disturb us" arises. No economy in government can be realized without disturbing someone and the cost of government cannot be kept on a reasonable level, if everyone is to have what he desires.

OFFICE OF THE EXECUTIVE SECRETARY OF THE SUPREME COURT OF APPEALS 

In 1952 the General Assembly created the office of Executive Secretary to the Supreme Court of Appeals and charged this official with assisting the Chief Justice and Court in the administration of the judicial branch of the government, to the end that litigation might be expedited and the administration of justice improved in the courts of the Commonwealth. The 

Executive Secretary of the Court has compiled statistical records of the activities of the several courts which are of invaluable assistance to the Court in its attempt to distribute the work load of the judicial system, insofar as is possible, among the judiciary and assign judges both to fill vacancies when necessary and to assist in those courts with crowded dockets where the administration of justice can be expedited by the provision of assistance to the judges sitting in such courts. This has both relieved the 

Court itself of many detailed administrative duties and made it feasible to utilize judicial manpower in the most efficient manner possible. 

REASONS FOR RECOMMENDATIONS 

1. The Supreme Court of Appeals and the Council have examined the judicial business of the counties within the Twenty-ninth Judicial Circuit. The counties involved are Amherst, Nelson and Fluvanna. They are spread out over a wide area of central Virginia. The volume of their litigation does not justify their continuance as a separate circuit. It would be more convenient to the members of the Bar in that circuit to continue the Circuit but the same argument would apply to providing a circuit court judge for each county and city in the State. Since the business in the Circuit does not justify its retention the counties should be placed in other circuits. 

In a communication to the Governor under date of March 8, 1955, the 

Supreme Court of Appeals recommended that Amherst and Nelson Counties be combined with the Corporation Court of the City of Waynesboro, and Fluvanna be added to the Ninth Circuit. This would result in a net decrease of one judge. The Council concurs in the recommendation of the Supreme Court. 

2. The Fourth Circuit is composed of the counties of Chesterfield, 

Dinwiddie, Nottoway, Amelia and Powhatan and the cities of Colonial Heights and Petersburg. Colonial Heights has no circuit court and the circuit court of Petersburg has only civil jurisdiction. Chesterfield County is in the midst of a rapid industrial development, the extent of which is 9/

difficult to measure. The business in the Circuit is considerable but no one county has sufficient business to justify a separate circuit at present. In order to give some relief to the judge of the Circuit it is believed that the best arrangement will be to transfer Powhatan to the Ninth Circuit. This county is separated from that Circuit by the James River but bridges exist affording ready communication. 

3. The Seventh Circuit is composed of the counties of Patrick, Henry and Pittsylvania and the city of Martinsville. Here, too, tremendous industrial development is taking place. Pittsylvania County is the largest county in area and one of the largest in population in the State. The business of the Circuit is immense. In order to afford relief for litigants the Circuit should be rearranged so as to consist of the counties of Henry and Patrick and the city of Martinsville. We have been advised that a single judge can take care of the business of the Circuit as proposed to be constituted. 

Pittsylvania should then be added to the Thirteenth Circuit which now consists of Franklin County alone. These two counties would not constitute too much of a burden for one judge. 

4. The Fourteenth Circuit consists of the counties of Charles City, James City, New Kent and York and the cities of Warwick and Williamsburg. This area has experience and is experiencing tremendous population growth and a number of large industries have recently established themselves there. It has a relatively large number of separate courts and covers a considerable area. These factors have developed an increase in the work load which is borne by the judge of this Circuit to the point where it is impossible for a single judge to meet all the demands made upon him. To give the immediate relief to the judge of this Circuit, as well as to provide for the greatest possible flexibility dealing with the problems which are certain to arise with continued growth and development, an additional judgeship should be established for this Circuit. The senior judge should be, according to the pattern which has been established in other areas where an additional judge for a circuit has been provided, vested with the powers and charged with the duties provided by law for judges of circuit courts. 

5. The Sixteenth Circuit consists of the counties of Prince William and 

Fairfax and the cities of Alexandria and Falls Church. It is one of those circuits in which an additional judgeship has been created in order to provide for the prompt dispatch of business and to avoid creation of an additional circuit. The area is growing rapidly in population. Judicial business is quite heavy due to the number of heavily traveled highways, real estate developments, increasing business activity, and other factors common to a rapidly growing and densely populated area. The press of business has reached the point that the General Assembly is again confronted with the necessity of either creating an additional court or an additional judgeship. 

We believe the latter course the preferable one and so recommend. 

6. The Twentieth Circuit consists of the counties of Roanoke, Floyd, and Montgomery and the city of Roanoke. The city of Radford lies within the geographical area of the circuit but has no circuit court. The business of the circuit has increased tremendously within the last ten years. Roanoke 

City has only two full-time courts of record, in addition to the circuit court. 

The city of Radford has a corporation court which, under the Constitution, it can abolish by a vote of the people. The business is such that if it were divided between two judges, it would keep them both busy. The former judge of the circuit broke down under the pressure of business. The present judge has tried to keep abreast of the business by working continuously and his health has been impaired as a result. Designation of judges to assist has not been successful in bringing the dockets up to date, and some- 10

thing must be done promptly to afford litigants relief by giving them early trial dates and to ensure that the judge presiding over the courts will be able to deal with litigation with reasonable dispatch. The city of Roanoke and county of Roanoke are growing rapidly as a result of industrialization and the same is true of the remainder of the Circuit. It is believed that the best way to handle this situation is to reduce the Twentieth Circuit so that it will consist of the county of Roanoke and the city of Roanoke. A new circuit should be created to consist of the counties of Floyd and Montgomery and the city of Radford. In this way, an equal division of the work can be had and the administration of justice facilitated. We have been assured. that if this change is made, the present corporation court of the city of 

Radford will be voted out. 

7. One of the areas in the State which has been experiencing the most rapid population growth is that immediately adjacent to the city of Norfolk. 

The counties of Isle of Wight and Princess Anne and the city of Portsmouth, which lie adjacent to Norfolk City and compose the Twenty-eighth Circuit, constitute the most acute problem, from the judicial standpoint, in this area. Despite strenuous efforts on the part of the judge of this Circuit and its Bar, the judge has not been able to keep up with the volume of work within the Circuit. Designations of judges to assist him have been resorted to, but have given only partial and temporary relief. All indications are that, unless relief is afforded, the administration of justice in this Circuit must lag further and further behind and this condition will become more acute if anticipated population growth in the area continues. It is not felt that it would be feasible or desirable to create a new circuit in this area but the provision of an additional judge for the present Circuit would vastly facilitate the administration of justice and is recommended. 

8. The population of the counties and cities in Virginia adjacent to the 

District of Columbia is rapidly increasing, and this increase is expected to continue and even be accelerated. As these localities become congested, litigation increases at a rapid rate, as was noted earlier in connection with the Sixteenth Circuit. The General Assembly has created Arlington County as a separate circuit, and litigation has increased to such an extent that an additional judge had to be provided. These two judges are now overwhelmed. The best way to afford relief to the judiciary and the litigants in this Circuit is to create an additional judgeship with the senior judge of the Circuit being vested with the powers which he presently has. 

9. The recommendations of this report are designed to meet the most acute problems which currently affect the judicial system in Virginia. It is impossible to establish, for all time a system which will continue to be adequate for changing conditions in different areas of the State, both in respect to larger concentration of population and to urbanization and industrialization, with resulting volume and complication of cases coming before the court. For this reason it is recommended that continuing study be given to the work load of the courts, especially in those areas of rapid population increase and industrial development, in order that the needs for revision of our judicial system may be met as they arise. 

CONCLUSION 

The Council has been guided in its study by a desire to promote the administration of justice. It is opposed to the creation of unnecessary courts and judgeships, and has tended to be conservative in recommending the creation of additional judicial machinery. On the other hand it is cognizant of the injustice to litigants and the hardship imposed upon the Bar, the judiciary, and all who are concerned with the administration of 11

the courts by allowing congestion of dockets and overworking of judges to slow down the judicial procedure and impair one of the cornerstones upon which our system of government is built.

W. TAYLOE MURPHY 

I approve the report with the following exception:

I do not feel that taking Powhatan County from the Fourth Circuit will sufficiently relieve that Circuit. The county of Chesterfield is rapidly growing and the workload in that Circuit is already tremendous. Not only will the population of the Circuit increase tremendously in the next few years, but the construction of the toll road through Chesterfield County will result in a tremendous increase in the amount of litigation in this court. 

I believe that additional relief should be given to this Circuit by providing an additional judge for it.
