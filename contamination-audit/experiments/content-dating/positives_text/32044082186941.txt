Completely At Sea 

The woman at the rail readjusts the corsage on her bosom, recounting the orchids to see there really are six. An important looking gentleman with a sun-ray lamp complexion obligingly smiles for the press photographers. Here and there berets are making a self conscious appearance. Two women in flat heeled shoes complete an exploratory turn around the deck. A middle aged matron discovers that the door with the sign "Hommes" on it does not lead to her cabin and retires in confusion. An endless stream of luggage still flows from pier to ship. A whistle shrills and three school teachers nervously drop their Baedeckers. A surge of visitors starts ashore. Five thousand persons on the pier begin waving handkerchiefs and shouting goodbyes. In just fifteen minutes, if all goes according to schedule, the Ile de France will be on its way. 

Sixteen minutes later the next-to-last passenger comes panting up the pier with a convoy of four baggage-laden porters. 

Every one begins looking nervously at his watch. Five hundred first trippers try to hide their wonder whether it was such a good idea after all and the veterans have difficulty maintaining their appearance of boredom. A woman on the boat deck screams to a relative seventy-five feet below on the dock: "Make Aunt Mattie be careful of her asthma." From below comes the reply: "Yes, it's at the bottom of the brown box." Ten children begin crying because they aren't going and ten more because they are. 

The first gangplank is pulled away to the accompaniment of a prolonged cheer from ship and shore. "Goodbyes," now beginning to be the least bit impatient, swell the breeze. Cabin 524, which is one of the simplest the Ile de France offers, has two comfortable single beds and a superb shaving mirror. 

It has a telephone over which one can not only chat to the captain or the people on the deck above, but talk with the folks at home or even with any telephone subscriber in Australia, if one knew anybody there. The cabin is large enough for a short man to swing a small cat in without particular distress to either. It boasts perfect reading lights over each bed and a complete bath with running water, choice of fresh or salt, hot or cold. It has a wardrobe, dressing table, space for the largest trunk and even an electric radiator. It has a little man in a white jacket named Adrian who comes running when you ring the bell once, a woman named Gabrielle who dresses in black to match her mustache and arrives in a hurry when the bell is sounded twice. There is also a little boy in a red jacket who appears when Adrian and Gabrielle are out to lunch, but isn't much help because of the complete inability of him or the occupants of Cabin 524 to understand one another. There are moments when it seems he has confined his linguistic accomplishments to French just to be annoying.

GLORIFYING THE FEMALE TOURIST 

Even the plainest accommodations on the Ile de France are so grand that as an old lover of the sea in its simpler days we resent them exceedingly. If one is to get all the comforts of the best hotel on a boat, plus the unique convenience of a port hole through which the wind comes in and safety razor blades, discarded magazines, cigarette butts and hopelessly torn shirts go out, there isn't much. point left in going to sea.

A steamship cabin to be a steamship cabin should have two narrow bunks, one placed over the other, an iron wash basin offering cold salt water, a single light with the brilliance of one in an old fashioned Pullman before the train starts, a slight odor of bilge water, and a wardrobe door with a faulty catch so it will keep squeaking its way half open with the sway of the ship then bumping itself closed until one wonders whether it is better to go insane watching it or tempt death from seasickness by getting up to slam it shut. One really got a feeling of the sea in the older and sturdier days when such accommodations were all any boat knew and after he had lived on one for a week he realized he had been somewhere. But steamship cabins in the newer boats, like the old gray mare and most other things nowadays, aren't what they used to be and one who objects probably rightly must be termed a foe to progress. 

Judged from its most modest cabin, it may be correctly surmised that the public rooms of the Ile de France are of a grandeur beyond description. That being true, there is no point whatsoever in our trying to describe them. One point about them, however, is worthy of note. At every hand in some subtle way there is an expression of the gallant Gallic attitude toward women. 

The curving stairway to the dining saloon is so arranged that every woman can make a grand entrance, pausing and turning on a landing at just the right elevation to display her gown to perfection and, if she has that sort of a gown, turn every other woman in the place green with envy. The lights in the saloon where everyone congregates after dinner are sufficiently dimmed to show complexions at their best. Even the large mural of a hunting scene

Ile de France and it is a high tribute to their art that the 1. w. and the other 804 women aboard are eating it up. 

That hardy old breed of deck walkers is fast vanishing. 

They were the ones who at sea thought that day lost whose low descending sun found them with anything less than 50 turns of the deck done. And without cutting a single corner. Neither wind nor rain could halt them in their constitutionals and they could thrust aside little children who got under foot or glare down radicals strolling counter-clockwise without losing a stride. But there isn't a deck walker worthy of the name among the 1,400 happy souls aboard the Ile de France; not a one who could do five turns without suffering fallen arches.

This boat is not without its notables. There are two Comtes and a Vicomte, if the passenger list may be trusted, although the only person we have been able to find who looked the least bit like a member of the nobility, proved to be only a perfume salesman. There is a man who gave one of the 55 speeches seconding Roosevelt's nomination. The inevitable Japanese is present, going his way so unobtrusively everyone is convinced he is a spy. A retired British colonel has appeared who looks and snorts very much like a walrus. A retired folding bed manufacturer from Ohio has almost everyone convinced he is a card sharper. Four gentlemen are along, still very much incensed because the French won't open the dining room for dinner until a quarter to eight. And a Junoesque woman with dyed red hair.

A steamship is the only place where a newspaper is a complete failure. There is just enough news in the one slipped each morning under the cabin door to whet one's curiosity about what is happening back on dry land without in the least satisfying it. The 

European news is exclusively of the League of Nations and airplane fatalities and the American only brief reports of political speeches.

The food on the Ile de France is what one might term homey and adequate. Last night, for instance, at the Captain's Dinner, the passengers were offered a snack consisting of Volga caviar on ice; choice of turtle soup or "veloute camemia" (which proved to be only pea soup in disguise); sole fixed fancy; steak with sauce and potatoes; asparagus served as a separate course; turkey and stuffing;

It is a fact any good Kansan hates to admit, but it is something he learns beyond a shadow of a doubt, that a little wine is good for the stomach inclined to be upset by the rocking and pitching of a boat at sea.

For the past seven nights we have set our watch ahead an hour each evening on retiring which has had a most disturbing cumulative effect. The body somehow responds to resetting less easily than a watch. Here it is time for dinner and we feel more in the mood for our regular morning coca cola. 

It should be noted that the French are not without their reverence for American customs. They may not have paid their debts but they did recognize the Fourth of July at sea by serving red, white and blue ice cream.

A VERY HOARSE FOG HORN 

The sun has only worked its way through the clouds once in five days at sea, and then it retired in embarrassment at the end of five minutes. There has been rain every day and enough fog one night to require the use of the fog horn. It's a very fine fog horn the Ile de France has except that it would do better if it were given a good swig of cough medicine. The weather has been chilly enough even though it is mid-July to require an overcoat when one is on deck. The recurrent rain is a reminder that even the weather bureau has much to learn about improving its system of distribution. 

Rain out on the Atlantic doesn't even serve to make the ocean less salty, but just a little of it in Kansas would be worth a million dollars an inch.

The Ile de France is a boat of 43,000 tons which seems pretty big until you recall that the Normandie and the Queen Mary are nearly twice that large. Including crew and passengers it has 2,285 persons aboard, all of whom are eating regularly and probably more than is good for them. The name of the boat means simply Island of France, the French thereby proving themselves in one respect smarter than we because they have dropped the "s" from Isle since it isn't pronounced anyhow.

One becomes more intimate with his cabin steward even than he does with his favorite barber and by the second day out is freely exchanging all his life's secrets with him. Ours is Adrianlast name unpronounceable. He has a mustache, a slight knowledge of English and, as the French say, forty-seven years. He fought in the world war from 1914 on, except for intervals in hospitals when he was recovering from five different sets of wounds. Between trips he drops in on his wife and three children at their little home on the outskirts of Havre, bringing his fourth child, who is a bell boy on the boat, along with him. He has been on this ship since it was put in service nine years ago, and is of the opinion his job will prove a permanent one. He comes popping in and out of the cabin without knocking, regardless of what state of undress the 1. w. may be in and it doesn't seem to embarrass him in the least.

Somebody should look into the sea gull situation as well. 

There were practically none around the boat as it left New York, which might have been explained by the fact that at least four other steamers were leaving at nearly the same time and the gulls preferred some other kind of garbage besides French. But here we are less than a day out from England and not a gull in sight. The only possible solution that enters our mind is that the gulls have a relief complex and can no longer be bothered chasing steamers but wait patiently in their nests for someone to come and feed them. 

The French have a principle, interesting and perhaps sound, that no one is in a fit state to meet his fellow human beings before he has had breakfast, so this meal, consisting of a cup of coffee and a bun, should be taken in solitude, and in bed. After one gets used to it, it's not bad, but it has been quite a strain on this voyage for those hardy souls who like to arise at six, take a brisk canter around the deck, then dash into the dining room for large portions of ham and eggs. The sight of them gnawing their fingernails and hunger their vitals, waiting for the dining room to open at nine has been one of the exceptionally entertaining ones of the trip. 

There is some talk about the boat that the sailors will have their uniforms washed when they go home for a week before their voyage back to the United States. Having examined both uniforms and sailors carefully, however, our personal opinion is that they will wait until the ship is laid up for the winter. Another six months couldn't make them any dirtier.

Half of the passengers on the Ile de France are worrying at the moment how much they should tip the various stewards who have ministered to them for the past week. The other half, more seasoned travelers, are not giving the matter a thought, having long since come to the gloomy conclusion that whatever they give it will be either too much or too little. 

The most surprised persons during the next few hours before landing will be the dozen or so who are basking in the belief that they can elude the deck stewards and avoid paying the rent on their deck chairs, a charge amounting to little more than twice the retail value of the chairs. Even the stupidest deck steward is yet to be beat out of his rent, and the more suave ones are able to collect it two or three times without any of their clients realizing it. 12

England At Last 

The Ile de France slides into Plymouth on a cold gray dawn. 

The town is even more famous as a point of departure than as one of arrival. It was from here one afternoon in 1588 that Sir Francis 

Drake and his men set out to whip the Armada, and from here, too, that the Pilgrims sailed away for the New World. There is no time for us to see the Plymouth Rock that attests to the latter event and is allegedly even larger than the one in Salem on which the Pilgrims landed. By six the tender is alongside in a pouring rain to take three hundred or so of the passengers to the boat train and 

London 224 miles away.

The porter is accommodating. The customs inspection is so casual that the 1. w. regrets she didn't smuggle in a few more cigarettes than she did above the meager limit England allows visitors. In not a minute more than two or three hours the brakeman who looks exactly like John Bull gives a toot on his whistle and the "boat train" is on its way. 

The route lies through the Devon countryside which is entitled to the highest praise for looking exactly as one imagined it would. A rolling, lush green country cut up in small irregular fields by thick hedges or mossy stone walls. No isolated farm houses, but here and there a cluster of cottages of red brick or dark stone. Square spires of churches in the style the Normans brought across the Channel with them nine centuries ago, rise stolidly from the chimney pots of the larger towns.

There is an orderliness to this Devon land in comparison to anything to be found in the United States. Not a stone is out of place in the fences, the buildings are of a construction that requires no paint to keep them looking fresh, there are no pastures overgrown with weeds, and not a foot of land that has been wasted anywhere. 

The comparison between Devon and, say, Dakota, though, is not an invidious one. It has taken fifteen hundred years or so of steady work to give the English countryside its finished look with everything on the landscape having an appearance of permanency. Given 13

LONDON'S NOT THE SAME 

Station in London itself. All out and where in thunder in that mountain of baggage is our other suitcase? 

Even from a taxi window riding from railroad station to the hotel you can see that London in nine years has changed. True, the taxi driver says "Right-O, Guv-ner" in a way that no American could ever imitate. Neither he nor his car seem to have aged much in the interval, but little else is the same. The first and most vivid impression is that London, if not all England, has become thoroughly motorized. The streets are filled with a seldom moving mass of double-decker buses and dinky little automobiles that Englishmen fancy to evade taxes. The roadways are little more than wide enough for a single line of traffic each way and they seldom go more than a block without ending in a square into which at least six other streets are pouring their traffic.

Add to that a few slow moving stop lights; pedestrians who are given the right of way with a policeman on every corner to enforce it for them; quite a few bicyclists; and a good showing of sleek, fat, but phlegmatic truck horses and you can easily see that New York on its worst after theater traffic jam on a rainy night has nothing to offer like this. 

But the changes to London are not in the streets, traffic and its jams alone. Most of the buildings, at least in the shopping district, have had modern, but not very good modern fronts applied to them. On every hand tremendously big apartment houses have sprung up or still are in course of construction. Great office buildings which make up in ground space what they lack in height have been built. The government, instead of talking about them, has pushed to completion huge slum clearance projects to provide better housing for the poor. The few remaining cobblestones of the side streets have given way to macadam. And even The Old Lady of 

Threadneedle Street is having the bath she has greatly needed for at least fifty years.

There seems to be, too, although this may be imagination, something different in the air. There is more bustle and hurry to the crowds on the street than in 1927. The people are more pleas- 15

At least by reputation, a camel can go nine days without a drink. Compared to an Englishman, though, a camel is always hanging around the watering trough. Of course, if physiology may be accepted, an Englishman must take on a little water now and then, but apparently he regards it as something to be ashamed of and only does it in private with all the shades drawn. 

All that may be very well for the Britons, but it makes it a little tough on the tourists. Except rain incessantly falling from 17

TONGUE OUT IN THE RAIN 

the skies, the tourists in England see no water. Having been brought up on it, so to speak, it is not long before they notice the absence, and being strangers they are hard put to know what to do about it. There are no drinking fountains on the streets, no jugs of water in their hotel rooms and no water on the trains except that in the lavatories which the passengers are specially warned not to drink. But the hardest test of all for strangers is when they enter the restaurants and see not a drop in sight. 

All around them are happy diners munching dry bread and the like with nothing whatsoever to wash it down with except maybe a small jug of ale. And all of the other diners seem to be enjoying themselves. The longer he looks the thirstier becomes the tourist who has probably had nothing to drink for two days except what he could get by sticking his tongue out in the rain. He tries to eat but his tongue is too thick and his throat too dry for him to swallow. Pride prevents him from acknowledging his weakness, but along about the third bite of the ubiquitous Yorkshire pudding, thirst conquers all. "Water," he croaks in an agonizing voice to the nearest menial. "Water?" the waiter replies incredulously. "Water," the tourist repeats in a hoarse whisper. "Thank you," says the waiter. Five minutes later he returns with a tall pitcher of tepid water, and sets it contemptuously on the table, leaving the tourist to pour his own.

Douarnanez is at the tip of one of the fingers of that long arm of France reaching out into the Atlantic. It's a night's ride from Paris; as far as from Kansas City to Chicago. The 11 or 12 thousand Bretons who dwell in the mossy old stone houses along the narrow streets that wind up the hill from the water live entirely of, by, and for the humble sardine. At early morning the men of the town scramble into their several hundred small boats and go out with the tide to the places where the sardines play. The departure of the men leaves Douarnanez a dead village. The departure of the tide leaves the harbor a morass of mud that only one word can describe. It stinks. 

Late afternoon, though brings the boats, the tide, and the town's one big hour of the day. It is picturesque. The boats come racing down the bay, with sails of burnt sienna, vermillion, and every lighter shade of orangey red, over hulls of bright blue or green. 

They elbow one another for places at the quay. Out of the holds begins to flow a torrent of silvery sardines. They are poured into flat wooden trays and hurried to the fish depots and canning factories that line the shore. A day or two poking around a typical town over here gives one a clearer perspective on both his own country and France. Take 

Tours, for instance, because that is where we happen to be at this writing. It's 150 miles southwest of Paris in the direction of Bordeaux. The new city hall of Tours, the front of which is covered with sculptures not too good, but not too bad either, was built about the time of our Civil war, or slightly before most of the towns of Kansas were even planned. The new bridge over the Loire river to replace the old one which finally wore out, was completed not long before our Revolutionary war. Life is different here. You go to a saloon to find a railroad time table. There probably isn't a sash window in all France; they are hinged at the sides like doors. There are no screens anywhere and only an occasional fly who looks ill at ease as though he knew he hadn't any business there. You toss your baggage out the window of a train as you come into a station and a porter always seems to be there to catch it. It is not etiquette to get on a bus or street car without running down the street after it. Stamps are purchased at tobacco stores.

The legend of beautiful French women, like most other legends, is only a fable. We haven't seen even a single good looking one in a week, and it hasn't been from lack of looking. 

Along about the time of the Renaissance it became the fashion of the members of French court to build country houses along the banks of the Loire and its tributaries. In a radius of forty miles from Tours there are dozens of them; some in ruins, some now private homes, and some two dozen now the property of the government and available for public inspection. These chateaux of the Loire valley are the principal reason for all the tourist business in these parts. And the business is good, although fully seven-eighths of it is strictly French. 

Compared to Versailles these palaces are not so big. Only a few hundred rooms in some of them, with stables for 1500 horses. 

But they sufficed, so that when the Duc told his good wife that the King and his court would be over for the week-end, she simply started one hundred maids fixing the spare bedrooms and was not the least upset.

Although most of them have a slightly military look and a few were built on the towers of earlier castles, they were not fortresses, but simple country places. Rising above thick groves of trees on the hillsides with white stone facades and slate roofed Norman towers, even today they remain picturesque and not without their beauty. Their history, though, is what gets you. 

Some of the chateaux were built by the kings themselves.

The Medicis were quite a bunch. They had the acquisitive sense to a degree that makes John D. Rockefeller in his active days seem wastrel in comparison. The family started as simple merchants, traders and money lenders. It is not coincidence that pawnbrokers have adopted as their universal symbol the three gold balls from the Medici coat of arms. Each generation of these early 

Medicis not only kept hold of everything its ancestors had handed down to it, but added considerably to the store. The result was that by about the time of Columbus, the Medicis owned most of Florence. 

It was only a natural development that they should be made Dukes and entrusted with the government of their city. 

They held that position until the family died out two and a half centuries later. And not only did they rule Florence but other members of the prolific group became popes, cardinals, and generals, while their spare women folk were being married off to most of the reigning houses of Europe.

The Medicis may have been grasping, but to their credit, they did well by their city. Most of the buildings and art treasures of Florence today are by-products of the Medician glory. The castles they and their cohorts built as residences were constructed so lavishly and so well that most of them are still in use today as public buildings, art galleries, and homes. So many of these structures have survived that Florence has even now a distinctly medieval appearance in its older parts along both banks of the Arno. 

When you consider the native bad taste of the Medicis, Florence was lucky to accumulate as much first rate art through them as it has. The reason is probably that the painters and sculptors of those days were not artists living off crusts in attics, but had just as highly developed commercial senses as the Medicis, themselves.

There is a greater variety of uniforms to be seen on the streets of Italy than probably in any other country in the world today. Venice is incredible. It is unique, strange, and beautiful. 

So much so that it all seems a little theatrical. Even after you have spent several days soaking it up, looking at St. Mark's, taking in the

Venice reached its day of glory fully seven hundred years ago and hasn't changed much since. It is a city of nearly 200,000 built on a series of tiny islands in what were little more than salt marshes at the head of the Adriatic sea. In its day of power it had great fleets that traded with all the eastern Mediterranean. Its sailors brought back a liking for things Bysantine which is still reflected in most of its architecture. It was rich in a sense that surpasses modern imagination. The homes of its rulers and its wealthy traders that still line the Grand Canal were so lavish and picturesque that the hotels and antique establishments and insurance companies which today occupy them have been unable to destroy in the least the sense of grandeur they convey. 

Venice is probably the only city of its size in the world today that gets along without wheeled vehicles of any kind. Its trafficway are canals and everything moves by boats. Gondolas for passengers, barges for heavy hauling and small steamers in lieu of street cars along the Grand Canal. There are motor boats, but they seem an anachronism. 58

At Last, Paris 

There is one Paris that is not a very attractive place. It is made up of street hawkers of maps of the city, dirty postcards, and American newspapers. Of shops that sell New York merchandise at New York prices. Of hotels where travelers may obtain all the comforts of home including eggs for breakfast. Of porters in railway stations who always demand more, regardless of how large a tip they are given and curse loudly when they do not get it. Of long lines of tourists patiently waiting for letters from home at the American Express. Of sight-seeing buses with English speaking guides. Of the Louvre which requires thirty pages in a guide book for the casual listing of its treasures and wears off to the ankles the feet of anyone who attempts to see them all. Of bars where a spoken word of French is almost as rare as conversation in Russian in Kansas. Of cabarets where champagne is eight dollars a bottle and the fan dancers have outgrown their fans. 

That Paris is, unfortunately, the one that all too many tourists see. After a day or two in it before rushing to their steamers, it is no wonder that they write letters to their congressmen demanding that the war debts be paid and insist for years thereafter that the French are a scurvy, thieving race who should fight their own wars unassisted hereafter. 

There is another Paris, however, inhabited by nearly three million Frenchmen, which is one of the most pleasant and satisfying cities in the world. Its principal streets are tree lined and filled with grey stone buildings of a monumental sort. Its side streets are quietly colorful. Its churches, particularly the small ones that get only footnotes in Baedecker are charming. Everywhere it has parks with vast expanses of formal gardens and long shady avenues of trees with pieces of first rate statuary at intervals along them. At every hand there are archways through modern buildings that lead to courtyards lined with houses that still look exactly as they did in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Its neighborhood markets spread out along side streets, its squares with children sailing toy 66

And even the subways in which the air has not been changed since they were built thirty years ago have an atmosphere all their own.

The Frenchmen who inhabit this Paris are doubtlessly a self-seeking lot, intent on getting ahead. They find time to take two hours for lunch with their business houses tight shut meanwhile, however. They manage to take an hour for a political discussion over glasses of beer in the cafes at any hour of the day or night they have a mind to. In many cases they shut up shop completely during the month of August for their annual vacations. To tourists they may not have quite the affability and graciousness toward strangers the Italians have, but they do concede the strangers the full right to do exactly as they please, so long as they are accorded the same privilege in return. 

This Paris is no flashy town with points of interest that can be seen in a day leaving one with a desire to catch the first trian out in the evening. It requires a month to know it and six months fully to appreciate its charms. But the time, if one had it to give, would be well spent. 

Most of the more celebrated sights of Paris owe their preservation to the thriftiness of the French, a quality they have possessed to a high degree since the days Julius Cæsar was describing them. They have gone on the theory that while there were times when it was desirable to decapitate a king, common sense dictated against throwing his filthy gold into the river or tearing down his palace out of sheer spite. On rare occasions an outburst of Gallic temperament may have resulted in the destruction of some building of irreplacable beauty because it once housed royalty, but normally during the uprisings the contents of the royal purses have been providentially distributed among the populace and the palaces turned over to more democratic uses in good states of repair. 

This thriftiness has also exhibited itself in times of peace for the benefit of succeeding generations' sight seers.

As far as we're concerned that's still only hearsay, but it is true we had only been there a few minutes yesterday, killing time and a "pernod" when we heard booming in our ear the "Well, Well, Well!" that always denotes the approach of some old friend who is overjoyed by the meeting. Turning, we found our ears were not wrong. 

It was one of our oldest and best loved friends. A fellow who had been on the Ile de France coming over and with whom we had swapped at least a couple of "Good Mornings" as our paths crossed while marching the deck. Nor is that quite as paradoxical as it may sound. Ask any traveler in foreign parts. Coming over one gets to recognize the faces of most of the other passengers. He may have nothing to do with them at sea, may even avoid them, but despite himself he comes to recognize them on sight. And what a comfort that later is to prove.

Spend a couple of weeks knocking around in strange places where all the people except yourselves have the most annoying habit of speaking quite fluent French or German, or Greek, and when one of those familiar shipboard faces shows up, you greet it as that of a most intimate friend and immediately start exchanging inner-most secrets. All of which is the reason there was such a joyous reunion at a table outside the Cafe de la Paix yesterday afternoon and why, once his tongue had been lubricated by a couple of small beers, he proceeded to give us a more intimate account of his travel experiences than he ever will supply his friends back home. 

He was exhibit A of why some Americans should never come to Europe. He had had a horrible time. He hated the French, France and everything that pertained to them. His only solace was that he was sailing for home the following day. 69

He was from New York. Either a bachelor or a widower of a good many years standing who had finally gratified his life long ambition to go abroad. To display our temerity, if not our sound judgment of art, we submit that Rodin is the greatest sculptor who ever lived. 

Michael Angelo's and some of the other old boys' statues may give the observer the sense of greater technical skill, but no one could hack on a piece of stone and produce an object with as much force, power, and feeling in it as could Rodin. Most tourists, though, seem to miss it, even though it is practically across the street from Napoleon's tomb. 

There are vacant rooms and "For Rent" signs everywhere in Paris which everyone who can recall the days of '31 and '32 knows is a sure sign of hard times. There is none of the new building that was so conspicuous in London. 

Early August is the time when all the famous Parisian dressmakers hold their showings of new models for the coming season. 

What a break it is for traveling Americans (male) however, that the exhibitions are private and that sales are made only to buyers for American department stores.

The French government owns the railways, makes cigarettes, sells salt, and distributes gasoline, but its biggest business is doubtlessly the Historical Monuments. They are operated on a strictly commercial basis under the direction of a member of the cabinet. 

The government has acquired title to dozens of the best of the old chateaux, prisons, churches, castles, echoing canyons, and spots of scenic grandeur. In the case of the buildings it has spent tremendous sums restoring them to substantially the condition they were in during the good old days. It has staffed them with old soldiers who act as custodians and guides and it charges three francs, or approximately twenty cents admission to any one of them, thus earning a very good return on its investment. Stormy Weather 

The signs in Europe are so clear today, that even the most casual tourist cannot mistake them. War is in the air. Another big war that conceivably could be in progress within a week, possibly will have started before a month has passed, but inevitably will be raging within two years.

There is no joy in Europe at the prospect. No desire for a place in the sun. No fierce lust for conquest. No new generation of youth eager for its battles. Only a deep sense of fatalism about it on the part of young and old, everywhere. No nation wants war. 

All realize that the next war, from the standpoint of destructiveness of life and property, will be as much worse than the World war as it in turn was contrasted with the Civil war. All appreciate that this onrushing conflict offers as genuine a threat to 20th century civilization as the invasions of the Germanic tribes did to that of the Romans. Yet each nation is convinced that the actions of its neighbors have made another great war inevitable and each is grimly resolved to fight to the bitter end for what it considers its very existence.

The reasons for this situation are, of course, economic. A majority of the European nations, as the treaty of Versailles established them, were economic impossibilities. In some of the others the disturbances of the world depression have left large portions of the populations unable to provide for themselves. In the remainder so much substance has been dissipated in vain attempts to maintain the positions of political dominance won in the last war, that their people are in no better condition than those of the others. 

The dictatorships and the new forms of government since the World war have been possible because they promised economic cure-alls. Particularly in the Fascist countries, Italy and Germany, they have supplied many surface indications of success. They have carried to completion great public works programs, cut unemployment, established order, and brought more material comforts to a majority of their peoples. But the weakness of their currencies 74

THE COLLAPSE OF FRANCE 

is proof of how far they are from reaching their goals and how near their day of reckoning is at hand.

The conspicuous development in Europe today is the collapse of France, both internally and as a dominant world power. 

France came out of the last war the number one nation on the continent. She was supreme from a military standpoint in western 

Europe. She buttressed her position by creating the Little Entente of Poland, Jugo-Slavia and Czechoslovakia and training and subsidizing their armies. The cost of maintaining that position is probably one of the principal reasons France today is on the way back to being a second rate power.

The Little Entente is getting away from France. She is no longer feared. The division of her own people within has sapped her power without. Italy senses the situation. Germany sent her troops back into the Rhineland in open defiance, as proof of its appreciation of the fact. France's present government is belatedly attempting to meet the depression problems with widesweeping new dealish actions, but its position is not secure enough and it has acted too late to restore French prestige. 

England has beat back astonishingly. As always she has succeeded to a high degree in playing off the continental nations, one against the other, to her own economic advantage. But England, too, has her troubles. She realizes, without admitting it, that Mussolini has openly and successfully defied her. His conquest of 

Ethiopia has created serious empire problems for England for which she still vainly seeks solution. Airplanes have taken away completely her former isolation and England is hysterically spending incredible sums for armament. England's present campaign to provide gas masks for the civilian populations of its larger cities is the most significant proof of her uncertainty and disquietude.

Spain today is the open sore spot. Its revolution is even more serious and more sanguinary than American newspapers thus far have pictured it. There are many factors in it-land policies, the church, the Carlists, the Basque separatists, the republicans, the army, the monarchists, and a dozen more or less separate leftist factions. Spanish. The general lines, however, have not only European, but world wide meaning.
