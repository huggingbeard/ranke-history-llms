3 May 1956 

Original: English UNITED NATIONS DISARMAMENT COMMISSION ANNEX 9. SUB-COMMITTEE OF THE DISARMAMENT COMMISSION UNITED STATES OF AMERICA: SUMMARY MEMORANDUM 

(Presented in relation to the remarks of the United States representative at the eighty-fifth meeting of the Sub-Committee)

The following eight interrelated points reflect the basic policy of the United 

States Government on disarmament: 

1. An immediate beginning on important, gradual, multilateral reductions in armaments, armed forces, and military expenditures can be made under a sound disarmament agreement with adequate inspection; for example, as a basis of measurement and in a specific manner to be mutually agreed, reducing to force levels of 2.5 million men for the U. S. S. R. and the United States. 

2. Comprehensive disarmament and drastic reductions can only be carried out safely as parallel progress is made in the solution of important political issues in the world, as otherwise the dangers of war would be increased. 

3. The nuclear threat can be brought under control and this can be facilitated on a scientifically feasible basis by working out suitable and safeguarded arrangements so that future production of fissionable materials anywhere in the world would not be used to increase the stockpiles of explosive weapons, and so that, in addition, joint and repeated transfers of fissionable materials from existing stockpiles would be made for peaceful purposes. 

4. An adequate and effective disarmament inspection system in this age of swift jet planes and nuclear weapons, and in view of the vast territories involved in the world, must include effective aerial inspection, as well as ground and financial inspectors; should encompass forces and facilities maintained outside of their own national borders; and must constitute a system to provide against great surprise attack; and such an inspection system is essential for a sound agreement. An unsound agreement would soon be a source of suspicions, doubts, charges, and countercharges and would increase tensions. 

5. The objectives of the first practical steps of a disarmament plan should be: a. decrease the nuclear threat over the world; b. reduce the burden of armaments; c. provide against surprise attack; d. increase economic development; e. lessen tensions; f. prevent a future arms race of either a conventional or nuclear nature; g. prepare for further stages of a comprehensive programme for disarmament; and h. improve the prospects for a just and lasting peace. 

6. Preliminary steps of small-scale demonstration of inspection methods and technical exchange of inspection teams would be desirable to facilitate joint study of the practicable problems involved and to advance the negotiations for a prospective sound agreement.

1955, would prove to be a beginning for a significant move toward disarmament and a lasting peace. 

8. Within the foregoing broad concepts and for the purpose of advancing the stated objectives, the United States Government continues to be ready and willing to move to meet the views of other Governments. ANNEX 10. SUBCOMMITTEE OF THE DISARMAMENT COMMISSION General DC/SC.1/46 

4 May 1956 English

Original: English/French CANADA, FRANCE, UNITED KINGDOM, AND UNITED STATES OF AMERICA: DECLARATION 

I. In order to promote peace, security, and prosperity through disarmament, the delegations of Canada, France, the United Kingdom, and the United States reaffirm their determination to seek agreement on a developing programme of disarmament in accordance with their mandate from the United Nations. This programme should begin without delay and should include measures, under effective international control, to end the present competition in armed forces and armaments of all kinds. (1) The programme should proceed by stages. Progress from one stage to another must depend upon the satisfactory execution of the preceding stage and upon the development of confidence through the settlement of major political problems. (2) The programme should begin, under effective international control, with significant reductions in armed forces, to such levels as are feasible in present unsettled world conditions. There should be corresponding reductions in conventional armaments and in military expenditures. Further reductions would be carried out as world conditions improved. (3) The programme should provide that, at an appropriate stage and under proper safeguards, the buildup of stockpiles of nuclear weapons would be stopped and all future production of nuclear material devoted to peaceful uses. (4) The programme should provide for a strong control organization with inspection rights, including aerial reconnaissance, operating from the outset and developing in parallel with the disarmament measures. The control measures should also provide against major surprise attack. This is particularly important so long as it is impossible to account for past production of nuclear material. (5) Preliminary demonstrations of inspection methods on a limited scale would help to develop an effective control system and could bring nearer agreement on a disarmament programme. (6) Provision should be made for the suspension of the programme, in whole or in part, if a major State failed to carry out its obligations or if a threat to peace under Chapter VII of the United Nations Charter should occur. 

II. The proposals introduced in the Disarmament Subcommittee by the delegations of France, the United Kingdom, and the United States are inspired by these principles. The Soviet delegation did not accept these proposals. The differences between the position of the Soviet delegation and those of the other four delegations were not reconciled in the meetings of the Subcommittee. 

III. The four delegations recognize that in accordance with the instructions of the Disarmament Commission it is now time to report to the Commission. 

They express their conviction that a reconciliation of the opposing points of view is possible and necessary and they reaffirm their determination to continue their efforts within the United Nations, and particularly in the Disarmament Commission and its Subcommittee, to seek agreement on disarmament in accordance with the desire of the peoples of all nations.

NECESSITY OF A SENSE OF BALANCE 

Mr. STASSEN. Next I would say in my opening statement that of the greatest importance in this matter is that we all maintain a sense of balance. By that I mean there are tremendous difficulties_and complications in the road of attaining any sound agreement. It is important we never lose sight of these difficulties and complications and that we do not lose sight of the dangers of the world situation when you have a center of tremendous power on the other side of the face of the earth ruled by a group of men who follow an ideology so different than ours, an economic system so divergent, a social approach so opposite, a denial of religion, and yet have tremendous military strength.

When I say "balance," then I mean that with all of that, we should constantly have in mind that the objective of a lasting peace requires persistent work and that there are some grounds for encouragement that we are moving toward a sound agreement in this limitation of armaments.

ADVANCES IN SOVIET POSITION AT LONDON 

Then I turn to some of the specifics of the London session. The 

Soviets came forward in London with the most comprehensive ground inspection system that they had ever advanced and there was real progress on the possibilities of working out a ground inspection system. They also recognized that an inspection system must be in place before reductions could occur and this was an advance on their part. They also recognized that the approach of requiring a ban of the bomb when you could not have effective controls over that ban would not be an effective basis for agreement. In fact, in their 

March 27 proposal they advanced a reduction only in conventional armaments.

REQUIREMENTS OF AN INSPECTION SYSTEM 

It is the view of the United States and of the western nations as a whole that an effective inspection system, having in mind the great territories that are involved in Russia, in China, and in fact in Alaska, Canada, and the United States, cannot be worked out unless you include an aerial survey or aerial reconnaissance or aerial inspection system along with the ground inspection.

Inspectors limited to be on the ground on foot or in jeeps could never cover those vast territories rapidly enough or effectively enough to be reliable. We continue to be very firm in the United States position that an agreement must be covered by an effective and reliable inspection system or it is a bad agreement. It is our view that a bad agreement would be worse than no agreement and therefore we persistently negotiate to seek a sound agreement adequately covered by inspection.

SOVIET POSITION ON BANNING NUCLEAR WEAPONS 

Senator HUMPHREY. I don't want to interrupt the continuity of any statement that you wanted to make. I was interested in one of your observations, namely, the "Ban the bomb" slogan and proposal of the Soviet Union.

Is this committee to understand that the Soviet Union has altered its position relating to that earlier proposal?

Mr. STASSEN. That is right. In their March 27 proposal, they did not reiterate it as a part of the operative clauses of that proposal. In other words, while they had some preamble that referred to it, when it came down to the specific matters that they were submitting for negotiations, they recognized that no scientist anywhere in the world at the present time knows of an effective control method over this fissionable material out of which the bombs are made or any effective way to locate the bombs if they are once built and then hidden and shielded from view.

SOVIET PROPOSAL FOR GROUND INSPECTION 

Senator HUMPHREY. Are we to understand that the Soviet proposal on ground inspection was considerably more elaborate and comprehensive than anything they had proposed prior to this time? 

Mr. STASSEN. That is correct. It was a more advanced position, and I can give you a small reference to that to indicate how much they did advance in that regard. 

Senator HUMPHREY. Please do that if you have that available.

Mr. STASSEN. In their proposal of March 27, 1956, which is in the committee documents, and annex 5, and in that document, U. N. Disarmament Commission Subcommittee Document DC/SC.1/41 section 

2, paragraph 3, they say:

The control organ shall have permanently in all states parties to the agreement its own staff of inspectors recruited on an international basis having within the bounds of the control function which they exercise unimpeded access at all times to all objects of control. The objects of control are military units, stores of military equipment and ammunition, land, naval and air bases, factories manufacturing conventional armaments and ammunition. 

You see that that is quite a comprehensive statement of ground inspection and of the access of inspectors. This is the first time that they spelled out what objects of control would be under their proposal. 

And it was also the first time that they indicated clearly that the inspection should be in place before the reduction took place. 

Senator HUMPHREY. They exclude from those objects of inspection and control nuclear and thermonuclear development centers? Is that correct?

Mr. STASSEN. That is correct. This entire proposal of theirs on 

March 27 dealt with conventional armaments only so far as its worldwide applications are concerned. They had some nuclear clauses on some of the European applications.

Senator HUMPHREY. Does this Soviet proposal for ground inspec tion accord in general with the United States views so far as ground inspection is concerned?

Mr. STASSEN. I would put it this way. If they went relatively as far on aerial inspection I would say an agreement would be near at hand, that is, they have gone so far on the ground side that the remaining negotiation would seem to be something you could look at quite hopefully. 

Senator HUMPHREY. In other words, the areas defined in the Soviet ground inspection proposal would be adequate to meet our present requirements relating to ground inspection?

Mr. STASSEN. There would still be the necessity of negotiating how many of these control posts there are and what kind of equipment they could have and their right of communicating rapidly to their own control centers and to the international center, so there would still be some negotiating to do. But the indications are that they opened up for ground inspectors in their proposal to an extent that makes it look like the remaining negotiation on the grounds would be rather hopeful.

SOVIET POSITION ON AERIAL INSPECTION Senator HUMPHREY. Senator Pastore? 

Senator PASTORE. What are the arguments that they use in their persistent objection to the aerial inspection plan? How do they justify their position?

Mr. STASSEN. They say that they do not want the United States to be able to have a complete picture of the Soviet Union. They say that they feel that ground inspection would be enough. They say that the United States wants too much inspection. 

Senator PASTORE. Then on what do you base your opinion that the outlook is several degrees brighter as to a permanent agreement, when the crux of the whole thing is aerial inspection?

Mr. STASSEN. Because of two things. One, that in this March 27 proposal for the first time they made a reference to aerial inspection. 

They said that "At a specified stage of the execution of the general disarmament program, when confidence among states has been strengthened, the countries concerned shall consider the possibility of using aerial photography as one of the methods of control." 

Of course, there was extensive discussion then of how that would be brought in and under what circumstances and how would confidence be strengthened. And in my talk with Mr. Khrushchev he made a direct reference that aerial photography would come with greater confidence. There would be needed more confidence before you had aerial photography and aerial reconnaissance. Then you have this big question of how do you improve confidence between the two sides. 

I believe that the visit of General Twining to the Soviet Union, for example, which is one thing that came up subsequent to, and not entirely disconnected from, our 7 weeks' talks may lead to a reciprocal visit as the President indicated yesterday in his press conference and that little steps like this may gradually lead to this mutual openness of the air that is so essential.

SOVIET POSITION ON PRELIMINARY STEPS 

Furthermore, we presented at London a proposal that each side take a small strip of land, 100 miles wide and two or three hundred miles long, a strip of land that had within it some military installations but none that were supersensitive and let each side go in with inspectors on the ground and aerial photography and try the system out and compare notes. 

They never rejected that sampling proposal. That has not been accepted. They asked questions about it but knowing how slowly these matters must move in each government and how much is at stake I would be inclined to feel that this matter of exchanging technical missions and trying out inspections is a matter under active consideration in the Soviet Union even as we continue to study how we turn the long negotiations into practical steps. 

SIGNIFICANCE OF EXCHANGE OF MILITARY PERSONNEL Senator HUMPHREY. Governor, in reference to General Twining's visit, you indicated that this may have some relationship to the 7 weeks' conferences in which you participated. Do you have any idea as to what General Twining will be permitted to see in the Soviet Union? 

Is this limited to the air show or is this a more extensive visit? 

Mr. STASSEN. I would not make a prediction at this time as to how much he shall see. But I would say that I believe that he will see more than any United States general has seen since the end of World War II of the Soviet armament situation. 

Senator HUMPHREY. Do you look with favor upon this exchange of technical and military personnel?

Mr. STASSEN. I do under proper safeguards and with being intelligently alert as to all the ramifications of it. 

Senator HUMPHREY. Do you feel that we have as much to gain from this as the Soviet Union or more than the Soviet Union? 

Mr. STASSEN. I would put it a different way. I think the cause of peace gains from it. In other words, if we are going to improve the chance of a durable peace then we have to look at these things from the standpoint of whether they advance the mutual interests of the Soviet Union and the United States and mankind as a whole in a durable peace. That is a somewhat different emphasis that I put upon it than you will sometimes hear. But the basis of our studies is this underlying fact. With weapons of the destructive capability that the 

H-bomb now represents, with these weapons having been completed on both sides of the world, it is elementary that a war, regardless of how it began or how it ended, would result in a net loss to both sides. If that basic principle is correct, then it means you begin to develop a mutuality of interest in steps to prevent such a war, even among nations that have very little else that they have a mutual interest about. 

SOVIET RECOGNITION OF DESTRUCTIVENESS OF THIRD WORLD WAR 

Senator HUMPHREY. Do you feel that the Soviet leaders have come around to that appreciation and recognition of the facts of the possibility of thermonuclear war?

Mr. STASSEN. I feel there is increasing evidence that they have and that it predominantly dates from the summit conference at Geneva in the forthright discussions there. I believe that at Geneva President Eisenhower turned the face of the world toward peace. We will only know when somebody can look back at the matter from a century later.

But we are now living in the midst of a very important decade. 

Senator HUMPHREY. The recent visit of the American physicists and scientists to the Soviet Union bears upon this problem of disarmament, if not directly at least indirectly. I noticed in the press, reports that the Soviet Union had had some difficulty with some of its scientists in terms of the development of weapons of the thermonuclear type similar to that which our Atomic Energy_Commission had once had to wrestle with relating to Oppenheimer. Is there any indication that the fact that there is a growing number of competent trained skilled scientists, who understand the full potentialities of thermonuclear destruction, is having a bearing upon Government policy in the Soviet Union?

Mr. STASSEN. In my judgment the Soviet scientists and Soviet military leaders are quite likely advising their top governmental officials as to the extreme destructive quality of these weapons, even as our scientists and our military leaders are advising our Government and our senatorial and congressional committees. 

Senator PASTORE. May I ask a question?

Senator HUMPHREY. Yes. 

INTERNATIONAL SITUATION SINCE GENEVA 

Senator PASTORE. I am interested in what you said, Governor, about the meeting at Geneva. We held a meeting at Harvard University in 

Cambridge several weeks ago and most of the students of political science who testified before our committee that day were unanimous in their opinion that the free world had lost ground since the meeting at the summit. Apparently you don't agree with that?

Mr. STASSEN. No, I do not agree with that. 

Senator PASTORE. Would you tell us why?

Mr. STASSEN. Because I believe that the free world will have its greatest future under conditions of a prolonged peace. That is, I have a deep faith in the future of the concept of freedom and the dignity of the human being and that if the summit meeting improved the prospects of a durable peace-and I believe it did-while that is an advantage to the Soviet also in the sense of avoiding the mutual devastation that would come from war, I think in the long run, it will prove out that the concept of freedom and of individual dignity and worth of the human being will win.

In other words, I take a longer view than the group you speak of. 

Senator PASTORE. Well, if that be the case, then how do we explain the fact that the Foreign Ministers meeting last fall at Geneva fell flat on its face? Why wasn't that spirit carried on?

Mr. STASSEN. That concept didn't fall on its face. What was evident at that time was that the Soviet Union was not ready to make concessions on immediate difficult political problems like the reunification of Germany in freedom or at that time the opening of the Soviet Union to extensive reciprocal contacts. They are very rigid

and very negative on the question of reunification of Germany in freedom. On matters of East-West contacts, they have moved more slowly and more bit by bit since Geneva rather than entering into a widespread agreement at that time as to what exchanges would take place.

I think, for example, the recent election in Italy is further evidence that the summit meeting was successful from the free world standpoint. There were some who were saying that the summit meeting meant that the Communists made great gains in support in the rest of the world. Actually the Communists lost ground in Italy in the popular vote, which I think has many factors entering into it. 

I do feel we have had there one of the most brilliant and successful ambassadors in the whole world in Ambassador Clare Booth Luce and many considerations are involved. This was one of the first nationwide tests of sentiment since Geneva and the Communists lost ground.

NEW TYPE OF STRUGGLE WITH COMMUNISM 

It is very difficult to judge these intangible matters as to who is winning or who is losing until you get to a concrete instance. There isn't anywhere in the world that the Communists have taken over any country in these last years. It is taking a new form-this struggle is. 

We seem to be now moving into a comprehensive competition that involves economic, social, political, and in instances, subversive activities, with some reduced danger of the open conflict of world war. 

But I feel that if we move up in an alert and intelligent way to the new factors in this worldwide competition, that the outlook is favorable.

Senator HUMPHREY. I think this was the point that the men who testified at MIT and Harvard were emphasizing: that the Geneva 

Conference did give what you say, a sort of a reduced tension at least temporarily, and gave us what we might call time. They extended their argument to the assertion that recently the Soviet Union has moved rather rapidly into this new area of challenge on the economic, political, and diplomatic front. Admiral Radford in testifying before the Foreign Relations Committee recently said positively that the major weakness was on the political front in terms of our relationships with the Soviet Union. 

Insofar as NATO and the Far East were concerned, I remember questions were asked of him specifically. He said he thought militarily we were able to keep abreast and our position was relatively good, but in specific response to a question directed I believe by Senator 

Fulbright or Senator Sparkman, he felt that in the political area we had fallen back somewhat. That testimony I am sure has been brought to your attention. I think this was the agreement of Rostow and Millikin and others who testified before us at Boston-not so much that the Geneva Conference itself had not done good in terms of the removal of some tensions militarily, but that the competition since had been stepped up considerably on the economic and political fronts. 

There we have been somewhat inflexible in not meeting and moving rapidly enough.

I don't think they were despondent at all, but they were concerned. 

Senator PASTORE. That is General Twining's impression, but the impression I got from the witnesses at Cambridge was that because of

the effect of the attitude that was built up at Geneva, the free world had been lulled into a sense of false security.

Senator HUMPHREY. I think they were concerned about the danger of this lull.

REASONS FOR SOVIET OBJECTION TO AERIAL INSPECTION 

Senator PASTORE. Let me ask the Governor this question: Do you feel that, as wrong as they may be, the Russians are sincere in their objections to aerial inspection? Or is their objection another method of talking nice and speaking with smiles and then when it gets down to signing their name on a piece of paper always having another excuse not to do it? They can always come forward with a plan that looks better as an alternate and use that as a means of refusing your proposal. Do they feel it is too wide a range to cover, or do you feel that it is one thing they can pin their objections on in not signing an agreement or coming to terms?

Mr. STASSEN. I would answer that in this way. I do not think that anyone can ever be categorical as to what the intentions of the Soviet Union may be. It is a different system of government. It is a very difficult thing to analyze with any assurance. I would say that there appeared to be an approach of a serious desire to negotiate an agreement in these 7 weeks in London. We have to observe their continuing conduct and their proposal. Each of these things are studied very carefully as to their alternative possible meanings. 

We do have the fact that after a long period in which they said, "No, no, no," in Austria, they finally one day said, "Yes," and they have actually pulled the Red Army out of Austria; they have actually made an economic settlement with Austria. 

Austria today has its sovereignty reestablished and is a happy independent nation with the opera in full swing and a much brighter outlook in that area of Europe.

We do have the fact that on the atoms for peace development they said, "No," for four distinct times. Then finally they said, "Yes," and since that time, you see, United States scientists have been in Moscow and have been given considerable information as to the research programs and activities on the peaceful side of the Soviet atomic effort. And they are getting this international atomic energy agency definitely set up for cooperation on the peaceful alternative in the use of nuclear energy. So we must always be wary and cautiousthat I am thoroughly in accord with. And we approached this 7 weeks' negotiations on that basis. 

Senator PASTORE. May I ask you one more question? 

Mr. STASSEN. Yes.

OBJECTIONS TO SOVIET GROUND INSPECTION PLAN 

Senator PASTORE. Using that as a predicate, then why don't we accept their ground inspection plan? What would we have to lose by it!

Mr. STASSEN. It would not in fact provide against the possibility of a big surprise attack in our view because it is not comprehensive enough, it is not fast enough, too many things could be happening that

Original: ENGLISH/FRENCH France and the United Kingdom: Working document: proposed synthesis First stage of disarmament SECTION I 

1. On the signature of a disarmament treaty all participating States declare that they regard themselves as prohibited, in accordance with the provisions of the Charter of the United Nations, from the use of nuclear weapons except in defence against aggression. 

2. Meeting of the general assembly of the international disarmament organization to designate the nonpermanent members of the executive committee and approve the appointment of the director-general of the control organ. 

3. Recruitment of the first elements of the control organ. 

4. Freeze, consisting of— (a) Initial declaration of the levels of armed forces and conventional armaments, including submission of military blueprints and relevant budgetary and accounting documents.

(b) Undertaking not to increase beyond declared levels or to exceed total declared expenditure. 

5. Simultaneously with the entry into force of the freeze, the control organ, in order to provide against surprise attack, starts to operate by means of aerial surveys, and by establishing control posts at ports, railway junctions, main highways and airports. In order to verify the information supplied, the control organ has the right to send out mobile control teams and financial inspectors. 

6. While the control organ is building up its efficiency, meeting of general assembly of the international disarmament organization to determine levels of armed forces and conventional armaments for other participating States, which should be considerably below the levels fixed for the five permanent members of the Security Council. 

7. Report of the control organ that it is ready to supervise the initial steps in reducing armed forces and conventional armaments. 

8. Report is accepted by executive committee. 

9. Initial steps in agreed reductions by the five Powers, and consequential reduction in military budgets. 

10. Establishment of special branch of control organ which will supervise the limitation of nuclear test explosions.

NOTE. For procedure for passage from first to second stage, see Annex. 

Second stage of disarmament SECTION II 

1. Limitation of nuclear test explosions comes into effect. 

2. Completion of first half of agreed reductions by the five Powers and consequential reductions in military budgets. A proportion of the savings would be allocated to improving standards of living throughout the world and in particular in the less-developed countries. 

3. Continuation and development of the functions and methods of control and inspection. 

4. Other powers start on first half of reductions to agreed levels. 

5. Development of the control organ so that it will be able to verify the cessation of nuclear production for military uses.

NOTE. The procedure for passage from second stage to third stage shall be the same as shown in Annex for passage from first stage to second stage. 

Third stage of disarmament

SECTION III 

1. Prohibition, under control, of nuclear test explosions for military uses; nuclear explosions directed toward the application of atomic energy to peaceful uses may take place under controls, subject to the approval of an international scientific committee. Simultaneously, prohibition of manufacture of nuclear weapons. 

2. Carrying out of the third quarter of the agreed reductions by the five Powers. 

3. On completion of the above-mentioned reductions, a complete prohibition of the use of nuclear weapons. 

4. Other Powers start on the second half of their agreed reductions. 

5. Five Powers start on last quarter of agreed reductions; reductions by all participating States to be completed at the same time. 

6. Consequential reductions, in military budgets and allocation of a proportion of the savings to improving standards of living throughout the world and in particular in the less-developed countries. 

7. The control organ will remain in operation in order to insure continued observance of the provisions of the disarmament treaty. 

SECTION IV 

Suspension of prohibitions, limitations and reductions If the executive committee considers that an irregularity which is covered by Chapter VIII of the Charter has occurred, it shall immediately inform the Security Council or General Assembly, and shall suspend such of the prohibitions, limitations and reductions provided for in the treaty as it may consider necessary, pending a decision under the Charter by the Security Council or 

General Assembly.

SECTION V Final measures 

1. Convocation by international disarmament organization of an international scientific conference to examine the possibilities of eliminating stocks of nuclear weapons. 

2. Review by general assembly of the international disarmament organization of possibility of further reduction of armed forces and armaments.

1. Troop level

Do we and our western allies on the disarmament subcommittee project in our proposals an ultimate level to which the armed forces of all major nations would be reduced? In other words, is the 2,500,000 considered an intermediate step toward further reduction? 

Answer: The United States did not propose at London a specific force level figure below 2,500,000 since our proposal was for a beginning on arms control, limitation, and reduction that could take place prior to any political settlement. It was contemplated, and so stated, by the United States representative that, upon satisfactory completion of the proposed reductions by all parties, further reductions would be considered in light of the then existing state of international affairs and progress on the unresolved political questions that now divide the world. It is the belief of the United States Government that implementation of the proposed reductions to 2,500,000 in conjunction with improvement in the political situation would lead to further reductions.

Can the administration furnish the subcommittee with information as to why the earlier figure (1,500,000) was found to be inadequate and why the present figures are considered more suitable under an arms limitation agreement?

Answer: As indicated above, the United States has endeavored to formulate a plan of action that could be implemented under existing conditions, without requiring resolution of the various political issues. 

It has been our view that after 10 years of discussion of the arms question, it would be important if some initial action could be taken 

With regard to the previous American proposal of a 1,500,000 force level, it should be noted that it was never contemplated that the United States would reduce its forces to that level so long as there now.

, continued to be threats to international peace posed by the Communist world's refusal to conclude just political settlements, nor so long as there continued to be threats of aggression in Europe, the Far East, or the Near East. You will recall that the United States stated in 

1952 and subsequently-that the reduction then proposed should be made in a series of steps concurrent with progress on the solution of the major political problems. It must also be recalled that the 1952 force level proposal was based upon the premise that all nuclear weapons could and would be eliminated from the stockpiles of nations. 

It was then stated that with the verified elimination of nuclear weapons and with the settlement of political issues, the force level figures: of 1,500,000 would be adequate for American and free world security.

Unfortunately, however, the extensive studies undertaken during the last year and a half by the United States Government confirmed a fear which has been increasingly expressed from 1952 on, that because of the growing stockpiles of nuclear material it is no longer feasible to verify scientifically past production of nuclear material. Even though our search for scientific methods of accounting for past nuclear production continues, we are forced to accept the fact that in the forseeable future nuclear weapons will be a part of the arsenal of the Soviet Union. Under these circumstances the obligations of the United 

States military forces will have to include one important addition not contemplated in the 1952 proposal-the capacity to effectively counter with the continuing nuclear capability of the Soviet Union. 

It is our view that the 2,500,000 figure, representing as it does a level that could be reached under existing circumstances, offers the best prospect of making some initial forward movement of significance and at the same time retaining the necessary military strength to meet American security responsibilities throughout the free world under conditions in which political settlements have not yet been achieved, and at the same time maintain a capability to deal with the nuclear threat. Thus it best serves the objective of a just and durable peace. 

2. The aerial inspection plan 

(a) Has the United States obtained the concurrence of the countries in which United States bases are located to admit Soviet inspectors under an inspection plan? Have we carried on negotiations with any of these countries to obtain such concurrence? If so, would it be possible for the subcommittee to have a report on the progress of negotiations? 

Answer: The United States has maintained a very close liaison with its principal allies during the course of the disarmament negotiations. We have, of course, discussed the question of inspection of American facilities under the terms of the President's response to 

Marshal Bulganin.

The Soviet representative to the disarmament subcommittee first raised the question of inspection of bases on foreign soil in connection with President Eisenhower's "open skies" proposal. In the subcommittee the United States representative has taken the position that it would be realistic to include such a provision in the inspection agreement, subject, of course, to the concurrence of the states involved. 

Our allies of principal concern on this question, Great Britain and France, have been present at these negotiations, and are well aware of the ramifications of the problem. Specific negotiations would be

premature until the Soviet Union accepts the principal of the aerial survey. (b) If the United States includes its foreign military bases what is our position on exchanging military blueprints and conducting aerial reconnaissance with the Eastern European satellites and Communist China?

Answer: The United States proposed that the extension of the 

President's Geneva proposal to military installations of the United

States and the Soviet Union outside their national territories should be done upon a reciprocal basis. The degree of the extension of inspection to Soviet facilities within what is now the Communist bloc would be a subject for negotiation and would be conditioned by the number of United States military facilities outside our national territory which would be subject to inspection by the Soviet Union. 

While it is important to include ultimately all significant military powers within any arms agreement, it is considered most urgent, as an initial step, to provide reciprocal coverage of the greatest strength now in being in the U. S. S. R. and the United States. This beginning will serve as an example to all the world that the two greatest powers are sincere in their intent to renounce aggressive force as an instrument of national policy, and will encourage others to subscribe to an arms agreement for mutual benefit and future development of atoms for peace. (c) In the opinion of the executive branch, what type of legislation or Executive action would be needed to put the aerial inspection plan into effect in this country?

Answer: The exact type of legislation and congressional action which would be needed to put the aerial inspection plan into effect in this country would depend upon the final terms of the agreement, but it is clear that appropriate approval and concurrence of Congress would be essential and would be in accordance with our constitutional processes. The Department of Justice is represented on the interdepartmental President's Special Committee on Disarmament, and the legal problems of the nature to which you refer have been under study by the Department of Justice for some time. (d) Approximately how many inspectors would the United States need to carry out its part of the proposed inspection program if it were confined to the Soviet Union and the United States? How many qualified inspectors do we have at the present time? How long would it take to employ and train additional inspectors?

Answer: The number of American inspectors required, in the context of the President's Geneva proposal, would not be large if the inspection were done solely through aerial reconnaissance; this is due to the capability of aerial reconnaissance for rapid and extensive coverage of large areas and to the photointerpretative capability possessed by modern aerial reconnaissance units. The present United 

States aerial reconnaissance units are capable of performing this task at the present time. If the Soviet proposal for additional inspectors at key points such as airdromes, rail centers, and highways, etc., were included there would, of course, be a requirement for additional inspectors on the ground. The number of the inspectors would depend upon the number of check points which would be included. The 

Soviet Union has not, up to now, indicated the number of check points

in its proposal. Nevertheless, the United States believes that it would easily be able to meet the requirements of qualified inspectors for any reasonable number of such check points and the number of inspection personnel, while substantial, would be a very small fraction of the number of personnel now in military establishments of the respective countries. (e) If the inspection plan is carried out on a near worldwide basis, how do we propose that the question of the training and assignment of inspectors from many countries be handled? 

Answer: It is obvious that as any arms control arrangement is extended to additional countries, the problems of training and assignment of inspectors becomes more complicated. It is also true, however, that generally speaking the major military parties are the very nations with land mass so extensive as to require fairly large numbers of inspecting teams; moreover, in many of the states of the world the areas of concentration of military power are fairly limited and therefore subject more easily to inspection. The problem of the training and assigning of inspectors is nevertheless a very real one. It is for this reason that the United States has proposed-most recently in its working paper submitted at the London meetings-that the five members of the subcommittee of the United Nations Disarmament Commission agree promptly to conduct "a limited technical exchange mission for the analysis of the problems of control and inspection of conventional armaments designed to facilitate study and negotiation of a disarmament agreement and to prepare the exchange personnel for service. in inspection team leadership in a subsequent agreed system." It should also be pointed out that one of the ways in which the United States has proposed that the problem of effective assignment of inspectors be handled is through the use of member states as executive agents designed for specific assignments, with 60 percent of the personnel used by the executive agent to be nationals of that state. (f) What are the advantages and disadvantages to the United States of conducting a trial inspection plan, say between the United States and a member of NATO such as the United Kingdom or Canada?

Answer: The United States has given some consideration to the trial inspection plan suggested. The more obvious advantages would be that some practical experience in the day-to-day problems of an inspection operation would be obtained by the participating states and their participating personnel. It is also possible that the results of such a trial inspection would contribute to solution of the present differences between western nations and the Soviet Union on the inspection issue. However, it has been the view of the United States that the most beneficial trial inspection would be one involving the two major military parties, the United States and the Soviet Union. 

The more obvious needs of an effective inspection system are more likely to be seen by the Soviet Union if it is itself a participant; moreover, both parties are more likely to gain realistic experience if the trial inspection is carried out in territory with which neither side is familiar for this would be the case under any actual arms agreement. 

As you know, this Government has proposed that the United States and the Soviet Union carry out a small demonstration test of control and inspection including ground posts and aerial survey in a limited nonsensitive area, in both the United States and the Soviet Union, of

20,000 to 30,000 square miles including at least 1 port, 1 airfield, and 

1 railroad terminal. Such a demonstration test would not only give practical experience to both parties, but would facilitate the negotiation of a disarmament agreement. (9) Would an exchange of military blueprints and aerial inspection between the Arab countries and Israel contribute to peace in the Middle East, since on both sides there appears to be fear of surprise attack? Have we considered proposing that the United Nations indorse an inspection of this kind as a means of maintaining the truce in that area?

3. Level of armaments (a) Is it our present position that an agreement on the reduction of conventional armaments cannot take place independent of one on nuclear weapons? If so, what reasoning lies behind the position?

Answer: It is the United States position that the growing nuclear threat is the most fundamental factor in the present growth of de-. structive capabilities in the modern world. No arms agreement would ever be really sound until the nuclear weapons race is brought to a halt and measures initiated to begin to reduce the nuclear threat. 

Because nuclear weapons information is among the most sensitive of military information and because the nuclear control problem is very complex, it appears that complete control of nuclear weapons cannot come at the beginning of an arms agreement. It is also true that the limitations on means of delivery that would occur in any conventional agreement would have significant effects at least on the strategic capabilities of nuclear striking forces. To say all this, however, does not mean that the nuclear side should, or safely can be, ignored, for to ignore the question of nuclear weapons for any extended period of time while limiting conventional armaments would have certain very serious implications: a false sense of security and even unjustifiable complacency could result not only with regard to the degree of Soviet reasonableness but also with respect to the limitation that had actually been placed on the growth of destructive capabilities. If the nuclear area of armaments remained uncontrolled for an extended period of time there would be an inevitable concentration of military development in the uncontrolled nuclear field in a way that would in fact, circumvent the spirit of the limitations agreement. (b) Eventually what type of inspection system would be needed to check the armaments?

Answer: The inspection system- 

(1) Should be comprised of a single composite organization incorporating the requirements of each and all of its national and international components, with all necessary elements in place, approved, and ready to function at the beginning of each phase of inspection operations. (2) Must be capable of discharging its responsibilities at any point in inspection operations in the most effective and efficient manner, at

a minimum of cost in personnel, time, and in funds, and should be acceptable and feasible on a reciprocal basis among all participating states. (3) Should provide for the inclusion of integrated military ground, sea, air, and budget inspection, and consist jointly of personnel of signatory powers in appropriate numbers and types, including United 

States and U. S. S. R. nationals. 

(4) Control and inspection responsibilities should be carried out principally through the use of member states as executive agents designated for specific assignments. The majority of the personnel used by an executive agent on an assignment should be nationals of the executive agent's state. (5) Must be supported by a communications system which is designed to insure full, free, rapid, and uninterrupted exchange of information between and among elements of the control organization. (6) An inspection agreement should be designed to achieve the following objectives: (a) Decrease the nuclear threat over the world; (b) Reduce the burden of armaments; (c) Provide against surprise attack; (d) Increase economic development; (e) Lessen tensions; (ƒ) Prevent a future arms race of either a conventional or nuclear nature; (g) Prepare for further stages of a comprehensive program for disarmament; and (h) Improve the prospects for a just and lasting peace. (c) If conventional armaments are limited but nuclear weapons excluded from an agreement because they presumably cannot be controlled, does it follow that if a war starts the armed forces of the major powers will be equipped primarily with nuclear weapons? 

Answer: Not necessarily. First of all, any limitation of conventional armed forces and armaments will take into consideration the degree of settlement of major international issues. It is the policy of the administration that any arms agreement which we might endorse, regardless of how sound or adequate, must not involve deep reductions until progress is made in the solution of primary global issues. (d) With an agreed level of armaments conforming to an agreed level of manpower, how do the major powers propose to resolve the question of allowing the major powers to produce arms in surplus of their allowed levels to be sold to smaller nations having no arms production of their own?

Answer: Provisions governing the production and sale of arms to other countries could be provided for in the agreement. Such production and sale of these weapons, of course, would have to be strictly controlled within the terms of the agreement, and at the same time would be the subject of discussion and negotiation between the signatory members as each requirement was presented. It must be remembered, however, that ultimately the other countries would themselves be limited as to the amount of arms permitted, through their adherence to the arms limitation agreement.

4. Enforcement 

Does the current position of the United States Government respecting an arms limitation agreement among the major powers envision a system of penalties for violations, some method of enforcement?

Secretary Dulles discussed the possibility of an escape clause in an agreement. He said: "What we have in mind here, in the studies that we have so far carried on, would be an escape clause which would be considerably more clearcut and explicit and which, without necessarily involving a total renunciation of the disarmament agreement, would automatically allow the insured country to take action under the agreement, which would more than compensate for the disadvantage of the infraction which had been discovered."

Would it be possible to elaborate on this concept, as it is involved in present policy? Is a role in enforcement contemplated for the United 

Nations or other international agencies?

In view of the United States experience with the embargo imposed by the United Nations against Communist China and the blockade of North Korea, what if anything was learned from them which might be useful for enforcement under an arms control agreement?

Answer: Under present conditions, penalties can be levied only insofar as they can be enforced. The sanction power with regard to major states is presently limited. The United Nations could assert itself by recording a vote of moral condemnation upon a nation found to be in violation of an arms agreement, even if the violator were a permanent member of the Security Council; or its members could individually and collectively impose sanctions. You are, of course, aware of the very real power of the United Nations in the event that the permanent members of the Security Council are in agreement. However, the real teeth in an arms agreement, at least at the present time, lies in the right of individual signators to reconsider the terms of the agreement or abrogate the agreement and take such measures as are necessary to fulfill the requirements of their own defense. The right to take immediate steps for defense and security is inherent in the U. N. Charter. This prerogative is of especial significance today, because of the extremely narrow time gap that can exist between peace and war by virtue of the speed of modern delivery vehicles. With respect to the major powers, there would be in a sense a built-in sanction so long as inspection is adequate, for a violating major power would know that uncorrected violations on its part would probably result, at a minimum, in a resumption of a costly and dangerous arms race which would be hardly compensated for by the marginal advantage gained through an initial violation on its part, so long as the inspection system were able to ensure that a continuous series of violations could not go undetected. 

5. Nuclear weapons control (a) Are there any significant aspects of the Baruch plan which might be applicable today in an arms limitation agreement?

Answer: The Baruch plan was the result of a comprehensive and detailed analysis of the problem of the control of nuclear facilities, to ensure the use of nuclear energy for peaceful purposes only. It was designed to prevent the situation in which the world now finds itself.

It was never accepted by the Soviet Union. It has served as a basis for our present continuing search for adequate international control of the nuclear threat. We must face the fact of life that there exists today on both sides very large stockpiles of nuclear weapons. The 

Baruch proposal was designed to prevent the possibility of any slight diversion of nuclear materials for weapons purposes, for it envisaged a situation in which no nation would possess nuclear weapons, and under these conditions even the smallest diversion to weapons purposes could have significantly altered power relationships. (b) What is the opinion of the executive branch on the suggestion that an international scientific committee or conference be set up to work on a system for the detection of nuclear material?

Answer: It is the view of the executive branch of the Government that all measures should be exhausted to devise scientific methods for the detection and verification of stockpiles of nuclear material. It has been proposed in the past that the United States join with other nations of the world on a scientific committee to discuss ways and means for achieving this goal. Eventually such participation on a multilateral basis may be possible. However, at the present time, because of the sensitivity of certain aspects of the subject it is proposed that each individual nation conduct its own research and provide for appropriate consultation between itself and other governments. (c) If an agreement were reached among the major powers to ban the testing of nuclear weapons or, at least, certain types of nuclear weapons, could an inspection system be devised to determine within a high degree of accuracy that the agreement was not being violated? 

Answer: It is theoretically possible that within certain limits the normal large-scale testing of nuclear weapons in violation of an agreement could be detected. However, it is also theoretically possible that certain measures could be taken to prevent detection if a violating state were willing to put forth sufficient efforts to this end. It must also be borne in mind, however, that the degree of effectiveness of any such inspection would depend upon the number of inspection posts which the nuclear powers were willing to permit. The further complicating factor is that if the detection were to be obtained merely through observation carried on outside the borders of a nuclear power, there would arise questions as to the exact magnitude of the test even though it was discovered that a test had taken place; i. e., was the test just below or just under the agreed limits? These are some of the reasons why the United States has proposed that limitations upon nuclear tests should be a part of a more comprehensive agreement which would permit greater inspection facilities and would ensure greater accuracy in determining compliance. Answer: Since World War II, in the absence of a sound agreement, the most effective instrument for insuring peace has been the construction and maintenance of an adequate military posture. The lifeblood of this military strength has been a research and development program. 7. Chemical and bacteriological weapons

Witnesses before the subcommittee have stated that poison gas was not used during World War II, not because of agreements prohibiting its use, but because it was not militarily advantageous. It would be helpful to have information as to why this weapon was not considered militarily useful. Are bacteriological weapons subject to the same limitations as to utility as is gas? Are nuclear weapons, particularly of the tactical type? Are bacteriological and chemical weapons potentially as destructive of life as nuclear weapons? Is it as essential to cover these weapons as it is to cover conventional weapons in an arms control agreement? If so, has consideration been given to means by which they can be controlled through inspection or other systems?

Answer: Bacteriological weapons have special characteristics with respect to their limitations in comparison with chemical weapons. 

While there are obvious security considerations which prevent public treatment of this question, it is obvious that an aggressor nation could not be sure that the utilization of bacteriological weapons of certain characteristics would not ultimately backfire. Furthermore, there was considerable reluctance to employ chemical weapons during 

World War II because of the extreme fluidity of the battlefield.

Theoretically a case could be made for the thesis that bacteriological and chemical weapons are potentially as destructive of life as nuclear weapons, though the world generally is impressed with the proven destructive capability of nuclear weapons on a scale much greater than that of bacteriological or chemical weapons. It would certainly be necessary, in any comprehensive arms agreement, to provide against clandestine development and buildup of bacteriological and chemical weapons. While it would be fairly easy to secretly

concoct virulent batches of viruses, certain practical problems such as storage and delivery requirements would make reasonably effective control more practical than would appear to be the case at first glance. 

The principal safeguards to insure against the significant development of bacteriological and chemical capabilities are to be found in an effective and continuous system of disclosure and verification of all armed forces and armaments. In this way the possibility of massive-and thereby effective use of such weapons would be eliminated.
