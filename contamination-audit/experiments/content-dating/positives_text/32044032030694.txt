BASIS FOR CONCLUSIONS 

We undertook the survey without prejudice or predilection as to whether the duties should be performed by a civilian or by a military officer, and the considerations affecting that question are beyond the scope of the survey. It is obvious that a certain amount of the conclusions contained herein rest in part upon matters of opinion and judgment, as to which there may be fair differences. The conclusions stated are based, on the whole, on actual review of the operations of the individual jobs studied and upon the justification for the positions which has been furnished us in each case by the responsible military department. Where differences of opinion appear, the report shows both the survey conclusion and the department justification. 

JUSTIFICATIONS FOR DUAL POSITIONS 

On the subject of the departmental justifications for the dual staffing, it was frequently indicated to us that the duality is justified for the reasons (a) that a complete and direct military line of authority must be maintained, or (b) that the actual benefit from the work of the military incumbent is the training and development of the career officer whose assignment to the particular installation may be relatively brief. These considerations are beyond the scope of the investigation and are not evaluated by us. As to those officer billets justified on the basis that a military officer must be available to be accountable for the public property on a post, we are able to report that one of the services, the Army, has announced to us forthcoming revision of its policy to remove that requirement. 

CONTINUING STUDIES 

It is understood that the committee will start staff studies on the practices covered by the survey, and accordingly the findings on justifications are reported in their current status. To a considerable degree the justification for the dual staffing with military and civilian supervisors is now still in process of determination by the departments. 

The departments are continuing their studies to determine clear justifications for the supervisory positions on which questions raised by the survey still are unresolved, in anticipation that these questions will be resolved and justifications will be available during the committee's staff studies.

INTRODUCTION 

The chairman, House Post Office and Civil Service Committee, by letter of April 15, 1953, to the Comptroller General of the United States, requested the General Accounting Office to make a survey of a limited number of installations, or organizational segments, of the Departments of Army, Navy, and Air Force, to determine the extent of, and justification for, the use of civilian personnel in positions, that is, supervisory areas, to which military personnel also were assigned. 

This survey was to be limited to about 3 field installations of each of these 3 Departments, and selected organizational units of each operating at Pentagon or headquarters level, particular attention to be given to support activities or housekeeping functions. 

Preliminary discussions with the committee staff member designated to maintain liaison during the survey and with the committee counsel disclosed that the committee's immediate interest is in the supervisory positions where either dual supervision (military chief and civilian chief) or pyramiding (military chief and civilian assistant chief) is involved.

Action on the survey was initiated by the furnishing of preliminary advices to the Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Manpower, Mr. James M. Mitchell, who expressed both his great interest in the possibilities that might be afforded, by use of the survey findings, to effect economies through more effective use of available manpower, and his confidence that policy level officials of the Departments of the Army, Navy, and Air Force would welcome such a survey for this reason and lend all possible aid to its conduct. 

Later in June 1953 Mr. Mitchell arranged a conference attended by the committee counsel, a committee staff member, representatives of the General Accounting Office, Mr. James P. Goode, Deputy Assistant

Secretary of Air Force for Manpower, Personnel, and Organization, Mr. John H. Dillon, administrative assistant to the Secretary of Navy,

Col. A. Hartman, Chief, Organization Branch, Management Division, 

Office of the Comptroller of the Army, Mr. Mitchell for Department of Defense, and by other representatives of the named departments. 

The nature of the survey, as well as the guidelines tentatively established for its conduct, was explained at this conference, and the departments were invited to offer suggestions as to any possible improvements in contemplated survey techniques which might tend to give greater assurance of validity of the survey findings. The 

Army, Navy, and Air Force representatives gave assurances of utmost 1

cooperation with the survey teams and urged that constant liaison be maintained at policy level with each department that they might see that appropriate arrangements were made (1) for survey teams to get promptly all materials required for study, (2) for all sources of information, including personnel for interviews, to be readily available, and (3) for top-level review, and revision if necessary, of justifications furnished in the field.

The representatives designated for liaison by the Assistant Secretary of the Army for Manpower and Reserve Forces, the Assistant Secretary of the Navy for Air, and the Deputy Assistant Secretary of the Air Force for Manpower, Personnel, and Organization were: Brig. 

Gen. Herbert B. Powell, Mr. B. Benton Bray, and Maj. Gen. K. Ď.

These representatives, working in the main through their staff organizations but with constant contact personally with progress, maintained a continuing interest in the survey work and took action to see that position requirements not found by the survey to be based on workload be reexamined and reevaluated for purposes of obtaining a clear statement of justification for the Department's official position or of taking such action as appeared to be necessary. The Assistant Secretaries themselves lent impetus to the action on realistic reevaluation of requirements by keeping their interest in sound justifications constantly before all concerned through their representatives. The Assistant Secretary of the Army in at least one instance personally reviewed proposed justifications grounded in policies not recently reevaluated and new policies were stated. 

The degree to which corrective action was taken along the lines indicated by survey team findings affords the best measure of the real interest of the departments in obtaining a more realistic approach to determination of manning requirements. 1 At the beginning of the survey the Department representatives were requested to show just what action had been taken to eliminate dual, or pyramiding, military and civilian supervisory staffing. The survey covered selected installations, 16 in number, of the Army, Navy, and Air Force, and comptroller, procurement, and civilian personnel activities of each at headquarters level in Washington. Supervisory areas were studied at each place to determine (1) the extent of the practice of staffing of such areas with a military chief and a civilian assistant or deputy, or a civilian chief with a mili tary assistant or deputy and (2) the justification for such staffing. 

The survey teams examined job descriptions, workload data, interviewed all personnel involved, as well as other informed personnel, relative to the supervisory workload and work flow, and discussed the findings therefrom with appropriate installation authorities to determine whether the supervisory staffing was clearly justified by supervisory workload and, if not, what the justification was. 

The justification, where still in doubt, was referred to the department involved for a final statement of position or for action.

The organizations surveyed, broken down in each case to the lowest organizational entity at which dual staffing was found, consisted of 

2,979 organizational entities, directorates, divisions, branches, etc., of which 372 were dually staffed, and of these 232 were studied in the survey.

SCOPE OF THE SURVEY 

The survey was limited to 3 field installations of the Navy (a shipyard, a depot, and an air station), 3 of the Army (a training base, a quartermaster depot (with 2 tenant organizations), and an industrial

installation), 5 of the Air Force (2 Strategic Air Command bases, 1 Air Defense Command base, 1 Air Materiel Command base, and 1 Áir 

Materiel area), and 9 organizational areas at headquarters level in Washington. The Washington survey covered top-level organizational units of Army, Navy, and Air Force in Washington concerned with procurement, comptroller, and civilian personnel administration activities. 

To determine the extent of the practice of staffing of supervisory areas with both a military and a civilian supervisor, there were examined at each installation surveyed all organizational areas in which there appeared to be a possibility that the dual supervision or pyramiding relationship might exist. Examination was made to the extent necessary to determine whether it in fact did exist, even though job descriptions or job titles might not so indicate. The findings were recorded, and selected supervisory areas in which the counterpart type of staffing did exist-whether duality or pyramiding-were studied for determination whether the justification appeared to be workload. If doubt existed that the workload justified both jobs, such doubt generally was referred to the appropriate installation authority for advice as to the justification. 

The survey teams examined organizational and functional charts, job descriptions of military and civilian supervisors, work flow, and workload. They made a comparison of the education, training, and experience record of military incumbents with the United States Civil Service qualification standards set for their civilian counterparts. 

They interviewed incumbents of the jobs, installation authorities, and other personnel with peculiar knowledge of particular operations, to determine whether workload in fact justified the dual staffing, and, where the workload appeared insufficient for both, whether installation authorities could effect reassignments to afford better utilization of whichever supervisor, military or civilian, appeared to be surplus to the need.

As a final check on the correctness of the justifications offered, at installation level, for the staffing in those cases not clearly justified by workload, or to determine justification when not ascertained at the installation, those representatives of the Army, Navy, and Air Force designated for policy level liaison were offered an opportunity to verify and confirm or revise the stated justifications. 

The organizations surveyed broken down in each case to the lowest organizational segment at which dual supervision was found consisted of 2,979 organizational entities having supervisory areas, of which 372 were found to be staffed with both a military and a civilian supervisor, and, of these 372 supervisory areas, the survey team studied 232, that is, 62 percent.

SURVEY FINDINGS 

The extent of the practice of dual staffing of military and civilian supervisors in the same supervisory area is indicated by the fact that in 2,979 organization entities, 372 were staffed with both a military and a civilian supervisor. The dual staffing is more predominant in supply activities.

The justifications advanced were various. Of course, workload at the supervisory level is the best justification and where it is apparent, other considerations appear to be secondary. There is a general

attitude in the military departments that where the activity supervised is a support activity for a military operation, a combination of military and civilian supervision affords a total competence not afforded either by 2 military or 2 civilian supervisors. The civilian provides continuity and specific know-how of the technical aspects of the particular organization and the military supervisor provides (1) a fresh viewpoint constantly due to rotation, and (2) a broad knowledge of field problems of the military operations being serviced. 

The justifications as found are not complete due to numbers of supervisory areas (29) being restudied by the Departments at this time, as a result of survey team findings, to determine whether adequate justification for the staffing exists. Some instances (13) studied were stated to be essentially billets provided for on-the-job training of officers. A number of instances were found where military supervision is provided on an additional duty basis only, in order to obtain full utilization of the officer's services. In the main, except for on-the-job training and additional duty assignments, it appears that the departments had considered that the staffing was justified by the supervisory workload. Restudy and reevaluation during the course of the survey disclosed that in many instances this justification failed and positions, some (40) military, and some (14) civilian, were eliminated. The staffing in 86 supervisory areas was found to be justified by workload.

Detail of the survey teams' findings, justifications furnished, and actions taken by the Departments follow. For purposes of reporting, joint supervision by military and civilian supervisors is referred to generally as "dual" without distinction as to whether military chief and civilian chief (dual) or chief and assistant chief (pyramiding) is actually involved. Both types were covered by the survey.

Bureau of Aeronautics Reorganization of Contracts Division 

At the time of survey the Contracts Division consisted of the Division Director's office, a group of 4 special assistants reporting to the Director, 5 branches, and 20 sections. On January 8, 1954, the Chief of the Bureau of Aeronautics officially informed the Chief of Naval Material that the following reorganization would be effected for Contracts Division: 

a. Airframes Purchase, Components Purchase, and Contract

Review, and Procurement Planning.

It is estimated that Contracts Division will begin operating under the reorganization during the month of March 1954.

Survey team: With respect to production, production planning, and production engineering groups, it is clearly indicated that the officer and civilian concerned are responsible for performing practically the same functions, but the civilian, in addition to his assigned duties, is required to assume a substantial portion of the responsibilities of the officer. This is brought about because the officer is largely in a training, or orientation, status when he first reports for duty and

usually lacks adequate specialized industrial experience to perform fully the overall supervisory work. The civilian, who is stationary and, therefore, provides the necessary continuity of experience, in the main assumes the additional responsibilities until such time as the military incumbent has demonstrated to the overhaul and repair officer his readiness for assuming increased responsibilities. Except for the problem of assuring that there is complete responsiveness to "fleet" needs and the policy of applying military supervision, well versed in fleet operational needs, at this level to assure this responsiveness, there would appear to be no reason why a well-qualified civilian supervisor could not handle the supervisory load at the group level. 

Alternatively, but for the necessity of rotating officers without adequate provision for overlap to cover orientation and the problem of additional duties always incident to military assignments, there would appear to be no reason why competent aeronautical engineering duty (AED) officers, possibly with a somewhat higher degree of specialization in prior billet assignments, could not handle the supervisory workload alone.

The inspection group presents a different problem. The supervisory workload other than flight test can be adequately handled by one supervisor with specialized technical experience. The supervision of flight test and of flight test officers is probably best handled by a pilot preferable with recent fleet experience in as many as possible of the aircraft being checked through inspection.

The officers recently assigned in supervisory positions in 6 of the 7 divisions under the production group are presently attached to the production manager as project officers and program officers. While the reassignment of the division officers is ostensibly for other duties, it is not altogether clear that such is the case. 

Department report: Justification submitted for the dual staffing in

Overhaul and Repair Department, and explanation of action taken on the project officers, is as follows: 

1. General. The sole reason for existence of the Overhaul and 

Repair Departments of the Naval Shore Establishment is to support the fleet and render it specialized service. The people who render this service must be instinctively and completely responsive to the needs and demands of the naval operating forces. The Navy's experience dictates that the military, who are the only ones with an innate understanding of operational needs, shall occupy the billets in question. This, in general, is what is referred to as fleet thinking. 

Rotation of officers in Overhaul and Repair Departments not only develops career officers, but also has the great advantage of bringing to the job a fresh, competent viewpoint. Three of the billets in question will be staffed by aeronautical engineering duty officers.

Included in the aeronautical engineering duty career program are tours of duty at the Bureau of Aeronautics, tours of duty at commercial aircraft plants as Bureau of Aeronautics representatives, tours of duty at naval research and development activities, and postgraduate studies in management engineering, aeronautical engineering, and so forth. With a background such as this, the average aeronautical engineering duty officer has the necessary education, has seen what commercial industry does, and has a much greater grasp of the Navy problem as a whole than the average naval civilian employee. 

---

2. Specific. (a) Production planning officer: The military billet for this position is an aeronautical engineering duty officer. In addition to the above general justification for a military billet, this job specifically calls for close contact with the fleet. As a matter of fact, each Overhaul and Repair Department has established a customer service organization in the production planning group to insure intimate liaison with and support of the fleet. New, modern management programs are under the production planning group; for example, the present program of modern mechanized production control. 

(6) Production engineering officer: This area of the organization is the heart of the modern management concepts of BuAer. Under this group is the engineered performance standards program which involves the use of a stopwatch. It is felt that an aeronautical engineering duty officer will add much to this program which is new to the Navy civilian force. The general comments in paragraph 1 apply equally as well. (c) Production officer: General comments apply and also comments under 2. (d) Inspection officer: The officer in this billet will be a straightline aviator with recent fleet experience. He will guide the flight-test officers to get a final product that will meet with fleet approval. The civil-service employee in this position will have the specialized, technical experience needed for running the detailed inspection service. (e) The new allowance for overhaul and repair, San Diego, is 18 officers. The 4 project officers and the 4 program officers have been eliminated. In this area the new allowance calls for two officers as assistants for production. These two officers will be used on special production problems and the experience of the Navy is that there will be numerous problems to keep them occupied. 

3. The positions in question are key executive-type jobs in the 

Overhaul and Repair Department organization. It is our experience that you cannot apply work measurement indices of so many manhours of work to measure workload in high-level executive positions such as these. Modern management strives to relieve executive-type positions of the day-to-day routine details to enable them to be free to think constructively about important matters and to plan, organize, and control.

Department report: The Planning Division serves in a staff capacity to the head of the Department and, as the name implies, is engaged in the planning of the allocation of funds and personnel to all other divisions of the Department. In addition this Division has a responsibility for evaluating procedures and instructions in existence and for coordinating new instructions and procedures that it, or other divisions, may propose. Because of the requisite knowledge of all aspects of the supply and fiscal operation it has developed that the civilian supervisor of the Planning Division is the senior civilian supervisor of the Department. As such, he serves in a dual capacity. 

On the one hand he occupies the position as relates to the Department organization. On the other hand, he is senior employee of the 

Department. In the latter capacity, he is the Department representative on principal station civilian organizations and is required

to be spokesman for the approximately 1,000 employees of the Supply and Fiscal Department. În order to properly carry out the duties of officer in charge of the Planning Division it is necessary to assign a commissioned officer with extensive experience in most of the phases of supply and fiscal operation. If such an officer is available it is considered that there will be little, if any, duplication of functions as between the officer and civilian head of the Planning Division. 

The Navywide experience of the commissioned officer in conjunction with the wide knowledge of local procedures possessed by the civilian supervisors permit these two to serve as a team in carrying out the overall staff responsibilities of the Planning Division. However, it is believed that the assignment of both officer and civilian supervision is largely a matter of the individual capacity of both the officer and civilian supervisor. If the officer is comparatively junior in rank and/ or limited in experience, his assignment as officer in charge represents duality of supervision and should be eliminated. Similarly, a civilian supervisor with only limited knowledge of overall supply procedures cannot effectively serve as head of the Planning Division. Staffing of the Planning Division depends upon the capabilities of personnel assigned to such an extent as to make unwise a policy statement with respect to staffing of the Planning Division. It is considered, however, that as outlined above, it is feasible to have a sound organizational structure even though outwardly there may appear a duality of supervision. It is considered that in the instance of naval air station, San Diego, there was no duality of supervision at the time of the inspection since the Planning Division officer was an experienced lieutenant commander and the Planning Division civilian supervisor was a GS-12 employee with wide experience in all phases of the supply operation.

In all supervisory areas studied either the incumbents were assigned different duties and responsibilities (each having a full-time job) or, where the duties and responsibilities were similar, the workload apparently justified both positions.

The survey team learned that there is no on-the-job training program conducted at headquarters level. The policy of staffing key supervisory positions with military_and civilian supervisors, when justified by workload, was said to have been promoted by two advantages of the combination: (1) The civilian, who is not subject to rotation, gives maximum assurance of continuity of operation essential for promulgation of policy and effective programing. (2) The military supervisor, with experience gained in the field, can function to improve prevailing systems.

HEADQUARTERS, KEESLER AIR FORCE BASE, OFFICE OF THE COMMANDING GENERAL, 

Mississippi, October 29, 1952.

Subject: Malassignment of supervisory personnel. 

To: All staff officers and group commanders. 

1. A review of manning documents, job descriptions, and personnel assignments on this base reveals an entirely false concept of supervisory requirements necessary to accomplish functions in activities utilizing the assignment of both military and civilian personnel. In many instances top management is laboring under the misconception that civilian workers must be supervised by civilians and military by military. Dual supervisory assignments, based upon the belief that each category requires supervisors in the same category, displays improper managerial foresight and operational know-how. It is of importance to take cognizance that the primary purpose of civilian employees is to acquire personnel stability and/or technical skills not available in sufficient numbers through military procurement channels. Dual supervision in itself defeats our goal of a fully integrated and harmonious civilian and military work force. 

2. The establishment of duplicate supervisory positions causes a necessity for utilizing personnel authorizations which may be critically required in intragroup or base functions. Personnel austerity will not permit one activity to be over- 45791-54-7

staffed at the expense of equally important functions. It cannot be stated with authority that morale is enhanced by dual supervision. The position itself must be the prime consideration rather than the status of the incumbent assigned. In most cases involving dissatisfaction between workers and supervision, it is apparent that the personnel involved were not aware of the reasons underlying the assignment of the supervisor involved. While such structure is warranted in certain circumstances, in most cases it cannot be justified when measured against austere manpower standards. Elimination of such duplication presents a potential source for absorbing many of the graded space reductions recently imposed on this command. 

2. Unity of command is a basic management principle. In any organization, whether directorate, division, or branch, there can be only one chief at any particular time. Only during the absence of the chief does the deputy assume the chief's responsibilities, whether the position is military or civilian. In a majority of instances, the position of deputy can be justified only by the duties he performs to assist the chief; not by his occasional substitution for the chief. Nor can such duplication be justified solely to provide continuity for officer rotation. In the interest of good administration, executive development, and improvement of staff morale, a qualified subordinate should be designated to act in the absence of the chief rather than create a permanent deputy position.

1. Personnel requirements as stated and justified by the using organization or command. 

2. T/O's and T/D's now in existence. 

3. Standards validated and published by the Air Force. 

4. Staff visits and surveys in the field. 

5. Directives from higher agencies such as the Department of Defense, the Congress, or Executive order. 

6. Studies validated and accepted by the Air Force. 

7. Technical ability in the staff agencies of headquarters, USAF, and other echelons of command. 

8. Civilian practices when applicable to Air Force operation. 

All, or combinations of the above are used to evaluate manpower requirements based on mission and workload. While workload and mission are the primary consideration when determining manpower requirements, other factors such as climatic or local conditions, type of equipment used, and rates of operation must be considered.

Manning documents are reviewed by the commands and this headquarters to determine where reductions can be made. Survey teams from this headquarters and lower echelons of command consisting of manpower specialists and technicians from the staff agencies make frequent visits to the field for on-the-spot evaluation of requirements. In addition to this, each base commander is charged with the responsibility for efficient utilization of personnel. To assist the commander, a manpower management section conducts surveys and studies designed to validate requirements.

The procedures and functions as outlined, collectively provide the Air Force with a firm overall statement of personnel requirements. 

DEVELOPMENT OF MANPOWER REQUIREMENTS FOR THE NAVAL SHORE ESTABLISHMENT 

1 Theoretically, Shore Establishment manpower requirements are generated from fiscal year program objectives which indicated where the naval effort is to be concentrated, the operating levels of fleet support required, and the material mobilization to be accomplished. Bureaus and offices, based on the mission of the activity concerned, develop detailed studies of material and manpower requirements. Both civilian and military requirements for an activity are made with full recognition of the workload to meet the assigned mission of each activity. 

These tentative manpower requirements are then submitted to the controlling authorities Office of Budget and Reports for civilian requirements and the 

Deputy Chief of Naval Operations (Personnel) for military requirements. In consideration of limiting factors such as budget, procurement feasibility, and the overall Navy requirements, these controlling authorities determine respectively civilian block allocations to bureaus, and military allocations for specific billets in each shore activity.

As to civilian or military for a particular job, it is the policy of the Department of the Navy to employ military personnel in naval shore billets (including those of Marine Corps) where one or more of the following criteria are met: Law requires a military person; task requires military training; military person requires training for future assignments; for rotation requirements; security considerations dictate use of military person; duties, location, and hours of tasks: best filled by. military person; interservice relations or public relations dictate use of military

person. Wherever the case is borderline, preference shall be given to filling by a civilian. In all other instances civilians shall be used. 

The total manpower requirement for the Naval Establishment is then reviewed and coordinated by the Secretary of the Navy. This completed package then becomes the Navy's manpower requirements for the given fiscal year. 

More recently in actual practice, the manpower fiscal year ceiling for both civilian and military personnel as imposed by the Secretary of Defense in consideration of anticipated budgetary limitations has necessitated a revision of the above-described processes. Since the civilian and military manpower ceilings for a given fiscal year are provided by higher authority, it then becomes necessary to apportion the available manpower as nearly as practicable to meet the determined requirements. In such a case, the determinations result from a series of adjustments in order to arrive at allocations which make maximum effective utilization of numbers within strengths allowed. 

In all cases, prior to or after the implementation of a personnel plan, continuing reviews of manpower requirements are conducted by the secretarial staff, the 

Office of Budget and Reports, the Deputy Chief of Naval Operations (Personnel), the bureaus and offices exercising management control of shore activities and the 

Shore Establishment Survey Division of the Office of the Naval Inspector General. 

Such management tools as staffing ratios, work-measurement data, organizational patterns, and methods analysis are continuously being used at various levels of Navy management to insure greater utilization of the available manpower. Daily revisions to the personnel allocation plan insure that changes in workload and tasks of the numerous activities of the Shore Establishment are being reflected in adjustments of military manpower allocated to these activities.

1. In order to properly discharge the responsibility for operation of an effective manpower utilization and control program, accepted manpower management techniques and systems of control are used. It is through a balanced combination of these techniques and systems that maximum results accrue to the commands, the Department of the Army, and the Nation as a whole. Mission control, performance evaluation, work measurement, cost accounting and related systems, and work simplification, efficiency awards, supervisor training, effective cost consciousness and other management devices and techniques are all important. elements of a well-balanced manpower management program. 

2. Civilian employees of the Department of the Army are accounted for on tables of distribution, documents which list personnel space authorizations by grade, title of position, and classification. These tables are prepared by the installation or activity commander, for all personnel spaces authorized him, and are approved by the major commander concerned. Major commands (chiefs of technical and administrative services, continental United States and overseas commands) submit to the Department of the Army each quarter a report of requirements requesting adjustments of personnel space authorizations and reporting programed actual and anticipated personnel requirements. A monthly report is also submitted by the major commands showing the strength of each activity which is related to measurable workload factors and is used as a check against their personnel authorization. 

3. An on-the-site review or appraisal of manpower requirements and utilization is an indispensable, basic tool used by the Army to obtain increased economy and ever-increasing effectiveness of one of its three primary resources-men. The manpower survey system employed is based upon the concept that the agency which issues personnel authorizations to using activities will be responsible for physical inspection to assure proper personnel utilization. The office of the Chief of Staff surveys staff agencies within the Department of the Army; Assistant 

Chief of staff, G1, Personnel, survevs maior continental and overseas command. headquarters and the offices of the chiefs of technical and administrative services; and the major commanders and chiefs of technical and administrative services conduct surveys of their using activities and installations. The nature and extent of these surveys depends upon the type of activity concerned, its scope, and the character of the manpower problems encountered. The surveys vary from a straightforward quantitative appraisal of manpower needs, to a combined organ-

ization and methods review and personnel utilization study in which the operations of the activity are thoroughly explored and overhauled, the work force is analyzed to insure workers are assigned in accordance with skills they possess, and the whole translated into the minimum number of employees of various categories required for efficient operation. Until the end of 1953, surveys were required on an annual basis. In order to meet continuing demands for increased economy in Army manpower expenditure, and to permit accomplishment of a more thorough and comprehensive type of manpower survey, the system was revised effective January 1, 1954, to schedule and conduct manpower surveys of at least 50 percent of the activities and 75 percent of the operating personnel annually, provided each activity is surveyed at least once every 2 years. 

4. The importance of individual judgment in a survey is recognized. It is because of this critical factor, the personnel assigned to survey activities are very carefully selected, highly experienced, well-trained manpower management analysts. Both officers and civilians are used. 

5. When a survey is to be made, the activity commander concerned is required to have information relative to workload, staffing, organization, and the functions prepared as of a prescribed date and available to the survey team when it arrives. 

These basic documents in addition to information and data obtained by the team during the conduct of the survey are used in making the determination of requirements. When an appraisal of an activity or installation is completed and the recommendations are submitted based upon the workload at the time of the survey, authorizations of personnel are adjusted accordingly by means of a new authorization voucher to the activity commander, who then revises his table of distribution. 

It should be emphasized that the analysis of manpower requirements for an activity is made based upon workload factors existing at the time of survey and upon known workload rejections. Other adjustments are necessarily made during the interval between surveys by analysis of the report of requirements and the justification contained therein. 

6. All of these reviews and appraisals are under the monitorship and supervision of the Assistant Chief of Staff, G1, to insure proper implementation of prescribed policies and procedures and to recommend appropriate corrective action. In industrial type activities for years we have used only a small number of military personnel. This has been the result of enforced policies designed to insure that the maximum number of military personnel are available for combat type duties. 

The few military that are used are continuously subject to critical review. Specific criteria have been developed which identify those military positions in this area which are essential for command, control, and supervision of these Army activities, and for the proper development of competent military personnel able to take over similar activities in tactical areas and overseas commands. More recently, with such a large part of the Army deployed overseas, an additional need arose to provide spaces into which personnel returning from overseas could be placed in order to preserve and further develop their particular specialties in preparation for their return to overseas stations. 

7. Application of these staffing patterns to any specific position must of necessity be determined by the availability of qualified personnel, either military or civilian, since these criteria not only cover positions which must be military but also include positions appropriate for assignment of military personnel (for purposes of career development or because of the nonavailability of properly qualified civilians).

Department of the Army conduct annual classification of utilization reviews which have as one main object identification of any "duplicate" staffing. d. In this connection, Mr. invited your attention to Senate 

Document No. 30 of the 83d Congress, 1st session.

With regard to the matter of appointing a civilian to the naval officer billet of industrial relations officer at the 

Naval Shipyard, I find that the shipyard commander has tentatively decided to order Commander United States Navy to assume these duties after he has familiarized himself with the personnel of the yard departments. Commander is a 36-yearold engineering duty officer with a master of science degree in naval construction and engineering from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. He had 35 months of duty, as engineering officer and gunnery officer, on a battleship following his graduation from the Naval Academy in 1939. This tour of duty was followed by 2 years at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, after which he was awarded his masters degree. From 1944 until 1946 he was assigned to production and planning billets at the 

Naval Shipyard. This was followed by 41 months on the maintenance staff of the commander, service forces, Pacific Fleet. In 1950, he was ordered to the United States Naval 

as the industrial and laboratory officer.

at The Bureau is endeavoring to broaden the qualifications of its engineering duty officers by assignments to various types of billets involving industrial administration. The tours of duty for the officer described above lead to such broad qualifications. By this means, the Bureau is endeavoring not only to accelerate the development of future production officers and shipyard commanders who are alert and sensitive to the requirements of efficient and effective handling of largescale industrial working forces, but is also utilizing the professional competence which engineering duty officers may already have acquired in industrial relations work during the discharge of previous line assignments in our shipyard shops and on the waterfront. As an aside, the Bureau has noted that big industrial concerns in private industry are today by a similar means endeavoring to develop executives capable of knowing how to manage huge industrial enterprises. Private industry calls this executive development. 

While I understand the feelings of those who would like to see civilians elevated to department-head status in naval shipyards, I have a very strong feeling that

the industrial relations officer billet at this yard and at other naval shipyards should, except in the most extraordinary and special circumstances, be filled by a military officer for the following reasons: (a) Department head and other naval officer billets in United States naval shipyards are working billets for working engineering duty naval officers, who are ordered to them and held responsible for results throughout their industrial careers in the Navy. But a most important byproduct of the rotation of the engineering duty officers through these various billets at different localities ashore is the experience they obtain through such assignments. Rotation of engineering duty officers is primarily to and among shore billets which require naval engineering and industrial training and qualifications. (b) The industrial relations officer billet, which this Bureau has set up as one of the ranking department head billets in the shipyards, is one which deals primarily with the working forces in the yards and the policies laid down by the Navy Department for employment under civil service, and also with the policies of the Civil Service Commission as they relate to tenure, rerating, promotion, separation, etc., of civilian employees, both of the blue-collar and white-collar category. Quite obviously, experience gained by engineering duty officers while filling such a billet is of vast benefit to them and to the Navy as they fill billets of increasing responsibility, finally culminating in shipyard commander, or other commands. Since the industrial relations officer reports directly to the shipyard commander, this close staff association permits him to participate in command decisions. (c) Officers with military experience and background and wearing the uniform have certain inherent advantages in dealing with the ship's personnel who are at the yards to get their work done. A thorough and complete understanding of the forces afloat point of view and an ability to interpret their needs, desires, and requirements to the civilian population of the yard is a matter of considerable importance in the case of all heads of departments. (d) Civil-service employees in general are not charged with accountability to make them readily responsive to military command, as are naval officers, and I do not think nor do I propose that they should be so charged. However, I feel it is of primary importance that the heads of departments in a United States naval shipyard be military personnel, especially when one considers the very small number of officers at shipyards, in comparison to the total number of civilian personnel. 

At the 

Naval Shipyard today there are 14,322 civilians and 106 naval officers.

This bureau is familiar with the contents of Senate Document No. 30, of the 

83d Congress, 1st session, which discussed military personnel in civilian occupations. In this document, two recommendations were made. One had to do with the elimination of employment ceilings for graded civilian employees and the other with the enactment of legislation respecting the utmost utilization of civilian employees by the Armed Forces. This Bureau is vitally concerned with the utmost utilization not only of civilian employees but of the naval personnel in and under the Bureau of Ships. In Senate Document No. 30 it is pointed out that rotation policies and on-the-job training requirements for officers make necessary the assignment of some military personnel to certain types of civilian work. This 

Bureau and its shipyards collectively represent a vast industrial enterprise, for the efficient management of which the Chief of the Bureau and other military personnel are responsible. The assignment of military personnel to department head and other billets in shipyards is the best known way of holding the military responsible for acts of commission or omission, and at the same time, of furthering the individual professional development of engineering duty officers into wellrounded and better qualified administrators. 

This Bureau is convinced that background experience in industrial personnel administration is an essential element in the successful executive direction of our industrial shore establishments, and officers having such experience will be better fitted for future assignments to command. I do not believe it is possible to cut off the opportunity for such development by restricting present rotation policies, and at the same time, over the next 10 or 15 years, produce naval officer industrial administrators of the type and capacity to manage our vast programs which involve the direction of the design, construction, fitting out, and maintenance of the greatest array of naval vessels in the history of the world. 

Few people appreciate that this Bureau, since 1940, has constructed or acquired more than 100,000 ships of all sizes totaling over 15 million tons. These ships represent about 5 million man-years of industrial effort. If there is anything that flag rank engineering duty officers have learned from this wartime experience, it is that our naval officer talent in the engineering duty category must be developed as indus-

trial executives up to the point where they can give to our countrymen the efficient and well-managed industrial support this country and the forces afloat are entitled, to.

Considering all of the foregoing, I can only state that the Bureau strongly desires to adhere to the commitment already made which will result in the industrial relations officer billet at the 

Naval Shipyard being filled as planned. Contrary to Mr. views, we do not consider that commissioned personnel in department head billets in naval shipyards are more or less superficial. Neither do we feel that there is economy in reducing engineering duty naval officer career development opportunities. Rather, over a period of years, engineering duty naval officer talent must be developed on a magnitude designed to achieve constant improvement in shipyard management. The matter of producing such talent in the military engineering duty officer ranks ashore is at the heart of achieving enlightened industrial administration, of the type requisite to the proper military defense of our country and the support of our fighting forces. 

I feel apologetic for this rather lengthy discussion. On the other hand, it is an important subject and has many ramifications, which should be considered collectively, rather than as individual spot issues. The enclosure to your letter is returned as requested.
