The Commonwealth of Massachusetts SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

This study of the practice of nursing is primarily concerned with the questions of legal controls, the number and membership of licensing boards, and the personnel to serve such an agency in Massachusetts. These were the questions over which significant differences of opinion arose during past discussions of related legislative proposals. 

PRESENT MASSACHUSETTS NURSING PRACTICE ACT. 

Sections 74 to 81C of chapter 112 of the General Laws, as amended by chapter 350 of the Acts of 1953, provides for (1) the examining and licensing of registered nurses and practical nurses and, (2) the inspection and approval of schools for nurses and practical nurses. 

These administrative powers are vested in two separate boards, namely, the Board of Registration in Nursing and the Approving Authority for Schools for Nurses and Schools for Practical Nurses.

The Board of Registration in Nursing is a seven member board, consisting of four nurses, two physicians and the secretary of the Board of Registration in Medicine, ex-officio. 

The Approving Authority for Schools for Nurses and Schools for Practical Nurses is a six-member board, consisting of two nurses (who are also members of the Board of Registration in Nursing), one physician, a trustee and an administrator or assistant administrator of a hospital having an approved school of nursing, and the secretary of the Board of Registration in Medicine, ex-officio. 

This act is a licensing statute of the so-called permissive type, which is limited to the prohibition of the use of the

[Feb. 

titles of registered nurse and licensed practical nurse by those who have not been licensed as such under the law. 

It does not define nursing functions nor prohibit nursing practice by those who are not properly licensed within the Commonwealth.

LEGISLATIVE PROPOSALS. 

House Document No. 705 of 1955 proposed to substitute for the above two boards a single licensing board of seven members, to consist of five nurses, one physician and either one administrator or trustee of a hospital with an approved school of nursing. It vested in that board the responsibilities of (a) examining and licensing nursing practitioners, (b) inspecting and approving all schools of nursing, and (c) appointing a qualified registered nurse as a full-time executive secretary. It provided for the legal control of the practice of nursing, its licensed practitioners and their legal designation.

House Document No. 2918 of 1955 also proposed a single licensing board to consist of nine members by adding to the seven members proposed by House Document No. 705 of 

1955, (a) a second physician, and (b) both a trustee and an administrator of a hospital with an approved school of nursing, instead of only one of the two. In addition, House No. 

2918 provided that one of the five nurses could be a licensed practical nurse. The responsibilities vested in the new board were left substantially unchanged from the earlier bill. 

House Document No. 1964 of 1956 is the same as House, 

No. 705 of 1955.

LEGAL BASIS OF CONTROL. 

Licensure of nurses in this country is a function of the state government under its "police power," which serves to protect the health, welfare and morals of the people. This inherent authority of the State requires the enactment of laws which control professions requiring skill and education for their adequate performance and thus protect the public from unskilled and unprincipled practitioners.

1956.] SENATE — 

No. 545.

Under the present permissive-type licensing law in Massachusetts, the public does not have as much protection from nursing licensure as is afforded by professional licensing laws of other States. In this Commonwealth any person has the legal right to engage in the practice of nursing; the present statute controls only to the extent that it prohibits the use of the title of registered nurse or of licensed practical nurse by any person who has not been licensed as such under the law.

The statutory trend in occupational licensure has been to limit all practice in a particular field to those properly licensed. Such appears to be the intent of the proposed nursing bills, but the exemptions they contain would modify this effect markedly.

DEFINITION OF NURSING. 

Any adequate licensing law calls for a clear, concise definition of the practice to be regulated and protected. The report quotes the definition of the American Nurses' Association which reflects various elements of nursing practice emphasized by the occupational groups represented in this professional organization. It points out how the definitions of nursing in the proposed legislation under scrutiny vary from the ANA definitions of both professional and practical nursing. It includes statutory definitions from selected 

States in the Appendix. 

PERMISSIVE VERSUS MANDATORY LICENSURE. 

If Massachusetts changes from its permissive to a mandatory licensing law, a basic question will arise as to whether an adequate supply of trained nursing personnel would be available. The nursing profession has a responsibility to achieve a balance between the adequacy of service and the competency of care offered to the public.

Seven jurisdictions are reported to have mandatory licensure for both professional and practical nursing, and twelve other States for registered nurses alone. 

Mandatory licensure bills should not be enacted if condi-

[Feb. 

tions in Massachusetts are such that the provisions of the law cannot be carried out. Criteria to be considered in preparing mandatory licensing legislation include: the numbers of persons affected, the availability of replacements on the labor market for those displaced, and the dependence of the existing facilities for nursing care upon those to be displaced. The report contains statistical data related to this subject.

Available factual data are presented with respect to the fear expressed that the enactment of mandatory licensure for nursing would cause a prohibitive rise in the cost of nursing care; many questions are raised in the report over the validity of the limited information now available. 

WAIVER PROVISIONS. 

Extended waiver provisions (so-called grandfather clauses) are included in the legislative proposals, which are designed to protect the livelihood of those who might be displaced from their practice of nursing upon passage of the proposed bills. Critics of the proposed bill find the waiver provisions to be indeterminate and contradictory. 

NATURE AND COMPOSITION OF LICENSING BOARDS. 

The Licensing Board in nursing is the administrative agency created by the Legislature to act as a quasi-judicial body in administering the provisions of the nursing practice act. In the majority of States, the practice of nursing is controlled by one licensing law. More than one law in a field with a fundamental core of principles basic to its practice creates problems in control both for the public and for practitioners. In the majority of States, the nursing licensing law is administered by a single Board, and in all but thirteen States Board membership is exclusively composed of nurses.

In the proposed legislation, the responsibility for examining and licensing practitioners, and inspecting and approving schools of nursing, is vested in one Board.

1956.] 

BOARD PERSONNEL AND FEES. 

The nursing licensing statutes in all but three States provide for the employment of a full-time, registered nurse, executive officer, commonly known as the Executive Secretary, whose duties are usually defined by the Board. 

Massachusetts nursing licensure fees were found to fall below those charged in the majority of States; nonetheless, an excess of receipts over disbursements and encumbrances was noted for the Board of Registration in Nursing for each of the years studied. 

LEGISLATIVE TASK 

The issues in the proposed legislation which the General Court must decide are concerned with the legal control of nursing practice, as well as the practitioners and their titles; the placing of responsibilities for the licensing of nursing practitioners and for approving schools for the training of nurses in one rather than two boards; the appointment of a full-time nurse executive secretary in place of the present part-time, non-nurse, ex-officio secretary; and the alteration of the present Licensing Board membership.

I. INTRODUCTION. 

Origin of Study. 

This study of nursing was ordered under Senate, No. 846 when it became chapter 130 of the Resolves of 1955. The resolve authorized and directed the Legislative Research Council "to make an investigation and study relative to the practice of nursing in the Commonwealth," and further stipulated that "in the course of its investigation and study said council shall consider the subject matter of current house documents numbered 2918 and 2982."

This study is concerned primarily with the basis and process of the legal control of the practice of nursing and its practitioners within the Commonwealth.

Legislative Background. 

In January, 1955, Representatives Johnson of Worcester and Toomey of Cambridge, upon petition of the Massachu setts State Nurses' Association, and Senator Silvio O. Conte filed the proposal, House, No. 705, to regulate the practice of nursing and the registration of nurses. This proposal provided for a single licensing board of seven members, to consist of five nurses, one physician and one administrator or trustee of a hospital with an approved school of nursing. It vested in that board the responsibilities of (a) examining and licensing nursing practitioners, (b) inspecting and ap-

[Feb. 

proving all schools of nursing, and (c) appointing a qualified registered nurse as a full-time executive secretary. In essence, it provided for the legal control of the practice of nursing, its licensed practitioners and their legal designation. 

In June, 1955, the legislative Committee on Public Health, to whom the above petition had been referred, reported House, No. 705 with changes, as House, No. 2918.

This new proposal expanded the single licensing board from seven to nine members, (1) by adding a second physician, and (2) by adding both a trustee and an administrator of a hospital with an approved school of nursing, instead of only one of the two. In addition, it provided that one of the five nurses could be a licensed practical nurse. The responsibilities vested in the new board were left substantially unchanged from the earlier bill.

In June, 1955, the Committee on Ways and Means favorably reported House, No. 2982, authorizing the Committee on Public Health to sit during the recess of the General Court to investigate the advisability of abolishing the present Approving Authority for Schools for Nurses and Schools for Practical Nurses, and of transferring its powers to the Board of Registration in Nursing. 

In July, 1955, the Committee on Ways and Means reported Senate, No. 821, which called for a broader investigation and study by a special commission relative to the practice of nursing within the Commonwealth. Senate, No. 846 was, however, substituted upon motion of Senator

Scope of Study. 

Because of the specific nature of the authorizing legislative resolve for a study, the limitations of time, and the dearth of available statistical materials on nursing resources within the State, this study has been confined to consideration of the above legislative proposals and to the pros and cons of the controversial issues which were raised during the legislative hearings when these bills were discussed last year. 

The factual and statistical materials which have been collected are directly related to various aspects of licensing legislation for nursing. An attempt has also been made to include documented information on nursing licensure in general, on legislative practice in other States, and on practice in the Commonwealth under the present statute. 

The provisions of proposed bills are analyzed and the findings of the study group are summarized in each case. By means of legislative notes, the attempt has been made to place in clear focus the facts relating to those issues which have been classified as controversial, namely: 

The nature of the legal control over nursing practice and practitioners. 

The investment of the responsibilities for licensing nursing practitioners and approving schools for their preparation in one or two boards. 

The composition of the nursing licensing board. 

The appointment of a full-time executive secretary. 

Increased costs of nursing care and nursing education.

II. THE BASIS AND PROCESS OF LEGAL CONTROL IN NURSING. 

Licensure of nurses is the legal process by which the States grant to those individuals who have fulfilled the necessary requirements the right to practise nursing, to assume the privileges of representing themselves as registered nurses or licensed practical nurses, and to use the abbreviations R.N. or L.P.N. after their names.¹ (Throughout this report the "States" here mentioned almost invariably include the District of Columbia and the territories.)

A license for purposes of this study is the document which conveys the legal rights and privileges described above.² 

Purposes of Licensure. 

Nursing legislation, like similar legislation for other professional groups, protects the public by establishing certain requirements for individuals who engage in the practice of nursing. A nursing practice act defines the prerequisites for obtaining a license or "formal permission from the proper authorities to perform certain acts"4 and for retaining it in good standing. The license protects the health and welfare of society by establishing minimum standards which qualified practitioners must meet.

Licensure of nurses, therefore, aims "on the one hand to secure to society the benefits which come from the services of a highly skilled group, and on the other hand, to protect society from those who are not highly skilled, yet profess to be, or from those who, being highly skilled are nevertheless

Methods of Licensure. 

The three most common methods of becoming licensed to practice nursing are: 

1. Licensure by examination, whereby a candidate demonstrates her competency to practice by passing the examination given by a licensing board. 

2. Licensure by endorsement, whereby a license to practice nursing is granted by a State to an out-of-state applicant who is already licensed in another State. In any profession this type of licensure is dependent upon the need for interstate mobility of the licensees, the degree of similarity of standards in the various States, the extent to which interstate machinery exists in order to facilitate reciprocity, and the existence of recognized national examinations. 2 

3. Licensure by waiver, whereby some or all of the qualifications required for licensure by examination have been waived in certain cases for a fixed period of time. 

Types of Licensing Laws. 

There are two principal types of licensing laws, namely, the so-called permissive and the so-called mandatory types of statutes.

The permissive type of statute prohibits the use of a title, but does not in general prohibit the performance of acts. 

Hence it permits any person to perform a nursing or other task he may be equipped to do, regardless of the possession of a license, as long as he does not do it under the representation that he is licensed."

The mandatory type of legislation legally requires licensure to be eligible to engage in the practice of a specific occupation or profession.

Basis of Legal Control. 

Licensure of nurses in this country is a function of the state government under its "police power," by which protection is given to the health, welfare and morals of the people. This inherent authority of the State is the basis for the enactment of laws which control those professions requiring skill and education for their adequate performance, and thus protects the public. 

Since each State is free to establish such laws and standards as it is able to maintain in the light of its economic and educational development, some disparity exists in the provisions of the nursing practice acts and board rules of the various States. However, the requirements for licensure as stated in the licensing laws of the States generally are of four kinds, as follows: 

1. Personal requirements as to citizenship, age, character and sometimes residence. 

2. Educational qualifications. 

3. Experience qualifications. 

4. Formal qualifications, such as the application, examination and administrative approval for a license.

Steps in the Evolution of Legal Control.2 

Legal control over any activity under the authority of police power is predicated upon the assumption that The activity has attained the status of a trade, business, occupation or profession. An activity must have achieved sufficient stature to warrant legal control before the law can place restraints and limits upon that activity.

Exact preparation for the perfecting of the practices of the occupation is recognized as indispensable. The degree of control by law is related

Implications for the Profession of Nursing. 

In view of the criteria mentioned above, the proponents of the proposed legislation emphasize that: 

The nursing profession is committed to the control of practice through licensure of all who engage in nursing, since it is only through such licensure that complete protection of the public is obtained. 

It is a legitimate function of the profession itself to assist the public in securing protection through the promotion of adequate nursing practice legislation.

It is a matter of public right, and not of privilege, to provide control over both practice and practitioner in nursing, since the value of the benefits of skilled nursing services and the seriousness of the consequences of unskilled nursing practice have been demonstrated for over half a century in this State.

III. LEGAL DEFINITION OF NURSING. 

Need for Definition.

If any licensing law is to be appropriate to its legal purposes and operations, there is need for a clear, concise definition of the practice it is expected to regulate and to protect. Such a definition should be stated in terms of the functions which constitute professional practice in the opinion of the profession in question, those working closely with it, and such public agencies as may be called to pass upon it. 

Since such laws are the basic authority for establishing areas of practice, are regulatory in nature, and carry penalties for infraction, they should define the acts which only those who are licensed can perform and the acts which are prohibited to others.

The need for such identification of nursing functions and areas of control is apparent in proceedings against malpractice. If a particular function is legally identified, a standard of behavior is prescribed, and proof of deviation from that standard is possible. Thus, to identify nursing functions is to increase professional responsibility and to protect the public from acts of malpractice by enabling those injured to seek legal redress.

The need for a legal definition for the control of nursing practice has become more urgent as each new auxiliary group within the occupation seeks licensure. Professional nursing is being challenged for identification by practical nursing, and the latter by non-professional workers in the nursing field, including psychiatric aides.

Problems in defining Nursing Practice.¹ 

Because nursing activities are so closely allied to medical and other related health practices, and because many of them are performed jointly and/or conditionally with relation to medical and related practice, the identification of the independent nature of nursing practice has been a slow process. Hence, nursing licensure has not paralleled that of other related professions, such as medicine, dentistry and pharmacy, which were more easily defined and subjected to mandatory control.

In the licensing laws controlling these related professions in this and other States, the specific practice is defined in such a manner as to indicate the legal functions of the licensed practitioner and to prohibit others from engaging in the practice by providing penalties. The differentiation of nursing functions has been further complicated by the expanding role of the various auxiliary non-professional workers.

Within the last decade, many States have instituted nursing practice acts based on a definition of practice, to replace the earlier nurse practice acts, which were limited in scope to practitioners rather than practice. The history of nursing practice reflects an increased willingness on the part of legislators to incorporate a definition of nursing areas of control in state laws. In addition to the 18 States which have mandatory laws, 25 other States and territories ² include a definition of nursing in the licensing law, although wide variations in definition are observed. IV. PERMISSIVE AND MANDATORY LICENSURE. 

Review of Nursing Practice Legislation. 

By the turn of the twentieth century, the skill and training required for the proper performance of nursing had acquired sufficient occupational status to warrant the exercise by Legislatures of the "police power" to protect the public. The legal effort to achieve the protection of society in relation to the services of the practitioners of nursing in this country brought enactment of the first nursing practice act in North Carolina as early as 1903. Early licensure legislation was permissive in nature and controlled only the use of the legal designations mentioned in the law. 

Within the next twenty years, every State passed some form of licensing law for registered nurses. Today all except two States (Colorado and West Virginia) and the District of Columbia have also passed legislation controlling the licensure of practical nursing. 

To distinguish a profession from other occupations, it is suggested by the authority quoted and by others that the following considerations be borne in mind:¹

A profession is a relatively specific, socially necessary function upon the regular performance of which the practitioner depends for his livelihood and social status; requires competence in a special technique based on a 

¹ Occupational Licensing Legislation in the States, op. cit., pp. 58-59.

1956.] 

body of generalized knowledge, requiring theoretical study; includes a generally accepted ethic which subordinates private interests to the most effective performance of function; its practitioners have a formal association that fosters the ethic, sets standards of competence, and improves performance.

The welfare criterion suggests that only those occupations which directly affect the public health, safety, morals, and general welfare, and which distinguish themselves from the other occupations, should be licensed.

Of all the professions, nursing is the only one in which licensing has continued for over half a century to be only permissive in nature for the most part. Hence, as has been indicated, the privileges of the law are largely limited to the person who can represent herself to be a licensed nurse and use the legal abbreviation R.N. or other titles.¹

It is only within the last decade that a definition of nursing has been instituted in some States to control by law the practice of nursing. Before 1947, all nursing licensure acts were permissive in nature. New York was the first State to write a mandatory law on its books in 1938, but it was not put into effect until 1947.

As nursing skills expand, educational requirements increase, and procedures and technics advance, it is likely that statutory changes will occur to provide better protection for the public by controlling both practice and practitioner in nursing. 2 Standards of qualifications will undoubtedly be raised as knowledge increases and conditions permit, thereby keeping standards of competence in line with social progress. 3

V. EXEMPTION CLAUSES AND WAIVER PROVISIONS IN NURSING PRACTICE ACTS. 

A bill for licensure must include (1) exemption clauses for those with nursing activities of a nature which should be excluded from the stringencies of the law, and (2) waiver provisions, or the so-called grandfather clauses, to protect the livelihood of those currently employed as nurses who are not able to qualify for licensure.

I. Exemption Clauses. 

Exemption clauses in the licensing statutes of the States exempt certain individuals who would otherwise be subject to the practice act because of the nature of their activities. 

Mandatory nursing licensing laws set forth such exemptions as the following: 

Gratuitous care of the sick by members of family or friends.

Incidental care of the sick by domestics, primarily employed as housekeepers. 

Nursing assistance in an emergency.

The practice of nursing incidental to the program of study of students enrolled in nursing education programs accredited by the board. 

The practice of any legally qualified nurse of another State who is employed by any federal agency, while in the discharge of official duties. 

The practice of nursing by individuals awaiting licensure, as stated in the law.

II. Waiver Provisions. 

Licensure by waiver occurs when some or all of the qualifications for nursing licensure by examination have been waived in specified cases for a fixed period of time (so-called grandfather clauses). On this basis persons now actively engaged in nursing can become licensed, even though they may not possess the qualifications required of recent graduates of accredited nursing education programs.¹ This provision properly protects the livelihood of those currently engaged in the practice of nursing, but does, of course, open. the possibility that unearned privileges may be granted to those with political influence. 2 

Waiver Provisions in Other Statutes, Waiver clauses in the nursing practice acts of other States and territories show great variation. These include waivers: 

With and without examination. 

With written and/or oral examinations.

Permitting licensure of those who have practiced under particular titles or of those who have practiced under any title. 

Covering periods from ninety days to five years.

Specifying varying educational and/or experience requirements. 

Containing specific clauses of provincial significance, such as "the armed forces medically trained workers" provision in the California law, and the ability to read and write the English language" provision in the 

Maryland law.

VIII. COMPOSITION OF THE BOARD OF LICENSURE IN NURSING. 

Most state boards engaged in licensing occupations are composed of representatives of groups with direct interest in the areas regulated by the Board. About three fourths of all occupational licensing statutes require that board members be licensed practitioners in the occupations regulated by boards on which they serve. In about one half the States, state officials serve as ex-officio members on one or more of the occupational boards. Usually they are heads of departments which are directly concerned with the licensing of the occupations in question. Aside from the ex-officio members, it appears from available data that less than a dozen state licensing boards in the entire nation have any lay members representing the general public. 

The quasi-judicial functions of boards would seem to require that the qualifications of the appointee be such as to make him an expert in the area of practice controlled by the board, and that minimum educational and experiential qualifications be clearly stated in the law. One authority has stated this principle as follows: 2 

Board members are public servants, not controlled by either representatives or lobbyists of the profession. As public servants, their concern is the welfare of the public. Compromises effected in creating agencies of hybrid composition of professional nurses, practical nurses, physicians, institutional or lay representatives are the results largely of resolvements for the benefit of public relations and not public welfare. The law is intended to secure the people.

Although a variety of methods is noted for financing the administration of occupational licensing boards in this country,¹ two broad patterns seem to predominate: 

Financing by means of the various fees collected by the Board. 

Financing from regular General Fund appropriation acts passed annually or biennially by the Legislatures. 

In those States operating under the first pattern, the boards retain the fees either in their own accounts, or deposit them with the State Treasurer in a special board fund.

XI. NURSING EDUCATION COSTS. 

Nursing education costs were discussed at length in closed and open 1955 legislative committee hearings on the proposed nursing bills. Whatever may be their bearing on the bills under study, the Advisory Committee for this study suggested that a discussion of these costs be included in this report to the General Court, since the same issues will undoubtedly be raised again in relation to new legislation. 

One of these issues arises from the question of the extent to which the patient or the third party (Welfare Department, Blue Cross, etc.) supports nursing education as he pays his hospital bill.

Several groups of figures are available in the Commonwealth which relate to these costs. One group of statistics from 14 Greater Boston hospitals for the year ended June 30, 1954 (submitted on form MDPH 200 to the State Division of Hospital Costs and Accounting), shows an average total hospital cost of $25.25 per diem, of which $2.93 per diem is for nursing education. These figures indicate that about 

13 per cent of the patient's hospital bill is earmarked for nursing education.

[Feb. 

However, these figures, it is emphasized, fail to take into account the tuition and fees paid by the student nurses for their education, the funds to pay for nursing education which are derived from other sources, and, most important of all, the numbers of hours of uncompensated nursing service returned by the student in the course of her student nursing experience. No widely accepted dollar value for this student nursing service is available at the present time. 

If, however, a reasonable monetary value is placed on the nursing service rendered by students, and if the fees and tuitions received from students are taken into consideration, the above estimated $2.93 per diem for nursing education is greatly reduced or even ruled out completely in some instances.

Several other compilations of similar statistics were also reviewed. One of these reported an average nursing education cost of $2.19 per diem out of an average total hospital cost of $19.81 per diem for 33 licensed hospitals in Massachusetts in the year ending June 30, 1955. These figures show 11 per cent of the total per diem cost as being earmarked for nursing education. 

Critics of this ratio emphasize that analysis of the data underlying the above hospital costs indicates that this conclusion should be tempered by the following considerations:

The value of the nursing service rendered by the students is not included in the computation.

The hospitals vary greatly in size of school of nursing and bed capacity for patient care.

The schools of nursing represented vary greatly in the standards of nursing education maintained, as indicated by their accreditation status on the national level.

The accounting systems from which the individual totals for this compilation were derived are quite variable in the manner, completeness, thoroughness and similarity of their reporting on MDPH 200 to the 

State Division of Hospital Costs and Accounting. 

Another group of figures studied presented the costs per student per annum for nursing education in eleven schools of nursing in the Commonwealth. These figures for the fiscal year 1954 ranged from $1,400 to $3,176 per student.

1956.] 

If these figures are recomputed, allowing to the students the value of $1 per hour for uncompensated nursing service given in that year, the above $1,400 figure becomes $201. 

Similar computations show a reduction in the cost per student of approximately two thirds in several other schools of nursing in the same group. 

Another financial issue studied related to the educational costs of physicians and other related medical personnel which are also supported in part by the patient as he pays his hospital bill. It was found that the amounts paid for the education of such workers are considerably smaller than those paid for nursing. This is so because, in general, the preliminary education costs of these students are borne by their families, the students themselves, the educational institutions they attend, or the community at large (in the case of the tax-supported school of higher education).¹ 

Nonetheless, the patient in the hospital bed does pay some of these educational costs; but their magnitude cannot be estimated since these costs are often buried under other titles on hospital accounting sheets. For example, a single dietary cost per diem is computed, and the segment of that amount which is properly earmarked "Dietitian education" is not separated in any of the hospitals studied. 

Another question which every community must face is whether or not its own hospital can continue operation without student nurse service. Nursing educators, nursing service personnel, hospital administrators, comptrollers, and trustees from several schools of nursing agree that one third to one half of the hospital rooms and wards would have to close for lack of nursing coverage if student nurses were not on the job.

The executive secretary shall be a registered nurse and shall have been registered in the commonwealth at least eight years prior to his appointment, with an academic degree in nursing or its equivalent, have at least eight years of experience, five of which shall have been in teaching or administration in a school for nurses or a combination of teaching and administration.

No person shall practice or offer to practice nursing, or use any title, sign, card, device or abbreviation to indicate that he is practicing nursing, without first complying with the provisions of section seventy-four or seventy-four A, receiving from the board of registration in nursing a registration or license as therein provided. Section 80A. A. The practice of nursing for the purposes of this section is the performing of services requiring technical skills and specific knowledge based on the principles of scientific medicine, such as are acquired by means of a prescribed course in an approved school for nurses, and practiced in conjunction with curative or preventive medicine as prescribed by a licensed physician and the application of such nursing procedures as will safeguard life and health; excluding all those who have completed prescribed and recognized courses in other branches of the science of medicine.

B. A registered nurse within the meaning of this section and sections seventy-four to eighty, inclusive, is a person who has met all the legal requirements for registration as a registered nurse in this commonwealth, and who for compensation or personal profit engages in nursing as defined in this section.

C. A licensed practical nurse within the meaning of this section and sections seventy-four to eighty, inclusive, is a person who has met all the legal requirements for licensure as a licensed practical nurse in this commonwealth, and who for compensation or personal profit cares for selected convalescent, sub-acutely and chronically ill patients, and assists the registered nurse in a team relationship, especially in the care of those more acutely ill. He will work only under the direct orders of a registered physician or the supervision of a registered nurse. Whoever, not being lawfully authorized to practice as a registered nurse within the commonwealth, practices or attempts to practice as a registered nurse, or uses the abbreviation R.N., or any other words, letters or figures to indicate that the person using the same is such a registered nurse, and whoever, not being lawfully authorized to practice as a licensed practical nurse within the commonwealth, practices or attempts to practice as a licensed practical nurse, or uses the abbreviation L.P.N., or any other words, letters or figures to indicate that the person using same is such a licensed practical nurse, shall, ex-

A person practicing as a registered nurse or as a licensed practical nurse in violation of any provision of this section shall recover no compensation for his services.

Section 81.
