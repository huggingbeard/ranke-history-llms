APPENDIX A NEW MANUSCRIPT OF THE DESTRUCTION DE ROME AND FIERABRAS BY H. M. SMYSER THE forthcoming supplementary Catalogue of Manuscripts of the 

Egerton Collection, accessible in proof in the Manuscript Room of the British Museum, contains a notice ¹ of a newly-acquired version 2 of the Destruction of Rome, and Fierabras. It is evident, the notice reads, that the Destruction portion "was composed as a prelude to [the Fierabras portion], or some version resembling it, for both differ in the same sort of way from the vulgate version, and the character Espiart, the sultan's spy, who occurs in both, is not found in the other French version. It is also clear that both [portions], or some version closely resembling them, lie at the back of the English poem The Sowdone of Babylon (ed. E. Hausknecht, Early 

Eng. Text Soc., extra series xxxviii, 1881).” 4 Without knowing of this version, I endeavored in the 1931 volume of 

Studies and Notes (Vol. XIII)³ to reconstruct the original Destruction of Rome, and Fierabras (D Fi), using as authorities the Hanover version of the Destruction (H D), the common, published version of Fierabras (K Fi), and the Sowdon of Babylon (S). I have had the opportunity since that time of examining the new Egerton version and have found that several corrections of, and additions to, my remarks in the Studies are in order.

Although the two poems are so closely related, the Sowdon is, nevertheless, not a translation of Egerton, for it agrees with H D or K Fi in a number of particulars in which Egerton differs. The discovery that the Sowdon is not a translation (however much abridged) of D Fi itself but of an intermediary redaction (X) forces us to accord it less authority in the reconstruction of D Fi. Moreover, not all characteristics of the Sowdon which cannot be ascribed to D Fi may henceforth be safely attributed to the English translator. Three of these characteristics must in fact be ascribed to X. In the first place, the author of X and not the translator is responsible for the peculiar order of events in S and is accordingly the memory-worker to whom that order points. In the second place, the garbling of Charlemagne traditions which appears in S is in part at least the fault of X. It will be recalled that, at the close of the Sowdon, Ganelon is executed for his treasonable acts at Mantrible. Since Egerton also contains this execution, it must have been found in X, whose author, therefore, must have been ignorant of, or indifferent to, the tradition embodied in the Chanson de Roland. This fact accords perfectly with the substitution in X of Isere for Garin, upon which I have already commented, and which also reflects independence of the Chanson tradition. Finally, the faults
