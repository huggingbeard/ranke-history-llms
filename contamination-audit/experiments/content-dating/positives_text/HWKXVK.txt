HISTORICAL SKETCH OF NORTH BERWICK By SAMUEL BUFFUM 

URING the first ninety years of the history of Maine the town of Kittery included Eliot, Berwick, North and South Berwick and a part of Lebanon. The charter for the Colony of Maine was given to Sir Ferdinando Gorges, a very excellent English gentleman, who had been of great service to the country and stood very high in the estimation of the King. He had a worthy ambition to be the leader and protector of a colony of first-class Englishmen, who should establish in this new land English laws and customs and add to England's fame and power. He took great care in gathering mostly in the south of England people of character and property and credit as well as of courage and the spirit of adventure. As I read and think of this devoted man and his faithful followers, who first settled Kittery and York, I feel a greater admiration and reverence for their memory than for that greater and far more pretentious Puritan group, who, at about the same time, landed in 

Boston with John Winthrop as leader. Gorges was a

Church of England man but there is no evidence that he or those who joined his enterprise had any special religious convictions to parade. But they were honest men with a high sense of honor and integrity, with a spirit of independence and progress.

The first settlement was at Kittery Point and the islands and shore near where the Navy Yard is located. Anyone visiting the east bank of the Piscataqua river can easily see how attractive it must have been to the ship loads of good people who had been tossed indefinitely on the Atlantic in the little crowded cabins. They soon had land cleared and log houses built all along to the head of tidewater at the mouth of our own river. There was little need to spend hard labor in the construction of roads or bridges. They established ferries and were always on terms of cordial

They were on good terms with the Indians who were not then very numerous and not warlike. Their grovelling habits were not conducive to great strength and longevity. The fox holes and bear dens were about as sanitary as their wigwams. These dwellings were constructed by setting up poles in a circle of twenty and thirty feet in diameter, the tops of the poles being bound together by birch or alder withes. Then with sharp edges of flat stones they cut from large birch trees sheets of thick bark. With this they covered the poles using hemlock boughs to add to the warmth. Cooking was done by building great fires on hearths of flat stones in a circle of movable stones. When the fires were reduced to coals the vegetables and meat and fish were poured upon them and covered with green grass and spruce foliage. Our clambakes are an imitation of aboriginal cookery. My grandfather has told me of seeing an old Indian eat a pickerel. All the cooking the fish had was by lying in the sun. He took head in one hand, tail in the other, and got large mouthfuls. Then by a skillful use of tongue and teeth the fleshy part went to the savage throat while the bones fell from each corner of the broad mouth. It was said that there were two localities in our town where there were little clusters of wigwams of these peaceable aborigines. The region was unexplored except by trappers who put up temporary huts in their eagerness to secure the one product of the new world which brought ready money, that is, the furs of beaver and mink. 

This northeast corner of the great town was early called Kittery Common and it was understood that all good citizens had rights up here. The friendly natives came by their trails and were ready to exchange their furs for fish hooks and lines, knives or other utensils. A kettle would buy all they possessed.

This locality in very earliest years got the name of 

Doughty's Falls. Judge Hobbs once told me that much time had been spent in trying to find out something about the man who left his name here, but it was all a failure and there is no record of any Doughty owning property or transacting any business here.

Returning to those noble and aspiring families who had made homes for themselves on the bank of the Piscataqua and were clustering around the mouth of the Great Works river, we find they had learned to treat each other justly, to get along peaceably with the Indians and had schools and some religious service. They lived happily in their hard, progressive labor and high hopes till the troubles came for which they were not responsible and which grew to be tragedies almost too horrible to record.

To understand this era of gloom and horror one must go back to the old country. Charles I, the honored sovereign and royal friend of their patron and protector, Governor

Gorges, undertook to rule England by absolute power and without a parliament. So when the Long Parliament was chosen there was civil war and the parliamentary forces chased the King and his army around England till they captured him and beheaded him, and the executioner held up the King's head on a pole in the public square and said "This is the head of a traitor." Then the charter of the 

Colony of Maine was annulled and the career of Governor 

Gorges, their great patron and protector, was ended. His agent, Walter Neal, was an upright man but seems to have lost all his authority after this time. The colonists were quite dismayed. Parliament ordered them to submit to the jurisdiction of the Puritan colony of Massachusetts. They objected and refused for a while but there was no alternative and so Maine became a part of Massachusetts and remained so for 170 years. Now the revolutions and successions to the throne in England brought on wars with France. France had a permanent hold on Canada and a more successful way of getting along with the Indians. 

They easily converted them to the Roman Catholic faith, intermarried and gave them the French language. They occupied New Brunswick and Nova Scotia and claimed Maine. They had strongholds at Castine on the Penobscot and at Norridgewock at the headwaters of the Kennebec. As soon as war started in Europe these half French 

Indians thought they had a right and duty to kill all the English or frighten them from this coast. The Kittery settlers saw their danger and called on Massachusetts for protection. Those were the years when the Puritan religion-

Whole families were tomahawked and their dwellings burned and practically the whole village at Quamphegan and the settlement along the bank of Salmon Falls river were destroyed. There was gloom everywhere. 

Garrisons were built to which all could flee for defense. 

At Wells, a few years later, a company of English soldiers aided the settlers in building a strong stockade and defending it against an attack by French and Indians which lasted three days. The attacking party lost so many that they retreated towards Canada in despair. These, going and coming, must have trailed over this region but no English settlement was here. High bounties were now offered for 

Indian scalps and companies were enlisted for pursuing the same kind of savage warfare. One, under Captain Lovell, went as far as Fryeburg and suffered a sad defeat. The place of this disaster is now shown on the shore of Lovell's Lake in Fryeburg. This company was made up of men from Kittery, York and the new town of Berwick and were attracted by the bounties as high as fifty pounds ($250) offered by the Massachusetts authorities for Indian scalps without discrimination as to whether they were cut from the heads of good or bad Indians. ing:

It is interesting to see that the settlers of Kittery Common came from both sides of the Salmon Falls, Cochecho, and Piscataqua rivers as they were all very cordial and for a time hardly knew where their boundaries were or under what jurisdiction they lived. Hanson, Hussey, Meader, Roberts, Nutter, Quint, and Ford seem to have come from Dover. 

Plaisted, Knight, Grant, Grover, Wentworth, Hammond, Weymouth, Abbott, Nowell, and Billings were among the early families of Kittery.

Now appears the name of John Morrill. He was not an original settler but he is at this time a resident on the river bank which is now Eliot. He and his wife were members of the Society of Friends, and their home in England had been in the county of Kent. He had money, and perhaps he had an eye to speculation. Perhaps he had a sincere desire to aid the stricken neighbors. The far-off wilderness of Kittery Common had just been surveyed, and lines established, called range-lines, and check-lines had been mapped and numbered in square lots called checkers. These checkers were allotted by numbers to voters in Kittery. Many were glad to get a little money for their shares, while others who were young and ready for adventure came later to occupy and clear the land. John Morrill bought a very large number of these checkers, so that it was said that he and his family owned fully half of what is now the town of North Berwick. He was certainly the first permanent settler, and in 1693 built his log-house where Leslie Sherbourne now resides. Later we find his grandson, Peter Morrill, owning all the water power and the adjacent land on both sides of the river. Some local historian has put on record that a daughter of Peter Morrill was killed and scalped by the

Indians. This is untrue. The story as told to me by my mother is this. The young girl, Sarah Morrill, was in feeble health, and one spring morning walked out into the woods near our Wells street to gather hemlock boughs for a broom. 

She almost stepped on an Indian, who lay concealed behind a log. The Indians were always skulking just like partridges and foxes, perhaps on account of their very rude dress and their lack of knowledge of any of the courtesies of civilization. The young girl screamed with all her strength, and the Indian tried as hard to tell her that he would not hurt her. He was as much shocked as she at the thought of rousing the whole settlement, and he put his hand over her mouth and choked her, so she was literally scared to death. The Indian gave himself up, and the Friends believed his story, and he was never punished and never did any harm. Here was an opportunity for revenge and for an Indian war. The fact is that no scalp was ever cut from any human head in Kittery Common.

The great town of Berwick was set off from Kittery in 1713 and the men would go to Blackberry Hill for their colonial town meetings. This was some 70 years before the war of independence and 107 years before Maine became a State. In the marriage certificates of that period is a clause which states that they are loyal subjects of their gracious sovereign, the King of England. 

One can get a pretty good picture of early family life here by reading some portions of Whittier's poem "Snowbound." Whittier's ancestors were members of the Society of Friends and lived in Dover. Perhaps one-half of this little community were Friends and were closely connected and somewhat subordinate to Dover in matters of discipline. Old people have told us of that kitchen life where a large kitchen served for dining room and living room. 

Here in winter the great fire was kept with back log and forestick in the open fireplace where swung the crane with hanging kettles in which the family cooking was done: "No matter how the night behaved 

No matter how the north wind raved, Blow high, blow low, not all its snow 

Could quench our Hearth-Fire's ruddy glow."

Great brick ovens were in use whose walls were intensely heated and then filled with pots of beans and pork and loaves of brown bread. No cast-iron cooking stoves were even thought of. The Franklin Stove came into use some 50 years later and was not much more than a movable fireplace with a reflector for an oven. Potatoes were baked and corn popped in the hot ashes. 

In the War of the Revolution this part of Maine furnished a large number of men who were credited to Massa chusetts. At the battle of Saratoga where General Benedict 

Arnold forced the surrender of General Burgoyne's army, Captain Ichabod Goodwin commanded a company enlisted wholly from Berwick. Quakers, of course, did not enlist.

They had a very great respect for the opinions and maxims of Benjamin Franklin. Franklin believed that by steady and persistent argument and statement of facts the independence could be gained without war and without the heritage of hate which war engenders. Franklin's sojourn in England had a tremendous influence on English statesmen. Today's historians say that American Independence was fought out by Burke, Fox and Pitt in the British Parliament far more effectively than by Washington's Army. 

Benjamin Estes and Joshua Buffum, first of those names, did not reach here from Salem, Massachusetts, till 1743. This was 50 years after the arrival of the first settlers, but even this date was 40 years before our country's independence. The Buffums kept some record of the way of life during those long eight years of war and non-intercourse with England. They picked thorns and shaped them for needles and pins. Plates, spoons, knives, and forks were made of wood. Strips of the inner bark of elms were used for twine or braided for ropes. Clam shells of all sizes were in common use, and all pails, tubs and baskets were made in the farmer's kitchen. A razor was a neighborhood common property and was passed from house to house to keep smooth friendly faces.

Many Revolutionary soldiers settled here, and we boys used to think that some of them had donations of land in compensation for services, but this was a mistaken notion.

Silas Heard, Benjamin Hurd, Joseph Heard, Joseph Savage, Goodwins, Chadbournes, Abbotts, and others. They all worked hard and paid for the land. The government had no power to levy taxes or collect money to pay bills for some ten years after its formation. Major Savage, a retired army officer, was the first postmaster at Doughty's Falls. The first post office was in what is now the ell part of the residence of Mrs. Marcia E. Welch. The two-story part of that house was built by Levi Rogers who kept the Rogers Inn. 

This Rogers family seems to have been large property holders.

Daniel McCrellis, ancestor of the families of that name, bought his land of Squire Rogers.

In the war of 1812-1815 there was a general condition of gloom. In those days there was no steamship, railroad, or telegraph. The stage-coach was the quickest way to carry news. When the year 1815 came in, Commissioners were sent to meet British Commissioners in Belgium to conclude a treaty of peace. The long, long delay caused great impatience. So it was arranged that when the wished for news came great bonfires should be lighted on all the highest hills. Accordingly, when the news came at last a great fire was seen on Agamenticus and there was a night of illumination and a continuous cry of "Peace."

My great-uncle, Colonel Samuel Buffum, was a recruiting officer in that war. There is a record of his having a pretty hard time. He did his duty and held his rank in the Maine militia during life. He had a command at Fort 

Fairfield in the time of that farcial performance called the Madawaska War when there was a dispute over the Maine- 

New Brunswick line. 1816 was a year of gloom and threatened starvation. The losses and distractions of business occassioned by the war, and the burden of taxation, were added to by a frost in June, July and August which destroyed the crops of corn and beans and almost spoiled the potatoes. A few fortunate families had rye bread. Food could not be brought as now from other sections. In September of that year a heavy gale came which uprooted trees and blew down frail barns. 

Then began the great migration to Ohio. Covered wagons were built containing a whole outfit for a family,

drawn by oxen, or horses. Thus families would sleep by night and move by day over the difficult roads the whole length of the States of Massachusetts and New York to settle on the shores of Lake Erie. I do not think our town entered into this migration as much as some nearby towns. 

To hunt up the fragments of record or to call on the elderly people and gather what is now held in memory of what the fathers and grandfathers have passed along gives one a feeling of reverence for those who met the hard conditions in making homes on this soil. In the first place it took wisdom to choose a location. They knew where the soil supported great trees bearing nutritious beech nuts and acorns and walnuts they could raise crops of rye, wheat and barley. From 1695 to 1740 began the very hard labor of cutting, burning, hacking, to clear stumps for sowing grain and to produce grass for the proper fattening of cattle. 

Then came the harder work of picking stones large and small from the fields and enclosing them with stone walls. 

In Colonial times there was an attempt by Massachusetts to set up their ecclesiastical machinery down here. 

They sent a sound Puritan to Blackberry Hill and tithing men were appointed to see that all went to hear him and pay him by general taxation. But this did not suit the independent spirit. Baptist preachers found ready hearers. 

The Bible was quite generally read and rather clear opinions formed and liberty of interpretation was conceded. 

Benjamin Randall, founder of the Free-Will Baptist Church in this country, was at one time a member of the Baptist Church which at that time was located near Great Hill in South Berwick. This Church was finally moved to its present location in North Berwick village. It is the oldest Baptist Church in Maine. Benjamin Randall received his thrilling call to the ministry after hearing the great evangelist, George Whitefield.

Although "Elder" Randall did not settle here as a regular pastor he had a powerful influence upon the spiritual life of this locality. He preached temperance, a better system of education, and a general higher scale of thinking. 

Ecclesiastical shackles have never fitted here nor have there been serious attempts to impose them, and there is a general feeling of thankfulness for the liberty enjoyed.

Living not far from the coast, some of the boys went to sea and gave their neighbors good and true accounts of what the world was doing. At certain seasons of the year it was customary to break away for a little while from the regular routine of the daily toil for a general good time of social intercourse. One of these occasions was the 26th of June known around here as "Cure-All Day." At five o'clock in the morning the procession would begin-lines of vehicles filled with happy people driving to the seashore for an all day picnic and a dip in the cold sea water. An ocean bath on that special day was a sure cure for all their ills. No one seems to know where or when the custom originated. On some of the farms there were great crops of yellow corn. As soon as the long ears were mature the top stalks above the ears were cut and bound in bundles while green, and dried for cattle fodder. Then on some pleasant day in October the remaining stalks and ears were cut up clean and put in one long, high pile on the barn floor. 

Lanterns were hung over it and seats of boards placed around it and an evening set for husking. 

The farmer and his boys did nothing but carry off the baskets of ears to the corn house, a structure set on posts covered in part with tin to keep out the rats and mice. 

When all was accomplished these co-operative huskers were feasted with pork and beans, brown bread, and Indian pudding. All this was generally done in good social order. 

There were many apple orchards in this locality and, in different sections of the town, cider mills were set up and cider became quite generally a popular and social beverage. 

Rum from the West Indies was brought by sailing vessels to Quamphegan (South Berwick Landing) and hauled from there in large quantities by teams of oxen and sold along with groceries and other goods. Gradually a sentiment began to grow against the use of hard cider and alcoholic beverages. Then came the earnest labors and eloquence of Neal Dow, the appeal of the women, the agitation of the 

Sons of Temperance, the Good Templars, and other organizations, until at last prohibition became the law of the State and finally of the Nation.

A hundred years and more ago flax was cultivated on most of the farms. It was a long, tedious task to get this converted into threads suitable to be woven into the homespun cloth. After cutting the flax, it had to be put through the rude process of rotting in order to separate the pulpy part of the stock from the strong fibre. This was done by placing it along the shore of some pond. Then there was a process of breaking and swingling and combing, and so getting out all the short fibres which were called tow. The beautiful, long, silky fibres were spun on a different wheel from that used for wool. This flax wheel was run by a treadle. In the attics of old farmhouses, you sometimes come across one of these funny little wheels. By this long, tedious process they got a very strong linen thread which, when woven with yarn, from long staple wool, would wear for a lifetime. But how men and women could muster the patience and perseverance to bring up families with all this burden of labor on their backs is quite a mystery. When we get a little perplexed by our problems it is well to think of our ancestors in these backwoods as they took up their unending tasks.

Persons no older than the writer can remember seeing a flock of several hundred sheep wandering over a common pasture ground, each farmer having a special mark for the members of his own flock.

So the family of healthy boys and girls had their food and clothing pretty well assured.

I will touch briefly upon some of the pioneer families who built up the framework of our community.

We very early find Thomas Hobbs operating a mill for sawing shingles where the Hussey Plow Company is located. He owned a large tract of land stretching from that point away north beyond and including the mill privilege a mile up the Great Works river. Here his sons built a sawmill, a grist mill, and afterwards a carding mill. "Squire"

Hobbs, as he was always called, (I think his name was William) was the one man here fully qualified by scholarship and general information to represent the town of Berwick at the Massachusetts Legislature. He was elected for several successive years. It was a half-holiday always when "Squire" Hobbs saddled his horse, arranged his saddle bags, shook hands all around, and started to attend the great and general court at Boston.

Every gentleman had his horse and would not be dependent on the uncertain coaches. Col. Nathaniel Hobbs kept a popular and rather celebrated tavern at the house now made over into two residences and occupied by Judge 

Spinney and Cashier Wesley Johnson. I well remember the swinging sign reading "N. Hobbs Inn 1806." There was a wide courtyard at the south where two four-horse coaches could drive around and meet. Two large stables fronted on this court. Fresh horses were taken here and passengers dined here. Lafayette rested and perhaps lunched one day at N. Hobbs Inn, when the street was arched with evergreen and flowers. Every dweller in the town turned out to welcome this great friend of Washington's and the village resounded with tumultuous cheers. 

This Colonel Hobbs was grandfather of the late Judge

Hobbs. It is this same Colonel Hobbs to whom we are indebted for the fine old elm trees planted by him on both sides of Elm street one hundred years ago.

We find the names of Humphrey Chadbourne and 

Daniel Goodwin prominent in the leadership and progress of the settlers on the Piscataqua and at Quamphegan. 

Humphrey Chadbourne erected a house at "Strawberry 

Banke" (Portsmouth) in 1631, just 300 years ago, and his descendants were up here among the very earliest settlers. 

One of his most brilliant descendants was Paul Ansel Chadbourne. Leaving home when quite young he fitted for college at Exeter Academy, earning his own way, graduated at

Williams as valedictorian of his class, in a few years was called back as professor of natural sciences and afterwards succeeded Mark Hopkins as president of Williams College. 

For several years he filled the chair of natural sciences at Bowdoin College and also for a few years served as president of the University of Wisconsin. He was author of a number of books on theology and the natural sciences. After retiring from his nine years' term as president of Williams College he took up the lighter duties as president of the Massachusetts Agricultural College, having been the first president of this college years before. Dr. Chadbourne's visits to his native town were always hailed with joy. There was often a sermon by him on Sundays in one of the churches and at other times a free public lecture which gave his hearers inspiration and aspiration. Some of his subjects were Geology, Newfoundland, Iceland, Mining in the

Rocky Mountains, Utah and the Mormons, and the Resources of the United States. He always brought in humorous allusions to boyhood days and local issues. While Dr. 

Chadbourne was a professor at Williams College, James A. 

Garfield was a student under him and a friendship was formed which lasted through life. At the time President 

Garfield was shot he was on his way to attend commencement exercises at Williams College. After the commencement, Dr. Chadbourne came to North Berwick and while here President Garfield's death occurred and at special services held here, Dr. Chadbourne delivered the memorial address. This address together with other manuscripts of his are in the archives of Williams College.

New Hampshire, and president of the P. S. & P. R. R., whose daughter became the wife of Admiral Dewey. This Ichabod 

Goodwin had a brother, Dr. Daniel R. Goodwin, who became president of Trinity College and afterwards president of the University of Pennsylvania.

Of the Morrill family Anson P. Morrill and his brother, Lot M. Morrill, both Governors of Maine, are descended 'from our North Berwick Morrills but not born here. Daniel Morrill, who became president of the Cambria Iron & Steel Company, of Johnstown, Pennsylvania, was born on the farm now owned and occupied by Sumner Morrill. Daniel Morrill was a member of Congress and chairman of the commission that inaugurated and carried through the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia in 1876. His sister, Mrs. Vienna G. Ramsey, was a woman of marked ability as preacher and author.

Peter M. Neal, who lived in the house which is now the home of his nephew, William Frank Neal, was Mayor of

Lynn during the Civil War. He used to tell us of his visits to the City of Washington and of his interesting conversations with President Lincoln.

James N. Buffum, prominent abolitionist and also Mayor of Lynn, was born in the house which was the home of his niece, the late Phebe Buffum Varney. Mr. Buffum, accompanied by Frederick Douglass, the negro orator and former slave, held many public meetings advocating the cause of the black man.

In the year 1833, Obed Hussey, a native of this town, then living in Cincinnati, Ohio, brought out and patented his great invention, the reaper. The essential features of this invention were the finger bar and reciprocating knife which made the Hussey reaper the first successful grass and grain cutting machine in the United States, and probably the first in the world.

The name of Neal occurs at the first landing at Kittery Point and the progress of the colony and clearing of land at Kittery Common. At my first recollection there were two branches, the singing Neals and the quiet Neals. Reuben

Neal gained fame by building a church organ at his farmhouse. What kind of music this afforded I do not know but all his descendants were musicians or singers, some very fine. They yield us no conclusion as to what is better, music or quiet, as both branches are highly and equally trustworthy, industrious and exemplary in life.

The name of Peter Morrill is associated with the early industries in which water power played an important part. 

It was through his efforts that the smelting and working of iron was undertaken. These operations were carried on somewhere near the present dye house of the North Berwick Company. Bog iron ore was dug from the ground and hard labor was spent in attempting to produce the much needed iron. On some of the old farms there are ox chains made from Doughty's Falls iron. A crowbar made of this iron and now owned by Daniel P. Morrill has been in the 

Morrill family for several generations. Although the iron was said to be of poor quality, yet it seems remarkable that iron of any kind could be made by men without experience in the art and with their crude appliances. Peter Morrill had money, and a carding mill was built and equipped with the best machinery for changing the wool of the farmers into rolls and rovings, which were spun and woven into heavy cloth at the farmhouses. Later he had machinery for giving a nap and finish to the cloth. 

Peter Morrill's daughter married a good Quaker whose name was Thomas Stackpole. This man Stackpole became much interested in the process of manufacturing wool into cloth. He had the assistance of John Mayall, an Englishman from Yorkshire, who thoroughly understood the manufacture of woolen goods including carding, weaving, spinning, dyeing, and finishing. Mayall carried on hand weaving in his home in the house now owned and occupied by Mrs. 

Marcia E. Welch. His mind was completely taken up with his occupation and the story is told that when he lost his cow he went hunting for her with his eyes fixed on the sky. 

It was through the efforts of Peter Morrill and his sonin-law, Thomas Stackpole, and the Englishman, John Mayall, that the woolen industry was established here. 

There was a famous clock maker, Paul Rogers, who lived in the house now owned and occupied by Frank W. 

Grant. His tall, grandfather clocks are still found in a number of our older homes.

In my boyhood great droves of cattle went through here, which had been gathered from farms north of us to be driven through to Brighton, the great market near Boston. Hides were plenty and at one time there were five flourishing tanneries in our town. Hemlock bark for tanning was an important commodity and easily obtained. 

Footwear was all made by skilled itinerants who went from

house to house with bench and kit of tools and a cowhide and side of sole leather. This shoemaker would take measurements of all the family feet and construct cowhide shoes and high top boots. Many shoes of the boys were made from the father's old boot legs. These cobbling shoemakers took apprentices and were men of exemplary patience and resourcefulness as well as skill. They had to deal with all kinds of families and meet emergencies. After the first 

40 years several families from Lynn, Massachusetts, settled here. There were sloops plying regularly from Lynn or 

Salem to Kittery. They could sail clear up to Quamphegan. 

This made easy and cheap transit for passengers and goods. 

After a time there came to be an intimate acquaintance, friendship, and business connections with Lynn which eventually made quite a revolution in industry here. Some went to Lynn and learned the finer arts of finishing shoes and new branches of light work. Capitalists in Lynn found it very profitable to cut their stock to the right pattern and

Shoe shops were built on the farms and all the available space in the village was occupied by busy workmen, lasting, sewing, shaving, scraping and polishing shoes. The man in Lynn for whom this work was done was always called the boss and the place to which the finished product was sent was called the seat of work. Maine became a sovereign state in 1820. Eleven years later the good State granted a charter to the town of North 

Berwick. Soon the North Berwick Company was incorporated and a new canal constructed which greatly increased the water power.

Some of the elderly men can remember as boys the woolen mill of the North Berwick Company with its immense water wheel which turned so slowly and carried the fulling stocks, of tons weight, to pound and pad the long thick fabric which was carried out on the backs of a line of men and fastened to the tender bars, dried and brushed and carried in to be finished.

Times have changed and finer machinery set up and finer goods made, and everybody is hoping that the same generous and thoughtful owners may have a profit while they still make it the mainstay of the village life. 

The manufacture of plows has been carried on in this locality for more than a hundred years. A man by the name of Hoag brought with him from his home in Vermont a cast-iron plow. An improvement was made in the furrowboard of this plow and from the pattern thus obtained the first cast-iron plows in the State of Maine were made. 

The lumber industry, which from early days has occupied an important place in this town, has undergone great changes.

The first sawmill was located on the Negutaquit stream near the present dam of the Hussey Plow Company. The deed for the land where the sawmill stood has the signature of Sir William Pepperrell.

In the year 1855 William Taber, of Fairhaven, Massachusetts, built a sawmill operated by steam power for the manufacture of shooks for boxes. 

This business changed hands in 1869 and steadily increased in volume. The logs were drawn to the mill in winter, in earlier times mostly by ox teams. It was not unusual to see a line of ox teams reaching all the way from the mill yard to Snow's bridge, each awaiting his turn to enter the yard and be unloaded. The pine growth in this section of Maine with the box mill offering a cash market for the logs was a source of ready money for the farmers, in fact the pine trees were considered the best crop the farmer produced. The box mill is now known as the J. H. 

Dunning Corporation. This company has one of the best planned and most up-to-date mills in the country, equipped with the most improved machinery for making lock-corner and other styles of packing boxes.

The business of the manufacture of stove polish must not be omitted from our annals. From a very small beginning this industry in the hands of the Prescotts, men of broad vision and of unusual business acumen, grew steadily from year to year until the plant was finally removed to Passaic, New Jersey, and became the largest factory of its kind in the world.

In 1842 the P. S. & P. R. R. was built, the North Berwick station erected, and the name Doughty's Falls faded from view everywhere except in the yearly records. At each town meeting it bobs up when the moderator cries out "Bring in your ballots for selectman from Doughty's Falls district." The railroad company for its first 30 years or more used wood for fuel and this made a great market here. 

In winter our streets were filled with loads of wood drawn by long strings of oxen. Pitch pine which was worth little for boards or timber sold readily for fuel. Men were employed with old bucksaws and long sheds were erected and a general scene of clutter prevailed where all is tidy now. 

Gone, too, forever, is that general tumult of the old farmer dialect with its "back star" and "gee buck" and "whoa hishe" and "Heaow's the sleddin' up your way." The ox yoke and the goad are fast becoming curiosities for the museum, the farmer who speeds his auto at 40 miles an

hour will no longer walk by the side of Josh Billings' "horned creatures that scarcely seem to go."

In connection with the railroad another business sprang up which spread the fame of our town from coast to coast-"North Berwick Sponge Cake." Mr. William C.

Briggs, a cripple to whom the railroad company felt an obligation, built here what was called a "restorator" and all the trains were stopped with the announcement "Ten minutes for refreshments." Now Mrs. Briggs was a wonderful genius in cookery. She searched the village till she found the well with the purest water and had a cask constructed and suspended between four wheels and no other water was allowed to come in. The eggs must be perfectly fresh and produced by the happiest hens. All other material was just as faithfully inspected. This was long before the invention of the Dover Egg Beater and she had a way of beating which brought a rich yellow foam which no human power could beat. Then she had a special oven of peculiar construction and an exact temperature. The result was a kind of sponge cake which everybody wanted and nobody could duplicate. One has to search ancient poetry for the adjectives applied to the ambrosial delicacies made for the food of the gods. It was a sight worth beholding to see the crowds of men and women rush from the cars to the "restorator" to buy a large piece of the etherial cake and if possible drink a cup of the delicious coffee. Charles Dickens at one time passing through the town in company with 

Kate Douglas Wiggin, a little girl then twelve years old, stepped off the train and obtained some of the North Berwick sponge cake for his little friend.

A business developed at this period which deserves mention and which gave to a section of our village the classical name of "Rabbitboro." Some boys had pet rabbits which multiplied, and sale was found easy for the cunning little creatures. Bits of board from the box factory and wire which the telegraph company had thrown away alongside the railroad furnished material for cages and it is a very pleasant recollection, the childish voices of those boys as they stood by the door of the "Restorator" and called out, "Rabbits 50 cents." The seller was pleased to carry home the money while the boy in the city to whom they went had

the greater pleasure. These boys who made this venture in business became efficient officers in a distant city.

The building of the pumping station and erection of the reservoir on high pillars and the introduction of pure water from Perkins Brook was a great boon and blessing to the town. Health, comfort and security have come with our system of faucets and hydrants and it seems to be thankfully appreciated. 

The oldest man now living will remember Horatio G.

Herrick, the embodiment of law and order. He came here from Alfred a young man, was a fine attorney, a good scholar, always dignified and yet ready to converse cordially with the ignorant and dependent. He was Justice of the Peace and in many ways impressed his character on the town. 

He went from here to Lawrence, Massachusetts, where he was a successful lawyer. His father had been high sheriff of York County and as a boy he became familiar with all rules of criminal procedure, and he too aspired to an executive office. After a few years' residence in Lawrence he was elected Sheriff of Essex County which contains the cities of 

Lynn, Salem, Beverly, Gloucester, Newburyport and Haverhill. He was popular and efficient and held the office to his old age. 

Sumner I. Kimball came to us from Sanford. He served us well for several years as attorney-at-law. He did good work and was appreciated and no doubt our village life was beneficial to him. He got an appointment to the 

Treasury Department at Washington where he became the founder of the Life Saving Service. It is acknowledged that he was the organizer and efficient head for many years of that great and beneficent service which has grown to be the Coast Guard, saves life, and guards against smugglers and other law breakers.

I recall my first excursion as a boy to the town meeting. It was held in the Elder Lord Church, a quaint structure at Oak Woods. It had old square box pews, each enclosed by a swinging door, and a very high pulpit. The chancel in front of the pulpit made an excellent place for the moderator while the communion table held the ballot box. At the second visit the middle pews were taken out and good open space made for the rough crowd to stand and

talk and smoke. It was a rare entertainment for a boy to get into the high pulpit and see the voting go on and listen to the wrangling over questions pertaining to roads, bridges and schoolhouses while the moderator shouted his orders and decisions.

The Elder Lord who had consecrated and made this building a strong religious center must have been a man of great force of character. There was somehow cultivated a rugged honesty and a spirit of sturdy independence which are still an inheritance for the town to appreciate. 

About 1845 to 1850 the cultivation of tomatoes began. 

This was done not for obtaining ripe ones. All were gathered while unripe, boiled in a very sweet syrup and served about as we serve prunes. After a few years somebody found that the ripe tomatoes, or "love apples," were palatable when treated with sugar and soon a taste was acquired. Then it was found that the plants must be started. in March, and kitchen window sills were filled with boxes of earth with growing plants. Then somebody built a greenhouse and boxes of growing plants became an article of merchandise at the gardening season. This, with a variety of flowering plants, has become an important industry. Ripe tomatoes are a necessary food supply at all seasons. Attempts to produce our own sugar were not as successful. 

Sorghum seed was distributed free in 1857 and it was thought that from this Chinese sugar cane our farmers could supply New England with syrup and sugar, but our season is not long enough. Some 20 years later the manufacture of beet sugar was attempted at Portland and the farmers here found a market for carloads of beets. But our children would not creep all day on their knees weeding beets and our grown people found it too low down to suit their tastes. So that business in Maine faded out and still we keep sweet.

Some benevolent man of wealth seeing the great need of higher education in the community would found and endow an Academy and the State Legislature would donate land or money to aid it. In time it would receive other aid so that education could be obtained at small cost. Academies were founded at South Berwick, Limerick, Lebanon, Parsonsfield, and in other towns. Our town furnished its full quota of the "Forty Niners."

When in 1848 gold was discovered in California a great thrill shook the young men of New England who were quite tired of the monotonous farm life. There was a captivating lure to this opening Eldorado. Some made the long voyage around Cape Horn. A way of crossing the Isthmus of Panama was soon found, and in a few years the construction of that short tropical railway was undertaken which cost the lives of thousands in its building. Some of our finest young men died from exposure in the new climate and the tent life of the diggings. Others, in a very few years, returned with no gold in their pockets and with the spirit of adventure subdued, glad to get where civilization prevailed and life and property were secure. Many Maine men lived through the era of outlawry in California and helped to form the stable government, became judges, merchants, fruit growers, and deacons of great, flourishing churches. I think if the census-takers of that state this year had made note of all whose ancestors had husked corn in North Berwick the list would have done honor to the town.

The remarkable rise of Sanford gave a corresponding lift to Bauneg Beg and Oak Woods. In the last century when Great Falls became a great cotton manufacturing center our town had to build and improve the roads to New Hampshire to make easy outlet for hay, wood and other products which found an improved market. But at the building of the plush and worsted factories in Sanford there was a good down hill road already there and Sanford was willing to improve it and was glad to see it well patronized. Here was a ready market, not only for farm products, but also for the labor and genius of the young men and women. The automobile and the smooth and durable roads have brought the two towns into closer contact and we are well united in the general desire and promise to seek the highest welfare of the good State of Maine. 

The same year that saw the incorporation of our town saw also the erection of three brick dwelling houses, the only ones ever built here. The brown clay which deeply underlies our soil is excellent material for bricks of red color and a level space by a mill stream formed the ideal site for a brick yard and kiln. Joseph Drew built the fine residence on Wells street now owned by Arthur Snow, Thaddeus Morrill, the house on Oak Woods road owned by Sumner Morrill. Dr. Came, who was the town's popular physician, built the present Harvey mansion. Each building is a good specimen of the bricklayer's art and architecture. The era of brick factory building was from 1816 to 1840. About that time Elijah Neal erected the fine brick blacksmith shop near the Wells street bridge. The building of the woolen factory in 1862, Commercial Block in 1893, our substantial brick schoolhouse later, and the bank in 1923, have given our village an air of dignity and permanence. Our structures being nearly all of wood it was a beautiful and appropriate thought of Mrs. Hurd to present as a gift to the town the brick fire station, an ornament to the village and fully equipped with up-to-date fire fighting apparatus and modern system for giving the alarm. Each year we are reminded of the practical interest the late Judge Hobbs had in his native town when he set apart in his will a sum of money the income of which is to be used annually for the improve-

ment of our roads. North Berwick is grateful to her sons and daughters who thus express their kindly feelings towards the town of their birth. 

The beautiful public library, recent gift of our genial and generous fellow citizen, Daniel A. Hurd, and built near the end of our first century stands as a symbol prophetic that intellectual and spiritual forces are to hold their rightful place in the century to come.

THE MILLER 

I will sing of the miller who lived here and died, Lived for others, for us, while himself he denied. 

Wholesome food he produced in his water-turned mill 

But his chief distribution was love and good will.

What the season afforded of corn, rye and wheat Was confidingly laid at this honest man's feet. 

All the corn got converted which rude farmers brought While he sought to convert, too, their speech and their thought. 

The village life grew, business moved toward success But progressive refinement seemed less and still less; 

So there came to his well-balanced up-turning mind 

A high moral grist which his mill could not grind.

Now to join in glad worship each Sunday he took 

A long weary journey to Beaver Dam Brook.

Thus he found himself filled with a longing desire For a church and a preacher with pulpit and choir. 

Let us never forget that the miller's pure thought Planted church service here and its great blessing brought. 

As now we draw nigh to our century's end We will utter our song for our town's truest friend; 

That sweet name of Friend had bloomed fruitless for years; 

My muse looking back notes its fragrance with tears. 

How the world's incrustation turned blossom to blight 

Behold the sad sequel, a lost divine right.

So while Friends held the name with high sounding pretense The miller's friends gathered with sound sober sense. 

The planting of Free Will thus humbly begun Has brought a broad friendship which blends all in one. 

The great name of Ebenezer we thankfully cherish,

In our town its rich memory never can perish; 

It means that God's help has been ours hitherto 

With promise of leadership loyal and true.

When the corn-growing business fled to the Great West New England's corn-grinders were left sore distressed. 

When the whir of the wheels which supported him ceased His love of strict righteousness only increased. 

He would have church educate, reconcile, lift 

To the happiest standards of honor and thrift;

And like Luther who nailed his contempt of the Pope 

He wrote Prohibition, the nation's great hope. 

Were he living today how engrossed he would be

In our present church services, wise, helpful and free; 

How cordial his friendship with brave girls and boys 

Who take the church pledge with its cares and its joys.

Looking back to those days when church service began The great warm moving spirit was this saintly man.
