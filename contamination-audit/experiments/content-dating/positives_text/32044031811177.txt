PREFACE 

The principles of each topic within this subject are presented in the form of LEGAL GEMS. (1) If the principle is brief, clear and easily understood, it is left simply in the form of a statement. (2) If the principle is a bit complex or may not be easily understood, it is illustrated by a simple example immediately following the LEGAL GEM. 

(3) If the principle is very important or difficult to understand, it is illustrated by a CASE which follows the LEGAL GEMS on the topic. The CASE is also intended to present an effective method of constructing an opinion in the solution of a legal problem.

REAL PROPERTY I. FREEHOLD ESTATES 

There are three freehold estates (a) fee simple, (b) fee tail and (c) life estates.

Seisin means the possession of one who claims a freehold estate in land. Hence, every freehold tenant is seised. 

1. Fee simple.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A fee simple estate is always (a) real property, (b) inheritable and (c) of indefinite duration, that is, may last forever. 

2. A fee simple estate is the largest estate in land and the owner of such estate has five rights as to the landa. he may use the land, b. he may take the fruits of the land, c. he has the right of exclusive possession of the land, d. he may abuse the land, and e. he has the right to dispose of the land by deed or by will. 

3. There are four classes of fee simple estatesa. fee simple absolute, e.g. A grants "to B and his heirs". This creates in B a fee simple absolute which is wholly indefeasible. b. fee simple determinable or subject to special limitation, e.g. A grants "to B and his heirs as long as B or his heirs person-

ally live on the premises". This creates in 

B a fee simple because it may last forever. 

The special limitation is that it is to continue only so long as B or his heirs personally live on the land. If and when he or his heirs cease to live on the land then such fee simple estate automatically comes to an end. The possibility of reverter in the grantor, A, then takes effect and reverts to A giving him the possession in fee simple. Such is the expressed intention of A in his deed. c. fee simple subject to condition subsequent, e.g. A grants "to B and his heirs but if he or they ever sell intoxicating liquor on the premises, then I reserve the power to re-enter such premises and terminate such estate". This creates in B a fee simple because it may last forever. But even if intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises by B or his heirs the fee simple does not automatically terminate. Two things must occur to terminate the estate. (1) Intoxicating liquor must be sold on the premises, and (2) A, or his heirs if A is dead, must do some affirmative act of re-entry onto the premises or bring a law suit thus exercising the power of termination. Only when both these things take place will B's fee simple be terminated. d. fee simple subject to executory intereste.g. A, having a fee simple absolute in land grants it "to B and his heirs this deed to take effect five years from this date, January 30, 1956". In this case A has a fee simple subject to a springing use in B and upon the coming of January 30, 1961, a use springs up in B and the Statute of Uses creates in B a legal estate in fee simple. 

Thus, upon the delivery of A's deed to B, has a fee simple estate subject to the executory interest created by the deed in B. If

11 such interest is created in B by will instead of by deed it is called an executory devise. 

4. To create a fee simple estate at common law two things had to concur, (a) there had to be a ceremony of feoffment and (b) the feoffor had to use words of inheritance following the name of the feoffee, e.g. A, feoffor, and

B, feoffee, went onto the land to be conveyed. 

A handed B a clod of earth symbolical of the land and said, "I hereby enfeoff B and his heirs of Blackacre". The words "and his heirs", called words of inheritance, were absolutely indispensible. They were the only words which would create in B a fee simple estate. Had A used the words, "I hereby enfeoff B in fee simple", B would have taken only a life estate because only "B and his heirs" and no other words had the effect of creating a fee simple estate. The words, "and his heirs", were words of limitation.

That is, they described the size of the estate created in B. They were not words of purchase. That is, they did not describe persons who were to have an interest in the land. 

This means that B's heirs took no interest. 

Only B took an estate in the land and that estate was a fee simple. 

5. Under modern statutes the deed of conveyance takes the place of the ceremony of feoffment and "words of inheritance" are not necessary to create a fee simple. The statutes usually provide that the grantee takes a fee simple unless it is indicated that he shall take a lesser estate. e.g. A grants "to B". Under such modern statutes B takes a fee simple. 

In such case at common law of course B would take only a life estate. 

CASE 1 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, grants it "to B and his heirs". B grants Blackacre "to C and his heirs". B and C

REAL PROPERTY 

die intestate at the same instant, killed in a common accident. 

B leaves H his sole heir. C leaves no heirs lineal or collateral. 

A statute provides that the property of one dying intestate without heirs shall escheat to the state. Both H and the state claim 

Blackacre. Who owns the property?

Ans. The state is the owner of Blackacre. A's grant to B created in B a fee simple because the words of inheritance, “and his heirs", are words of limitation describing the estate in B. 

They are not words of purchase describing persons to take. 

Hence B's heirs take nothing. B, having the fee simple estate has power to dispose of the fee simple and does so to C who likewise takes a fee simple estate. There is then no interest left in B which can descend to his heir, H. Hence, upon B's death intestate H takes nothing by descent or otherwise. At C's death he is the sole owner of Blackacre. His fee simple estate will descend to his heirs, if any. In the absence of heirs the fee simple estate escheated to the overlord at common law in the feudal system. But today under modern statutes such as we have in this case the property of an intestate leaving no heirs escheats to the state. Hence the state takes C's Blackacre by escheat under the statute.

CASE 2 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, conveys it "to B in fee simple". B dies intestate leaving H his only heir. Both A and 

H claim the property. Which should prevail? 

Ans. (a) Under the common law A should prevail. (b)

Under modern statutes H should prevail. (a) Under A's conveyance at common law B has a life estate only. A life estate is not inheritable and hence upon B's death there is nothing to pass to his heir, H. The reason is this. Without the magic words of inheritance, "and his heirs", following the name of the grantee, B, A's conveyance could not create in B a fee simple estate which could be inherited by B. These words "and his heirs" were absolutely indispensible. The words "in fee simple" could not create more than a life estate. Hence, at common law A must prevail. (b) On the

FREEHOLD ESTATES 

13 other hand under modern statutes where words of inheritance are not essential to the creation of a fee simple estate and where the estate created is deemed a fee simple unless an intention to create a lesser estate is evinced, it is clear that B takes a fee simple estate for two reasons, (1) the conveyance expressly so states and (2) there is no intention that a lesser estate be created. Thus, under modern statutes A's intention to create a fee simple estate is given effect and B's fee simple estate passes to his heir, H, by descent. 

CASE 3 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre and Whiteacre, grants Blackacre "to B and his heirs so long as intoxicating liquor is not sold on the premises". He grants Whiteacre "to B and his heirs, but if intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises then I have a right to terminate the estate". B sells liquor on each of the premises. (a) Who owns Blackacre? (b) Who owns Whiteacre?

Ans. (a) A is the owner of Blackacre. (b) B is the owner of Whiteacre. A's deeds created in B a fee estate in each piece of property because the estate in each may last forever. Any estate which may last forever is a fee. But A's grant of Blackacre created in B a determinable fee which by its very limitation (the time it was to last) is automatically terminated by the sale of liquor on the premises. The determinable fee is sometimes denominated a base or qualified fee. But A's grant of Whiteacre created in B a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent, i. e. the selling of liquor on the premises. But such estate is not to come to an end automatically. B's deed says, "I have a right to terminate the estate". This means the fee simple continues in B until (a) intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises AND (b) A (or if he be dead then his heirs) exercises his power to terminate B's estate by some affirmative act such as entering on and claiming Whiteacre or bringing an action in court for that purpose. The difference is important and substantial and of course is a matter of interpreting the language used in the conveyance. As to Blackacre B's fee

REAL PROPERTY 

simple comes to an end automatically by the sale of liquor thereon. There is no forfeiture. As to Whiteacre B's fee simple continues until A exercises the power of termination. The exercise of such power causes a forfeiture of B's existing estate. But in this case such power is not yet exercised by A. 

He may or may not exercise it. Hence, A is the owner of Blackacre by B's estate coming to an end, and B is the owner of Whiteacre until A exercises his power to terminate such. When these deeds were delivered A had a possibility of reverter as to Blackacre and a power of termination as to Whiteacre. 

CASE 4 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B and his heirs, to have and to hold for the term of B's natural life and then for B's two children, C and D and their heirs forever, provided however if either C or D dies without issue during B's lifetime, then all of said Blackacre is to go to the survivor of C and D". C, during B's lifetime, dies without issue. Then 

B dies leaving W, his widow, who claims dower in Blackacre and which D resists. Does W have the right to dower? 

Ans. No. There are three distinct parts in A's conveyance, (a) the granting clause "to B and his heirs", (b) the habendum clause, "to have and to hold for the term of B's natural life" etc., and the proviso, "provided however if either C or D dies without issue" etc. If A's conveyance created in B a fee simple estate, then his widow, W, has a right to dower consisting of a life estate in one third of Blackacre. But if B has only a life estate in Blackacre then W has no right to dower. Whether B has one or the other of these estates is a matter of construing A's conveyance. In construing an instrument of conveyance the finding and giving effect to the intention of the parties is paramount and all rules of construction are subservient thereto. In other words, rules of construction are resorted to only in case the intention of the parties cannot be found. To find such intention the instrument should be read as a whole and, if possible, every part thereof should be given effect and made to consist with all the other provisions. In the case of a

FREEHOLD ESTATES 

15 conveyance all parts take effect at the same instant, that is, when the instrument is delivered to the grantee. In this case the granting clause used words of inheritance, "to B and his heirs”. 

If we stopped there and used the rule of construction that the "first deed and the last will governs", then we would say B has a fee simple. Or if we used the rule that the granting clause having created a fee simple, neither the subsequent habendum clause nor the proviso can derogate from nor cut down such estate, then B would have a fee simple. But such rules are not applicable when the intention of the grantor can be found and made effective as it can be in this case when the conveyance is read as a whole. It is clear that the granting clause taken alone created a fee simple in B. It is also clear that the habendum clause limited B's holding to a life estate. 

It is just as clear that the proviso limits B's estate to the term of his natural life. But it is possible by reading all three of these provisions together to find A's intention, and to give effect to each exactly as A intended. A did use "and his heirs" following the grantee's name, B, but he then proceeded in the habendum clause to explain just who were intended by the expression in the granting clause, “B and his heirs". He intended 

B and his two children, C and D. Thus he used the expression, "to B and his heirs" as synonymous with "to B and his children, C and D". He used the words "and his heirs" to designate persons who were to take the property after B's lifetime, thus making them in this setting words of purchase and not words of limitation which they normally are. By so reading the instrument A's intention is clear and there is no inconsistency between any two provisions of A's conveyance. It would thus read, "to B and his children, to have and to hold for B's life and then for C and D and their heirs forever, provided if either C or D dies without issue during B's lifetime then to the survivor in fee simple". The effect of A's conveyance is to give 

B a life estate with contingent remainders in fee simple to C and D. Upon C's death without issue during B's lifetime, the contingency is resolved and the fee simple becomes a vested remainder in D subject to B's life estate. Upon B's death his life estate drops out and D has a fee simple estate in possession.

B, having only a life estate, his widow, W, has no right to dower.

CASE 5 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B and his heirs, to have and to hold for the term of B's natural life". 

B dies intestate leaving H his sole heir. The terms of the deed constitute the sole evidence of A's intention. Both A and H claim Blackacre. Which should prevail?

Ans. H should prevail. In construing the meaning of an instrument of conveyance all rules of construction should bend to the paramount rule of finding and making effective the intention of the parties. In this case A's granting clause "to B and his heirs' creates in B a fee simple estate. The habendum clause, "to have and to hold for the term of B's natural life" clearly limits B's estate to a life estate. There being total inconsistency between the two provisions of the instrument, the habendum clause is repugnant to the granting clause for B cannot have at the same time in the same property both a fee simple and a life estate. There being no other evidence from which to glean A's intention resort must be had to applicable rules of construction. Any one of the following four rules of construction is applicable, to wit: (1) a deed is construed most strongly against the grantor for he has used the words causing the ambiguity, (2) the grantor cannot derogate from his grant, (3) where the habendum clause is repugnant to the granting clause the habendum clause is void and the granting clause will govern, (4) where two clauses of a deed contradict each other the former will govern over the latter. In our case A's granting clause having granted to B a fee simple estate, A cannot thereafter derogate from his grant and thus cut down the estate granted, or by construing the deed most strongly against A, it must be held that B has a fee simple estate. Thus A's conveyance creates in B a fee simple estate which, upon B's death

17 intestate passes to his heir H. After A's conveyance to B, A has no interest left in Blackacre.

CASE 6 

A, being fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life and after B's death, then to B's heirs". B conveys Blackacre to C and his heirs. Then B dies intestate leaving H his sole heir. H sues to eject C from Blackacre. May he succeed? 

Ans. No. Under the Rule in Shelley's case A's conveyance created in B a fee simple estate which he could and did convey in fee simple to C, leaving no interest in the premises in B which could descend to his heir, H. The Rule in Shelley's case provides in substance that if in a conveyance a life estate is created in the ancestor (B in our case) and thereafter in the same conveyance the property is given to the heirs of the ancestor, then the heirs take nothing and the ancestor takes the fee simple (or fee tail as the case may be). This is a rule of property and not a rule of construction. In the former the intention of the parties is not material. In the latter, that is, rule of construction, intention is all important. In this case A's intention is clear. He intended B to have only a life estate and that B's heirs, when determined at B's death, should have Blackacre. But the Rule applies and gives B the fee simple irrespective of A's intention. Having the fee simple and having disposed of it to C, B had no interest left in Blackacre which could descend to H. Thus H cannot eject the fee simple owner, C. 

2. Fee tail.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. In England, prior to 1285, the forerunner of the fee tail estate was the fee simple conditional. It operated as follows. A conveys 

Blackacre "to B and the heirs of his body".

The effect of this conveyance was to give B a 

Smith's Review of Real Prop.-2

REAL PROPERTY 

fee simple conditional and to leave a reversion in A. A's intention was to give B a life estate and then that the "heirs of his body" take as purchasers when B died, thus keeping the land in B's family. But such conveyance was construed to give B a life estate until he had a child born alive. Such birth had the effect of barring A's reversion. Such birth also empowered B to convey Blackacre in fee simple. 

Thus, a child being born alive to B, he conveys Blackacre "to C and his heirs". Now C owns Blackacre in fee simple and both A's reversion and the entail in the heirs of B's body are cut off. 

2. The Statute De Donis passed in 1285 abolished the fee simple conditional and substituted for it the "fee tail" estate. This Statute provided that when land was conveyed by A "to B and the heirs of his body", B could do nothing which would either bar A's reversion or cut off the right of B's bodily heirs to inherit Blackacre from B upon B's death. It was thus made an inheritable estate but deprived B of his power to dispose of the property in such a way as to prevent its descent to his bodily heirs. Such estate was not favored by the courts because it interfered materially with the free alienability of property. By the time of the Revolutionary War in 1775 in such case the fee tail tenant could by a deed inter vivos cut off both the grantor's reversion and the entail of his bodily heirs and thus convey a fee simple estate. 

3. Today in the states which recognize fee tail estates, the fee tail tenant usually has the power to convey a fee simple estate. e.g. A conveys "to B and the heirs of his body". B then conveys "to C and his heirs". B dies intestate. Result-A's reversion is cut off, the heirs of B's body are cut off, and C is the fee simple owner. B's bodily heirs take nothing upon B's death intestate.

19 4. In most states the statutes provide that an estate which would have been an estate in fee tail at common law is deemed a fee simple. e.g. A conveys "to B and the heirs of his body". B has a fee simple estate. There is no reversion in A. B has all the power of a fee simple owner. In such states there is only one inheritable estate, the fee simple. 

See fee tail exhaustively covered as to states, 1 Rest. of Property, pages 201-327. 

3. Life estate. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1.

At common law there are six types of life estates- a. life estate for the lifetime of the tenant, e.g. A, fee owner of Blackacre, grants or leases it "to B for and during B's natural life", b. life estate for the lifetime of one other than the tenant, an estate pur autre vie, e.g. A, fee owner of Blackacre, grants "to

B an estate for the lifetime of C", c. life estate which results from fee tail special tenancy after possibility of issue extinct, e.g. A, fee simple owner of Blackacre grants "to B and the heirs of his body by B's wife, W". W dies without having issue. B now has a life estate in Blackacre because there can be no issue of B's body by this particular wife, W. Upon B's death 

Blackacre will revert to A. d. life estate by dower, e.g. 

H and W are husband and wife and H owns Blackacre in fee simple. H dies, W surviving. W has the right to a life estate in one third of Blackacre under common law dower. 

Only a wife has dower.

REAL PROPERTY 

e. life estate by curtesy, e.g. H and W are husband and wife. W owns Blackacre in fee simple. W has a child, X, by H, and X still lives. W dies, H surviving. H has a life estate in all of Blackacre under common law curtesy. Only a husband has curtesy. f. life estate by and during coverture. H and 

W are husband and wife. W owns Blackacre in fee simple. During the time H and 

W are married, which may be for life, all of W's lands are subject to H's control during the coverture due to the wife's common law disabilities and because H and W are one and he is the one. 

2. If an estate MAY last for a lifetime it is a life estate even if it may be cut short before it naturally comes to an end, e.g. A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, leases it "to B for as long as B wishes to live in City X". Because B may wish to live in City X during his entire lifetime he has a life estate in Blackacre even though he has the right and the power to terminate such life estate by moving elsewhere. 

It is not material that A has no right or power to terminate the estate and such fact does not make B's interest a tenancy at will. 

3. A life tenant may have a determinable life estate, e.g. A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, grants "to B for life as long as no intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises". B has a determinable life estate which will terminate automatically if and when intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises. But it is a life estate because it MAY last throughout B's lifetime without any such liquor being sold on the land. 

4. A life estate may be one subject to condition subsequent, e.g. A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life, but if intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises then A

FREEHOLD ESTATES 

21 has the right to enter and terminate the estate". B has a life estate which will continue until (a) intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises AND (b) A by affirmative act exercises his power of termination. But it is a life estate because it MAY last throughout B's lifetime without being terminated. 

5. A grant to a named grantee creates a life estate when the conveyance fails properly to specify the type of estate intended to be created, e.g. A, fee owner of Blackacre in a jurisdiction which requires words of inheritance, "and his heirs", to create a fee simple estate, conveys it "to B forever". B takes a life estate only. 

6. An estate created in a grantee for the lives of several persons and the survivor of them is a life estate, e.g. A, fee simple owner of 

Blackacre, conveys it "to B for the lives of B, C, D and E and the survivor of them". B has a life estate which will last as long as the survivor of the four persons. 

7. A life tenant has most of the rights of a fee simple owner over the land except that he must preserve the corpus of the property substantially without injury for the reversioner. 

He may (a) use the property, (b) take the crops and fruits thereof, (c) dispose of his life estate, thus creating in his transferee an estate pur autre vie, (d) possess it exclusively except that the reversioner has the privilege to come onto the property to collect the rent if any be due, to make repairs to preserve his reversionary interest or to determine if waste is being or has been committed. 

8. The life tenant has no right to commit waste by injuring the corpus of the property and may not open new mines or quarries thereon. 

9. The life tenant has some duties respecting the premises. He must make ordinary repairs to

CASE 7 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for the term of B's natural life". B then conveys "to Call of my interest in Blackacre". C dies intestate leaving B surviving.

There is no statute. Who is entitled to the term between the death of C and the time when B dies?

Ans. The general occupant is so entitled at common law. 

In the absence of a statute such problems should be solved according to the common law. It is clear that A's conveyance created in B a life estate for B's life. B had a right to dispose of his life estate. When he did convey all of his interest in 

Blackacre to C, it is clear that C had an estate pur autre vie, that is a life estate for the life of B. When C died intestate his interest in Blackacre, being real property, could not pass to his personal representative because only personal property passes to the executor or administrator. Further, a life estate cannot be inherited. Therefore such term cannot pass to C's heir for an heir can inherit only an inheritable estate, a fee simple or a fee tail. Nor does the reversioner, A, have any right to such estate because he has granted it away for as long as B lives. The same is true as to B. At common law, then, the general occupant, which means the first person to take possession of Blackacre after C's death, could hold the property until B's death. This was not because such general occupant had any right to the property but because no one had any right to put him off. Hence, he held merely by right of prior possession. This is one of the so-called "loopholes" of the common law. Generally by statute today C's personal representative is given the term and may hold the property until the death of B.

See 1 Rest. of Prop. § 151.

CASE 8 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, grants it "to B for life". 

B leases Blackacre to C for 99 years. C takes possession and uses the premises for 10 years at which time B dies. During the lease C has installed valuable trade fixtures on Blackacre and seeks to remove them which removal is prevented by the reversioner, A. C sues A for conversion of the trade fixtures. 

May he recover? 

Ans. Yes. There are three freehold estates, fee simple, fee tail and life estate, of which the life estate is the smallest. 

But every freehold estate is, in legal contemplation, larger than the longest non-freehold estate. Further, every freehold estate is real property whereas every non-freehold estate is only personal property. In our case B is granted a life estate out of which he carves a 99 year leasehold. There is a reversionary interest left in B even though, of course, his life estate may end before the 99 year term. But in no event can B convey more than he has. C's 99 year lease can in no event extend beyond 

B's life in point of time. Hence, when B dies the 99 year lease in C comes to an end because the house cannot stand when its foundation has been removed. In the field of trade fixtures the rule for removal is this. If the term is for a definite time then the tenant must remove the trade fixtures before the end of the term. This is reasonable because he knows exactly when the term will end and he has no right to interfere with the possession of the reversioner thereafter. But if the term is for an uncertain time and the tenant never knows when it will come to an end, then the tenant has a reasonable time after the term ends in which to remove the fixtures. In our case the 99 year term is of certain duration. But it is based on and depends for its continued existence upon the uncertain term of B's life span. Therefore C's leasehold is in substance of uncertain duration and he should have a reasonable time after B's death in which to remove the trade fixtures. 

Having such right and A having prevented the removal of the trade fixtures within such reasonable time, A is guilty of converting the trade fixtures and is liable to C for so doing.

NON-FREEHOLD ESTATES 

There are four non-freehold estates (a) tenancy for years, (b) tenancy from period to period, (c) tenancy at will and (d) tenancy at sufferance.

Since seisin requires possession by one claiming a freehold estate, a tenant of a non-freehold estate is never seised. 

1. Tenancy for years.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The tenancy for years is by far the most important non-freehold estate. 

2. Every tenancy for years must begin at a certain time and end at a certain time. Unless it does so it is not a tenancy for years. e.g. L's lease of Blackacre to T for the period "June 1, 1950 to and including May 31, 1956", is a tenancy for years. 

3. The expression, "tenancy for years" is only a name and includes a tenancy for a definite time for less than a year, e.g. L leases Blackacre to T for the period "June 1, 1955 to December 1, 1955". This is a tenancy for years because it has a specific date for beginning and a specific date for ending even though it continues only for a period of six months. 

Similarly, a lease "for three months beginning January 31, 1956", is a tenancy for years. 

4. A tenancy or estate for years is PERSONAL 

PROPERTY, a chattel real. It is an estate in real property but IT IS NOT REAL 

PROPERTY. Upon the death of the owner of a tenancy for years such estate passes like any other personal property to his personal representative; never to his heir. 

5. An effective creation of an estate for years always includes the followinga. an estate in the tenant or lessee,

25 b. a reversion in the landlord or lessor, and c. possession and control of the land in the tenant.

These constitute privity of estate between the landlord and the tenant. Leases usually contain agreements between the parties which constitute privity of contract between the parties. 

6. The creation of a leasehold is a conveyance and need not contain any element of contract, e.g. L leases Blackacre to T "for one year beginning January 1, 1956", the instrument or lease containing no item of agreement for payment of rent, for repair, for payment of taxes, or otherwise. This is a valid lease involving only privity of estate with no privity of contract therein. Mere delivery of the lease creates the leasehold estate in T. 

7. When an estate for years fails because of noncompliance with the Statute of Frauds or otherwise the result is usually a tenancy at the will of the parties, but subsequent events such as payment and acceptance of rent for a year or a part thereof may result in there being a periodic tenancy from year to year, month to month or week to week, e.g. L orally leases Blackacre to T for a period of five years at an agreed annual rent when such lease is required by the Statute of Frauds to be in writing. T takes possession of the premises. At this point T is a tenant at will. 

Thereafter T pays and L accepts rent for a year. From such acts it may be inferred that the tenancy at will is transformed into an estate from year to year. 

8. An estate for years may be determinable, e.g. 

L leases Blackacre "to T for 10 years until intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises". 

If T sells intoxicating liquor on the premises the lease automatically comes to an end.

9. An estate for years may be subject to condition subsequent or power of termination, e.g. 

L leases Blackacre "to T for 10 years, but if intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises the lessor reserves the right to enter and terminate the lease". The lease continues until (a) such liquor is sold on the premises AND (b) L by affirmative act exercises his power of termination. 

10. As between landlord and tenant the lessor impliedly agrees that he will do nothing to interfere with the lessee's quiet enjoyment of the premises; but the lessor does not impliedly agree to protect the lessee's possession against the trespasses of third persons, e.g. L leases Blackacre to T for five years.

T takes possession and is physically evicted by X. T sues L for such wrongful eviction. 

T cannot recover from L in the absence of an express agreement as to such. Of course, T may sue and eject X. 

11. If a tenant agrees to pay rent to the landlord such obligation continues even though the premises become wholly uninhabitable during the term of the lease, e.g. L leases Blackacre to T for three years for dwelling house purposes. Lightning strikes the house and fire destroys it. T remains liable to L for payment of rent for the balance of the term. 

Such is the common law but it has been changed by statute in many states. 

12. In a landlord-tenant relationship the tenant purchases the leasehold under the doctrine. of caveat emptor-let the buyer beware.

Hence, the lessor is not liable for injury to the lessee from a dangerous condition of the premises which a reasonable inspection of the premises would disclose, e.g. L leases

13. If a tenant agrees to pay rent to his landlord for a single use to be made of the premises and that use becomes illegal, the tenant, in the absence of an agreement to the contrary, is still liable to pay the rent, e.g. L leases 

Blackacre to T for use solely for the sale of intoxicating liquors. A statute makes such sale illegal. T remains liable at common law to pay the rent because he still owns the leasehold and must pay for it. This involves no impossibility of performance as in contracts because T has not agreed to sell liquor but only to buy an estate.

CASE 9 

L leases Blackacre, an apartment in an apartment house, to T for a period of three years, it being agreed that L shall furnish sufficient heat from a central heating system to keep T and his family comfortable therein. During a cold winter the central heating system fails to function part of the time and when it does function it pours smoke and soot into T's apartment in such volume as to make the apartment uninhabitable and injurious to health. T and his family are made ill by the effects of the smoke, soot and cold and their family physician directs them to move out to conserve their health. This condition continues for two months without interruption. T and his family move out and L sues T for rent. May L recover?

Ans. No. When L made the lease to T he impliedly agreed that he, L, would do nothing which would interfere with T's quiet enjoyment of the premises. There are two types of eviction, (a) actual and (b) constructive. Of course L would breach such covenant for quiet enjoyment if he physically evicted T from the premises and such eviction would release T from his obligation to pay rent. In legal effect there is no difference between actual and contructive eviction. Either on the part of the lessor is a breach of his implied covenant for quiet en-

REAL PROPERTY 

joyment and will release the tenant from his obligations under the lease. In our case L's failure to furnish heat is a breach of an actual provision of the lease, and his casting smoke and soot into T's apartment in such volume as to make living there both unbearable and dangerous to health constitutes a constructive eviction which violates L's implied covenant of quiet enjoyment and releases T from his obligation to pay rent. Hence 

L cannot recover.

CASE 10 

L, being in possession of and honestly thinking he is the fee simple owner of Blackacre, leases it to T for three years. 

During the term one of the neighbor's boys throws a baseball through a small second story window. The boy tells T and pays to T the cost of replacing the window. Thereafter T has ample time to have the window replaced before a violent wind and rain storm causes great quantities of rain to blow through the broken window and seep through the second story floor which causes the plaster on the ceiling of the room below to fall. L sues T for the damage to the building by the falling of the plaster. T sets up in defense (a) that L is not the owner of Blackacre but that O is such owner and (b) that T is not liable for the falling plaster. Is either defense good?

Ans. No. This set of facts raises two principles of the common law, (a) a tenant is estopped to deny his landlord's title and (b) a tenant is duty bound to make ordinary repairs to preserve the property and is liable for permissive waste. In this case the possession of Blackacre came from L to T. It was 

L who cast the benefits of the possession and use of the land onto T. From this base the common law forbad the tenant to question the title of the landlord without first tendering the term and turning over possession to such landlord. Hence, T having received such benefit from L, he is estopped to deny L's title and cannot therefore set up the title of O, a a third person, in defense of L's action. On the subject of repairs it may be said that the tenant is neither duty bound to prevent ordinary wear and tear or depreciation to the buildings on the leased

NON-FREEHOLD ESTATES 

29 premises nor is he duty bound to rebuild a structure which is destroyed through no fault of his own. However, a tenant is duty bound to make ordinary repairs which will preserve the leased buildings from serious injury from the elements. In this case the tenant is in a peculiarly advantageous position to replace the broken window because he occupies the house, has been told of the damage and paid for its repair. In such case the lessor often has no knowledge of such damage and having to interfere with the tenant's possession to make repair is not in a position to make minor repairs. T had ample time to make such minor repair and he was duty bound to make such. His failure to perform such duty caused substantial injury to the building and for such breach of duty and the resulting damage 

T is liable to the landlord, L. 

See Townshend v. Moore, 33 N.J.L. 284 (1869).

CASE 11 

L, fee simple owner of a rooming house, Blackacre, leased it to T for five years. Both L and T knew that the handrail along the rear outside stairway leading to the second story of the rooming house was unsafe. Nothing in the lease referred to such stairway nor to L's obligation to repair such. T requested L many times to repair the handrail and each time L promised to make such repair. Finally L undertook to make such repair but did it in a negligent manner so that the handrail was still unsafe. One of T's roomers, X, descended the rear stairway carefully, and leaned on such handrail. The handrail gave way and X fell to the ground and was injured. X sues L for the damage. May he recover?

Ans. Yes. In the absence of express agreement there is no duty on the landlord to repair leased premises. The lessee takes such leasehold under the caveat emptor doctrine as to all conditions thereon which are visible or apparent on inspection. 

Hence, L is not liable to repair the handrail on the rear stairway by virtue of the lease. And there being no additional consideration for L's promise made subsequent to the lease to repair the handrail, such promise is nudum pactum and places

REAL PROPERTY 

no enforceable obligation on L. However, when L actually undertook to repair the handrail and did it negligently which caused X's injury, then L is liable to X for the damage. It should be noted that the lessee, T, could have recovered had the same injury happened to him on this set of facts. If that be so, then T's roomer or invitee, X, has a similar remedy against the lessor for once L undertakes the affirmative course of action to repair, his duty is owed not alone to the lessee but also to the tenant's business guests to act with due care. A landlord is also liable to the lessee for any dangerous condition of the leased premises which is known to the landlord and which an inspection by the lessee does not reveal. This is on the ground that the lessor is bound to give notice of such condition and if he does not, then he leads the lessee into a trap. 

This case does not involve such principle because both parties knew of the dangerous condition. 

See Olsen v. Mading, 45 P.2d 23 (Ariz.1935).

CASE 12 

L, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, a section of land on which are grown crops of barley, corn, oats, rye and others, makes an agreement with T whereby T is to furnish the machinery, seed grain and labor for preparing, planting and harvesting specific crops to be raised on the premises during a three year period beginning March 1, 1953. T is to enter on the land at any time for the purpose of accomplishing the purpose of the agreement. For the use of his machinery and the labor required in raising the specified crops T is to be paid in “kind”, that is, in a fractional part of each crop raised when said crop is harvested. The fractional part which T is to have is one half. It is agreed that L will make division of the crops and deliver one half of same to T. What legal relation obtains between L and T?

Ans. Landowner and hireling or master and servant. This set of facts represents one type of "share cropping", which is an ambiguous term. L, being owner in fee simple of the land, has actual or constructive possession thereof. The agreement

NON-FREEHOLD ESTATES 

31 entered into with T simply provides that T is to furnish the machinery, seed grain and labor for raising and harvesting crops and that for such accomplishment T is to be “paid” in a fractional part of the grain raised and that L is to make the division. Nothing is said about a change in possession of 

Blackacre. And to accomplish the results sought to be had it is quite unnecessary for T to have possession of the land. Of course he must enter and work on the land but that does not require his having possession thereof. Hence, we must conclude that L is to remain in possession of the land. That being the case there can be no landlord-tenant relation. Such relation always requires that the landlord deliver possession of the leased premises to the tenant. Hence, the gist of the agreement is merely that T is to plant, cultivate and harvest crops on Blackacre for which he is to be paid one half of the crops harvested. This is a contract of hiring in which the landowner, L, retains possession of his land, remains the owner of the crops grown thereon and divides those crops and delivers half thereof to the hireling for his efforts expended in the production thereof. It should be carefully noted that possession of the land is the crux of such a problem. If the landowner retains possession of the land and pays for the labor in production of the crops, there is a landowner and hireling or master and servant relation. If the landowner delivers possession of the premises to the one who is to produce the crop and such possessor pays the landowner rent for the use of the land, then the relation is one of landlord and tenant.

On November 1, 1954 L demands rent from T who refuses to pay such. What, if any, is

If he chose the former he could hold T liable for the reasonable value of the use and occupation of Blackacre from March 1, 1954 until the time of T's leaving the premises. 33 the original lease the rent was an annual rent although paid in equal monthly installments. From this it is concluded that T is a tenant from year to year on the same terms as the original lease except as to the term. That being the case the year to year tenancy would continue indefinitely until either party notified the other not later than six months before the end of any rental year, which would be not later than August 31st of a given year in our case, that the tenancy is to end on the following last day of February. III. CONCURRENT ESTATES 

There are four concurrent estates (a) joint tenancy, (b) tenancy by the entirety, (c) tenancy in common and (d) tenancy in coparcenary. Of these (a) and (c) are by far the most important.

A concurrent estate is one owned by two or more persons at the same time. When one person owns an estate it is termed tenancy in severalty, e.g. A owns Blackacre. 

1. Joint tenancy.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Joint tenancy is always created by deed or . will and there must be two or more grantees or devisees. 

2. Joint tenancy is preferred over tenancy in common at common law whereas under modern statutes tenancy in common is preferred over joint tenancy. Hence, a grant "to A, B and C and their heirs" creates joint tenancy at common law but a tenancy in common under modern statutes. 

3. In every joint tenancy there are four unities, (a) time, (b) title, (c) interest and (d) possession. e.g. X, fee simple owner of Blackacre in severalty, conveys it by deed "to A, B, C and their heirs". At common law A, B and 

C take Blackacre as joint tenants because (a) there is unity of time because title vested in all three at the same time, that is, at the delivery of the deed, (b) there is unity of title because all three took title from the same source, that is, from X's deed, (c) there is unity of interest because each grantee, A, B and C, owns the undivided whole of Blackacre with the other joint owners, and (d) there is unity of possession, which means the possession of each joint tenant is the possession of

REAL PROPERTY 

all the joint tenants and the possession of all the joint tenants is the possession of each of the joint tenants as a matter of law. The key to unities lies in understanding that the joint tenants all together constitute a single fictitious person because joint means oneness. So when A, B and C own all of Blackacre, then A owns all, B owns all and C owns all of the property. A DOES NOT OWN

ONE THIRD, B DOES NOT OWN ONE THIRD AND C DOES NOT OWN ONE THIRD of the property. EACH OWNS THE WHOLE WITH HIS JOINT TEN- ANTS. 

4. The GRAND INCIDENT or chief characteristic of joint tenancy is SURVIVORSHIP. 

This means that upon the death of one joint tenant the survivors of the joint tenants own the whole of the jointly owned property. e.g. A, B and C are joint tenants of Blackacre in fee simple. A dies testate or intestate.

Blackacre is owned in joint tenancy by B and C because all the four unities still obtain. and C take nothing from the decedent, A. 

They owned the undivided whole of Blackacre all the time. One joint tenant merely drops out as an owner. Furthermore, the fact that A leaves a will attempting to dispose of his interest in Blackacre is immaterial because survivorship is effective immediately upon A's death and takes precedence over the provisions of the will. Suppose then 

B dies. Then the surviving C owns Blackacre in severalty in fee simple. Suppose then

C dies intestate. The result is that C's heirs alone take Blackacre. The heirs of A and B have no rights therein. 

5. Each joint tenant has a right and power to dispose of a fractional interest which he does not own. e.g. A, B and C are joint tenants of Blackacre in fee simple. A conveys either "all of my interest in Blackacre to D and his

CONCURRENT ESTATES 

37 heirs" or "my undivided one third interest in Blackacre to D and his heirs". In either case an undivided one third interest passes to D in fee simple. Of course there is no logic in recognizing that A as a joint tenant owns the undivided ALL of Blackacre with his two joint tenants B and C on the one hand, and that A can dispose of an undivided fractional one third part of the property on the other. But such is the result of the oneness of ownership in such tenancy and the right to sever and dispose of his interest by one of the joint tenants. And what are the positions of the parties after A's conveyance to D? (a)

B and C are still joint tenants as between themselves as to an undivided two thirds interest in Blackacre. (b) As to D the unities of time and title do not exist for he took his title at a different time and from a different source, namely A, than did B and C. Therefore, D is a tenant in common of an undivided one third of Blackacre on one hand and B and

C, as to D, jointly constitute a tenant in common as to the other undivided two thirds. 

Note that B and C are one as to D, because they hold their two thirds jointly. Now suppose D dies intestate leaving X and Y his sole heirs. His one third will descend to X and Y, each taking one half of D's one third or an undivided one sixth of Blackacre. Suppose now B dies. Then the two thirds which 

B and C held as joint tenants become the sole property of C by the doctrine of survivorship. 

Result-C, X and Y are tenants in common of Blackacre, C owning an undivided two thirds, X an undivided one sixth and Y an undivided one sixth. 

6. A joint tenancy is destroyed by a conveyance inter vivos (never by will) or by partition. e.g. A and B are joint tenants of Blackacre. 

They agree that A shall take the north half and B shall take the south half, followed by

REAL PROPERTY 

each taking his specific part. The joint tenancy is destroyed. A owns his north half in severalty. B owns his south half in severalty. 

7. Joint tenancy may be created between husband and wife if such is the clearly expressed intention. e.g. A grants "to H and W, husband and wife, and their heirs, as joint tenants and not as tenants by the entirety". 

8. Because each joint tenant has a right and power to dispose of an undivided fractional interest in the jointly owned property voluntarily, a creditor of any joint tenant has a right to levy upon and sever involuntarily the fractional interest which the debtor can convey.

B is the owner in fee simple. It should be first noticed that A is the grantor and he also appears with B as grantee. Of course it is agreed by all that one cannot convey to himself. A cannot convey to A, for such is a futile thing and accomplishes nothing. But that is not what A's conveyance does. The crux of the matter lies in the meaning of the word joint. Joint means oneness. In joint tenancy it means that two or more persons for the purpose of being a grantee (notice the singular number) and holding in joint tenancy constitute one fictitious person in contemplation of law. That is the very meaning and purpose of the word joint in joint estates. Applied to our set of facts it means that "A and B", as grantee, is a distinct, separate and different person than the grantor, A. Upon A's death the surviving life tenant, B, continues to hold for his life by survivorship because during the joint life · period each joint tenant owned the whole of the property and not a fractional part thereof. A's death merely dropped out and leaves B sole owner for B's life with A's reversion descending to his heir, H, in fee simple. Such is the common law.

2. Tenancy by the entirety.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Tenancy by the entirety is created only by deed or will and can exist only between husband and wife. 

2. Tenancy by the entirety is a species of joint tenancy in which each spouse owns the undivided whole of the property. 

3. Five unities always obtain in such tenancy, (a) time, (b) title, (c) interest, (d) possession and (e) person. The first four are the same as those in joint tenancy. The fifth means that the husband and wife are in contemplation of law one person. 

4. A conveyance "to husband and wife and their heirs", without more, creates a tenancy by the entirety. 

5. In tenancy by the entirety neither spouse alone has a right or a power to dispose of any interest therein. They must join in the conveyance. 

6. Because neither spouse alone has the right or power to dispose of any interest in the property owned in tenancy by the entirety, a creditor of either spouse cannot levy upon any interest therein. 

7. Survivorship obtains in tenancy by the entirety. e.g. H and W own Blackacre in tenancy by the entirety. H dies testate or intestate. W owns the whole of the property by survivorship. If W dies first then H owns the whole by right of survivorship. 

8. Divorce destroys the unity of person and the spouses become tenants in common thereby. 

9. Neither spouse has a right to have the property held in tenancy by the entirety partitioned.

3. Tenancy in common.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Tenancy in common may be created either by deed, will or by operation of law. e.g. H and

W own Blackacre by the entirety. A divorce is had. H and W are then tenants in common of Blackacre by operation of law. 

2. Under modern statutes tenancy in common is preferred over joint tenancy.

A con- veys Blackacre "to B and C and their heirs".

Whereas at common law B and C took as joint tenants, today they take as tenants in common, each taking an undivided one half interest. REAL PROPERTY IV. INCORPOREAL INTERESTS IN REAL PROPERTY 

These interests consist of mere rights and cannot be possessed physically. They are rights or interests held by one person in the land of another person. They include (a) easements and profits, (b) covenants running with the land at law, (c) equitable servitudes and (d) licenses. 

1. Easements and profits.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A profit is the right of one to go onto the land of another and take either part of the soil itself or a product of the soil. e.g. A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, grants to B the right to come onto Blackacre and quarry marble and cut the timber thereon. Note the marble is soil itself whereas timber is a product of the soil. Such right includes the right to come onto Blackacre, quarry the marble,' cut the timber, and take them from the land. 

2. An easement is the right of one person to go onto the land of another and make a limited use thereof but without any right to take either the soil or product thereof. e.g. A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, grants to 

Ba right to use a 20 foot strip across the north end of his property over which B may drive cattle. 

3. In the United States the principles applicable to and governing easements are alike applicable to and govern profits, including classification, creation, extinguishment and assignability. Except for the differences set forth above, for purpose of analyses the use of the word easement hereafter may include profit. 

4. There are two classes of easements— (a) appurtenant and (b) in gross.

INCORPOREAL INTERESTS 

45 5. An easement appurtenant requires (a) a dominant tenement, (b) a dominant tenant, (c) a servient tenement and (a) a servient tenant, and it exists for the purpose of serving the dominant tenant in his use of the dominant tenement. Such an easement is attached to the dominant estate and passes with it as an incident when the dominant estate is conveyed. 

6. An easement in gross requires (a) a dominant tenant, (b) a servient tenement and (c) a servient tenant, and it exists for the benefit of the dominant tenant. There is no dominant tenement. 

7. An easement is real property when it is to last as long as a freehold estate, a fee simple or life estate; it is personal property when it is to last for years. 

8. Easements and profits, appurtenant and in gross, are assignable, devisable and descendible. 

9. If doubt exists whether an easement is appurtenant or in gross, it will be construed as one appurtenant. 

10. Whether or not an easement or profit is apportionable is determined by the intention of the parties, e.g. A, owner in fee simple of 

Blackacre abutting the ocean, granted to B, owner of Whiteacre, the right to come onto 

Blackacre and take all seaweed necessary to use as fertilizer on Whiteacre. B conveyed half of Whiteacre to C. Both B and C may continue to take seaweed from Blackacre to fertilize their respective pieces of land because such continuance will not overburden 

Blackacre and it seems the intention of the parties. Thus the profit is one which is apportionable. 

11. Easements or profits are created in three ways, (a) by grant or devise, (b) by prescription and (c) by implication.

REAL PROPERTY 

12. The scope of an easement created (a) by grant or devise is determined by the intention of the parties, (b) by prescription by the extent of the use during the prescriptive period and (c) by implication by the intention inferred from the circumstances. 

13. Easements or profits are extinguished by (a) merger, (b) abandonment, (c) prescription, (d) estoppel, (e) bona fide purchase under the recording acts, (f) destruction of the servient tenement.

CASE 15 

A, being fee simple owner of Blackacre, on which is an extensive sand and gravel pit, grants "to B and his heirs the right to come onto Blackacre and dig, take and carry away, any and all sand and gravel which may be found thereon, payment of five cents per cubic yard to be paid A at the time of removal". 

Thereafter A digs and removes sand and gravel from Blackacre and sells it to X. B seeks to enjoin such acts on the part of A. 

May B succeed?

Ans. No. Three cases should be distinguished, (a) conveyance of the corpus of the property, that is, the sand and gravel itself, (b) conveyance of an exclusive right to take sand and gravel, and (c) conveyance of a non-exclusive right to take sand and gravel. It is obvious if A conveys the sand and gravel on 

Blackacre to B then A would have no right thereafter to take the sand and gravel which belongs to B. It is just as clear that if A conveys to B the exclusive right to come onto Blackacre and take the sand and gravel, this would exclude everyone else, including A, from any right to take sand or gravel. B would be the only person who would have such right. In both these cases B would have a right to the injunction. But our case is (c) above, a non-exclusive right or profit granted to B. Under such a conveyance A still owns the sand and gravel. They are still part of his land. B merely has the right to come onto the land and dig and carry away sand and gravel, and a duty to pay for such as he removes such. But B did not make any agree-

INCORPOREAL INTERESTS 

47 ment to take any sand or gravel or to pay for such. He may or may not use the profit granted to him. Hence, it is clear that this conveyance does not deprive A of the right to use his own sand and gravel as he sees fit, including digging and selling it, provided that he does nothing to interfere with B's exercising of his profit if and when he sees fit to do so. The injunction should be denied. Two other items may be noted. 

Had B paid A a lump sum of money instead of agreeing to pay for sand and gravel as it is removed, it would be strong evidence that the grant was either (a) or (b) listed above. The profit to take sand and gravel includes the incidental easement of ingress and egress for the purpose of accomplishing the intended result.

CASE 16 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, grants "to B and his heirs the right of way to place and maintain iron pipes eight inches in diameter, through the north six foot strip across Blackacre, such pipes to be located under the surface of the ground at least three feet deep, such pipes to be used for the transportation of oil". B owns no real property other than this grant. 

B assigns such right to the X Oil Company which undertakes to install and use such pipes. A seeks to enjoin such use by X 

Oil Co. Should the injunction issue?

Ans. No. This easement granted to B cannot be an easement appurtenant for there is no dominant tenement to which it can be attached for the reason that B, the dominant tenant, owns no other real property. The easement is an easement in gross in which (a) B is the dominant tenant, (b) Blackacre is the servient tenement and (c) A is the servient tenant. An easement appurtenant is an appurtenance to the dominant tenement and can be conveyed or assigned with such dominant tenement as an incident to the dominant estate. But it cannot be detached therefrom without suffering legal death like detaching a tail from a cow. In England an easement appurtenant was not assignable. In the United States an easement in gross of a commercial nature such as we have in this case for the transportation of oil, is assignable and the assignee has the right to use it.

Thus, when B assigns his easement in gross across Blackacre to the X Oil Company, the assignment is valid and X has a right to install and use the oil pipes. The injunction should be denied.

CASE 17 

A, owner of Blackacre in fee simple, grants "to B the right to use a way 20 feet wide along the north edge of the property to be used for the sole purpose of trucking cream and milk from B's Whiteacre to the cheese factory on Pinkacre". Thereafter B used the 30 foot strip along the north edge of the property and used it not only for trucking cream and milk but also for personal automobile travel and for driving cattle over Blackacre. A seeks a decree declaring the easement extinguished. 

What decree, if any, should issue? 

Ans. A decree should issue enjoining the excessive use of the easement. The scope of an easement created by grant is determined by the terms of the instrument. In this case the easement is limited to a strip 20 feet wide and for the single purpose of trucking cream and milk from Whiteacre, the dominant tenement, over Blackacre, the servient tenement. B has no right to use more than the 20 foot strip and no right to use such strip for any more than the single purpose of trucking cream and milk. But excessive user does not extinguish an easement. 

It merely gives the servient tenant a right to enjoin the excessive user and compel the dominant tenant to limit his use of the easement to the terms of the grant. Therefore B should be enjoined from using more than the 20 foot strip and from using such strip for any purpose beyond trucking cream and milk from Whiteacre to the cheese factory on Pinkacre.

CASE 19 

A is the fee simple owner of Whiteacre and Blackacre, two adjoining city lots. Blackacre is west of Whiteacre, a corner lot which abuts X Street on the west. There is modern plumbing in the houses on both lots and a common sewer pipe extends from the rear of the house on Blackacre through and under Whiteacre connecting with the main sewer on X Street. A holds both lots for sale and shows both to B who buys Whiteacre. B has no actual knowledge of the sewer pipe under his lot. B discovers the sewer pipe extending from Blackacre onto his lot and plugs it up. A sues B to enjoin such obstruction. 

Should the injunction issue? 

Ans. Yes. There are three ways by which easements arise, (a) by express provision in a deed or will, (b) by prescription and (c) by implication. If the easement by implication is in favor of the grantee it is called an easement by implied grant, if in favor of the grantor it is called an easement by implied reservation. In this case had A sold Blackacre and retained 

Whiteacre, then B would have claimed an easement over Whiteacre by implied grant. But the reverse is true on the facts. 

Whiteacre is sold to B and Blackacre is retained by the grantor. 

Hence, A, the grantor, is claiming an easement by implied reservation. There are five requisites to the existence of an implied easement, (a) there must be a quasi-easement. This

INCORPOREAL INTERESTS 

51 means that one person owns two pieces of land and one is used to serve the other. In this case this item is present because A owned both city lots and he used Whiteacre to serve Blackacre by extending the sewer pipes through Whiteacre to Street X. (b) There must be a conveyance of one part of the property and a retention of the other part by the grantor. This is present when A sold Whiteacre and retained Blackacre. (c) The quasi-easement must be apparent at the time of the conveyance. 

This means that the quasi-easement would have been disclosed by a reasonably prudent investigation and inspection of the quasi-servient tenement, that is, Whiteacre in our case. B inspected both Blackacre and Whiteacre and knew they were equipped with modern plumbing and while the pipes are underground it seems not unreasonable to determine that a prudent investigation would have led to the discovery that the pipes were located as they are and that Whiteacre is burdened. with the sewer pipes coming from Blackacre onto it and continuing on to Street X. If that be true, then the quasi-easement is one which is apparent. This also seems present in this case because Whiteacre is permanently adapted to serving Blackacre by carrying the drainage from the latter to X Street. (e) The quasi-easement must be reasonably necessary to the enjoyment of the dominant tract if the dominant tract is conveyed and the easement is claimed by implied grant in favor of the grantee; and strictly necessary to the enjoyment of the dominant tract if the servient tract is the one conveyed and the easement is claimed by implied reservation in favor of the grantor, as it is in this case. Hence, for A to have his claimed easement by implied reservation across B's Whiteacre the use of the sewer over Whiteacre must be strictly necessary to the enjoyment of Blackacre. The term, strictly necessary, is a variable and lies somewhere between "reasonably necessary" and "absolutely necessary” and on its application to facts there is latitude for difference of opinion. We conclude that the use of this sewer over Whiteacre is strictly necessary to the enjoyment of Blackacre and that such element exists in this case. Thus, A, the grantor, has an easement by implied

CASE 21 

A and B own adjoining lots in City X and make an agreement to build a party wall on the common boundary line which will constitute a common wall to support their buildings to be erected on their respective lots. The party wall is built and supports the buildings as agreed. A fire destroys the building on 

A's lot and renders the party wall wholly unsafe to support the remaining structure on B's lot. B props up his building and the party wall is removed. B then undertakes to rebuild the party wall to support his building, half such contemplated wall being on A's lot. A seeks to enjoin B's rebuilding the party wall with any part of such wall on A's lot. Should the injunction issue?

Ans. Yes. When the original party wall was constructed under the agreement between A and B the results thereof were these, according to the generally accepted American view. That half of the wall which was on A's land belonged to A and the half on B's land belonged to B. But there were cross easements as to the wall, A's half of the wall owing support to B's half and B's half of the wall owing support to A's half. Further, there were cross easements as to the buildings to be supported by the party wall. A had a right to extend his supporting joists not only through his half of the wall but through the entire wall so that his building would be supported by the strength of the whole wall. Likewise B had a right to extend his supporting joists through the entire wall so that it would be supported by the entire wall. However, when the party wall was

REAL PROPERTY 

ruined by the fire and taken down these cross easements were totally and forever extinguished, the rule being that when an easement exists on an artificial structure as a servient tenement, the destruction of the servient tenement extinguishes the easement. The rule applies in this case because each half of the party wall was at the same time a dominant and a servient tenement as to the other half. 2. Covenants running with the land.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A covenant running with the land lies somewhere between a contract on one hand and an interest in land on the other; it is more than a contract and is not an interest in land. 

2. A covenant is a contract. A covenant running with the land is a contract which is so connected with or attached to an interest in land that the owner of such interest in the land may compel performance of the contract or be compelled to perform such contract merely because he is the owner of such interest in the land, and without being a party to the original contract when made. The right or the liability under the contract arises because of ownership in the land; so also such right or liability ceases with cesser or termination of ownership in such land. 

3. A covenant may run with land when it is conveyed in fee simple, or it may run with the leasehold or the reversion when either of these interests in a landlord-tenant relation is assigned. 

4. The following elements must exist for a covenant to run with land, either in fee or in the landlord-tenant relation-

INCORPOREAL INTERESTS 

55 a. There must be a covenant. Today this usually means a signed writing which complies with the Statute of Frauds. b. There must be an intention that the covenant shall run with the land. Intention may be found from the use of the words, assigns or successors, or from the circumstances. c. The covenant must touch and concern the land. This means that the effect of the covenant makes the land more valuable to the covenantee or less valuable to the covenantor. d. There must be privity of estate. Privity of estate always exists in a landlord-tenant relation. One meaning in connection with a covenant running with the fee is simply the succession to the estate of one of the original contracting parties. 

5. The covenantor is always the promisor and he or his assignee always has the burden of the covenant. 

6. The covenantee is always the promisee and he or his assignee always has the benefit of the covenant. 

7. Usually if the burden runs with the land, the benefit does also and vice versa, but the benefit and burden may run independently and in solving cases each should be analyzed and treated separately. 

8. A covenant which is purely personal will not run with the land. e.g. L leases Blackacre to T and T covenants to drive by L's house each morning and take L to his office. T assigns his leasehold to A. This covenant is personal to T and L and cannot bind A, nor can it touch and concern either the reversion or the leasehold in Blackacre.

REAL PROPERTY 

9. A covenant which touches and concerns other land cannot run with the land. e.g. L leases 

Blackacre to T. T covenants to keep L's

Whiteacre in repair. T assigns his leasehold to A. A is not bound to keep Whiteacre in repair. 

10. To run with the land a covenant must both be intended to run and touch and concern the land. 

Either alone is insufficient. 

11. The following are types of covenants which have been held to run with the land, to pay rent, to pay taxes on the leased premises, to renew a lease, to build on the leased premises, to repair the buildings on the leased premises, to insure the buildings, an option to purchase, not to assign or sublease, not to sell liquor on the leased premises, to supply water, light or heat on the leased premises. Covenants to pay taxes on other land, to pay a promissory note of the covenantee, not to compete, are collateral and do not run with the land.

In this case T is one of the original parties to the contract to paint the house. Being a party to the contract, he is liable for any breach thereof during the continuance of the agreement. But to whom is he liable? For the failure to paint the house during the year 1951 he became liable to his landlord at that time. That was L and not the plaintiff, X. The contract made in this case is a separable contract, there being a specific act to be performed during each of five years. A breach of this contract for failure to paint during a particular year becomes a chose in action at the time of the breach, which in this case was at the end of the year 1951. A covenant which is breached and transformed into a chose in action cannot and does not run with the land. Therefore, while T is liable for his failure to paint the house during 1951, his liability runs to L and not to

REAL PROPERTY 

L's assignee, X. Hence, X cannot recover from T for T's failure to paint the house in 1951. Of course, T is liable on the contract to X for failure of A to paint during the years 1953 and 1954, but that question is not raised in the problem given.

THE LIABILITY OF A 

It has been determined above that the covenant to paint the house on the leased premises is one which runs with the land. It runs in favor of the reversion and it runs with the leasehold. The entire contract is a continuing one although it involves five separable parts. Therefore, when 

L assigned his reversion to X such assignment carried with it the benefit of the covenant to have the house painted. 

Such assignment being made in 1952, X has the benefit of such contract thereafter by virtue of the fact that he owns the reversion to which is attached the benefit of such covenant. 

Furthermore, the assignment of the leasehold to A being in 1952, A is liable for performance of such covenant thereafter so long as he remains owner of the leasehold. He continued to be the owner of the leasehold and breached the contract during the years 1953 and 1954. For such breaches he is liable in damages to X, the assignee of the covenantor. Suppose A had assigned his leasehold to M on January 1, 1953 and thereafter M had failed to paint the house during 1953 and 1954. T and M would be liable for such breaches but not A. A is not a party to the original contract. His liability arises when he becomes owner of the leasehold. It would also end when he disposes of or assigns such leasehold, except as to any breaches he may have committed while he owned such leasehold. 

3. Equitable Servitudes.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. An equitable servitude is a restriction on the use of land enforceable in a court of equity.

INCORPOREAL INTERESTS 

61 2. The basis for the enforcement of an equitable servitude is this: if a person takes land with notice of a restriction on its use, then it is inequitable for him to be permitted to violate such restriction. 

3. An equitable servitude may be created by (a) an instrument which complies with the Statute of Frauds, (b) showing an intention that there be a restriction on the use of the land involved. A deed poll accepted by a grantee may create an equitable servitude without the signature of such grantee. 

4. To be enforceable as an equitable servitude the instrument must show an intention that the land, not merely the person, be bound by the restriction. 

5. Under modern recording acts, easements and equitable servitudes are enforceable alike against any one who purchases the servient land with notice of the easement or the restriction. Conversely, an easement or equitable servitude is not enforceable against a bona fide purchaser of the servient land without notice of the easement or restriction. 

6. The intention of the parties gathered from the instrument and surrounding circumstances determines who is bound by and who may enforce equitable servitudes. 

7. A transfer of the piece of land for the benefit of which an equitable servitude exists, carries with it the benefit of the servitude as an incident. 

8. For the burden of an equitable servitude to run with the land and be enforceable against a transferee of one of the original parties to the servitude three factors must co-exist, (a) an instrument complying with the Statute of 

Frauds, (b) an intention that the use of the land be restricted and (c) the transferee must take with actual or constructive notice of the restriction.

CASE 24 

A is the fee owner of Blackacre and Whiteacre both of which are city lots each 60 feet wide by 150 feet long. A lives in the house on Blackacre which house is 30 feet back from the curb in front of the house and is 10 feet from the common boundary line separating the two lots. Whiteacre is a corner lot and the only one between Blackacre and Z Street to the east, both lots facing north on Y Street. Whiteacre is a vacant lot. A conveys Whiteacre to B by deed poll signed only by A which deed contains this provision, "provided the grantee, B, or his successors shall not build any building on said Whiteacre closer to the curb in front of the lot than 30 feet and not closer to the west boundary line thereof than 10 feet, thus leaving at least 20 feet between the house on Blackacre and any building to be built on Whiteacre". B accepted such deed and recorded it at once. 

Thereafter B conveyed Whiteacre to C who actually knew nothing of the building restriction in A's deed to B and bought with the intention of building 20 feet from the front curb and within 5 feet of the west boundary thereof. 

C prepares to build as he intended and A sues to enjoin any building within the limits provided for in his deed to B. 

Should the injunction issue?

Ans. Yes. This answer presupposes an enforcible equitable servitude. 

(1) The Instrument 

The first requisite for an equitable servitude is an instrument which is in writing and signed as required by the Statute of Frauds. An exception to such requirement is a deed poll accepted by the grantee but signed only by the grantor when it is the grantee who is the party to be charged. In our case 

B accepted a deed poll signed by A. This deed and the acceptance thereof fulfills this essential element.

INCORPOREAL INTERESTS 

63 

(2) The Intention 

It seems clear that the intention expressed in A's deed is for the purpose of protecting Blackacre on the one hand and to restrict the use of Whiteacre on the other. The purpose of the restricted use is to prevent shutting off the view from A's house on Blackacre down Y street to the east and to protect A's house from having neighbors too close on the east side thereof. It is obvious that such restrictions were not placed in the deed for the sole purpose of gratifying A personally nor to satisfy him merely while he remains owner of Blackacre. Indeed, the use of the word "successors" makes it quite certain. 

They were to protect Blackacre on one hand and bind Whiteacre on the other. Thus, the requisite intention is present. 

(3) The Notice 

An equitable servitude is not enforceable against a bona fide purchaser without notice of the restricted use of the land. In this case C had no actual notice of the restriction nor of the provision in A's deed to B. However, under modern recording statutes every purchaser is bound by notice of the contents of the deeds and other instruments which are on record and which constitute part of his chain of title. Such properly recorded instruments give constructive notice of their contents. In this case the recorded deed from A to B containing the proviso setting forth the restrictions on the use of Whiteacre is an essential link in C's chain of title and gives constructive notice to C and he is bound thereby. Hence, there is a proper instrument, a proper showing of an intention to bind Whiteacre, and constructive notice to the purchaser, C. 4. Licenses. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A license is a privilege, arising out of consent of the landowner, permitting a person to come onto his land without being a trespasser. 

2. A license may be oral, in writing, with or without consideration. 

3. There are three classes of licenses, (a) a license or mere license which is always revocable, (b) a license coupled with an interest and irrevocable, and (c) an executed license which is irrevocable. 

4. An ineffective attempt to create an easement or profit results in a license. 5.

A license creates no interest in land. 

6. A license is personal and not assignable unless the licensor clearly intends it to be so.

V. FUTURE INTERESTS 

Five classes of future interests are a. three always in favor of the conveyor (1) reversions (2) possibilities of reverter (3) powers of termination b. two always in favor of a conveyee (4) remainders (5) executory interests.

The first four were recognized by the common law but executory interests came in with the Statutes of Uses (1535) and 

Wills (1540).

See Chart, FUTURE INTERESTS COMPARED WITH AND DISTINGUISHED FROM EACH OTHER, Smith,

1. Reversions.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A reversion arises by operation of law involving three elements, (a) conveyor has an estate, (b) conveyor conveys a lesser estate, (c) the interest left in the conveyor is a reversion. 

It is a mere matter of subtracting a lesser from a larger estate with residue being the reversion. e.g. A has a fee simple estate in 

Blackacre. A conveys to B a life estate in 

Blackacre. Because the fee simple is larger than the life estate there is a reversion in the conveyor, A. 

2. A reversion may be indefeasibly vested, e.g. 

A, fee owner of Blackacre, conveys to B for 99 years,-A's reversion is vested and cannot be defeated; or a reversion may be vested subject to defeasance, e.g. A, being fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life and if C pays X $500, then to C and his heirs".

REAL PROPERTY 

C's payment to X will divest A's reversion and make C's contingent remainder into a vested remainder in fee simple. 

CASE 29 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life, remainder in fee simple to my, A's, heirs". Thereafter 

A conveys "all of my interest in Blackacre to X for life". 

A dies intestate leaving H his sole heir. B dies and X takes possession of the property. H sues to eject X from Blackacre. May he succeed?

Ans. No. There are two ways by which title to real property can be received. One is by purchase, which means by the act of the parties such as a deed by a grantor delivered to the grantee, and the other is by descent, which means passing from ancestor to heir by operation of law. Under the Doctrine of Worthier Title a person cannot make his heirs take from him by purchase; they can take only by descent. 

In A's deed to B for life there is created in B a valid life estate. But the provision following "remainder in fee simple to my, A's, heirs" is void under the above Doctrine for the reason that A is attempting to make his heirs take from him by his deed inter vivos. That cannot be done. Therefore, the legal effect of A's deed is simply this, "to B for life". 

There is a reversion left in A in fee simple. Every reversion is a vested estate. If it cannot be defeated but is bound to come into possession at a future time, it is said to be indefeasibly vested. If it may be defeated it is called defeasibly vested. In our case A's reversion is bound to come into possession upon B's death so it is indefeasibly vested. Every reversion is alienable in whole or in part and subject to the claims of creditors, but because it is a future estate, that is, cannot be enjoyed in possession until a future time, it lies only in grant and not in livery. This means it can be transferred only by a deed because the reversioner having no right to present possession cannot deliver possession which is essential to a livery of seisin in the ceremony of feoffment. 

When, then, A, having a reversion in fee simple, conveys

FUTURE INTERESTS 

73 to X for life, there is created in X a valid life estate but which X cannot enjoy in possession until the death of B. 

But A still has a reversion in fee simple for X's life estate is a lessor interest than A had. When B dies X has a right to take possession of Blackacre and to enjoy it during his lifetime because A conveyed to X a life estate which he carved out of his reversion in fee simple. When A dies intestate his reversion descends by law to his heir, H. But 

H received by descent only the estate which his ancestor, A, had, a reversion which was subject to X's life estate. Therefore, H cannot eject X from Blackacre.

See Chart, "REVERSIONS COMPARED WITH AND DISTINGUISHED FROM VESTED REMAINDERS AT COMMON LAW”, Smith's Survey of Real Property, page 126. 

2. Possibilities of reverter.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A possibility of reverter is the interest left in a fee simple owner when he conveys a determinable fee. 

2. Typical words creating such are, "as long as", “until”, “during", "while”, e.g. A, being fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B and his heirs until intoxicating liquors are sold on the premises". B has a determinable fee and A has a possibility of reverter. 

3. A possibility of reverter is a future contingent interest always in favor of the conveyor, not subject to the Rule against Perpetuities and is transferable, descendible and devisable. 

4. A possibility of reverter may be attached to a reversion and alienable as an incident thereto, e.g. A, fee simple owner, conveys "to B for life while no intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises". A has a reversion with a possibility of reverter attached thereto. A conveys his reversion to B. The possibility of reverter passes to B with the reversion.

5. The distinguishing feature of a possibility of reverter is the fact that it takes effect automatically without any act of the grantor when the estate on which it is dependent terminates, e.g. in the case given under paragraph 4 next above if intoxicating liquor is sold on the premises, B's life estate automatically terminates and under the possibility of reverter automatically reverts to A without any act on 

A's part.

Words of inheritance are inappropriate when the grantee in a deed is a corporation or municipal corporation and the words "in fee simple" indicate A's intention to create in the State a fee simple. Under modern statutes merely naming the State as grantee would have the same effect. Because the State MAY use the premises forever as a State Capitol it has a fee. And because the State MAY cease so to use the premises and the deed specifically provides for the property to revert to A or his heirs in case that happens, there is a possibility of reverter left in A. Note the nature of A's retained interest. It is wholly contingent on the State's ceasing to use Blackacre for State Capitol purposes. He may never enjoy the possession of Blackacre. But there is a possibility. And it is that

However, today it is considered a thing which is owned and under our philosophy that a thing which can be owned can be transferred, a possibility of reverter is alienable. So when A conveyed "to B all of my interest in Blackacre" the possibility of reverter passed to and is owned by B. 1. Remainders are classified as (a) vested and (b) contingent. 

2. Every remainder requires four elements which are listed and applied in the following example: A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life, remainder to C and his heirs". It must be created in the same instrument and at the same time as the prior particular estate which supports it. B's life estate is the prior particular estate which supports C's remainder and both are created by A in the same conveyance at the same time. c. It must be so described that it will take effect in possession immediately upon the termination of the preceding particular estate. In this case C's remainder is so described that it must take effect in possession immediately when B's life estate comes to an end. d. The preceding particular estate must be one of lesser duration than the interest of the conveyor at the time of the conveyance

He carves out a lesser estate, a life estate in B, thus leaving further interest in A which A conveys to C in remainder. 

3. The preceding particular estate may be a fee tail, a life estate or an estate for years. See

CASE 36 et seq. 

4. The remainder itself may be a fee simple, a fee tail, a life estate or an estate for years. 

5. A remainder is always created either by deed or will and the remainderman takes as a purchaser. 

6. Today both vested and contingent remainders are alienable, devisable and descendible and are subject to the claims of creditors. 

7. The Rule Against Perpetuities applies to con tingent but not to vested remainders. 

8. Vested remainders may be defeated by condition subsequent, e.g. A conveys "to B for life, remainder to C and his heirs but if C dies before B, then to D and his heirs". C has a vested remainder in fee simple but his death before life tenant B will defeat it completely in D's favor. 

9. Every contingent remainder is subject to a condition precedent, e.g. A conveys "to B for life, remainder to C and his heirs if and when 

C pays A $100". C's remainder is contingent.

It does not vest until the condition precedent is complied with by payment of the $100. Until then there is a reversion in A which is defeated by such payment of the $100. When the $100 is paid, then C's contingent remainder becomes a vested remainder and ready to become an estate in possession upon B's death.

CASE 32 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life, C for life, D for life, E for life, then to the children of F and their heirs". At the time of this conveyance F has one child, X, and thereafter has children, Y and Z. In the following order death takes E, C and D. What is the state of the title to Blackacre?

Ans. B has a life estate, with remainder in fee simple in 

X, Y and Z, as tenants in common, each owning an undivided one third interest. At common law there could be as many remainders as the conveyor saw fit to create provided they meet legal requirements. For example, there cannot be a remainder in fee simple following an estate in fee simple. 

Suppose A, fee simple owner, conveys to B and his heirs remainder to C and his heirs. C's remainder is void for the reason that after A has conveyed to B a fee simple there is nothing left in A which can pass to C. But in our set of facts

FUTURE INTERESTS 

81 the successive life estates in B, C, D and E are each and all together less than A's fee simple so that there is still an interest in fee simple to pass in remainder to the children of F. The life estates in C, D and E are valid vested remainders, future interests. They are presently owned but the enjoyment of each is postponed and if death overtakes one of such life remaindermen before the preceding life estates are terminated, then such life tenant never enjoys the possession of the property. Thus in this case E, C and D having predeceased B, just never got to enjoy the possession of Blackacre. But there is no other legal effect. B's life estate in possession remains as long as B lives. When A created the fee simple remainder in favor of F's children (notice F has no interest) and F had at the time a child, X, then X had the vested remainder in fee simple. But such remainder is vested subject to partial defeasance by the birth of other children to F. The remainder is given to the children of F and X is only one of such class. Therefore the remainder which is vested in X will open in favor of additionally born children. 

So when Y is born to F, then X and Y are tenants in common of such vested remainder, each owning one half. When Z is born to F, then the remainder opens still further and X, Y and Z are tenants in common, each owning an undivided one third interest. If more children are born to F, then the remainder will open to take care of all the members of such class, the children of F. Thus Blackacre is owned by B for life with vested remainder in fee simple in X, Y and Z as tenants in common but still subject to open and let in more children of F.

Ans. Yes. If an instrument of conveyance can be construed either as a vested or a contingent remainder it will be a vested remainder. CASE 34 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life or until he is imprisoned in a penitentiary, then to B's children share and share alike in fee simple". A then conveys "all of my interest in Blackacre "to X and his heirs". B is convicted of a felony and incarcerated in a penitentiary. X takes possession of Blackacre and B's children, M and N, sue X in ejectment. May they succeed? 

Ans. Yes. If all of the provisions in A's deed to B are valid, then there is nothing left in A which he can later convey to X and X received no interest in Blackacre. There is no question about the validity of the provision creating an estate in B, but what interest did B take by virtue of A's deed? 

There is at times a very thin line between a remainder and an executory interest. But a line there is. Note three propositions. (a) If an estate will take effect in possession at the

FUTURE INTERESTS 

83 termination of the preceding particular estate, then it is a remainder. (b) If an estate will take effect after the termination of the preceding particular estate, then it cannot be a remainder. It is an executory interest. (c) If an estate will take before the natural termination of the prior particular estate, that is, in derogation thereof or by cutting it short, then it cannot be a remainder. It is an executory interest. 

The estate to B is limited "for life or until he is imprisoned". 

B has a life estate because he may not be imprisoned and it MAY last for his lifetime. But it is a determinable life estate. If and when he is imprisoned B's estate automatically comes to an end because that is the very time during which it is to last. And if B is imprisoned he forfeits nothing because the estate granted to him by A was "until" such imprisonment. By his imprisonment B's estate naturally comes to an end. When then does the estate granted to B's children take effect "at", "after" or "before" B's estate naturally comes to an end? The answer is that such estate takes effect "at" the time B's prior particular estate comes to an end by his imprisonment. The interest then given B's children is a remainder in fee simple. That being the case there is nothing left in A which he could convey to X. Therefore, X receives nothing by A's deed to him. Hence, M and N should succeed in their ejectment suit. 

NOTE: Suppose, A's first deed had read, "to B for life, but if he is imprisoned in a penitentiary then to B's children in fee simple". In such case B's life estate is subject to a condition subsequent. He is first given a life estate and then it is provided that if the condition subsequent happens, namely, imprisonment, then the estate of B's children is to come in and cut short the life estate. The children's estate would forfeit B's life estate; it would take effect before the natural termination of the life estate. It could not then be a remainder but would be an executory interest under the Statute of Uses operating by way of a shifting use. 

See Blackman v. Fysh, 3 Ch.Div. 209 (1892).

CASE 36 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for 20 years, remainder to C and his heirs if and when C pays A the sum of $1000". X, a creditor of C, takes judgment against C and levies upon his interest in Blackacre. C seeks to enjoin X's sale of any interest under the levy claiming (a) C has no interest in Blackacre and (b) if C has an interest it is a contingent remainder and not subject to the claims of creditors. Should the injunction issue?

Ans. No. At common law every remainder had to be preceded by a particular estate of freehold and a remainder preceded by a non-freehold estate, a chattel real, was void, leaving the reversion in the conveyor. A non-freehold tenant, B in our case, could not be seised. He was only possessed. 

Therefore such a tenant for years could not hold the seisin for the benefit of the remainderman when the tenant's estate came to an end. Hence, the seisin remained with the conveyor, A in our case, who simply held the reversion subject to the 20 year term in B, and the named remainderman, C in our case, took nothing. Such was the logic and the result at common law. But today livery of seisin is obsolete and the reason for requiring that seisin be not in abeyance has long since disappeared. Hence, a remainder is permitted to follow an estate for years as well as a life estate. Therefore, the remainder conveyed to C following the 20 year leasehold in B is quite valid. But it is a contingent remainder until the condition precedent, payment by C to A of $1000 is performed. 

At common law a contingent remainder was not alienable or transferable inter vivos. Not being alienable voluntarily by the tenant, his contingent remainder was not alienable invol-

untarily by his judgment creditor. Here again the law has evolved and today with or without statute a contingent remainder is alienable inter vivos voluntarily and can be transferred involuntarily by a judgment creditor. Hence, it is concluded that C's contingent remainder is valid and is subject to levy and sale by C's judgment creditor, X, and the injunction should be denied. 

5. Executory interests.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. An executory interest is a contingent future interest which cannot qualify as a remainder, is always in favor of a conveyee and takes effect when the contingency happens as a springing use or shifting use under the Stat ute of Uses (1535) or Statute of Wills (1540). 

2. To create an executory interest three elements must co-exist, a. it must be in favor of a conveyee, one other than the grantor or devisor, b. it must be contingent because when it vests as a future or present interest, it ceases to be executory, and c. it must take effect either (1) before the natural termination of the preceding estate and cut it short, or (2) after the termination of the preceding estate. If it takes effect at the natural termination of the preceding estate it is a remainder. 

3. An executory interest always divests or cuts short the estate which precedes it. a. If an executory interest cuts short the grantor's preceding estate it is a springing use. e.g. A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, by bargain and sale deed, conveys it "to B and his heirs, the estate in B to begin five years from the date of this deed". This conveyance leaves the fee simple in the

FUTURE INTERESTS 

87 grantor, A, until five years after its date.

At that time a use springs up in B and the Statute of Uses executes it into a legal fee simple estate, thus divesting or cutting short the grantor's fee simple. If A's instrument of conveyance were a will B's interest would be called an executory devise. b. If an executory interest cuts short another grantee's preceding estate it is a shifting use. e.g. A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, by bargain and sale deed, conveys it "to B and his heirs, but if B during his lifetime sells intoxicating liquors on the premises, then to C and his heirs". This deed grants the fee simple to B and therefore leaves no interest in the grantor, A. But B's fee simple estate is subject to an executory interest of the shifting type in favor of C. 

If B sells intoxicating liquors on the premises, then the use shifts from B to C and the Statute of Uses executes the use in C into a legal fee simple, thus divesting B's fee simple completely and vesting it in C. 

Every executory interest is subject to the Rule Against Perpetuities but it is not applicable in this case because the deed provides that the contingency of selling intoxicating liquors must be effective "if B during his lifetime" does the selling.

Therefore, C's executory interest must vest, if at all, during the lifetime of B who is in being. (See CASE 37 next below). 

There are two reasons why C's interest cannot be a remainder. (1) A remainder cannot follow a fee simple estate and (2) a remainder cannot cut short the estate which precedes it. C's interest does both of these things. Had A's instrument of conveyance been a will instead of a deed, then C's interest would be called an executory devise.

An executory interest is indestructible and such indestructibility caused the emergence of the Rule Against Perpetuities.

1. Prior possession.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The right of a prior possessor is good against the whole world except the rightful owner. e.g. A is fee simple owner of Blackacre. B physically evicts A from the premises and takes possession. C takes possession of the land and holds it against B. B sues C in ejectment. B can recover. 

2. The interest of a possessor of real property (leaseholds excepted) is real property which is descendible, devisable and alienable. 

3. The jus tertii (right of a third person) is not available as a defense to a prior possessor unless the third person holds under the real owner or shows no prior possession of the plaintiff. e.g. O owns Blackacre but does not appear in the case. A takes possession of 

Blackacre and is put off by B. A sues B in ejectment. B sets up in defense that O is owner and has the right of possession. The defense is not good unless (a) B can show he holds through O, or (b) can show O's possession continued to the time that B took possession, thus showing that A had no possession prior to B's possession. 

2. Rights on, above and below surface. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Subject to the right of flight, a possessor of real property has the right of exclusive possession of the surface of the ground, the space below and the space above such surface with-

in his exterior boundaries extended vertically downward and upward. Illustrations follow: a. A, possesses Blackacre and B possesses 

Whiteacre which abut each other along a common boundary line 200 feet long. On

In the process of construction two "I" beams are so placed in the structure that they extend 16 inches across the common boundary line onto B's Whiteacre. One beam is 24 inches under the surface and the other is 15 feet above the surface of the ground. What remedies, if any, has B concerning such protrusions into his property below and above the surface thereof? B has five remedies, (a) he may use self help and cut off the beams at the common boundary line, (b) he may sue A in trespass for damages but damages will not remove the beams-they will still be there, (c) he may bring ejectment and have judgment that the sheriff put B back into possession of the space occupied by the protruding beams-few sheriffs will undertake such a task, however, at least without bond, (d) he may sue in equity to enjoin A from maintaining these continuing nuisances which should result in a mandatory injunction ordering A to remove the protrusions, or (e) he may sue in equity to enjoin A from these continuing trespasses which should result in a mandatory injunction ordering A to remove the protrusions. 

The equitable remedies under (d) and (e) are effective. The legal remedies are inadequate. The basis for these remedies is the right of B to possess exclusively the space below his surface and the space above his surface which is essential to the enjoyment of the surface, all being within his

exterior boundaries extended vertically downward and upward. b. A and B own and possess adjoining lots having a common boundary line 200 feet long. For the sole purpose of annoying B, his neighbor, A builds an ugly, unsightly but sturdy board fence along the entire common boundary line and within an inch thereof. B alleges these facts in a petition to enjoin A's maintaining such fence. A demurs to the petition. How rule on the demurrer? The demurrer should be sustained. REAL PROPERTY LEGAL GEMS 1. Title by adverse possession requires the pos session to be a. actual and exclusive, b. open and notorious, c. continuous and peaceable, d. hostile and adverse, and e. for the entire statutory period. 

2. The effect of adverse possession for the statutory period is to create in the adverse possessor an original substantive law title as effective as though it were created by deed or by will. It cannot be abandoned. 

3. The period during which one adverse possessor has held the land may be tacked to the period during which another adverse possessor has held the land provided there is privity between the two adverse possessors. 

Such privity may exist when the possession is passed from one to the other by deed, will, descent, contract written or oral, or by mere oral permission. 

4. If the landowner is under a disability at the time when the adverse possession begins, that is, at the time of the accrual of the cause of action against the adverse possessor, such disabled person is given by statute time beyond the removal of his disability in which to bring his action against the adverse possessor. 

5. The protection of the disability statute is personal to the person under disability at the time the cause of action accrues and cannot be claimed for his heirs or successors; hence, neither an intervening nor a supervening disability has any effect on the running of the statute on adverse possession. e.g. O, a boy of 9 years, owns Blackacre, when A takes possession thereof adversely. When O is 19 he is rendered non compos mentis in an automobile accident. A holds adversely continu-

ously until O is 32 when O's guardian brings suit to eject A. The statute on adverse possession is 20 years with 10 years beyond the removal of a disability existing when the cause of action accrues. The suit is too late and A is the owner by adverse possession. 

The statute gave O ten years beyond the removal of the disability of infancy which is the only disability existing when the cause of action accrued when O was 9 years of age. 

That gave O until he was 31 years of age to bring his action. He did not do so and is therefore too late. O's insanity disability when he was 19 years of age is a supervening disability and is totally immaterial. The only disability which has any effect and the only one protected by the statute is the one or ones which exist when the adverse possession begins and a cause of action accrues against the adverse possessor. 

6. The intention with which a possessor holds land is all important in adverse possession. e.g. Suppose A and B own adjoining properties. A puts up a fence on the line which he honestly believes is the true boundary and occupies the land up to that line for the statutory period. There is an honest mistake and A has been occupying a strip two rods wide which is on B's side of the true boundary line. B sues to eject A from the strip. 

In court A is asked this question, "A, when you took possession of that strip 25 years ago did you intend to claim it as your own even though it is on B's side of the boundary line?" If A answers, "No", then he is out of court for by his own testimony he has negatived the adverseness of his possession.

CASE 38 

O is the owner of Blackacre in a jurisdiction where the statute on adverse possession is 20 years. A takes possession

REAL PROPERTY 

of Blackacre. O sues to eject A from the premises. The parties stipulate that A's possession has been (a) actual and exclusive, (b) open and notorious, (c) continuous and peaceable, and (d) for the entire statutory period of 20 years. 

Upon this stipulation of facts A moves for a directed verdict on the ground that it shows A has title by adverse possession. 

How should the court rule?

Ans. The court should deny the motion. Taking title from an owner by adverse possession is a drastic procedure and the burden of proof of every essential element is on the adverse possessor to prove by a preponderance of evidence and some say by clear and positive proof. 

1. Actual and Exclusive Possession 

This means that the adverse possessor must actually occupy the land physically and that his possession excludes all the world including the rightful owner and that his method of occupancy is that of an average owner for the type of use to which the property is appropriated. For example the occupancy of land and a building for church purposes is quite different than occupancy for dwelling purposes. Often no one is on the premises for two or three days of the week when land is used for church purposes and the same is true as to land possessed wholly for mining or grazing purposes. But the possession is required to be that type of occupancy which an average owner would exercise for the purpose for which the land is used. 

2. Open and Notorious Possession 

This means that the possession is visible for all the world to see and that it is not secret or clandestine. It means that the possessor is acting towards the land as though he were the owner thereof in his going to and from it and in his use thereof. 

3. Continuous and Peaceable 

This means that the possession of the land has not been interrupted either by action in court to obtain possession of

the land or by the possessor's being physically evicted from the premises. Mere oral protests or writing letters to the occupant of the land are insufficient to constitute interruption. 

The physical act, to constitute interruption, must be such that were the occupant the actual owner of the land, the act would give him a cause of action. Unless there is such the possession remains continuous and peaceable. 

4. During the Statutory Period 

This means that the possession must continue uninterruptedly for the period provided for in the statute of the state on the subject of adverse possession. Any possession for a lesser period has no effect as far as maturing title by adverse possession is concerned.

The four items listed above are all essential elements of adverse possession. Each is essentially a question of fact and must be proved. But when they are stipulated to exist in a given case, as they are here, then they are considered as true without proof. And were these all the elements required for adverse possession, then the defendant's motion should be granted. But this stipulation does not include one very important element essential to the existence of title by adverse possession. It is that the possession must also be hostile and adverse. 

5. Hostile and Adverse 

This means that the possessor is occupying the premises as owner and not in subordination to the title of any other person including the ousted owner. Without such element being present and proved there can be no title by adverse possession. This, like the other elements listed above, is essentially a question of fact which must be determined by the trier of fact. So far as appears in the stipulation A may be occupying with the consent of the owner, O; he may be a licensee or a tenant of some type. If there be occupancy by consent there is no adverseness and no title by adverse possession. Because this question must be determined as a fact in the case, A's motion must be denied.

4. Lateral and subjacent support. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The right of lateral support means that every land owner has the right to have his land in its natural condition held in place from the sides by the neighboring land. 

2. Neighboring land is that land which is essential to hold any particular piece of land in place from the sides, regardless of how far away it happens to be. Such distance is determined by the nature of the soil. 

3. The right of lateral support is inherent in land ownership; it is part and parcel of the land itself.

4. 

The basis for liability for removal of lateral support is this-it injures the land in its nattural condition. 

5. Land in its natural condition means land without artificial structures or buildings thereon. 

6. Liability for removal of lateral support accrues when the damage occurs, not when the excavation is done. e.g. A's vacant land adjoins B's vacant land. A digs a hole on his land 30 feet deep in 1940 intending to build a building with a basement thereon. He does not build. In 1950 this excavation causes B's land to subside with substantial injury thereto. The cause of action accrues in 1950 and at that time the statute of limitations begins to run. 

7. In the United States liability for the removal of lateral support from land having artificial structures thereon includes damages only for the injury to the land in its natural condition and no damages for the injury to the buildings; in England and a few states the liability includes not only damages for injury to the land in its natural condition but damages to the buildings also as parasitic thereto. e.g. 

A and B own adjoining tracts. A's land is vacant and B's land has a building thereon built very close to the boundary line. A excavates for a building to be erected on his land and does so with utmost diligence. The excavation causes $1000.00 injury to B's land in its natural condition and causes the building to sink on one side with resulting damage of $3000.00. In most of the states B can recover only $1000.00 damage. In England and a few states B can recover $4000.00. Such liability accrues for the injury to a property right irrespective of negligence.

2. Under the statute of uses.

LEGAL GEMS 1. The Statute of Uses is the foundation of our modern law and methods of conveying real property. 

2. The two modern methods of conveying real property are 3. a. covenant to stand seised and b. bargain and sale deed.

A covenant to stand seised must be supported by "good" consideration. "Good" consideration means relationship by blood or marriage. e.g. A owns Blackacre in fee simple and has a son, B. By an instrument under seal (or today by a writing without a seal) A writes as follows, "For the love and affection I have for my son, B, I hereby covenant to hold Blackacre for the use of B and his heirs", and signs and delivers the instrument to B. The "love and affection" has nothing to do with the efficacy of the instrument as a conveyance. 

But the fact that B is A's son, a blood relationship, is all important. Such consideration with A's declaration to hold Blackacre for the use of B and his heirs, raises a use in fee simple in B. (Under modern statutes the words of inheritance "and his heirs" need not be used.) Then the Statute of Uses carries the legal title from A to the person who has the use which is B in this case. The Stat-

CONVEYANCES 

105 ute is said to "execute the use in B". This means that it conveys the legal title from A to B who has the use. Thus the fee simple is conveyed from A to B wholly by this writing without any physical delivery of possession of the land. This is one type of modern conveyance which is made effective by the Statute of Uses. 

4. A bargain and sale deed must be supported by a "valuable" consideration. "Valuable" means having monetary value. e.g. A, being fee simple owner of Blackacre, writes, signs and delivers to B the following instrument, "For the consideration of $1.00 and other valuable consideration, I hereby bargain and sell Blackacre to B and his heirs". The recital of the $1.00 in the deed which cannot be rebutted, together with A's declaration that he bargains and sells the land to B, raises a use in B in fee simple. Now, A has the legal title and holds the legal seisin for the use of B. The Statute of Uses then executes the use by carrying the seisin or legal title from the grantor, A, to the grantee, B. Thus the fee simple title is conveyed from A to B wholly by this deed without any physical act of "livery of seisin" on the land. This is the other type of modern conveyance made effective by the Statute of Uses.

3. Escrow.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Delivery of a deed in escrow means delivery on a condition. 

2. Delivery in escrow requires four distinct elements, a. a physical deed properly executed, b. a third person as escrow depositary, c. a manual handing over of the physical deed to the escrow depositary, and d. the grantor's giving up all control over the operation of the deed as a conveyance, sub-

ject only to the condition, whether that be the performance of a contract or the happening of an event. 

3. An escrow or conditional delivery to the grantee, the grantor retaining no control over the instrument except the happening of the condition, is an unconditional delivery and takes effect presently thus lodging title in the grantee. e.g. A, a soldier assigned to Korea during the war there, delivers his deed to Blackacre to grantee, B, with these words, "If I am killed in Korea, Blackacre is yours by this deed". A is not killed in Korea and returns.

By the common law rule followed generally, this deed became effective upon its delivery to B and B is the owner of Blackacre. See recent case contra and believed to be the better rule. Chillemi v. Chillemi, 78 A.2d 750 (Md.1951). 

4. In a true escrow the grantor gives up absolutely all control over the operation of the deed, subject only to the performance or the happening of the condition involved. This is true even though the physical deed is not handed over to the grantee because such is the grantor's intent when the escrow delivery takes place. e.g. A delivers his deed to 

Blackacre in escrow to the escrow depositary,

D, upon condition that when B, the grantee, pays the last installment of the purchase price at $50 per month over a 10 year period the deed shall be "delivered" to B. B pays the last installment but D refuses to "hand over" the deed. Effect-at the instant B paid the last installment the deed took effect and title lodged in B irrespective of where the physical deed is. But B also has the right to the physical deed as evidence of his title. 

5. When a deed is handed over to the escrow depositary it is designated the "first delivery" and when it is handed over by the escrow

REAL PROPERTY 

depositary to the grantee upon the happening of the condition it is designated the "second delivery". 

6. A deed delivered in escrow takes effect from the "second delivery" with these exceptions, (a) when the grantor dies before the second delivery, (b) when the grantor becomes non compos mentis before the second delivery, (c) when the grantor being a feme sole becomes a feme covert before the second delivery, or (d) when justice requires otherwise. In these exceptional cases the deed "relates back" and the title passes as of the date of the "first delivery". 

7. In an escrow transaction the escrow depositary is not the agent of the grantor and he is not the agent of the grantee; it is his duty merely to follow his instructions. If the condition is fulfilled he should hand the deed over to the grantee; if it is not fulfilled he should return the deed to the grantor.

CASE 43 

A, owner in fee simple of Blackacre, orally agrees to sell it to B for $3000 which sum is to be paid in three installments of $1000 each. A executes his deed to Blackacre to B as grantee and places it in the hands of an escrow depositary, D, who is instructed to hand over the deed to B when B has paid the third $1000 to D. When B has paid the second $1000 to D, he is instructed by A to return to B the $2000 already paid by him and to return the deed to A. D does neither. B pays the third $1000 to D but D refuses to hand the deed to B. B sues D for possession of the deed. May he recover? 

Ans. Yes. If, in addition to placing the deed in escrow,

A had entered into a written, specifically enforceable contract

with B for the sale and purchase of Blackacre, there would be no doubt about B's rights. Such, of course, is the usual case. But a conveyance is not a contract. Further, delivery is merely a matter of the grantor's intention. And in a true escrow the grantor gives up all control over the operation of the deed, subject only to the condition involved. Such condition may be the performance of a written agreement, an oral agreement, the arrival of a particular date or the mere happening of an event. An escrow is irrevocable between the time of the delivery to the escrow depositary and the time when the condition is to occur. That being the case, contract or no contract, written or oral, consideration or no consideration, when A placed the deed in escrow he invested B with a power to become the owner of Blackacre by performance of the condition-payment of the last of three $1000 payments. To such a situation the Statute of Frauds has no application for it was not so intended. The Statute of Frauds has application to an oral contract for the sale of an interest in land but has no application to the delivery of a deed in escrow subject only to a condition. Therefore, when B paid the last of the $1000 payments he became the owner of Blackacre and is entitled to the possession of the deed which A placed in escrow. 

This illustrates the true nature of delivery of a deed in escrow.

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, makes with B a written, specifically enforceable agreement for the sale thereof to B. 

Three copies of such agreement are made, one being kept by A, one handed to B which B records and the third copy is placed in the hands of an escrow depositary, D. be ordered to hand over the deed to B and that the defendants settle the right to the money among themselves. Does B have a right to the deed?

Ans. Yes. An analysis of these facts shows it is not a difficult problem. 

1. Consequence of the Right of Specific Performance 

The facts state that the contract between A and B is specifically enforceable. The consequence of the right of specific performance is equitable conversion. Applied to these facts it means that when the contract was executed B's personal property, $5000.00 in money, became equitably converted into real property, and A's real property, Blackacre, became converted equitably into personal property. Of course this is a fiction because there is no specific personal property, no specific $5000.00, although there is specific real property, Blackacre.

In terms of rights it means that A owns a contract right to be paid $5000.00 by B and is the owner of the legal title of 

Blackacre as security for the payment of the $5000.00; that 

B owns a contract right to have the legal title conveyed to him upon full payment and is the equitable owner of Blackacre. In terms of duties it means that A is duty bound to convey legal title to B upon B's full payment of the purchase price and that B is duty bound to make such full payment. 

2. Garnishment 

Taking the view that B owes A $2000.00 at the time of C's levy upon Blackacre, and that such debt is the principal thing which A owns and that the title to Blackacre is only security for the payment of such purchase price, C's most effective and proper remedy would have been garnishment of such debt, thus making B legally bound to pay the last two installments to C. However, the cases recognize C's right to levy upon Blackacre because the legal title is still in the judgment debtor, the seller, A.

3. Notice 

All subsequent purchasers and encumbrancers take real property with notice of what appears on the public records. Thus, when B recorded his copy of the contract between him and A, it gave notice to the world, including C, of the rights which B had in Blackacre. C's levy was made with notice of what 

A's and B's rights were in Blackacre and can give C no more right than the judgment debtor, A, had in the property, which was only a $2000.00 interest. 

4. Passing of Title in Escrow 

Title passes to the grantee named in an escrow deed at the time of the "second delivery". This is the general rule, subject to four exceptions, (a) when the grantor dies, (b) when the grantor becomes non compos mentis, (c) when the grantor is a feme sole and becomes feme covert and (d) when justice requires the title to relate back to the "first delivery". In this case, quite apart from notice, justice requires that the passing of the legal title be related back to the date of the "first delivery" for two reasons, (1) B should not have to pay twice and (2) C should not be permitted to take a greater interest in Blackacre than that which his judgment debtor had. 

5. Dicta

In this case we are given a specifically enforceable written contract which is duly recorded. But suppose there is no written or recorded contract in such case the other facts being the same. The result should be the same on the principle that the rights of a judgment creditor are derivative and his rights can rise no higher than the rights of his judgment debtor. Such creditor is not a bona fide purchaser whose levy cuts off equities such as B has in this case. 

He may be bona fide but he is not a purchaser because he gives no value at the time of the levy. Therefore it must be concluded that 

B has the right to the deed to Blackacre upon his payment of the last $2000.00 on the purchase price, and of course, such $2000.00 should be ordered paid to A's judgment creditor, C.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A conveyance of title to real property is not a contract which requires an offer and an acceptance. 

2. The law presumes one will accept that which is to his financial advantage. 

3. Assuming delivery of a conveyance, acceptance of the title by a grantee is presumed, subject to his disclaimer. 

4. An heir cannot prevent a title coming to him by descent but title cannot be forced on a purchaser.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The description of land in a conveyance must identify the property to be transferred. e.g. 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, a 40 acre tract, conveys "to B and his heirs one acre in my Blackacre". This deed is void for failure to describe the location of the one acre within the larger tract. And there is no data from which it can be located. It may be in the center, in a corner or on one side of Blackacre. 

2. A map or plat referred to in a deed describing the land to be conveyed becomes part of the deed for such purpose. e.g. A, being fee simple owner of Blackacre, a city lot, conveys "to B my Blackacre, being Lot 2, Block 3 of

Morningside Addition to the City of X according to that certain map or plat on page 66 

of Book V of Maps and Plats in the Office of the County Recorder of Sioux County, State of Y". For the purpose of identifying Blackacre the map or plat on page 66 of Book V as described becomes part of the deed from A to B. 

3. In identifying the land described in a deed the intention of the parties is controlling and rules of construction are simply methods of determining such intention. 

4. Rules of construction a. monuments on the ground, natural or artificial, such as a tree, a river or a steel stake, govern over courses and distances. e.g. A conveys Blackacre to B with description, "beginning at point X, then due west 100 feet to a steel stake, thence due south", etc. On the ground the steel stake is a bit north of "due west" and is not 100

117 feet but 110 feet from point X. The title passes to the steel stake which governs over the direction and the length of the line described. b. courses govern over distances. e.g. A conveys to B using the following description on the face of the deed, "beginning at point X, thence due west 100 feet, thence turning a right angle and going south 150 feet to Moss Avenue". No. Under the common law rule when a conveyance reads, "to a road and along such road", the grantee takes title to the land to the center of the road, provided of course that the grantor owned to the center of the road. The same principle holds true if the deed reads, "to a point on the side of a road and along such road", thus describing only one point on the side of the road. But in our case there are two points described on "the sideline" of the road and the description says "along the west sideline" of the road. There are two distinct views on the effect of such description. One view is that the language is clear and unambiguous, that it clearly describes the land as passing only to the sideline and no further and that the grantee takes title to the side of the road and not to the center thereof. Under this view the strip constituting the west half of River Road would still belong to A and he would recover. The other view takes into consideration not only the language used in the deed but the policy of the law in such cases. First, such narrow strips of land usually have little value to the grantor and are merely subjects of litigation. They cause strife in the neighborhood and interfere with amicable relationships between neighbors. 

Second, the minds of the parties actually do not advert to these strips of land at all at the time of the conveyance, regardless of the words actually used in the deed. It is only when a road has been abandoned as in this case and the land becomes

3. Exceptions and reservations.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. An exception excludes from the operation of a deed some part of the corporeal property described therein and is always in favor of the grantor. e.g. A conveys to B all of

X. THE RULE IN SHELLEY'S CASE LEGAL GEMS 

1. This Rule may be stated as follows: when in the same conveyance a life estate is given to the ancestor with a remainder to the ancestor's heirs (or heirs of the body) then the ancestor takes the fee simple estate (or fee tail estate) and the heirs take nothing. 

2. The Rule illustrated and analyzed-Example 

-A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life and thereafter to B's heirs". 

The Rule requires: 

a. the deed or will must create a life estate in the ancestor. In the example given A's deed creates a life estate in the ancestor, B. b. the same instrument must create a remainder in the heirs of the ancestor. In the example A's deed creates both the life estate in ancestor, B, and the remainder in 

B's heirs. c. the life estate and the remainder must be either both legal estates or both equitable estates. In the example B's life estate is a legal life estate and the remainder is a legal remainder.

The legal effect of the rule in this example then is this: A's deed creates in B a life estate; the Rule in Shelley's Case gives the remainder to B; and by the law of merger B's life estate is merged in his remainder in fee simple and B therefore has the fee simple estate in Blackacre and his heirs have no interest therein. 

3. The Rule in Shelley's Case is a rule of law and operates wholly without regard for the grantor's intention. It has been abolished in about three fourths of the states, in which states the grantor's intention is made effective.

CASE 48 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B for life, remainder to my heirs in fee simple". Thereafter A executes a deed conveying "to X all of my right, title and interest in Blackacre". A dies intestate leaving H his sole heir. B dies.

Ans. Yes. By the common law a title taken by descent was considered a worthier title than one which was taken by purchase. Thus when a grantor undertook to convey to his own heirs it was assumed that he intended such heirs to take by the worthier title of descent. The Doctrine of Worthier 

Title is therefore sometimes stated in this form-one cannot make his own heirs his purchasers. In this case A's deed creates a life estate in B. The words "my heirs" indicates no other intention on the part of A than that those persons who are determined at A's death to be his heirs shall take. 

That being the case such "heirs" cannot take the remainder by purchase but must take by the worthier title of descent. 

The remainder is therefore void and A's "heirs" take nothing by his deed. This makes the deed read as though it merely said, "to B for life", leaving a reversion in A. When, then, A conveyed to X all of his interest in Blackacre, such deed constituted a grant of the reversion to X in fee simple subject only to B's life estate. When A died intestate there was no interest left in A which could descend to his heir, H. 

Therefore upon B's death H had no interest in Blackacre and had no right to take possession thereof. X being the fee simple owner of Blackacre has the right to possess Blackacre and can eject H therefrom. The Doctrine has no application to the case where a named person is designated as purchaser even though such person turns out to be the heir of the grantor. Had A's deed read, "to B for life with remainder to H", then H would have taken the remainder even though upon A's death H would have been the heir of the grantor, A. XII. THE RULE AGAINST PERPETUITIES 

LEGAL GEMS 1. The simplest form of the Rule Against Perpe tuities may be stated thus-"No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than twenty-one years after some life in being at the creation of the interest". Gray, Rule 

Against Perpetuities (4th ed. 1942) 191. 

2. The Rule analyzed shows five distinct requirements: a. "no interest is good"—this means it is void ab initio if it offends the Rule. b. "must vest"-this means the contingent interest MUST VEST during the period either in possession, or in interest even though the possession be postponed. c. "if at all"-this means that the contingent interest must either "vest" or "fail" within the period to be valid. d. "not later than 21 years after some life in being" this means the sum of three periods of time, (1) a life or lives in being, plus (2) any periods of gestation, plus (3) 21 years. e. "at the creation of the interest"—this means when the creating instrument takes effect-if a deed, at the time of its delivery-if a will, at the testator's death. 

3. The Rule has no application to a. interests presently possessed b. reversions c. vested remainders d. possibilities of reverter e. powers of termination f. charitable trusts g. resulting trusts.

4. The Rule applies to a. contingent remainders b. executory interests c. options to purchase land not incident to a lease for years d. powers of appointment e. indestructible private trusts. 

5. The Rule as above stated operates in about two thirds of the states.

CASE 49 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, devises it "to B for life then to my grandchildren who arrive at the age of 21 years".

At A's death he leaves three children, X, Y and Z, surviving him, each of whom has children ranging from age 3 to 14 years of age. The residuary clause in A's will is in favor of 

C who claims to be the owner in fee simple absolute of Blackacre subject only to B's life estate. Is C's claim valid? 

Ans. No. C is the owner of the reversion in fee simple subject to defeasance by the executory interest in A's grandchildren. There is no doubt of the validity of B's life estate. 

It is an estate presently vested in possession to which the Rule Against Perpetuities does not apply because it is vested. As long as the interest in A's grandchildren is contingent there is a reversion in the conveyor, A. But A's will conveyed such reversion to the residuary devisee, C. All reversions are vested and to vested interests the Rule Against Perpetuities has no application. Therefore, the Rule has no application to 

C's reversion, even though it may be defeated. C's contention that he is the absolute owner in fee simple of Blackacre subject only to B's life estate must be based on the theory that the interest of A's grandchildren is void ab initio under the Rule Against Perpetuities. So the real question is whether or not such executory interest is valid or void under the Rule, it being properly assumed that all executory interests are subject to the operation of the Rule Against Perpetuities.

1. "No Interest is Good", Says the Rule 

Under the Rule Against Perpetuities every contingent interest must either (a) "vest in interest" with possession postponed (like a contingent remainder becoming a vested remainder) or (b) "vest in possession" or (c) fail completely, within the period of the Rule or it is void from the beginning. 

If the executory interest given to A's grandchildren offends the Rule, then it is void and C's contention is valid; if it does not offend the Rule it is valid and C's contention is without merit. 

2. "Must Vest", Says the Rule

It seems clear that A's will indicates an intention that no grandchild shall have a vested interest in Blackacre until such grandchild arrives at the age of 21 years. When such will took effect at A's death there was no grandchild older than 

14 years.

Therefore the interest of A's grandchildren is a contingent interest. It cannot be a remainder because it is not bound to vest at or before the termination of the prior particular estate of freehold which is B's life estate. Indeed it may vest long after B's death. Furthermore, if it does vest it may cut short the reversion in C which a remainder cannot do. Therefore, the interest of the grandchildren of A is an executory interest of the springing type because it may cut short the reversion in C. Because such interest is created by will it is called an executory devise and to such interests the 

Rule Against Perpetuities applies. This contingent executory devise must vest within the period of the Rule or it is void ab initio. If there is any possibility of its vesting at a time later than that provided for in the Rule, it is void. When then must the vesting take place in this case? 

3. "If at All", Says the Rule

This provision of the Rule means that a contingent interest may be valid in its creation if, even though it does not actually vest within the Rule, it is bound to fail within the period of the Rule. If none of A's grandchildren reaches the age of 21 and such failure is bound to happen within the Rule, then

REAL PROPERTY 

this requirement would be fulfilled. When then must the failure to vest take place in this case? The effect of such failure would mean merely that C's reversion would not be defeated. 

4. "Not Later Than 21 Years After Some Life in Being", Says the Rule

This item of the Rule involves several important principles, (a) the life in being must be a human life, not a life of one of the lower animals such as a dog or a horse, (b) the life in being must always precede, it must never succeed, the 21 year period, (c) if there is no specific measuring life or lives but only a gross period of years, then such gross period cannot exceed 21 years, (d) every human being is conclusively presumed capable of having children as long as he lives, regardless of age, and (e) the lives in the measuring group must not be so numerous that it is quite impracticable to determine the survivor such as "when the last of the persons now living in Los Angeles dies", which would make void the interest dependent on such determination. In our case the creating instrument is a will which took effect at A's death. 

Hence, A's life is not a measuring life. The beneficiaries are grandchildren of the testator which presupposes an intervening generation consisting of A's children who were living at the death of A. That determines the measuring lives as X, Y and Z. And these three lives must precede the 21 year period which is the age to be attained by the children of X, Y and Z before any vesting of interest takes place. X, Y and Z are the lives in being at the death of A. Must every grandchild of A become 21 years of age, if at all, within 21 years after the death of the survivor of X, Y and Z? In other words, must every child of X, Y and Z become 21 years of age, if at all, within 21 years after the death of the survivor of X, Y and Z? Suppose X, Y and Z died in that order, Z being the survivor. Z has a child, M, born 9 months after Z's death. 

Assume M arrives at age 21. M's attaining' such age would be how long after A's death? It would be (a) Z's life, plus (b) the period of gestation after Z's death, plus (c) the 21 years following M's birth to attain such age of 21 years. This

would be exactly the maximum period of time allowed by the Rule but not one instant longer, namely, a life in being, a period of gestation, and 21 years. 

5. "At the Creation of the Interest", Says the Rule

The time allowed by the Rule Against Perpetuities is calculated from the time the creating instrument takes effect. 

If the creating instrument is a deed, then the time is calculated from the date of its delivery. If the creating instrument is a will, then the time is calculated from the date of the death of the testator. In our case the instrument which created the contingent interest in the grandchildren is A's will. The validity of such interest must be calculated from the date of A's death when his will took effect. The maximum time thereafter during which the interest must vest to be valid is the sum total of three periods, (a) life or lives in being, plus (b) any permissible periods of gestation, plus (c) 21 years, from the time of the creation of the interest. From what has been said above it must be concluded that the interest of the grandchildren must vest, if at all, not later than (a) the lives of X, Y and Z, plus (b) any periods of gestation in the birth of any child or children to X, Y or Z, plus (c) the time such possible posthumous child would need in attaining the age of 21 years after his birth. There being no possibility of such interest vesting at a later time, it is valid under the Rule 

Against Perpetuities and C holds his reversion in Blackacre subject to B's life estate and subject to being defeated if any of A's grandchildren qualifies to take under the executory devise contained in A's will. The fact that some of the children of X, Y and Z may reach the age of 21 does not satisfy the Rule. There must be no possibility of their reaching 

21 beyond the prescribed period. For example, had A's will provided for his grandchildren who "arrive at the age of 22 years”, such interest would have been void ab initio for it would have been within the range of possibility that the interest would have vested beyond the allowable period.

CASE 50 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, conveys it "to B and his heirs, B to have no power to alien or transfer Blackacre". 

B conveys "to C for life remainder to the heirs of D if and when D pays to B the sum of $1000". Is the interest of the heirs of D valid under the Rule Against Perpetuities? 

Ans. Yes. Of course such interest cannot be valid unless

B's conveyance to C is effective. This raises the question of the effect of the provision in A's deed to B which reads, "B to have no power to alien or transfer Blackacre". Such a provision is called a "disabling restraint" on the power of alienation. One of the inherent qualities of a fee simple estate such as is conveyed to B by A is the power in the owner to dispose of such estate. Any attempt on the part of the grantor or devisor to limit the fee simple owner in his power of disposition of the property even for an instant is quite repugnant to the ownership and is therefore void. Hence, A's deed should be read as though it provided merely, "to B and his heirs", the remaining portion being void as a restraint upon alienation and having no legal effect. Thus B is free to dispose of Blackacre without limitation and the deed to C is a valid conveyance. The effect of B's deed is to create in Ca valid life estate. It creates in the heirs of D a contingent remainder. As long as such remainder continues to be contingent there is a reversion in the grantor, B, which is subject to defeasance by the contingent remainder becoming vested in interest or possession. The interest in D's heirs is a remainder because it must vest at or before the termination of C's life estate. Such remainder is subject to two contingencies, (a) that C may die before D and (b) C may die before D pays B $1000. In either case, at common law, the contingent remainder in D's heirs would be forever destroyed. On the other hand if D should die before C, then at that instant D's heirs would be determined, and if D had, before his death, paid the $1000 to B, then there would be a vested remainder in the heirs of D which would cut off B's defeasible reversion. 

Every contingent remainder is subject to the Rule Against

Perpetuities. Such contingent remainder must vest or fail within a life or lives in being and 21 years from the time of its creation. In this case the contingent remainder must vest or fail within the lives of B, C and D, all three of whom are in being at the time of B's deed to C. If C dies before D, then the contingent remainder is destroyed for the reason that there is no identifiable remainderman to take when C's life estate comes to an end. There can be no heir of a living person so until D dies his heirs must remain unascertained and unascertainable. Further, such contingent remainder cannot become a vested remainder until D has paid to B $1000 during C's lifetime. If either B, D or C dies before such payment is made, then the contingent remainder in D's heirs is irretrievably lost. But all of these events required for the vesting of the contingent remainder must occur during the lives in being of B, C and D from the time of B's deed to C. 

Therefore such contingent remainder must either vest or fail within the lives in being of B, C and D, and no part of the 21 year period is or can be used under the Rule Against Perpetuities. Thus, in its inception the contingent remainder in the heirs of D is quite valid under the Rule.

1. 

There are six covenants for title to real propertya. covenant for seisin, (b) covenant for the right to convey, (c) covenant against incumbrances, (d) covenant for quiet enjoyment, (e) covenant of general warranty, and (f) covenant for further assurances. 

2. Because covenant (f) is of little use in the 

United States and because covenants (a) and (b) are construed to mean the same and (d) and (e) are construed to mean the same, these six covenants can be reduced to three in number, to wit: (1) covenant for seisin, or covenant for the right to convey, (2) covenant against incumbrances, and (3) covenant for quiet enjoyment or of general warranty. 

3. Covenants for seisin or right to convey and against incumbrances are breached, if at all, when the conveyance is made and therefore do not run with the land. 

4. Covenants for quiet enjoyment and general warranty are breached after the deed is delivered and run with the land in favor of remote grantees.

CASE 51 

A, fee simple owner of Blackacre, dies leaving a will in which he devises the property to B and leaving H his sole heir. B executes his deed "to C and his heirs with usual covenants". C conveys the property to D. It is then discovered that the will of A is invalid for lack of proper execution and that Blackacre is, by descent, the property of H. H presents the matter to D who pays H $5000 for a deed to Blackacre. 

This is the same amount D had paid C for the property. D

133 sues B for $5000 damages for breach of covenant. May D recover? 

Ans. Yes. When B executed his deed to C "with usual covenants" B, by using such phrase, made the following covenants to C, (a) covenant for seisin or right to convey, (b) covenant against incumbrances, and (c) covenant of quiet enjoyment or covenant of general warranty. It is clear that B breached the covenant for seisin or right to convey at the very time he delivered his deed to C for the reason that at that time the paramount title was in A's heir, H. The covenants for seisin and for the right to convey are construed to be synonymous and covenant that the grantor is seised of the property and has a right to convey the title thereto. B, having no such right, breached the covenant by conveying to C with "usual covenants". Such covenants being breached and having become causes of action in favor of C, do not run with the land and therefore these covenants did not pass to D by C's conveyance to him. However, the covenants for quiet enjoyment and for general warranty, which are construed to be synonymous, were not breached by B's conveyance to C for the reason that such covenants require for their breach subsequent acts of eviction actual or constructive. It is possible that no one will ever interfere with C's quiet enjoyment of Blackacre in which case there would be no breach. But in this case H had the paramount title to the property and demanded that D buy such paramount title from him. Such title being established D did not have to resist either physical eviction or an action in court before purchasing such outstanding title from H. And upon so doing he may claim interference with his quiet enjoyment by being constructively evicted by H. But the covenants made by B were to his grantee, C, and not to C's grantee, D. It is true that D is no party to a contract with the covenantor, B. But B's covenants for quiet enjoyment and general warranty undertake to defend not only his grantee-covenantee, C, but also those remote grantees who take through C. Such covenants run with the land and may be enforced by remote grantees such as D in this case. But such remote grantee does not have a cause of action against the covenantor, B, merely because there is a breach of the

REAL PROPERTY 

covenant. Because such covenants are contracts of indemnity he must show both a breach of the covenant and that such breach has caused him damage. In this case B's covenants were breached by D's constructive eviction by H and such breach has caused D damage in the amount of $5000. Hence, D has a cause of action against the covenantor, B, and may recover from him damages in the sum of $5000. May C also recover from B? The answer is no. The covenants from B to C have been breached but C was paid $5000 by D and D has been made whole by the original covenantor, B. Hence, C has suffered no damage and without such has no cause of action for indemnity against B.

XIV. ESTOPPEL BY DEED LEGAL GEMS 

1. If a person by deed purports to convey an estate in land which he does not own or a larger estate than he owns, and at a later time acquires the estate which his deed purports to convey, then such after acquired estate passes to the grantee in the deed by estoppel. Such is the doctrine of estoppel by deed. 

2. The doctrine is based on the fact that the grantor intends to convey and the grantee intends to acquire the estate described in the deed. But the language used in a deed determines the intention and the application of the doctrine. Compare three casesa. A, not knowing what interest he has in 

Blackacre, executes a deed "to B of all the right, title and interest in Blackacre, which 

I presently own and no more". It turns out that at the time such deed is executed A is the owner of an undivided one third interest in the land. Thereafter A inherits the other two thirds interest in Blackacre.

Result: B owns one third and A owns two thirds of Blackacre. The terms of the deed are limited to what A presently owns and no more. This language negatives any intention of either A or B to deal with any interest other than that which A owns at the time the deed is executed. The doctrine of estoppel has no application and A may keep the after acquired two thirds interest. b. A, having no interest in Blackacre but not knowing whether or not he has an interest, conveys "to B and his heirs the fee simple estate in Blackacre and covenants for B's quiet enjoyment of the premises". Thereafter A purchases from X the paramount

REAL PROPERTY 

title in fee simple to Blackacre. Here not only did A grant the fee simple title to B but covenanted for quiet enjoyment of the property. Clearly both parties intended the fee simple to pass to the grantee. The doctrine applies and A is estopped to deny the efficacy of his deed to B and that the fee simple estate is in the grantee, B. Result: B owns Blackacre in fee simple and 

A has no interest therein. His after acquired title has passed to B by the operation of the doctrine of estoppel by deed. c. A, having no interest in Blackacre but being uncertain on the matter, executes a deed thereto "to B and his heirs". Thereafter A inherits Blackacre from X. Result: the doctrine applies and B owns 

Blackacre in fee simple by the doctrine of estoppel by deed for the reason that the language of the deed shows an intention on the part of the grantor to convey and of the grantee to receive a fee simple estate. The doctrine requires neither misrepresentation nor covenant of warranty for its operation.

For the purpose of estoppel by deed a mortgage constitutes a deed. When X gave his mortgage to Y he had no interest in Blackacre. Later X inherited from D the fee simple title to such property. At that time the doctrine of estoppel by deed operates and between X and Y and their privies such doctrine is effective and binding. Had Y foreclosed while 

X appeared as the title holder of Blackacre his foreclosure would have been allowed and the title which then vested in X would have been cut off and any later purchaser or incumbrancer would have had notice of such from the records. 

But this is not our case. No foreclosure was commenced while 

X appeared on the record as title holder by descent from D. 

In the meantime X conveys Blackacre to F who relies on the records for protection of his title. If F is not a bona fide purchaser under the recording statutes, then his title should be subject to the doctrine of estoppel by deed and he would hold exactly what X held and conveyed to him. If F is a bona fide purchaser under the recording acts and takes without notice of any interest claimed by Y thereunder, then F should be protected against Y's foreclosure.

REAL PROPERTY 

3. Is F a Bona Fide Purchaser of Blackacre 

It is the purpose of the recording acts to protect subsequent purchasers and incumbrancers who rely on the records as to title to real property. Instruments which are not part of the chain of title of a given piece of land do not impart constructive notice to subsequent purchasers and incumbrancers.

A deed or a mortgage by a mere interloper is no part of the chain of title and gives no constructive notice. The general rule is that a person must first appear as a grantee in the chain of title before he can appear as a grantor on the records in order that his conveyance or incumbrance give notice to the world. Therefore, when X gave his mortgage to Y, X was a mere interloper who appears for the first time as a grantor or mortgagor on the record. He has not theretofore appeared as a grantee in the chain of title. That being the case the mortgage from X to Y gave F, the subsequent purchaser, no constructive notice of Y's claim on Blackacre. Without such notice F is a bona fide purchaser and entitled to protection under the recording statutes and has a good defense to Y's action in foreclosure. 

See Wack v. Collingswood Extension Realty Co., 168 A. 639 (N.J.1933).

1. Under the recording statutes in the United 

States a properly recorded instrument of conveyance, usually a deed or a mortgage, gives constructive notice of its contents to all subsequent purchasers and incumbrancers. e.g.

A, owner of Blackacre through a perfect chain of title, mortgages the property to B but B does not record the mortgage. A then conveys Blackacre "to C subject to a mortgage given by me to B". C records his deed.

This recorded deed gives constructive notice to the world that C is the owner of Blackacre and that such property is subject to B's mortgage and prevents anyone thereafter being a bona fide purchaser of Blackacre as to B and C. 

2. To give constructive notice the recorded instrument must be in the chain of title, which means it must constitute one link in the series of recorded instruments from the patent from the sovereign to the instrument under which a party makes claim to the land. e.g. U. S. grants a patent to A; A deeds to B; B deeds to C; X deeds to Y; C deeds to D. Each is recorded. All these deeds except the one from X to Y are part of the chain of title to D and give constructive notice of their contents. The deed from X to Y is no link in the chain of title and gives no notice to subsequent purchasers or incumbrancers. See 

CASE 52 supra. 

3. The recording statutes are intended to protect (a) bona fide purchasers and incumbrancers and (b) those who take title through such bona fide purchasers and incumbrancers. 

4. A bona fide purchaser or incumbrancer of land is one who takes title thereto and (a)

gives value, which means a monetary value beyond a mere nominal sum and (b) takes in good faith without either actual or constructive notice of prior or adverse claims. 

5. When one takes title to a piece of land which land is in the possession of another person, such possession gives notice of any claim which the possessor makes to the land. e.g. 

A, fee owner of Blackacre which is in the possession of A's tenant, T, under a 5 year lease, conveys the property to B who has no actual knowledge of T's interest or of his being on the land. B attempts to eject T. 

Result: the law puts the purchaser, B, on the land to see what is there and he takes with notice of any claim which T has in the land which is a 5 year lease. B takes Blackacre subject to T's leasehold and cannot eject T.

LEGAL GEMS XVI. RENTS 

1. Rent is the consideration which a tenant pays to his landlord for the possession, use and enjoyment of land. e.g. L, fee simple owner of 

Blackacre, leases it to T for five years at $50.00 rent per month. L now owns the reversion in Blackacre to which the right to rent is considered an incident. T pays the $50 per month for the possession, use and enjoyment of Blackacre. 

2. Rent which is not yet due is real property and descends from the ancestor to the heir; rent which has become due and is unpaid is personal property and passes as chattel property to the personal representative. e.g. L, fee owner of Blackacre, leases it to T for three years at $100 per month. T pays the rent regularly for the first year. He pays none during the second year and owes L $1200 back rent. L dies intestate. H is L's heir and A is his administrator. The reversion and the rent for the last year which is not yet due, descend to H as real property. The chose in action for the $1200 rent in arrears against T passes as personal property to A. 

3. At common law rent is not apportionable as to time. e.g. 

L leases Blackacre to T for five years beginning January 1, 1950 at a rental of $600 per year to be paid on December 

31st of each of the five years beginning on 

December 31, 1950. Full payment is made the first two years. On Nov. 30, 1952 L conveys his reversion in Blackacre to X. To whom should T pay the $600 rent on December 31, 1952? Ans. The whole $600 should be paid to X for two reasons, (a) L's assignment of the reversion carried with it the right to rent as an incident in the absence of an agreement

RENTS 

143 to the contrary, and (b) rent not being apportionable as to time there is no rent due until the last day of the year and on that day the reversion and all the rent belong to X. 

4. Rent is apportionable as to amount. e.g. L leases Blackacre, a duplex all on one floor with one apartment on the west and one on the east end of the building, each built exactly like the other and being of equal value, to tenant, T, for five years at a rent of $100 per month. L conveys the east half of Blackacre to X and the west half of the property to Y. X has a right to half the rent or $50 per month. Y has a right to the other half of the rent or $50 per month. 

5. An agreement between landlord and tenant during the period of the lease to reduce the rent is nudum pactum and unenforceable unless based on a new consideration. e.g. L leases to T for five years at $50 per month. T asks L to reduce the rent to $30 per month for the last two years. L agrees. Thereafter T pays the $30 per month for the last two years and leaves the premises. L sues T for $20 per month for the last two years or $480. May L recover? Ans. Yes. There being no consideration for the agreement to reduce the rent, it is unenforceable and the original lease with T's obligation therein is still binding. Such is the general rule.

It is 1. Waste cannot be defined with precision. a variable and depends on the circumstances of each case. Generally waste consists of some act by a life tenant or tenant for years which at the same time constitutes an unreasonable use of the premises by the tenant and an unreasonable injury to the corpus of the property of the remainderman or reversioner. e.g. L leases Blackacre to T for dwelling purposes in a residential neighborhood. T tears down the dwelling house. This is a clear case of waste. 

2. There are four classes of wastea. voluntary waste is injury to the corpus of the property by an affirmative act of the tenant. e.g. See example under paragraph 

1 next above. b. permissive waste is injury to the corpus of the property caused by the tenant's failure to act when it is his duty to act. e.g. L leases Blackacre for dwelling purposes to 

T. Lightning strikes the house from which a small blaze starts to burn one corner thereof. T by merely using a blanket or bucket of water could put out the blaze and save the house. He does nothing and the house is destroyed. This is permissive waste because T is duty bound to make ordinary repairs and to use reasonable means to preserve the property. His nonfeasance caused the damage. T is liable. c. ameliorating waste is a change in the physical characteristics of the property by an unauthorized act of the tenant but which actually adds value to the land. e.g. L leases Blackacre, a vacant lot in a business

WASTE 

145 district to T for 10 years. A natural mound of earth 25 feet high stands in the center of Blackacre thus making the surface of such lot 25 feet higher than the ground floors of the surrounding business buildings. T removes the mound of earth and builds a business building on the same level as the other business houses surrounding it. This is ameliorating waste by T. 

Usually the tenant is not liable for ameliorating waste. of d. equitable waste is injury to the corpus of the property which is inconsistent with good husbandry and is recognized by the equity courts but not by the law courts. It usually arises in connection with a lease providing "without impeachment waste", which means that the parties agree that the tenant may use the premises as a fee simple owner would use the property. e.g. L leases to T a business building in a business area for three years. Business has always been good in this building in this locality. T tears down the building and builds a residence on the lot. At law 

T is not liable for such damage under the provision, "without impeachment for waste". Because no reasonable fee owner of the property would so use the premises, T is liable in damages to L for equitable waste because such use is wholly inconsistent with good hubandry and is an unconscionable destruction of the property. 

3. The general purpose of the law of waste is to permit the life tenant or tenant for years to have the maximum use of the occupied premises but at the same time to preserve the corpus of the property for the remainderman or reversioner who will take possession at the end of the tenancy. 

4. The remedy for voluntary or permissive waste by the tenant for life or for years is either

CASE 54 

L, owner of Blackacre in fee simple, grants it "to T for the period of T's natural life". There is on Blackacre an abundance of sand, gravel, building stone, coal and oil, but the premises have never been used for any purpose other than agriculture and nearly all of the surface is productive of crops. There is no provision in the lease permitting T to use the land for any purpose other than farming. T threatens to open sand and gravel pits, quarries, mines and oil wells, for commercial purposes. L seeks to enjoin any such acts on the part of T. Should the injunction issue?

Ans. Yes. In the absence of an agreement to the contrary a tenant for life or for years has no right to open new mines, quarries or oil wells on the leased premises. The reason is clear. It is destruction of the corpus of the property. On the other hand, if such mines, quarries and wells are already open on the premises then the tenant may continue to use and operate them on the theory that a former use is impliedly authorized. In our case the first principle applies. Blackacre has never been used for any purpose other than farming. In the absence of an agreement in the deed or lease, and there is none, T has no right to open new mines, quarries or wells on Blackacre. Because the law remedy is not adequate to deal with threats to commit voluntary waste, the court of equity should take jurisdiction and grant the injunction to L. If such waste has already been committed then the equity court will not only grant an injunction against further commission of waste but will give money damages for the injury already done.

CASE 55 

L, fee owner of Blackacre, leases it to T for 35 years. The premises consists of a section of land. About one third of the area of Blackacre is cultivated and produces abundant annual crops. 

Another third of the area is covered with scrub timber, small bushes and some trees which will make good lumber if cut. The other third is swampland covered with virgin trees which are excellent material for lumbering purposes but which land is wholly unsuitable for agriculture or any other purpose. T clears the third of Blackacre covered with scrub timber, bushes and trees and prepares it for cultivation and adds $50 per acre value to that part of the leased land. He appropriates and sells the valuable trees cut from such area for $900. He cuts trees from the swampland of the value of $2000. L sues T for waste seeking $2900 in damages and an injunction against further cutting of timber from the swampland. (a) How much, if any, should L recover in damages? (b) Should the injunction issue? 

Ans. (a) L should recover $2000 damages. (b) The injunction should issue. (a) Every tenant for years or for life has a right to take such timber or brush from the leased premises as is necessary for firewood, fence posts and repair of buildings and the like. This is called the right to estovers. If suitable dead wood is available for estovers the tenant should not resort to standing live timber. Any fee simple owner who is a good husbandman should aspire to make his land as productive as possible. When, then, T from that third cleared the bushes, scrub timber and trees of Blackacre which is suitable for cultivation, such acts constitute good husbandry and not waste. And because such a project is expensive and laborious the trees and bushes taken from the cleared area belong to the tenant as partial compensation for the efforts expended in producing such result. Therefore, the trees sold from this area and the $900 received therefrom belong to T and he need not account for them to L. However, the cutting of the trees

REAL PROPERTY 

from the swampland which land is suitable for no other use is clearly waste for which T is liable to L. This is pure destruction of the corpus of the property like opening new mines. 

To justify such acts on the part of the life tenant or tenant for years there must be an express agreement with the landlord or reversioner. Therefore, the trees taken from the swampland belonged to L both before and after severance and the money received from the sale thereof belongs to L and T is liable to L for such. Hence, L has a right to recover $2000 in damages from T for such voluntary waste. 

Because T has no right to continue to commit such waste in the swampland L has a right to the injunction decree. The question in each of such cases is whether or not the tenant's act gives the landlord or reversioner a reasonable ground for objection on the theory that such act is inconsistent with good husbandry.

XVIII. FIXTURES LEGAL GEMS 

1. 

A fixture is a chattel become real property. 

2. For a chattel to become a fixture it musta. be annexed to the real property either actually or constructively, b. be adapted to the purpose for which the real property is used, and c. be intended by the annexer to become a fixture, which means the objective intention determined from all the circumstances including (1) the nature of the chattel, (2) the manner of annexation, (3) the completeness with which the chattel is merged in the use made of the real property, and (4) the relation which the annexer bears to the chattel and to the real property such as owner, tenant, licensee of the land or owner, bailee or custodian of the chattel. 

3. Whether or not a chattel has become a fixture is primarily a fact question. 

4. An oral agreement that a chattel shall remain personal property is valid and enforceable between the parties even though it is later affixed to the land. e.g. V sells to P a furnace to be installed in the basement of P's house it being agreed that the furnace shall remain a chattel until it is paid for and if not paid for by a specific date, then V to have the right to remove it. P does not pay. V has a right to remove the furnace. But should

P sell the house to a bona fide purchaser with no knowledge of such oral agreement, V cannot remove the furnace as against such purchaser. 

5. A fixture is real property and an oral agreement to sell such is unenforceable under the

REAL PROPERTY 

Statute of Frauds for it involves an interest in real property. 

6. As to the Statute of Frauds there are two classes of fixtures, (a) removable and (b) unremovable. e.g. A bath tub which is attached to the house only by its connections with the water supply and drain pipes is a removable fixture and an oral agreement to sell such is valid and enforceable under the Statute of Frauds on the theory that the subject of the sale is the right of removal rather than the tub itself; a bath tub which is built into the house by cement is an unremovable fixture and an oral sale of such tub is unenforceable under the Statute of Frauds. 

7. "Trade fixtures" are chattels affixed to the real property by a tenant for the purpose of pecuniary gain during the tenancy. e.g. shelves built into a store building. (a) If the tenancy is of uncertain duration such as for life or at will then the tenant may remove trade fixtures within a reasonable time after the termination of the tenancy. (b) If the tenancy is for a definite time the tenant must remove such trade fixtures before the end of the term or they are forfeited to the landlord.

151 Usually the question of whether or not an article has become a fixture is a question of fact to be determined by the trier of fact. But when the facts are so clear that reasonable men may not differ thereon it is a question of law. It is believed to be a law question in this case. 

1. Annexation to the Realty 

The furnace in this case is bolted to the cement floor of the basement and is connected with the hot air ducts leading to the various rooms of the house. This constitutes an actual annexation to the realty. 

2. Adaptation to the Purpose for Which the Realty is Used 

In this case the realty is used for dwelling house purposes and a furnace therein is a proper method of heating such house for living purposes. Therefore, the furnace in the house is fully adapted to the purpose for which the land is being used and serves a very necessary purpose thereon. 

3. Intention of the Annexer 

In determining fixture or no fixture the intention of the annexer is a most important item and it is the objective intention as determined from the acts and circumstances rather than the subjective intention of the annexer which is to be determined. In this case the annexer is the owner of the chattel, the furnace. He is not merely a bailee or a custodian thereof. Further, he is the fee simple owner of the realty and not a tenant or a licensee thereof. He affixes the furnace to the realty by bolts and hot air pipes. Such hot air pipes must have been cut and fit to this furnace and connected with the various rooms of this particular house. 

Such furnace must have been selected as to size and capacity for the purpose of furnishing sufficient heat for this particular house and installed for no other purpose than to make A's dwelling house comfortable and livable. It seems to be completely appropriated to the purpose for which the house is being used. All of these items disclose a clear intention on the part of A to make the furnace a part and parcel of

his dwelling house and a fixture thereto. When A conveyed 

Blackacre to B without any mention of the furnace or a right reserved to remove it, the furnace passed as an integral part of Blackacre to the purchaser, B, and A had no right to remove it. The removal of the furnace by A was a removal of part of the land owned by B and for such removal A is liable in damages to B. 

4. Dicta 

(a) Had A brought the furnace onto Blackacre with no intention of using it in the house on Blackacre it would have remained a chattel. (b) Had A brought the furnace onto the land with an intention of annexing it to the house and using it therein it would have been constructively annexed and would have passed to B by the conveyance. (c) Had A brought the furnace onto the land and annexed it to the land and used it therewith it is actually annexed. This is our case above. (d) Had A been using the furnace in the house and removed it for the sole purpose of repair but with the intention of reinstalling it and it was still on the land, it would still be constructively annexed to the land and a fixture which would have passed to the purchaser, B. (e) Had A removed the furnace with no intention of reinstalling it in the house on Blackacre, then it would have been reconverted into a chattel and would not have passed with the conveyance to B.

XIX. EMBLEMENTS LEGAL GEMS 

1. Fructus naturales are fruits of the land without any aid from man's industry such as grasses, trees, bushes and their fruits. These remain real property until they are actually severed from the land at which time they become personal property. An oral agreement to sell wild grass, wild crops or standing timber growing on the land is enforceable without compliance with the Statute of Frauds on the theory that the item of sale is constructively severed and is considered personal property. 

2. Fructus industriales are fruits of the land which are produced primarily by man's industry, that is, by man's planting, fertilizing and cultivating. They include annual crops such as grains, beans, peas, and corn; also perennials such as apples, pears, grapes, and citrus fruits. 

3. Fructus industriales are called emblements.

On April 1, 1953 T plants a crop of rye on Blackacre. It is not ripe for harvest by May 1, 1953 when T's lease comes to an end. T has no right to come onto Blackacre thereafter to harvest the rye. It belongs to L. b. if the tenancy is for an indefinite term, such as a tenancy at will or for life or for the life of another, then the tenant has a rea-
