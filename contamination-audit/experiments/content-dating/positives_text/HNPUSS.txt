FOREWORD 

F YOU approach this book with the attitude of the old farmer who, upon his first sight of a giraffe, exclaimed "Thar ain't no such animal!" I shall not deny that, strictly speaking, you are right. Likewise, strictly speaking, there is no such thing as wireless telegraphy, since there are wires in the aerials and other parts of the mechanism. You may even run across an occasional bone in boneless codfish. Possibly, I might have avoided all this introductory apology by using the title "The Psychology of Debunking 

Yourself." But, I prefer short titles, even if hair-splitting accuracy has to be sacrificed on behalf of brevity.

Had I adopted the style of our pioneer authors, my title might have been "An Essay on the Art of Curing Yourself of a Nervous Break down Through the Application of Certain Selected 

Principles of Ancient and Modern Psychology." 

If you regard this old fashion in book titles as ridiculous, I submit that it is no more absurd than our modern fashion of expecting an author to write an apologetic foreword to warn his readers of the faults and shortcomings of his book. All I shall say on this score is that if you find nothing to criticize other than the title, you will be mighty fortunate, and so shall I.

Bunkless Psychology 1-FACING THE FACTS ON BOTH SIDES OF YOURSELF 

JOU can not find bunkless psychology in a book. At the cost of a nervous breakdown, I learned this bitter truth. After spending half a lifetime as a "psychology hound," reading everything I could get hold of on every phase of the subject, I nearly made a total wreck of myself through failing to realize the basic fact that anyone who really wants a bunkless psychology must make it for himself. It is a strictly personal product. It is never identical for two individuals.

You may be aided in developing your individual bunkless psychology by this account of my experiences and observations in overcoming my nervous attacks by learning how to divest my mental system of some of the bunk that upset its balance. 

You are warned, however, that the plans for devising my personal system of bunkless psychology will have to be modified and adapted to fit your individual interests and needs.

An overload of bunk in the mental system is a frequent cause of nervous breakdowns. This certainly was the cause of my crash and, from talks with others who have suffered similar troubles, I

believe that a vast number of nervous disorders are due chiefly to the same things which threw me off the track. 

Persistent self-deception in order to cling blindly to certain pet prejudices which conflict with our unfolding knowledge and reason develops many a case of nerves. In brief, many of us kid ourselves into mental illness. I confess that that is exactly what I did. seriously real even though I fooling myself.

Perhaps I am taking some liberties in making this small word cover so many things, using it as what has been aptly called an "omnibus” term.

As the original word, buncombe, has been shortened, its popular meaning has been broadened. 

Most of us, I believe, do not regard bunk as something deliberately deceptive. We generally use

Mostly Bunk this term to characterize the boasting assertions which self-deluded individuals make on behalf of their pet prejudices.

It is employed as our carry-all to include the unconsciously deceptive and misleading factors of traditional notions, dogmatic creeds, personal prejudices, petty pretensions, infantile superstitions, limited knowledge and other biased viewpoints which comprise the "human equation" in all advice, information and instruction that we receive from various sources. As our cartoon suggests, the kernel of truth frequently is hidden beneath these husks of personal prejudice which we are lumping together and labeling as bunk. A major problem in life and the chief objective of our study is to discover the best way to remove the husks and preserve the pristine beauty of the wholesome kernel.

5-BUNK IN ALL HUMANS 

HE mistake must not be made of thinking, because the bunk peddled by fake psychologists is misleading and sometimes mischievous, that the writings of scientifically trained psychologists are one hundred per cent pure wisdom, guaranteed absolutely free from any trace of bigotry or prejudices. 

I doubt if reputable teachers of psychology would deny that their opinions, like those of other human beings, are bound to be more or less colored by their personal prejudices. Frankly, I should immediately suspect the credentials of any professed psychologist who sets up the claim that his particular brand of psychology is absolutely bunkless.

Such a claim would be contrary to one of the basic teachings of psychology itself, which prin-

ciple has been expressed in the statement that "every human being is a bundle of prejudices.' 

This view has been set forth by the great American master of psychological instruction, Professor William James. He classifies all these personal prejudices and peculiar individual fads and fancies as "temperament," which he maintains "so influences even the greatest thinkers and most deliberate philosophers that their opinions are never wholly logical but always are more or less colored by their unconscious likes and dislikes."

This latter phrase gives us a clue to the underlying cause of the bunk that is in all human beings, regardless of their education, intelligence or rationality. Naturally there is more bunk in the opinions and beliefs of ignorant individuals than in those more intelligent. 

But, deeply buried in what many psychologists call the "unconscious" are these instinctive likes and dislikes, these traits and tendencies both inherent and acquired which greatly influence all our decisions and choices and thereby help to form our habits and to determine our character and personality. In this vast theoretical mental realm that modern psychologists have discussed so vigorously, with considerable disputing over many mooted points, lies the seat of our emotions.

6-HINTS ON DEBUNKING YOURSELF 

LL sensible persons, I believe, readily recognize the fact that most of our motives and desires are based upon our personal prejudices rather than upon cold, logical reasoning. Our emotions mainly determine our actions.

The first objective of our inquiry is to find out why human beings are addicted to bunk in their beliefs and their consequent behavior. With this objective made fairly clear, we shall endeavor to suggest a few practical pointers that may enable some of us to debunk ourselves of at least part of the prejudices, superstitions, bigotries, obsessions and intolerances which hamper our social progress and hinder our individual happiness. 

You must understand, of course, that I am not a professional psychologist and make no pretense of a complete scientific knowledge of the subject. 

On the other hand, I have been an ardent student of all phases of psychology for many years and have made sufficient experiments in applying its teachings to my own personal problems to feel justified in offering a few suggestions to those who may be interested. You may rest assured, however, that I shall confine my observations to the elementary and common-sense phases of the sub-

By sticking strictly to a small corner of this realm, we hope to achieve at least the distinction of brevity.

As you may readily assume, my excuse for relating these more or less intimate personal experiences is a sincere desire that you may benefit in solving some of your problems by taking advantage of such facts about human behavior as my account may disclose to you. 10-SPENCER'S DEFINITION OF LIFE 

PENCER'S famous dictum that "Life is the continuous adjustment of internal relations to external relations," despite the fact that I had read it scores of times, now took on a meaning quite new to me and became the cornerstone upon which, ultimately, I reconstructed my philosophy of life and began to build my personal system of bunkless psychology. 

Should anyone object to my making a close connection between philosophy and psychology, let me refer the objector to the old master, William James, who shows that certain aspects of these two subjects are so similar as to be practically inseparable. Anyway, I feel confident that anyone who builds a bunkless psychology for himself will thereby gain, as I did, a broader and more satisfactory philosophy of life.

One thing that deeply impressed me was the serenity with which my brother awaited the inevitable end of his illness which, from his previous experience in hospital work, he knew was a fatal attack. The calm and cheerful attitude he maintained in the face of what appeared to me as a terrible tragedy was in striking contrast to my fits -of worry and despondency over things that I now began to see as the trifles that they actually were.

LIFE: A CONTINUOUS ADJUSTMENT 

25 Meditating upon Spencer's definition of the process of life gave me the clue to my inner conflict and taught me the first lesson in facing the facts on both sides of myself. Two sides, I ponrelations, and the dered, the inside, or internal outside, or external relations. Since life is a continuous process of adjustment between our outside and inside relations, then the vital problem is to find to what extent and in what ways the individual can direct or control the adjustment between his internal relations and his external relations. 

As I renewed my research into books, both old and new, and began testing various psychological theories by attempting to apply them to my individual experiences, the first difficulty I bumped up against was the necessity of freeing myself, as far as possible, from personal prejudices.

In a book by one of my earliest instructors in psychology, Dr. Henry Churchill King's "Ration al Living," I was struck by this statement: "There must be definite conditions of growth, of character, of happiness, of influence. And it is psychology's highest task to instruct us as to these conditions of our own life. *** We get control of the principles of life only by some real working of them outonly by the laboratory method."

BUNKLESS PSYCHOLOGY 

It would be difficult to discuss any phase of psychology without venturing upon debatable ground. Here, for instance, even in our elementary examination of such subjects as our instincts and desires, our consciousness and, especially, the theory of our unconscious or our subconscious interior, we are taking up topics that are hotly disputed by the professionals. Bear in mind, however, that our practical objective does not require the making of fine distinctions on these disputed points. We know that we have a good many inner urges or desires and it is not necessary for us to draw definite lines to distinguish between our inherited and our acquired impulses in order to derive benefit from learning how these inner relations may be adjusted to outer conditions and circumstances. Since we are trying to utilize the discoveries and the theories of the psychologists to obtain a better understanding of ourselves and our world, let us employ every practical pointer we find in any psychological theory even though we may not clearly and completely comprehend all the points upon which the theory is formulated.

Actually, of course, there are no such definite mental realms as the three sections depicted in our pentagon diagram and labeled, consciousness, sub-consciousness and unconsciousness. What we call mind is by no means confined to particular organs or located in any special region of our body. Our thinking process involves our whole body and is not, as some folks mistakenly suppose, exclusive functions of the brain, the medulla and the nerves. Not too strongly can the fact be emphasized that we think with the whole body. The theories of the sub-conscious and the unconscious have been formulated to aid us somewhat in understanding certain phases of our memorizing processes. The theory of what is sometimes called the subconscious mind assumes that the conscious impressions of the incidents and events of our lives register some sort of a record upon the millions of cells which compose our body. After stating that, "The gaps between our machines and our ability to control them for human ends is widened because education has clung to old traditions and aims of culture in the face of the new industrial situation," Professor Dewey explains one phase of this situation by stating that "control of natural forces by means of machinery has brought to humanity the possibility of an amount of leisure from which the mass of men and women in the past were hopelessly shut out. You will observe, of course, that this is practically the same goal as the one advocated by Professor Jacks, as being "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness."

Since psychology, in general, affirms that our natural dominant desires are in accord with the goals of these famous educators and the "inalienable rights of man" defined by the American 

Declaration of Independence, it logically follows that true education must be a process of training individuals in the art of happiness as well as in the science of achievement. Likewise, it logically follows that education, considered from this viewpoint, should be a continuous process, enduring as long as life itself endures.

At any rate, that is the conception of education that is back of my determination to devote the remainder of my life to non-stop self-education.
