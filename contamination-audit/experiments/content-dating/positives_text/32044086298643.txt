A Remembrance of the Diplomacy of Peace for American Patriots (An Article Suggested by the Recurrence of the Anni versary of the Signing of the Kellogg-Briand 

Treaty Renouncing War as an Instrument of National Policy.)

INTERNATIONAL coöperation is to-day a recognized necessity among all nations. The forms that it may take vary but the essentials of compromise, persuasion and debate are part of the daily work of every government of the world in its dealings with all other governments. Just three years ago in a speech Aristide Briand, the old warrior of France in her postwar diplomacy, declared one of the principles of this period of coöperation among nations. He said: "It is time to realize that for the civilized world peace is not a luxury, but a vital necessity; that its organization is imperative to us, not only as an urgent duty, but still more as an obligation." 

It is in this organization of peace that the citizens of the United States are taking an increased interest, and it is in the results of this international cooperation that they see a larger security for their nation. Thus, for all who are friends of the peace of the world and the larger freedom of their own country, the anniversary of the signing of the Pact of Paris is a remembrance of peace and world citizenship.

We should never allow the implications of the Kellogg-Briand Anti-war Pact to lose their significance in our own minds or to become a dead letter in the public thought and policy of this nation. War comes because the institutions of peace are not organized, and in their establishment every sincere believer in international concord has a part to take. On August 

27, 1928, the Secretary of State of the United States crossed the Atlantic ocean, journeyed to Paris and participated in a world ceremony which binds the honor of the United States to that of the whole world. 

The nations there pledged their word that war as a national policy was renounced. It was the declaration of independence for the democratic governments of the world. It was the beginning of an international liberty, which under the guidance of the peace-loving peoples of peace-making democracies can bring blessing to all men. By the Pact of Paris, as the Kellogg- 

Briand Treaty is known, the people of the earth (the treaty was even made in their names and is so declared in the preamble) covenant to renounce war as an instrument of national policy. They declare that henceforth the diplomacy of peace, the methods of arbitration, of common counsel and conference, are the agencies of democratic governments in dealing with each other. For the first time in history nations solemnly pledged their honor that their future policy would be a diplomacy of peace, rather than a diplomacy of preparation for wars.

Only two years have passed since this Pact of Paris was signed. Yet since that August day of 1928 when the declaration of independence from war was declared (remember the institutions of peace are yet to be made secure) it has been revealed that this na- 4

tion can use its mighty energy for the peace of the world. This Pact of Paris is in line with every purpose of democratic government, and to-day the United States stands before the whole world as a leader in the outlawry of war. The United States has in effect declared that war is an outlaw, and has pledged her national honor, along with all other nations signatory to the Pact of Paris, to prepare for peace. The United States says to the world and to its own citizens that there need be no fear that the United States will become the Prussia of the twentieth century, or that a materialistic nationalism will menace the peace of the world as a military nationalism did before 1914. The purpose of the United 

States to insure the blessings of liberty is shown in joining its mighty economic and political strength to that of the whole world in keeping the peace of the world. The United States has affirmed again her courageous faith in the spirit of democratic institutions, and she has in part declared that there can be no isolation from the common ideals of mankind. 

Slowly and inevitably the community of spirit which makes America what it is as a nation will be shared with all peoples in the common efforts of international coöperation. But there is a duty upon citizens who see the destiny of our own democratic life in the destiny of all peoples to achieve their freedom, to make the peace-loving power of this nation a larger instrumentality for the will to peace in the world. 

Liberty in this century for America and for all the world is to be found only in coöperation. The other way is national suicide and international anarchy. 

Those who are serving most wisely their own country and the future peace of mankind are patiently trying 5

to see a way to strengthen the institutions of common understanding among men. It is in the way of understanding that the new economics and the new politics of the United States and of Europe and of the world are finding fresh means of expression. Eight brief years was a short time for any creative ideal to make itself felt, but international coöperation as an ideal has made tremendous progress in the world since the politicians gathered at the Versailles Conference after the war of 1914-1918 to make a peace. 

That peace, shabby and war-inspired as it was, was made after the bitterest war in history. Whatever has been done since then for international goodwill and common sense friendship among nations has been done against the cruelest fighting odds any noble ideal ever had to endure. What has been achieved by the 

League of Nations, the World Court, the Pact of Paris, and the London Naval Conference of 1930, is proof of the fine saying of Victor Hugo, that you can stop an army of men but you cannot stop an army of ideas. Europe has had to face, as the United States and the whole world has had to face, since 1918, an army of facts in trade and commerce and in political relationships which said: "Coöperate or die." History will record that fact.

The destiny of America in this century is in a world which must learn the habit and the mind of peace. 

Every institution which can control the complex relationships of modern peoples should have the courageous support of the United States. The anniversary of the Pact of Paris is a time to take stock. The citizenship of this nation must rally behind the ideals of the Pact of Paris in a practical way, and this country must vindicate her signature of the treaty which

renounces war as an instrument of national policy. 

The whole world can know in a practical way that this powerful nation is unwilling ever again to use the battlefield as the means for democratic nations to gain their ends. The foreign policy of the United 

States can give prestige and validity to the ideal of popular government throughout the world, for every nation can learn from the United States that this people put their destiny as a democratic nation behind the rule of law among countries as among individuals. Whether we join the League of Nations soon or late, though fifty-four nations of the world are members of this practical agency of creating the conditions of world peace, is not the question. But by a foreign policy of just and open dealing with every nation, an unfailing readiness to accept the use of conciliation and arbitration which the United States has urged upon the world, we can prove beyond all doubt that the United States builds its future upon the basis of peace. 

Again and again the ideal of a creative and dynamic peace must be taught by lovers of America. The ideal is simply not a warless world, though that is hard to gain, but it is an ideal which sees a world tackling the problems which have kept mankind in a perpetual servility to Force and ignorance and greed. 

It is freedom for man to have a chance: that is the ideal of citizenship in our times; and it is because man is betrayed by war and his hopes murdered by the dictatorship of force, which war stands for; it is because of this, that a living faith of patriotism is the obligation of citizenship in a democracy. 

The living faith of patriotism will not be afraid of American life. There is error, there is confusion,

there is corruption, and there are many contradictory and evil influences. But the living faith of patriotism will not retreat before ignorance, it will not accept the materialistic standards of prosperity and progress as final, nor will this living faith of patriotism acknowledge any lesser ideal of citizenship than the fullest service of liberty for all people in this country. The living faith of patriotism will see in the America of 1930 an America in the making, and with a nobly militant spirit will want America to help interpret what freedom means to all men and all nations in these times.

The living faith of patriotism will win the allegiance of all hearts when the future of the nation we love is seen as one with the meaning of freedom in the twentieth century. Whatever the future holds for the United States, we cannot be cowardly or of little mind. Greatness will come if we prepare for it and are worthy of freedom. We must look into the future. We must never lose hope. We must never give up. Our patriotism must work out the ideals of 

America.

GENERAL PACT FOR THE RENUNCIATION OF WAR 

(Signed at Paris, August 27, 1928)

The President of the German Reich, the 

President of the United States of America, His

Majesty the King of the Belgiums, the President of the French Republic, His Majesty the King of Great Britian, Ireland and the British 

Dominions beyond the Seas, Emperor of India;

His Majesty the King of Italy, His Majesty the 

Emperor of Japan, the President of Poland, the President of the Czecho-slovak Republic: 

Persuaded that the time has come when a frank renunciation of war as an instrument of national policy should be made. . . . Convinced that all changes in their relations with one another should be sought by pacific means. . . . Hopeful that, encouraged by their example, all the nations of the world will join in this humane endeavor ... have decided to conclude a treaty....

ARTICLE I The High Contracting Parties solemnly de clare in the name of their respective peoples that they condemn recourse to war for the solution of international controversies, and renounce it as instrument of national policy in their relations with one another.

ARTICLE II 

The High Contracting Parties agree that the settlement or solution of all disputes or conflicts of whatever nature or of whatever origin they may be, which arise among them, shall never be sought except by pacific means.

The Promise of American Life 

Is Yet to Be Fulfilled

THE impact of the United States as the mightiest power on earth is not growing less in the world of international relations. This fact of world influence has been accepted so long that certain of its implications are now almost overlooked, but this does not mean that the United States is any the less at a critical period in her world influence. The times in which we now live are not only critical if the views of the rest of the world of nations are taken, but in the United States there are many indications that social thought on national destiny is at the crossroads. It is well from time to time to consider afresh national destiny in the light of what other peoples are doing and thinking.

Any study of America at this time is a study in national energy. America and the whole world are amazed at the quick and vast sources of scientific invention in this country and the application of the results of the laboratory to every agency of mechanical and commercial progress. In addition to this, the United States is to-day the economic and financial center of the world. The political power of the 

United States has not only been a dominant fact in post-war international relations, but in many international questions the decisive control is American will and opinion.

The oft-quoted aphorism of Calvin Coolidge that "the business of America is business" seems to be true. The national wealth of the United States is estimated at over $400,000,000,000, and of a total world wealth of a trillion dollars, the United States 10

claims a solid forty per cent. No nation in human history has reached a position of material wealth comparable to the present strength of the United States. This can be put in this electrical age of ours in the graphic terms of competent engineers, who tell us that we have the equivalent of the labor of three billions of slaves in mechanical energy. This is seen in a striking way when it is known that this means thirty servants for every man, woman and child in America. It is well-known that one kilowatt hour of electricity equals the work of ten men.

Such tremendous mechanical and material progress combined with a vast social and political influence gives the United States the power and the peril of an unprecedented national destiny. Not only do 130,- 

000,000 American citizens ask "What of it?" but the whole world wants to know what the impact of American national power will be. The direction that the 

United States is taking in national and international affairs is a problem no thinking citizen can neglect.

The markets of the United States literally cover the earth. All world ports to-day are directly linked with the prosperity of this land of ours. Since the beginning of the World War the United States has increased its foreign investments fourfold, quadrupled its foreign trade, and become the creditor nation of the world. In fact since 1922 the foreign trade of the United States has doubled. Our economic and political problems embrace a region whose extent is beyond the Arctic circle in Alaska, southward to Samoa, and east and west from China and the 

Philippines to Liberia and Tangier. 

Facts of America's penetration of the economic and industrial life of the world are staggering. And these facts are not only significant because they repre- 11

sent a mighty nation with a standard of living for the mass of the people that the world has never before known; but also these facts are interpreters of world influence of which we are a part whether we will to be or not. The United States has six per cent of the world's population. That six per cent converts about fifty per cent of the world's withdrawal of natural resources-iron, copper, coal, oil, timber and cotton-into usable commodities annually. By the sheer force of economic supremacy the United States is the most powerful nation on earth. But how long will this dominant position last? It will not be so always, and it is the sanest patriotism to face the fact that the welfare and continued prosperity of the United States depend upon a future of coöperation with the rest of the world. Political and economic isolation is impossible for the United States in the world of nations.

Every school boy knows that the whole world to-day is a shrinking world. In reality, for trade and commerce, the world is just a few minutes round. A ticker in New York is heard wherever men sell goods or barter for economic advantage. And for all practical financial purposes oceans to-day are less obstacles than rough roads and mountain streams were one hundred years ago. This shrinking world is the one that 130,000,000 Americans have to live in for better or for worse. It is then the duty of American citizenship to understand what this changed world of international relations means. It should be the highest aim of American statesmanship to interpret America's leadership in this new world. This is real patriotism, this is the enduring security for our nation. 

America's first duty is to understand her past 12

spirit in courage, and this is necessary before she can adequately understand her second great dutythat duty which requires a study of her vast influence as a powerful democratic nation. The decisions of the United States in this century will go a long way toward making a new world of nations. Her adventurous spirit of idealism can be the power which will really change the purposes of governments and make popular control of institutions real. It cannot be urged too much before men and women who crave for their country enduring power in world civilization, that the decisions of the United States can give validity and integrity to the whole conception of democratic government. This is not only true in the national government of the United States but in the relationships of the United States with all peoples of the earth. The place of the United States is that of a leader in restoring the authority of democratic ideals, and of putting in practice these ideals. 

In world affairs to-day the United States can give direction to the forces of good will and justice which are seeking to establish the reign of law and the rule of reason. Common sense and sanity are slowly being worked out in international affairs, and the United States can prove her allegiance to the ideals and the institutions of peace.

This allegiance of the United States to a practical program of peace and coöperation with other nations is going to be of increasing significance. This allegiance has been proved in part by the signing of the Kellogg-Briand Anti-war Pact, by which this nation solemnly renounced war as an instrument of national policy. It was further affirmed by the ratification of the London Naval Conference Treaty of 1930, which established for the first time the prin- 13

ciple of the limitation of naval armament. These efforts have been, and, are nothing more than, the first mileposts along the way of common sense in international relations. They will help no doubt to create the will for peace among men of all nations, but lovers of a world that peace can build, and peace alone can build, should not count these pacific measures anything more than the first mileposts. The day to day hard work of international coöperation is ahead. 

The institutions and the habits of peace are yet to be solidly established. This is the work of citizens of all nations who not only hate war and the dictatorship of force, but what is more, love a city not made with hands whose image is in their hearts and whose building is the test of civilization. 

It needs always to be taught in this democracy that foreign policy should reflect the same honor and justice which high standards of domestic policy require. 

It was this ideal which really gave a noble meaning to the war aims of President Wilson, and he was the first of the leaders of nations to place this statement of war aims beyond any lesser strategies of victory.

This belief with regard to the relationships of democracy with other countries has appeared in the history of political societies only comparatively recently, and it has not yet secured a firm place beyond dispute in the scheme of things. "Is not diplomacy the field of lies?" and, "a diplomat is one sent abroad to lie for his country," are too popular echoes of old war talk not to have had much basis in fact. They represent a cynicism which cannot be a healthy conviction in a nation whose institutions and whose government are founded upon the integrity of the mass of the people. 

The decisions of foreign policy which are to deter- 14

mine whether we are to coöperate with the nations of the world are decisions which are to determine our security as a democratic power with ideals. These decisions cannot be based upon less than the whole interests of a new order of economic and political society which has been slowly coming to power. The decisions the United States makes will advance or will obstruct the progress of ideals of democratic life throughout the world. This is a significant fact the 

Christian must consider. War defeats every aim of democratic peoples, and it can destroy in the United States the hopes of working out the necessary ideals of liberty and freedom. The sound view of Lincoln that a nation cannot exist half-free and half-slave, may be urged with apt meaning in that wide field of present day affairs in which this country, having reached its place of overwhelming power and influence, now finds itself. The United States frontiers are now in trade and finance and social influence everywhere. If democratic government and its institutions are to survive within the wide territory of the United States under conditions of honor, then foreign policy must be as sane, and, as intelligently directed, as domestic policy. There are no moral dispensations for diplomats to be traitors to the aims of democratic government in the world while they claim to represent a noble people at home. It is not possible for a nation to be one thing at home in its dealings with its own citizens, and to be another where other peoples are concerned. This country, we all believe, at heart loves justice and respects liberty as much as any people on earth. These ideals of justice and liberty have not been worked out at home, yet there is common agreement that in this nation the unfailing intent is always present. The promise of American life is yet to be 15

fulfilled. But the fulfillment of the promise of the aims of liberty is not only a duty towards the 130,000,- 000 people within this country, but for their progressive interpretation the whole world waits. If America fails the world, the aims will be defeated inevitably at home. The law of the spirit applies to nations as it does unfailingly to all individuals. 

The decisions of the United States not only give validity to all the ideals of patriotism which a free people have held, but these decisions made in courage can be of great service in raising the level of decision on the problems which the whole world is facingfacing almost in desperation. This is the adventure of American decisions in the twentieth century. As 

America dares to give larger liberty to her citizens in their institutions of government and in the working out of ideals of justice in economic relationships, the old promise of American liberty will not only be restored but throughout the world men will take new hope because this most powerful democracy has set before itself the practice of the faith of democracy. 

In a nation and in a world that needs the fresh courage of adventurous statesmanship, making plain what are the foundations of national greatness is the greatest duty of patriots. The meaning of patriotism for these times of peace can only be recaptured in courage. The adventure of America's decisions in this country will show whether American citizens can interpret the enduring nobility of patriotism in terms of the needs of the common man and the common woman. To make secure the nation's and the world's hope of peace is to go far in creating the conditions of happiness and good living for all peoples. 16

These Years That Men Call Years of Peace 

(Written for those who go back in September to the battlefields of peace in America-the school and university.) 

President Edwin Anderson Alderman, of the University of Virginia, in his great address of December 15, 1924, on Woodrow Wilson before the joint session of the two Houses of Congress, majestically declared that the American president "had the heart to match the moral hopes of mankind against their passions." 

This is a noble saying about Woodrow Wilson, and the truth of it becomes plainer as men attempt "to substitute for the arbitrament of war and death the reign of law." There is a saner understanding in 

1930 than in 1920 of this further belief of President Alderman, that Woodrow Wilson "sought to give the twentieth century a faith to inspire it and to justify the sacrifice of millions of lives; and if there was failure in Jan Smut's words, it was humanity's failure."

Thoughtful men everywhere are concerned about the national destiny of the United States. What this country is to be in the world of nations to-day is a very practical question of American ideals and of the patriotism which springs from these ideals. The patriotism that will work out American ideals is a problem that is insistent in American citizenship. 

The power in these ideals is the story of the experiment of democracy in the United States, and the immediate future of this country is to be seen in the decisions which are made by the American people in the working out of the democratic experiment of self-government. 17

The twentieth century is to see the adventure of self-government made by all peoples in their domestic affairs, and there is increasing evidence that the democratic experiment of control by the people will be applied to international relations. In the United 

States it is certain that unless the whole energy of national life is directed to the working out of the experiment of self-government that there can be no future for liberty in this republic. There is as much danger for the United States in an intolerant, ignorant and uncritical democracy as there is in a dictatorship of a Bolshevist rule as in Russia or in the meglomania of a Mussolini. The United States can become degraded from its own failure to work out the ideals of freedom for which it professes devotion. 

But this devotion needs to be given intelligently to the institutions of freedom, which in our nation are preeminently the home, the Church, the school, the community, and the press; for it is in the institutions we create that we can find whether we are a free people or whether we are servile nationals of a complacent plutocracy. To be smug about the United States is now to betray her needs. 

It will be an untold tragedy if the decisions of the American people are based upon the caution and compromise of vested interest in religion, in education, in foundation-philanthropy, and in industry; the decisions of the American people can express their determination to work out in this nation a better order of society than the world has yet in its blindness and stupidity achieved.

At this time in the history of the United States we should reflect upon the fact that the greatness of a nation like that of an individual is established upon affirmations, not upon negations. The character of a 18

nation is in what the people of the nation want their country to be. The spirit of national decisions must be clear, and they must indicate that men and women are willing to believe to the uttermost in the validity of the democratic ideal of life. A noble patriotism in this century cannot be narrow in its outlook, nor allow a vicious class consciousness, a boastful deceit of prosperity, or a social snobbery to dictate American standards. If these low standards dominate, it will prove that we lack an understanding of what the United States can be if this nation continues in its determination to define in freedom the meaning of citizenship in modern democracy.

The duty of imagination about the future of the United States is a first necessity now of leadership in the nation. There is no limit to our greatness that can now be seen if social and economic freedom have fresh interpretation; nor can there be any recognition of the claim of one generation to bequeath to those who come after them the terrible penalties of befuddling prejudices. The limitations of one generation in establishing freedom need not be obstacles to the future pioneers of better government and juster society, if this period in American civilization will intelligently work out the duties of citizenship. Honesty and courage by citizens to-day will make it possible for the citizens of to-morrow to be braver and saner citizens in the American nation and in the world of nations.

The destiny of the United States is in her citizens and their patriotism. The patriotism of American men and women can be a living faith which transfuses and transforms all of American life. The quality of patriotism is of prime concern. American education, the battlefield of democracy, can dedicate 19

its energies to the working out of a system of training for its future citizens which can best fit the mind of this nation to study its complex tasks. American industry can put human values first, ceasing to utter that monstrous falsehood in work and profits which attempts to give a spiritual significance to the worst acquisitive passions in present day industrial society. 

The Church, in all its widespread communion, can teach that men are brothers, and when this lesson is applied in citizenship the whole community will reach new and more daring levels of social coöperation. 

The future of America is with a patriotism that sees the duty of making plain and concrete democratic ideals in national life. And this duty is heaviest upon men and women who possess in any measure leadership, whether it is in the school and university, the pulpit, the bank, the farm and the store, or in that influence which radiantly goes out from the lives of courageous saints. These years that men call years of peace challenge all citizens—all patriots of peace-to make this nation a power for sanity and goodwill both at home and abroad, and so interpret the democratic life within our borders that the world may see a people who have at last won their freedom by putting their faith in democratic government into practice. If the United States fulfills the promise of democracy in this century it will be because the mind of many patriots turn eagerly to working out in tolerance, in sympathy, in common sense, and in imagination the problems of American life. 

The great adventure in self-government under conditions of social and religious freedom which has been the history of the free peoples of the United States, is beyond all doubt the most tremendous ex- 20

periment in all times of men and women to establish liberty. The history of the nation is the record of the ideals of our home life, our school life, our commercial life, and all the forces of character and education and business which have developed thirteen original colonies into the mightiest political and industrial power on earth. But the greatness of America has always been in the ideals of real and substantial liberty which the citizens of the United States have put into practice, and the allegiance of Americans to these ideals is what is best defined as a love of country. This love of country and the ideals which are its life and greatness is patriotism. As men in a larger way understand the meaning of their nation's love of liberty they become finer patriots, and they are better able to serve the aims of citizenship. They live to make a greater America.

The patriots of the past whom we honor were men who lived in their times in courage and in honesty. 

We best give homage to their memory by having in our own times the same high qualities of adventurous citizenship which they possessed. Americans to-day can prove their respect for the founders of their nation, and the builders of its political and economic and social freedom, by using every means at their command to make America in the twentieth century the nation in which every man and woman and every boy and girl can develop their life to the fullest possibility. This is not a platitude of patriotism, at least it is not to those who have caught the meaning of a nation in world history which is founded in its greatness on the freedom of all its citizens in all their capacities as individuals. Citizenship to others does not mean this when their views of men are determined by 21

what they can profit from them either as workers or in the exploitation of their prejudices. 

The test of patriotism to-day is really in the way we look at the value of other people and other nations in the experiment of this country, and of all other countries, in learning to govern through self-government. 

This is the test of a patriotism which is not a weak sentimentality nor a shallow cosmopolitanism; for it is a patriotism that sees citizenship in terms of the village and the farm as well as of Washington and Geneva. Above all it sees ideals of nationhood as they are worked out in the business of living. It means for those who accept this view, that our country's life is a never-ending experiment, and that the great adventure of democracy which began in this country inspires our daily work and determines what we will do for the making of a greater America. 

Patriotism then becomes not only a respect for past greatness, which is beyond our praise, but patriotism thus defined is a living service of devotion-wise and courageous-to all that is good and noble in the national community of 120,000,000 people which we call the United States. This is a patriotism which is as sane as the daily needs of these millions of people, and it is a conception of citizenship which never forgets that men build their cities not only with their hands but by their dreams. It is a dynamic power then which makes citizenship less a duty and more a privilege of coöperation; for patriotism in its fine imagination reveals the intent of social ideals. 

What an adventure this really is for patriotic

Americans! It means we are making a greater 

America as we establish a larger security for the worker on the farm and the laborer in the factory; 22

it means a saner America when social hatred and narrow nationalism give way to friendship and cooperation; and, when patriotism is thus a living dedication of our best in citizenship, as we go about the daily duties we are helping to create the conditions of national greatness which will most surely make a greater America. The end of America as an experiment is not yet, for America is in 1930 in the making. Her citizens in their work, in recreation, in all their relationships, are proving-if it is to be done in any way at all-that they understand the meaning of American ideals.

America is yet in the making. It is in the patriotism of her citizens that her destiny as an experiment in democracy is to be proved, both in what freedom means to her own citizens and what America is as a world power.

The living faith of patriotism, if shared by enough people, can make sure the destiny of freedom in the United States; but it can be nothing less than a civic faith as wide as every aspiration of freedom in this land, of race, of creed, of worker, of student, and of scientist.

The fact that America is yet in the making is one of the great hopes of liberty in our times, for it can mean that all the energy of America can be joined with the work of men everywhere who are in earnest in the determination to broaden the basis of freedom in the world. Freedom has to be gained afresh by each generation, and they who fight for it alone know what its keenest joys are; and they who battle for freedom are convinced it is the noblest strategy that man can use to win an inheritance for himself and for others. 

There is to-day the one unending battle of all men of good will. It is that this generation shall have a chance to work out its destiny freed from the in- 23

tolerable curse of the war-system, which has defied all peoples in their progress up to the present time. 

Men before have said that there can be no freedom worth having if war was not abolished; but now since 

1914-1918 and the peace years which have followed, men know there can be no life worth living if war is not ended.

In this struggle for the freedom of democratic hopes and democratic governments from the warsystem, how proud and how necessary a place the

United States can take, for this nation has accepted a duty of leadership in the most definite assault ever made against the intrenched autocracy of man's subtlest and most brutal enemy-war. War is this enemy, because war is the deification of force, which spares no thing of life or spirit in this world. 

It was a noble utterance when President Alderman said of Woodrow Wilson that he "had the heart to match the moral hopes of mankind against their passions," but how great will be that nation which will never fail in its patriotism to vindicate and to make valid the moral hopes of mankind against their passions. This American leadership can do in the twentieth century. 24 74
