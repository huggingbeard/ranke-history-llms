ON JANUARY 7, 1594, Richard Jones entered at Stationers'

Hall "for his Copie by warrant vnder the hande of Master warden Woodcock The Arbour of Amorus delightes. by N. B. 

gent." No book with such a date and name is known to exist, but another edition, with a slightly altered title, The 

Arbor of amorous Deuises (1597),' is preserved in two copies.

One of these, very badly mutilated, belonged to Edward Capell, who bequeathed it to Trinity College, Cambridge. 

It was reprinted by A. B. Grosart, in the first volume of The 

Works in Verse and Prose of Nicholas Breton (Edinburgh, 1875-1879), with the gaps carefully indicated: the title-page (A1) and A4, D3, D4, E2-E4 (that is, fourteen pages in all) are missing, and eight other pages (A3, D1, D2, E1) are mutilated. Grosart noted that a second copy was sold in 

Topham Beauclerk's sale in 1781,2 adding that it seems to have "disappeared utterly." Instead, however, the book made its way into the Britwell Library, whence it passed in 1919 to that of Mr. Henry E. Huntington.3

The Arbor, wherein -so runs the pleasant title-page young gentlemen may read many pleasant fancies and fine devices, and thereon meditate divers sweet conceits to court the love of fair ladies and gentlewomen, contains forty-four poems, the last ten of which Jones had already included in a companion miscellany, Brittons Bowre of Delights (1591).

They seem a bit old-fashioned for the year 1597, and none except No. 13 and No. 26 has any particular literary merit. 

Furthermore, in its metrical and stanzaic forms The Arbor has a more antique air than The Bower. The word sonnet is applied only to Nos. 26 and 38, though three Shakespearean sonnets are included, two of them (Nos. 35, 40) regular, the other (No. 29) with the surprizing rime-scheme abab cdcd befe gg. Few novelties occur in other stanzaic forms, the favorite being the six-line iambic pentameter stanza, which occurs with the rime ababcc eleven times (Nos. 4, 6, 8, 11, 20, 21, 23, 30, 31, 34, 38). A similar stanza of six iambic tetrameter lines is used in No. 13 (ababcc) and No. 41 (aaaabb). 

Iambic tetrameter quatrains (Nos. 7, 39) and iambic pentameter quatrains (Nos. 3, 24) likewise appear. Acrostics determine the length of several poems, as Nos. 18 and 19 in eight lines, Nos. 14-16 in twelve, No. 17 in sixteen. The most common meters used are, apart from iambic pentameter, septenary couplets in three poems (Nos. 2, 9 [slightly irregular], 33) and poulter's measure in eight (Nos. 1, 5, 22, 25, 27, 32, 42, 44).

The book has some interesting lexicographical material. 

Its use of bashless, immodest (A3), is the latter of two cases I use this convenient abbreviation for Brittons Bowre of Delights (1591, 1597).

There can be no doubt that The Arbor was published in 1594 shortly after its registration on January 7, and that the two extant copies dated 1597 belong to a reprint presumably a second edition. Internal evidence shows that the preface was written towards the end of 1593 or at the beginning of 1594. There Richard Jones speaks of the excellence of his book, but apologizes, “had not the Phenix preuented me of some the best stuffe she furnisht her nest with of late: this Arbor had bin somewhat the more handsomer trimmed vp, beside a larger scope for gentlemen to recreate them selues." 

John Jackson secured a license on October 8, 1593, for R. S.'s 

The Phoenix Nest, which he published shortly thereafter.

Again, the preface informs the "gentlemen readers" that their absence from London "this long time of vacation hindered my poore Presse from publishing any pleasing Pamphlet, to recreate your minds," prays "unto God to keepe you all in health," and adds that to such gentlemen as have not yet come back to the city may God send "a happy and speedy returne to London," thus bringing comfort to all poore men of Trades." The foregoing comments apply definitely to the years 1593-1594, not to 1597. For the "long time of vacation" was caused by the ravages of the plague in 1593. By royal proclamation Trinity Term had been adjourned on May 28, 1593, because of the plague; another proclamation of September 24 transferred Michaelmas Term to St. Albans, and forbade the attendance at the court of persons exposed to infection unless they were personally ordered to come. 

Thereupon, Jones published "a sorowfull songe of Londons lamentacon for the losse of the terme &c.," and rival publishers, Thomas Creede and John Danter, sponsored ballads called "the Cun treymans sorrowe to see the tearme kepte at Sainct Albons" and "The cuntrymens Report of the vsage of them at Sainct Albons Terme." But by November 22 the pestilence had sufficiently abated for the queen to command that Hilary Term be kept, not at St. Albans, but at 

Westminster. Apparently Jones wrote his preface between 

November 22 and January 7 (when the book was licensed).

xiii The preface, then, indicates that The Arbor was actually issued in 1594, and this conclusion is enforced by a study of the mechanical make-up of the 1597 edition. The innumerable misprints are in themselves much more characteristic of a reprint than of a first edition. The types are from the same fonts as those used in the 1597 edition of The Bower. Both 

The Arbor and The Bower of 1597 are printed in worn roman and italic types; but the first edition of The Bower was in black letter and so, it seems reasonable to guess, was The 

Arbor of 1594.

Still more significant testimony is offered by the signaturemarks of The Arbor. The text of its poems begins on A3, with the title-page and preface on A1 and A2, the catchword on A2” (“A LO-”) pointing directly to the title of No. 1. It is, accordingly, plain that the title-page and the preface were set up first, and hence that the compositor had the entire "copy" before him when he began his work. The "copy" in this instance must have been the printed pages of the lost first edition of 1594. If the 1597 copy had itself been a first edition, almost certainly the text would have been set up before the preliminary pages. The 1591 Bower, for example, runs [a1, a2], A-G4, while the 1597 Bower begins its title-page as A1 and proceeds straight through F4 without a break, the text of its poems beginning on A3. The 

1594 Arbor no doubt had signatures marked as in the 1591 Bower. I suspect that The Arbor was reprinted in 1597 before The Bower, or, in other words, that it attempts to be an exact reprint of its first edition, whereas The Bower of 1597 is only a partial reprint of the 1591 text.

Perhaps Jones is glancing obliquely at Breton when he begs his gentlemen readers not only to buy and appreciate the book but also to protect him against "cauilling Finde faults, that neuer like of any thing, but what they doe themselues, and that, for the most part, is nothing at all."
