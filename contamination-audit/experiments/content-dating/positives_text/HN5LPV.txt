Let us et us go back down a vista of years and enter the side gate of a rambling Georgian Mansion, passing into decay, half-hidden in a luxuriant growth of Microphylla roses on a street now almost unknown. Up the brick pathway leading through clumps of day-lilies, calycanthus shrubs, poppies, larkspur and violets; under the catalpas, mimosas, and crepe-myrtles of various hues, to the white locust tree, drooping over a hydrant, endlessly pouring a refreshing stream for the benefit of the saucy sparrows and blue-birds.

Facing the hydrant, a low brick building containing [1]

a kitchen, laundry, and rooms for the use of the servants, at a short distance from the house was so placed that no odors of cooking penetrate stately rooms and halls. Meals were carried back and forth on great trays by those children of the sun, who spent their leisure hours basking in the warmth and light, singing, playing the banjo or "gittar," and gossiping jovially, for those who served the "quality" were a happy lot-and, though more than three decades had gone by since the Civil War, still clung to the old ways and cared little for freedom. As children we adored them and how they loved us, and excused our [2]

pecadilloes, though any brown-skinned imp who defied authority was instantly disciplined with a peach tree switch. 

Our widowed mother lived with her little brood of five children in the old house, surrounded by her retainers, who loved the ways to which they were accustomed and almost worshipped the Mistress of whom Bet, the cook, a person of iron determination, was heard to remark, she "warn't gwine trade Miss Gaybrulla fer freedom nor nuffin else."

Bet's rule in the kitchen was so autocratic that Mother, [3]

who was "Mis' Gaybrulla," had dismissed her on two occasions to be met with the statement: "Leavin' my Madam ain' in de leas' wise in my thoughts, I ain' never thought you gwine give me 'way Mis' Gaybrulla, an' I goin' set right here." And from that day forth, Bet was one of us "for better, for worse,” in faithful doglike devotion until her good old eyes closed forever on the "white folks" she loved.

Hearing her describe this episode, Stephano, the man who came in from the outside to do chores, said to me sagely: "Lil' Miss, if a nigger like he boss, yo cyarn drive [4]

him 'way wid a axe." "Sis" Bradley was undoubtedly a power among her race -she was a "Moderator" in Ebenezer Church, and a social arbiter of the kitchen. Once she wandered off to Saratoga, but found the climate too severe, as the children of the sun cannot stand a long cold Winter season, and she became homesick for "Faginny." She liked to refer to her travels, however, and took great pride in her one trip, after returning to her seat in the kitchen, where she made a pleasant picture near the glowing stove. [5]

She was large and well rounded, with a most expressive lower lip, protruding when she was at peace with the world, but which was completely drawn in when any thing annoyed her; this sign was respected by everyone-making words unnecessary and undiplomatic.

Looking back, one might see her sitting in state on a hickory chair, with a large cushion for her portly frame; the glow from the fire in the brick oven lighting up her white head-handkerchief, and apron. Beside her a cat, black as the old kettle and called by the name of "Miss 

Potts." "Sis" Bradley she was called by the servants and [6]

"Cook" by the children. What bread she made! And a treat it was to see her turn out waffles with a long-handled waffle iron, as she always scorned modern inventions. 

These delicacies were only produced on Sundays and birthdays.

Birthdays were quite notable for Mammy's appearance with a bottle of "Pah-goric" and some anatomical massage, punctuated with sage remarks. "Gawd knows, Mammy dun tole you, quit eatin' dem waffles, like yo' legs is holler. 

Yore Maw dun say hit seem like yo' ain' never got 'nuff no way; But you-all, chilluns, ain' nevah heah nuffin', an' [7]

now, see what yo' dun!-dun run to dis heah kitchin an git bits fum Mosby's tray an'et twel' yo' plum ready to bus'-an' yo' ain' gwine have no spot fo' yo' ice cream dis evenin'-" But we were healthy young animals and by evening the dissipations of the morning were obliterated in full time for the "party" which would be considered prehistoric by the blasé infants of the twentieth century. Our refreshments were home-made ice cream and Sis Bradley's sponge cake; our guests a few cousins, the two children of the "Mantua-maker," of whom we were found, and Mother [8]

always included as well, the small darkies related to Mammy and Cook with whom we played in the garden. 

We had the most delightful games: "Fly away Jack, Fly away Jill," "Chum, Chum a loo," "Here we go round the mulberry bush" and one we sang in shrill piping voices, "I come to cote Miss Ginger Jones."

This revelry was broken up at twilight by Mammy, who rose with great dignity and called vigorously: "You little niggers, git 'long wid you! Dese heah chillun prop-pin dere eyelids up wid straw-Cum on, les we go, Sandman gittin' bout." [9]

I was glad to be so old-twelve grand rounded years were mine and another hour was left for my enjoyment before bed-time.

When the fire-flies rose through the darkening grass like a flock of stars, I ran quickly around to the front door of the house where the Microphylla rose leaned over and touched the windows with waxen blooms, tapping ever so softly, and was replete with meaning to me. The house was haunted. Mother never allowed this to be mentioned, lest it frighten the younger children, but I was so old that I knew the secret. The fair Marie-Louise, whose glitter- [10]

ing golden beads I wore on great occasions, as her namesake and descendant, passed through that door at twilight, clad in filmy gray draperies, her bright curls held in place with a tortoise shell comb. A gentle presence was the "little gray lady" and many an evening I stationed myself by the door hoping she would pass by as the darkness came.

Surreptitiously, I spoke to Mosby, who turned and scowled at me. "Yore Maw," he said severely, "dun say you aint to say nuffin' 'bout her. Dat hant ain' nebber gwine trouble [11]

you, nor nobody else, sence you Maw done compliment her by namin' yo' arter her.

I ain' sayin' nuffin' now, but nex' time yo' says hant-I gwine tell Mis' Gaybrulla, sho."

Mosby was a person of great importance, the blood of an African King was said to be in his veins and I can well believe it. He had grown old in the service of the family and had acquired most elegant manners and was quite a social register.

He looked after the marketing, kept the silver immaculate, served the table and was custodian of the wine-cellar. [12]

Even the famous Club Chef could not brew a finer julep or one more calculated to stimulate the wits, and many were his opportunities; as, like all Virginians of that day, we had a constant influx of company. "Kin" from the James River Plantations and surrounding counties "dropped in" and remained as long as it pleased them-to consult a physician, a Mantua-maker, to purchase supplies, or simply for diversion. 

Mosby's gifts were in constant demand for male relatives, while light wines glittered in Bohemian bottles for the ladies. [13]

In the afternoon, Mosby appeared in livery, donned his hat with the cockade and drove his Mistress “Out" what is now Franklin Street, then almost the country. Serenely, behind the bay mare, in her little Victoria sat "Miss Gaybrulla," the veil of her widow's bonnet never doffed, floating around her Madonna-like face, framed in white folds tied in a decorous bow under her rounded, still youthful chin, for her type ages slowly. She was a fair and gracious picture and many an admirer would fain have seen her lay aside the veil, but this had probably never entered her placid mind. [15]

"Mis' Gaybrulla jest fo' all de world like one o' dem white roses," Mammy said, and as I look back, a sudden fragrance as of an old garden haunts me, and I see her once more. Holding the Good Book between slim white hands, her head under the illusion cap bent in reverence, she held her morning service with the assembled household kneeling around her, the servants a dark background for the group of fair haired Anglo-Saxon children. 

This ceremony over, a little leather-covered key-basket on her arm, she led the way to the storeroom, followed by a procession of servants and children. Cook bore the flour [ 16 ]

bucket ready for the "giving out" of many quarts to supply our hungry mouths. It was a daily joy to help, in our hindering way, this important business-flour, meal, butter, lard, sugar, bacon, sausage; rations for the day-even the "med'cine chist" stowed away in a corner was called on, for in so numerous a household some one always enjoyed poor health.

Cook was then ready to begin operations for that function, the mid-day dinner, or rather early afternoon, what is now the lunch hour, while Mammy appeared to prepare Bobby and me for the small and select primary school [17]

Miss Sue's Lover had lost his life in the war, so the legend ran, a saving grace to her and others of her kind, excusing their single states and, as one of our jolly cousins remarked, was a reason the war was called "Civil," in deference to many ladies, allowing them to go through life with a halo of interest and romance. 

In the afternoon, dinner over, Mammy scrubbed our faces until they shone, brushed curls in careful rows on feminine heads, "roached" up Bobby's, flattened out Peter's "cow-lick," and we started down the street to play in [19]

"Capitlum Square," as Amelia called it. This was a dear delight-there were then, as now, hosts of little gray squirrels waiting to be fed, and the pleasant company of friends.

Many people passed through the Square-sometimes even so great a man as the Governor spoke to us and sent a message to our Mother. Often a very beautiful "Old, old man," as we thought him then, came and called us "Little birds hopping from brahnch to brahnch," speaking in a strong foreign accent. He would pat our heads and tell us to be sure to be at Sunday School. This was Dr. Min- [20]

negerode, Rector of St. Paul's-the Church Mother attended. Once or twice he took me to ride behind his old white horse, "To travel a little," he said.

It may be in order here to say a few words about Dr. 

Minnegerode, known as the "War Rector" of St. Paul's, Richmond. He was exiled from Germany for political disturbances in his excitable youth, and fate sent him to Williamsburg, the ancient Capital of Virginia as a Professor. He studied for the Ministry and for many years held the charge of St. Paul's.

When Mammy thought we had played enough, she gathered us together with admonitions as to behavior, and we walked up the street by the beautiful house which stood under the sycamore tree where Uncle Cephas, a friend of hers, sunned himself daily on the ledge of the wrought-iron rail. [23]

"M-m-m," Uncle Cephas would say, "ain' dem chillen grow a passel!" "Yas, Lawd,” Mammy would reply, "an' how yo' feel you'self dis maun, Brer Cephas?" "Poly, poly, Sis Patsy, I got a sort o' misery on my chist-like, an' my ole 'oman, she gin me some mutton suet an' karysene." "M-m-m," said Mammy.

The Africans all knew how to keep their children in order and the effect of the gutteral "M-m-m,” a sort of grunt, can scarcely be described, but its various shades and inflections are as expressive as the French shrug.

We looked on Mammy as most beautiful. She was a light gingerbread color, her soft eyes showing gold flecks · in the sunlight and her temples were slightly gray. She [ 27 ]

was quite short, neither stout nor thin, just a comfortable roundness of person.

In memory I see her still, sitting in the dim nursery, her restless charges quiet, with folded arms, dozing, head low on her bosom and kind old eyes closed. Faithful, loving soul, watching far into the night lest "Mammy Lil' lambs” wake suddenly.

She and the Cook held the young negroes in stern contempt and were most severe with Lily-Lou, a new housemaid whom Mother was trying to train and who constantly failed to appear for her duties, as she lived on a distant [28]

hill. "Mis' Gaybrulla,” Mammy said indignantly one morning, “dat bright skin nigger whar slight yo' work, she dun et de scum offn' Cook's preserve, an tooken sick, an' dun sent wud she ain' able to cum to-day-M-m-m,” and Mammy, bristling with anger led in a regular Senegambian with an engaging smile, and two little piccanninnies clinging to her skirt, sent as a substitute. "Lilly-Lou" volunteered the Senegambian, "she reckon she be b'out to-morrow-she say I kin dust room fo' you." 

But Mis' Gaybrulla decided to let the Senegambian go, [29]

and gave her food for Lily-Lou and some cookies to the piccaninnies.

Stephano, weeding the brick walk, shook his head in melancholy fashion whispering in a dull bass rumble, the gist of which my sharp ears caught, to Mammy: "Miughty bad company Lily-Lou keep. Dat ash-heap nigger ain' lawful married an' she ain' got no call sendin' sich to Mis' Gaybrulla." Mammy immediately conveyed this information and to Mis' Gaybrulla's questioning the easy-going Senagambian replied, with a joyful smile, flashing her white teeth and gold earrings: [30]

"Naw'm, Mis' Gaybrulla, I haint got no idea o' gittin' mar'd twel dese here chillen growed up an' out o' my way." "M-m-m," groaned Mammy, “ain' dese stories sumpin' scanalous-Times fo' Mammy tuh be gwine fum heahshe gittin' like de ole cow's tail, goin' down."

Gossip waxed warm in the kitchen from Stephano's news, though we were sent to play out of doors during the discussion.

Stephano lived on the same hill with Lily-Lou and how he ever made the distance between our house and [31]

Shockoe must remain a mystery; for his garments, too large for his lanky frame, seemed always about to fall off entirely, only one suspender button being visible, large safety pins holding him in vicarious security elsewhere.

In all the years he worked for us I never saw him wear another garb and he never seemed uneasy about his apparently careless raiment. 

Another odd thing about him was the colored folks addressed him as Sherman, while to his employers he was known as Stephano-though it is often we find the negroes use several names, making it quite confusing to [32]

strangers. Another custom was that of addressing Mother as "Mis' Gaybrulla"-the old servants with pride retained the youthful appellation until the end of their lives—it showed their connection with the quality. Sometimes very amusing things happened, for the children of the family often fell into the same habit. "Yonder Mr. Arthur and Miss Janet," said a small cousin of his approaching parents, to the great merriment of a Northern visitor.

We had one other young negro in the household group -Mosby's nephew, Jim, then being trained under the [ 33 ]

Argus eye of that individual who was becoming rheumatic and in need of an active assistant. 

Jim was a product of the Patriarchal system, with a flavoring of new ideas-a mixture of good nature, capability and laziness. If there was anything on earth that 

Jim could not do, I never saw it. Broken toys he put together like magic, he waxed floors, rubbed furniture, touched up paint, and did odd bits of tinkering around the house—the silver shone beneath his deft fingers, while he told wonderful tales, showed us how to "pat Juba," clog dance, and when his work was done, picked his banjo [34]

and played a mouth harp at the same time.

While the old servants chided him, they liked him. He would never settle down to any special place, seeming to have a gypsy streak, but our kitchen pleased him. Cook was agreeable and he dined there so constantly, that 

Mother said one day: "Why Jim, I thought you were working for Mrs. Brown at present," naming a newcomer to the more modern portion of the town. "Yas'm, Miss Gaybrulla, I is workin' fo' Mis' Brown." "Then, Jim, how is it that you dine here every day?" [35]

"Law Mis' Gaybrulla, ain' you know. Mis' Brown she ain' got no kitchin-whar she live dey ain' no kitchin, nuffin' but fancy dabs o' vittles."

Jim and Lily-Lou were young, and, wearying of old ways, went "North," while changes forced their way into the house-Bobby, in the dignity of long trousers, was sent to the Episcopal High School in Alexandria. My curls were braided and I was initiated into classes of French Conversation and Music, leaving only Amelia, Peter and Evelyn still playing around the hydrant under the locust tree. 361

Mammy, Mosby and Bet were growing perceptibly older, though Mis' Gaybrulla changed little, but their kindly relationship was unimpaired, and the old negroes sat as of old before the kitchen door in the soft evening atmosphere, fanning and talking over old times. 

All are gone now, these faithful friends from another land—children of a tropic sun, friends who really loved us, who knew our cares and joys, rejoiced and grieved with us, yet asked nothing for themselves but our affection-we who laid our secrets in their keeping, knowing there would be no betrayal, yet knew naught of theirs. [ 37 ]

If they grieved or troubled, we knew it not, only they gave us their devotion, content with a kindly word or gift. 

Mis' Gaybrulla's busy hands are folded now, and she lies in a quiet country graveyard on the home of her happy childhood-at her feet, the old Bet who asked to be laid near by. The sons of her white folks carried her coffin under the Altheas to the Mistress she loved so well. 

That, too, was many years ago, and only I, and the 

Georgian house remain. [38]
