INTRODUCTION 

HERE are few who have traveled 

THE 

along life's pathway for any considerable distance without realizing the roughness of the way. The remains of wrecks that litter the road are proof that all has not been smooth traveling for those who have gone before. Sooner or later we all come to experience the storms and tempests, the parching heats, the pitfalls and the varied dangers that beset that highway over which the multitude must pass. 

Would it not be a blessing for those who are not strong enough to weather its storms and tempests, or who fear to encounter its dangers, to find some sheltered bypaths in which they would not only have protection from storms and dangers, but escape from the strife of tongues and the jostling of the ix

crowd? The object of this little work is to suggest such quiet trails. The traveler who enters them will be able to go serenely onward, finding relief from noise, and feverish strife, escaping the gossip of idle tongues, but especially the aftermath of having drunk too deeply in the poisoned fountains of the world's great highway.

These sequestered pathways eventually enable the traveler to attain to Heights Serene from which he can view the summits where all journeys end, and where the traveler lays down all his burdens because he is safely home.

SILENCE 

TH HERE are few people who enjoy absolute silence. The majority of human beings experience a sensation of uneasiness, such as one feels in the presence of the eerie or the mysterious, when they find themselves in solitude and silence. Those that have not tasted the divine beauty of things imagine that a certain amount of noise and excitement is necessary to their comfort. And yet, silence is to the soul what sleep is to the body-both nourishment and refreshment. It commends itself to those who appreciate a beautiful, and at the same time, a useful thing. "The gods speak in silences," said the ancients; and 

Holy Scripture tells us that God does not speak in the tumult, that there is no divine 1

whisper in the tempest, but only in the gentle breeze. Soothing and healing come to the storm-tried soul when a garment of silence is thrown around her. Calm, serenity, peace, steal in where silence reigns. 

It is to be noted that the wiser, the holier people grow, the more silent they become. Knowledge, and the experience of life are great promoters of silence. As our younger conceits leave us, we learn to weigh our words. The more we learn the less sure of ourselves do we become; we begin to listen more and speak less. We experience, too, that pople are not so keen to listen to us as we thought; that it was often courtesy, rather than interest, that made them listen at all. The Arabians say that wisdom is made up of ten parts, nine of which is silence, and the tenth brevity of speech. We know that all Saints were lovers of silence. We cannot imagine a garrulous Saint. We drown thought by the noise of words. "Be silent, be quiet," St. Arsenius heard

Men's appreciation of silence is found in the popular sayings of every tongue. We all recognize the wisdom of the thought that "Silence is deep as eternity, speech as shallow as time"; or, "The shallows murmur, but the deeps are dumb"; or again, "Mill wheels run fast when there is no corn in the hoppers." We ourselves have experienced that, in general, the less people have to say the more they talk. Great men, as a rule, are not great talkers. More than half their inspir-

ing influence is produced, not by what they say, but by what they radiate. 

Who ever had such sublime things to say as Our Blessed Lord! "Never man spake like this man." Yet how little He said compared to some of the great orators of Greece or Rome, for instance. And what He did say, how briefly He said it! "Follow Me"; "Come and see"; "Go, and sin no more"; "Thou hast said it." We read in the Gospel of Him, "And Jesus held His peace"; "But He answered them to never a word." Passion or agitated emotions cause men and women to lose their sense of the proportion of things; argument is useless at such times, and silence commends itself. 

It is astounding the trifles that cause men sometimes to lose their logic. The mania of having the last word causes more mischief than storms and tempests. It is a great art to know how to sacrifice the present advantage for the future gain, as it is to know exactly when to strike, neither being before nor after the hour.

SELF-POSSESSION 

THAT calm control of one's powers, that poise and serene state of mind that belongs to the finished character, and which is one of the beautiful gems of culture and refinement is the fruit of a conquest of self that has taken long and earnest effort. Those who have acquired it know how to adapt themselves to all classes of people, and are, in return, reverenced and respected, not only on account of their spiritual strength, their tranquility of heart, their sweet-tempered, well-balanced disposition, but because people feel that they can rely upon them. How rare they are, however, those that possess that exquisite poise that is the jewel of wisdom and holiness! When we think of it, we 13

imagine seeing our Lord as He walked among men here below.

We have within ourselves a kind of dual personality, which we call our subjective and our objective ego. The first is the seat of our sensibilities, our images, our impulses. It acts through our emotions, our imagination and our nervous states. 

The second concentrates, judges, decides and acts. Normally our objective should govern our subjective self; and when it does so, as a rule, we are said to be self-controlled, or self-possessed.

To attain to this desirable state we must undertake to control our emotional states, our imagination, our nerves, and our unruly impulses. Every time we are under the influence of an intense emotion, we lose our mental equilibrium until calm is restored. Words, actions and decisions that are dominated by emotion usually become subject matter for regret or vexation. Our refuge, when thus moved, is silence and abstention from everything that would in any

SELF-POSSESSION 

15 way accentuate the intensity of the emotion that sways us. Silence, and as much exterior calm as we can muster, are the great means of appeasing our emotional storms, and curbing our impulses. Silence here has a twofold result: it lessens the heat of the emotion and gives time for reflection. 

Every time we secure a victory over self through silence, we should use it for autosuggestion by repeating to ourselves several times in the day such expressions as the following: "Here is a victory I have secured over my impulsiveness. I begin to be master of myself. I am making daily progress. 

I can use my will now with energy, etc."

Auto-suggestion is a very powerful means of self-improvement, but is too rarely used because too little known. Works that treat of it have often rationalistic tendencies that are dangerous to Faith. But when autosuggestion leans on the truths of Faith, it participates in its solidity and communicates its strength to the will. In fact autosuggestion is a real tonic to the will, and

those who make proper use of it will soon notice excellent results.

A further degree of self-control will be achieved when we have acquired the habit of reasoning with ourselves and of making our decisions, our determinations, only after serious reflection. By analyzing the events of our daily life in their objective reality we obtain a sane interpretation of the difficulties of life and escape the possibility of being the plaything of impulse, caprice and passion.

It is of prime importance to serenity of mind to form for ourselves what is known as a philosophical outlook on life. This consists in taking the habit of viewing the events of life from a broad, calm and courageous point of view, which will enable us to accept the course of human vicissitudes, and our own painful experiences, in a serene and unruffled manner, placing thought always above feeling, and the general good above the narrow and selfish. 

We should persuade ourselves that most

17 of the difficulties we encounter in dealing with others come from the imperfections of human nature, which should call forth our pity rather than our blame. We should remember that the suffering man inflicts upon man is but the manifestation of a blind and inordinate selfishness, the result of poverty of judgment and defective qualities of the heart. With the exception of those rare individuals of perfect moral equilibrium or consummate virtue, the majority of human beings have no other law than the satisfaction of purely selfish pursuits and inclinations. Their tendencies, whether conscious or unconscious, are ever towards selfish satisfactions. 

To avoid disappointment, then, we must not expect anyone to be either useful or agreeable to us unless he finds some useful or pleasant advantage in being so. The best thing to do is to take the selfishness of others into account and find out how we can harmonize it with our own; to study our environment and adapt ourselves to it

as best we can. Struggle there will always be, for it is the condition of our existence. 

But "The Kingdom of Heaven suffereth violence, and the violent bear it away."

The territory of our struggle is both within and without ourselves. That within comes under the action of the will, and our victory there is all-important; for once we have made ourselves master of our own house, the external strife grows easier. 

Many outside forces attack us merely because they find us weak and undisciplined within. We cannot always change our environment, but we can by attention, observation and reflection, study and understand the laws that govern it. A clear knowledge of the causes that produce the effects with which we have daily to contend adds an interest to life, gives it an attraction, enriches thought, and banishes monotony.

We live among people whose ways of acting call forth reactions on our part that are anything but agreeable. Our confidence

19 has been betrayed; we have been slandered, scorned, neglected; we have been made the object of base jealousy; our best intentions have been misinterpreted; we lose heart; all our peace of mind, our serenity of soul, has been taken from us. We should have learned from them how little confidence we can place in men, how little we can expect from them, how much more vigilant and circumspect we should have been in dealing with them. One of the great sources of strength and serenity of soul is sincerity. It should be our aim to acquire as truthful an estimation of our personal worth as we possibly can. It is a grand and desirable thing to be able "to see ourselves as others see us," without letting the sight irritate us. It is a dishonest thing, as well as a waste of effort, to try to keep up appearances that fail to harmonize with reality. We should acknowledge our defects of character and the failures of which they are the cause. It is a transgression against truth and sincerity to try to explain away our faults, our weaknesses, by placing the blame for them on others, or on unfavorable circumstances, when we know that the cause of them is within us. When we have learned to look upon ourselves as we really are, and not as we should like to think we are, the words and gestures of those, who would satisfy

SELF-POSSESSION 

21 their vanity by criticizing us, will hurt us much less. All this does not, of course, mean that we should not aspire, and try very hard, to be better than we are. We must always be earnestly endeavoring to reach something more ideal, more perfect. 

But this must be done without any loss of serenity, or any impatience with our present low attainments.

If we wish to acquire the blessings of a serene mind and a tranquil heart, we must learn to bury our griefs in the depths of our soul, after consecrating them by uniting them with the sufferings of Christ. It is to Him we must tell our pains, for He alone is really interested. Men have always enough with their own sorrows, and they are few and far between who can give us any real help in our trials.

In the government of self we must not only legislate for our times of trials and hardships, but also for those of our enthusiasms and optimisms. In fact, we must foresee every occasion in which there is

danger of losing our heads. By looking too much through rose-colored glasses we risk seeing things in a false and unreal light.

We must beware of illusions. In weighing the chances of success we must never forget to leave room for the unforeseen, unfavorable circumstances that are always possible in human affairs. We must never take too much for granted, for a crushing defeat is always a moral danger to those who have not achieved a complete conquest of self.

A still higher degree of serenity is attained when we are able to maintain in all circumstances an imperturbable exterior calm. This calm surrounds us as a halo of strength. It prevents our energy from evaporating and gives us a powerful ascendancy on our environment. 

To acquire this desirable calm we should exercise ourselves, in trying situations, to conceal our interior agitation as much as possible. In time we shall be able to dominate all exterior manifestations of emo-

23 tion. We shall, however, as long as we are human, be more or less subject to the psychological influence of human events, sometimes so sudden and rude that the shock may upset us. We shall never succeed in being entirely impassible, nor is it desirable we should be, for it is proper at times to manifest our feelings under pain of passing for a heartless individual. There are times when the heart must speak, and be more eloquent in its own language than any noise of words. But this can always be done in full self-possession. We can read in the countenance of the person who lacks control everything that he is experiencing interiorly. The muscles of his face betray the lights and shadows within. His gestures, his movements of impatience, give added evidence of his enthusiasm or depression. He who is self-possessed maintains the same attitude in all seasons, somber or gay. He imposes on his countenance and on his exterior movements an immutable mask of gentle serenity.

Those who have practised this discipline of exterior calm know how far it goes to diminish interior trouble and agitation. It is really an exercise in auto-suggestion. 

The mind is greatly influenced by external deportment. Hence the importance of army discipline and bearing, as well as our attitudes and postures in prayer and worship. Those who express their mental anguish by tears, loud lamentations, and violent contortions soon reach the stage of paroxysm, while grief and anguish is soothed by quiet control.

The supernatural element has been only slightly touched in speaking of self-conquest as a means of securing serenity of soul. It would be unthinkable, however, for a Catholic to leave it out of any system of self-improvement; for there is nothing in the wide world so powerful an agent for self-discipline and control as the help that is afforded by the practice of religion and the exercises of the spiritual life. In fact, there is grave moral danger for a Christian

We might endeavor with all our will-power to keep the Commandments; we should never be able to keep them in their integrity without divine assistance, without divine Grace. Non-Catholics who deny the dogma of original sin give unlimited power to the human will.

But we, who are of the Faith, know how very weak the will is unassisted by grace; just as we know what marvels divine grace has wrought, and is every day working, in the wills of the Saints, those prodigies of will-power and divine grace.

Supernatural helps have no limit, and they never fail those who ask them by humble prayer. "Without Me," says Christ, "you can do nothing." That is, without the help of grace we can do nothing that will lead us to the Beatific Vision, the great ideal, the great destiny, for which we were made, and for which all self-improvement, all perfection, must prepare us. Effort is

the work of the will, but its success depends upon the Power upon whose good pleasure all events depend. Self-conquest is not only a means of success and happiness in this world, it is a magnificent virtue, the perfect model of which we adore in our Lord.

IN SPITE of man's professed relish for life in the raw to-day, in spite of his preference for the pleasures introduced by science and the mechanical arts, there is, at bottom of his being, a craving for the beauties of nature, for the chaste and wholesome sights and sounds of woodland, sea, and sky. His soul sickens sooner of every other beauty than that of nature. For nature speaks to every heart in language that it understands; so that the keenest joys of mind, and heart, and soul are possible to every child of God in presence of the beauties with which He has fitted out this waiting-room of ours, the earth. The beauties of nature, when we dispose ourselves in 29

the proper way to enjoy them, are a refreshment, a soothing, a healing to body and mind. They are also a source of inspiration that raises us above ourselves, ennobling our thoughts and giving us a power that we did not know was ours, to despise all that is petty and vulgar. We experience these sensations especially in springtime and summer, when the world is clothed in the splendor and youthfulness of May, or laden with the rich and colorful wealth of June.

Nature has a soul that she radiates and communicates to those who love her and are attentive to her movements. She speaks to us, in silences, of joy and sweetness, of love and peace; but also of sadness and mourning, of fear and death; in a word, of all human experiences. These moods of hers we reciprocate, especially when we receive them in solitude and silence. And with the poet we may repeat:

I made them shapers of my own moods, or wailful or divine, With them joyed or was bereaved. 

I was heavy with the even... 

I laughed with the morning's eyes. 

I triumphed and I saddened with all weather. 

Heaven and I wept together, And its sweet tears were salt with mortal mine. 

Against the throb of its sunset heart I laid my own to beat, And share communing heat. -Francis Thompson, "The Hound of Heaven."

Nature exhorts us also in figures and parables at times; at others she prays or sings with us. Dawn speaks of a glorious resurrection; morning of eternal youth; while evening and night remind us of how passing all earthy things are, that life is but a day in which we must work, because, as our Lord Himself warned us, it is followed by darkness in which no one can work.

Each season brings us its own lesson of life, transformation, death and resurrection.

All 

Nature sings to us and makes music to our souls by the murmur of brooks, rivers, and waterfalls; by the singing of birds, the lowing of distant herds, the sobbing of the winds, and the gentle rustle the breeze makes among the leaves; or, at times, by the rythmic falling of rain or snow. these sounds and motions touch the soul and raise it to the eternal source of beauty, the shadow of which we feel passing over oceans, plains and hills by the continual interplay of light and shadow. There is a prayer, too, that rises from the heart of nature to the throne of God. But this prayer is most real when man, moved by the beauty of creation, raises his heart in adoration, thanksgiving and praise to the

Author of all created blessings, and the 

Source of all harmony and beauty. 

There is something particularly majestic and sublime in the beauty of mountains.

They have silent messages for the early

riser, especially in summer, as he watches them gradually throw off the veil of mist, that hung round their shoulders, when the sun approaches to gild their summits with gold. But they are more entrancingly beautiful, clothed in the amber splendor of the sunset. 

Mountain scenery has something purer and more elevating than any other. It enkindles a deeper yearning for eternal beauty. We feel, somehow nearer Heaven when we have climbed the mountain heights, where the air is lighter, and we are above the region of tempests, and the noises of the plain.

Mountains are frequently mentioned in a poetical way in Holy Scripture, both in the Old and New Testaments. David sings in the Psalms: "I have lifted up my eyes to the mountains, from whence help shall come to me" (Ps. cxxiii). And Solomon, in the Canticle of Canticles, mentions "The Beloved" as "leaping upon the mountains, skipping over the hills." Again

he sings: “O my Beloved, be like to the roe and to the young hart upon the mountain of aromatic spices." The great events of 

Holy Scripture, both in the Old and New 

Law, were enacted on mountains. The

Commandments were given to Moses on a mountain; our Lord's greatest sermon was "The Sermon on the Mount"; Christ was transfigured on a mountain; He was crucified on a mountain; and from a mountain 

He ascended into Heaven. The solitude and silence of the mountain top is an excellent atmosphere for prayer.

Of the many haunts in which men seek to woo Nature there is none more frequented than the seashore. For one that loves mountain scenery there are a hundred who love the sea. The most unpoetic, unromantic, can spend hours gazing on the movements of the waves and the tide. And yet there are few scenes more monotonous than the open sea. For the senses, of course, there is the tang of the clean salt water, that bespeaks health and increased

vigor; there is the soothing rhythmic motions of lapping waves, or the ebb and flow of the tide, or there is the subtle odor of seaweed steeped in brine. For the more poetic, there is the play of light and shadow on the surface of the waves, or the dreams born of setting suns over western oceans, that raise the mind on the wings of fancy from the commonplace or the merely material. Or there is a sail borne onward by the breeze, and lost to sight on the far horizon, that awakes melancholy images or wistful yearnings in the soul. From time to time, perhaps, there may also be heard. the scream of the sea-gull above the noise of breakers, or one may admire its beauty, when at close range, it poises with exquisite grace on the crest of the waves.

But will any or all of these accidents account for the attraction the sea exercises on the heart of the race? Perhaps we could better account for it by saying that it is born of the craving of the human soul for

the infinite, which is typified by the boundless freedom and immensity of the ocean. 

There is also a mystical relationship between water and the soul. Notice the use the Church makes of water in her liturgy and her ceremonies. She makes it holy by her benedictions, and uses this Holy Water as a protection against the powers of darkness. We cannot help recalling also, how our Lord used water during the course of His public life. His first miracle was to change water into wine; the first act of His public life was to be baptized in the waters of the Jordan, when He sanctified that element that He was to use in His Church to be the means of the regeneration of souls. We hear Him use water as a figure of divine grace on more than one occasion, but notably in speaking with the Samaritan woman. "If thou didst know the gift of 

God, and who He is that saith to thee: Give me to drink, thou wouldst have asked of 

Him and He would have given thee living water.... Whoever drinketh of this water

shall thirst again, but he that will drink of the water that I will give him shall not thirst for ever . . . The water that I give him shall become to him a fountain springing up into life everlasting" (John iv:1014).

Many of the Saints had a predilection for water. St. Francis of Assisi speaking of it, says: "Our sister, the water is chaste." But none of the Saints loved water more than the great St. Teresa of Avila. She tells us in her writings that she needed in times of aridity to look at water to be raised to contemplation. "I always loved water," she adds. In traveling to her different foundation she would choose the routes that lay along rivers. "I find nothing so apt to describe spiritual things as water," she tells us. "What would become of the world if there were no more water," she continues. 

She then dwells on the properties and uses of water, drawing figures from it to describe the degrees and states of the spiritual life that she is explaining. Fountains,

rivers, aqueducts and watering devices are figures of comparison she uses to describe the different states of mystical prayer. 

Water is mentioned throughout Holy Scripture from the first lines of Genesis where it is said: "The Spirit of God moved over the waters" (Gen. i:2), to the last lines of the Apocalypse, where we read: "He that thirsteth, let him come, and he that will, let him take the water of life freely" (Apoc. xxii: 17).

Those who love to commune with nature may be called dreamers. But the hermit and the Saint are such dreamers, and from the contemplation of nature they rise to the serene regions of divine contemplation.

Canon Sheehan reminds us, in a timely way, that in the economy of our new orders and social systems we are sorely in need of having the Saint and the hermit in their rightful places, for they symbolize the gracious peace and rest of which the world will ever be sadly in need. Communing with nature keeps the heart unhardened,

and to keep it so until death is the achievement of a Saint.

As we find in nature a solace and a joy for heart and mind, as well as the source of our physical preservation, we must not forget the truth, that we owe it all to the 

Holy Ghost, the third Person of the Blessed

Trinity, who is the author of all visible creation. Holy Scripture assures us of this truth again and again. "By the Word of the Lord the heavens were established, and the power of them by the Spirit of His mouth" (Ps. xxxii). This Divine Spirit makes use of creation for our physical, mental and moral good. The earth, with its multicolored loveliness of flower, shrub and tree; the majesty of its mountains; the grandeur, vastness and power of its rivers and seas; the magic, atmospheric effects of light and shadow, the countless stars and planets, with their wondrous system, and bewildering magnitude and might, all proclaim the power, wisdom and goodness of God, the Creator. There is in all creation

a prayer of adoration, thanksgiving and praise to the Creator of unity in variety. 

Only one thing is necessary to see the beauty God has placed in creation, as He would have us see it: sincerity. Let us seek with sincerity the beauty God has placed in all His works, and love it because of Him, and it will be a balm to our souls, and draw us to the eternal source of all beauty.

Joy and pleasure are two ideas that are often confounded. Joy has its domain chiefly in the soul, pleasure chiefly in the senses. Joy is Christian, pleasure pagan. 

The action of joy is wholesome, exhilarating, refreshing, stimulating. Its effect is, like that of springtime, putting all the fountains of vitality into motion, and invigorating all dormant and deadened things. Pleasure is a fugitive sensation whose cause is as changeable as the wind. 

It charms, thrills, excites for a time, but leaves a feeling of want in the soul. Pleasure walks a flowery pathway and stops at all that dazzles or fascinates, until it reaches the stage of boredom and melancholy.

Those who have failed to grasp the true meaning of life take refuge in pleasure to

escape from the emptiness and monotony of their existence. For after all, pleasure is but a series of varied impressions devised to meet the need man has to forget the obvious, the actual, the real. It is something planned to hold his attention in an agreeable way to prevent his concentrating on what he does not wish should occupy his thoughts. But a constant pursuit of pleasure, however legitimate, is like chasing the light and fugitive spume of the ocean over its surface, when by plunging into its depths one might gather the precious pearls hidden there.

MEMORIES 

IT WOULD be a decided misfortune were we able to retain a permanent and vivid impression of all our past experiences. There are some of these, however, that have carved themselves so deeply in our impressionable parts that only the dissolution of these parts will destroy them. 

These we refer to as our memories. Some of these we recall with joy and happiness, others with varying degrees of pain. It is seldom we recall our painful memories, hence they become less and less vivid and so less painful. Thanks to this law of the memory our happy recollections give us ever additional pleasure, while our painful ones eventually lose their sting. Unhappy memories are not, however, without 71

their value in the economy of our lives. By means of them we live over again our past painful experiences, our sorrows, struggles or hardships, the recollection of which gives poise and maturity to our judgment. 

It is the experiences of childhood and youth that form the foundations of our memories, and leave the most vivid impressions of our lives. It is why the old are for ever recalling their childhood, whose events are more real to them than those of their later life. There is a great blessing in this, for just as youth lives in the future, the old live in the past, and the memories of childhood are, as a rule, the brightest and sweetest of life. The recollections of childhood, therefore, in some way make up for the loneliness of old age.

Is is not a fact that man lingers in the land of recollection and memory as much as in his actual experiences? But he finds more pleasure in these reminiscences than he did when they were realities, perhaps. At least in looking back at these past events, he thinks they were more pleasurable than they really were at the time. Memory gives them a glamor that they lacked, or of which the inconvenience of circumstances of time and place deprived them. In memory, only the spirit of the thing is present; the body, the material element, is missing and this is often an advantage.

KINDNESS. 

WHAT 

HAT an amount of human sufferings in this world results from man's selfishness, cruelty and injustice! It was the consciousness of this that prompted the lines:

Man's inhumanity to man Makes countless millions mourn. 

Still it is not exactly the exercise of justice that will remedy the sad condition. 

Many of us would have much to fear from the strict application of justice. Were God more just than merciful with us how we might tremble! The great remedy for 80

KINDNESS 

81 much of human woe is a little more kindness. Justice is certainly sadly needed, but kindness is the graciousness of justice.

Every little act of kindness practiced daily the world over means a little less pain on earth. 

While kindness and love are distinct, often kindness might be defined as love in action. Its first movement is often pity, and pity contains a germ of love. Those to whom we have exercised kindness become dear to us, hence, kindness produces love.

It is the exterior expression of love, as perfume is of the flower. It is that form of charity that consists in saying and doing good to others in a manner that comes from the heart and goes to the heart. To love is to give oneself, and kindness is defined as the overflow of self on others. It might also be characterized as a permanent disposition of heart to procure the good of others, and losing no occasion of making them happy at our own expense.

Difficulties of different kinds form the

daily bread of our existence here below. 

Our inevitable relations and contacts give rise to these as a result of difference of opinion, age, temperament, education, interests, health, misfortunes, illusions, and what not. Closest friendship is not proof against these countless irritations and hurts. 

In fact the more intimate our relations the more susceptible are they of being wounded by the slightest shock. The law of retribution which suggests itself to us in moments of vexation or bitterness only aggravates our condition and accentuates the shadows. A disposition of kindness maintained in the heart and expressed exteriorly in the countenance, if not otherwise, causes the sun to break through the clouds again and restores interior and exterior peace. The pain this costs sometimes amounts to the heroic, but it carries its reward. It is not difficult to be kind to those we love or who do us good; but to be kind to the unkind is truly virtuous. The practice of kindness supposes the existence of

KINDNESS 

83 many virtues. It is at times the practice of patience, at others, of zeal, of self-sacrifice or of the pardon of injuries. Kindness seals our lips against all that offends, pains, shocks or vexes. It keeps a smile on the lips for all who approach us. 

Kindness brings out the hidden good qualities of others; it arouses courage and self-respect where these were dormant. 

There are more noble projects that have failed for want of a little kindness than for want of money or genius. There are hearts that are aching from loneliness, good wills that are hungering for a word of encouragement, and whose lives would be different in themselves, and for God. did a little kindness come their way. It is to be feared that God will hold many people accountable for the acts of kindness they failed to sow along life's pathway.

It is possible to be religious and, at the same time, to be lacking in kindness. 

There is such a thing as selfish piety. But those who are pious without being kind do

a considerable harm to religion, especially in the eyes of the less enlightened, who consider religion as somewhat synonymous with kindness. The fact that one can be merciful and charitable without being exteriorly kind escapes them. But all our good actions lose half their acceptableness when unaccompanied by kindness. Hence, St. Paul recommends kindness when he says: "Put ye on, therefore, as the elect of

God, holy and beloved, the bowels of mercy, benignity, humility, modesty, patience" (Col. iii: n. 2). For kindness is the harmony of charity in the heart, expressing itself externally in the attitude, the countenance and the speech.

The practice of true kindness is an art, and like every other art, requires to be studied to be exercised successfully. This is particularly true with regard to kindness in conversation. Father Faber tells us that there are people who try to entertain by being amusing talkers, but who are the bugbears of real conversation. They are

85 "forever jostling charity by the pungency of their criticism, and wounding justice by the revelation of secrets." "Wit and clever repartee," he says further, "often finish by disgusting, for they are nearly always accompanied by puerility and vanity." "Talking is like playing on the harp,” says 

Oliver Wendell Holmes, "there is as much in laying the hand upon the strings to stop the vibrations as in twanging them to bring out the music." Much of the pain of daily life springs from social insolence: our irritating manners, our censorious judgments, our pin-pricks in conversation, all of which are forms of unkindness as well as being evidence of an ill-conditioned nature.

These trials might be avoided by the appreciation and practice of a little kindness.

We must not lose sight of the fact that the basis of kindness is good manners, the essence of which is respect for the rights and feelings of others. One who is always thinking of what is due to himself is not likely to be distinguished for good-man-

ners; his selfishness makes him forget what is due to others. A person who is continually hurting the feelings of others has something wrong with his philosophy of life as well as with himself. We must not despise good-breeding on the plea that it is only superficial, and that it does not affect the real things of life. When 

Whistler spoke of the good manners of the French people, a friend remarked that they were all on the outside. "But," replied

Whistler, "that would seem to be the best place for manners." They help make life more agreeable to those who practice them as well as those of whom they are the object.

We have all heard that kind words are the music of the world, and we know the truth of the saying by having experienced the effect of kind words. They cost so little and mean so much that it is saddening how much we begrudge them. Why do we care so little to increase the amount of harmony

87 in the world when there are so many adding to its discord?

There are three kinds of words we should forbid ourselves to utter: angry words, rude words, and words of complaint. 

Good breeding dictates our avoiding the first two kinds; as to the third, we should remember that, unless it be prompted by duty, a complaint is a kind of reproach to those who listen to it. All quarrels, Father 

Faber tells us, rest most probably on a misunderstanding, and only live by silence.

Hence, the importance of removing misunderstandings by kind words. Kindness. should make us study the dispositions of those with whom we have to deal in order to avoid in our words, our attitude, and our actions, whatever might irritate. Even the tone of our voice should be seasonably attuned. We should avoid all that annoys, which sometimes includes our best endeavors to please. True kindness is the last thing of which one tires. 

If we would be virtuously kind we must

not be kind merely by impulse. Isolated acts of kindness, when we just feel like being kind, do not make us truly so, or they make us only capriciously kind. The virtue of kindness often makes us act in opposition to our sentiments. No matter how good-natured we are, we cannot rely on feeling, hence we must watch those occasions when kindness demands an effort on our part.

When we are obliged to contradict we must do so with as much gentleness and humility as possible. St. Bernard would never reproach anyone before he retired for the night, for fear of spoiling his night's rest. It is not necessary to flatter wrongdoing in order to be kind. It would be decidedly wrong to do so. 

But it is possible to hate what is wrong and, at the same time, love the wrong-doer. In our severest reproaches we should avoid sarcasm and scorn. These have never converted anyone, but have done much to the contrary.

Kindness sweetly triumphs where cleverness and logic fail.

Kindness is always bound up with the thought of God. By its practice we imitate Him. If kindness is the overflow of self upon others, is not Creation and Redemption the overflow of God upon the world? If kindness is the putting of the happiness of others in the place of our own, is not our Lord's life and death just this? 

God's providential action on the world, and on all living creatures is a perpetual act of kindness.

Kindness is an indication of holiness, or at least a disposition for sanctity. It is one of the signs of the presence of the Holy 

Ghost in the soul. Men, even those who are not themselves religious, measure the holiness of people by their kindness, and it

is to its influence alone that they willingly surrender. It is by his kindness that St. 

Francis de Sales won so many souls to God, even from among his enemies. "If you pull out one of my eyes," he said, “I shall look at you with as much tenderness as ever with the other." It was the same Saint that reminded us that one catches more flies with a spoonful of honey than with a ton of vinegar.

The Saints were invariably kind mainly because they saw Christ in each creature with whom they had to deal, however poorly that creature reflected Him. Even in the enemies of God they saw possible members of the mystical body of Christ, for they reasoned that as long as life lasts there is always the possibility of the greatest sinner turning to God and becoming one of His elect. It is noteworthy that the virtues for which our Blessed Lord has promised us eternal life are none other than acts of kindness done to our fellow-

91 man, and which He considers as done to Himself.

It is truly marvelous how much real happiness true kindness can bring to human lives. We must not be disappointed, however, that the truest kindness and most devoted attention often fail to dispel the aching loneliness of heart which each of us must experience in turn, and which somehow forms the warp and woof of existence here below. Its final meaning is to compel us to seek the Divine, the Infinite for which our hearts were formed. We cannot live without love and be happy. Earth's tenderest devotion is but a stray ray of God's eternal and boundless love, without which the heart of man must ever ache. This love is the object and end of all other loves. 

Once the human heart is folded in the divine embrace, all pain, all grief, all restless yearning, all loneliness will have ceased, for the heart will have found its home.
