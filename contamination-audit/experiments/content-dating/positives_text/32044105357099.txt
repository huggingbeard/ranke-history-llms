FOREWORD 

The reign of Queen Elizabeth was distinguished by an awakening of interest in the discovery and settlement of that vague continent of America which might afford a shorter route to the orient. Like most sovereigns who believe that it is more blessed to receive than to give, she stirred among the adventurous of her subjects the lure of riches to organize and finance expeditions across the Atlantic. Her own desire was to extend her dominions and enrich her treasury as well as her private purse. 

This took form in the granting of charters to such as were willing to risk their capital with promises of title to land discovered and authority over prospective settlers, reserving to the Crown certain shares in minerals to be found. It may be said at once that no minerals were found and that adventures brought no return on the investment during the life of the Queen. 

The first charter granted was to Sir Humphrey Gilbert who made two attempts to further the ambitions of his Queen and to enrich himself. The first was dispersed by the Spaniards before it was well under way, and the second at Newfoundland was defeated by desertion and disease. This from 1578 to 1583.

He was to be succeeded by his half brother, Walter Raleigh, later Knighted, who for several years organized and dispatched to America expeditions principally to colonize the newly discovered land, from 1584 to 1591.

In 1587 he induced one John White to bring over a colony to settle in “Virginia” which included the coast of North Carolina, and it is the fortunes and history of this colony with which we are concerned in tracing the site of their first settlement and their eventual dispersion. 

Many scholars who have been interested in the subject of the so-called "Lost Colony" of John White and the conclusions reached in this monograph asked the writer, before this was published, how he came to hold this view, verbally expressed to them, and where he found authority supporting his argument that Cedar Island in Carteret County was the site of the original settlement 1x55

One has placed it on the mainland opposite the present Roanoke Island, but only a casual study of the famous narratives makes this inference utterly unacceptable. 

Some years ago it occurred to the writer that, if he could determine its true location, he might be able to uncover some facts that would shed light on the mystery.

The opinion that White's colony did not perish has been held by many for a long time. Some believe that they survived in and among the Indians of Robeson County, others that not only a mixed race survived but a pure race of English as well since the colonists' names are common among pure bred whites in eastern North Carolina. There is some claim locally, seemingly not without good reason, that the descendants of the colonists are still living in eastern Carteret County. 

According to John White there was no sign that his people were in distress. This fact made the search for "Croatan" the more interesting. As a result of a close study of the narratives written by those in charge of colonizing the coast of "Virginia", the location of "Croatan" has been definitely fixed. Having identified "Croatan" on the maps, old and modern, the writer came easily and naturally to the conclusions given in the following treatise. 

To the issue of the Norfolk Virginian Pilot Magazine of July 4th, 1937, the writer contributed an article contending that White's colony was not established on the present Roanoke Island but admitted that Lane's colony spent a year there.

Not content with the bare statement unsupported by factual argument, the writer decided to make a more comprehensive study of the records, and later came to the conclusion that Lane's colony also was not established there but spent only a short time on the island as they did in other places. The whole study became so engaging

CHAPTER I 

It is the purpose of the writer to show that John White's so-called Lost Colony was not established on Roanoke Island, as known today, but on another island about 75 miles south; that on White's return to the Carolina Coast three years later in 1590, he did visit Roanoke Island which his colonists had never occupied. 

We have so long looked upon this Lost Colony as an unsolved mystery that we have come to regard it as impossible of solution. Evidence that would be accepted as sufficient for other history we are wont to question when given to account for the establishment of White's colony. The reason for such refusal to consider evidence is not so much our love of mystery as it is that we have come to believe that nothing can be brought to light to clear up the mystery.

Amadas and Barlowe with Simon Ferdinando, their Spanish navigator, arrived on the coast of Carolina in July, 1584. In his letter to Sir Walter Raleigh, Arthur 

Barlowe describes an island twenty miles long extending to the west, with an ocean on the north and an ocean on the south. The fact that the land extended to the west shows that he was on the east end of it. If he had been on the east side of the inlet which he entered he would have written that the land extended to the east. Hatteras 

Inlet was not open at that time according to the DeBry map of Lane's expedition, making Ocracoke an island. 

That inlet has had open and closed periods. The last time it opened was on September 7th, 1846, according to the records. There is only one island on the North Carolina coast, as a modern map shows, answering Barlowe's description and that is Portsmouth Island, that part of the beach between Ocracoke Inlet and Drum Inlet, the latter being open at that time according to the DeBry map of the Lane expedition. Drum Inlet is shown open in 1709 on the map prepared by John Lawson, Surveyor General of North Carolina. It has been closed from time to time and was last opened in September, 1933. The "ocean" on the south is the Atlantic and that on the north is Pam-

lico Sound, North Carolina's inland sea. It was on Portsmouth Island that Barlowe planted the flag of Her 

Majesty, Queen Elizabeth.

Upon entering the inlet Barlowe anchored on the left side. The place of anchorage at Okracoke Inlet has always been on the left side. For this reason Portsmouth on the west side of the inlet was an important settlement in the days of sailing vessels. Tanner's map at the latter part of the 18th century shows Portsmouth as a place of consequence.

The reader will note that the outer banks in the vicinity of Roanoke Island extend to the south and could not have been the banks referred to by Barlowe. It is unreasonable to suppose that he could have made an error in the direction of the compass. Albemarle Sound, to the north of Roanoke and extending west, is not wide enough to have been called an ocean.

According to Hawks, the historian, the river "Occam," which Barlowe subsequently goes into in his boats from the place of anchorage, is the sheet of water known as Roanoke Sound, between Roanoke Island and the beach. 

For the instant let us forget that Amadas and Barlowe entered Okracoke Inlet, and be ignorant as to where he entered, in order to give Roanoke Sound a trial as the true designation of the river "Occam". In the first place, it would have been a stretch of the imagination to visualize a river from this body of water. Barlowe writes, "into this river (Occam) falleth another great river called 'Cipo'; likewise there descendeth into this Occam another river called 'Nomapana". Currituck Sound is the suggested "Cipo" which can hardly be reconciled as flowing into Roanoke Sound. One of the rivers emptying into 

Albemarle Sound is suggested as the river "Cipo", but of course does not flow near Roanoke Sound. Taking advantage of the suggested ignorance of Barlowe, although he said he went 20 miles up the river "Occam" which is 

8 miles longer than Roanoke Sound, the Chowan River is suggested as the river "Nomapana" descending into the "Occam". It will be still more difficult for the reader to

reconcile the description because the Chowan River is many miles from Roanoke Island. The explanation given by the early historian does not at all fit the description made by Barlowe.

Assuming again that Roanoke Sound is the "Great River Occam", the attention of the reader is directed to the map of Indian Localities of 1584. This map shows that Roanoke Island was virtually a peninsular from the beach. We would not rely on that part of the map with confidence were it not substantially supported by the knowledge handed down by the ancestors of the people of Dare County. One man points out that the information handed down by his forebears for 120 years that the waterway in the narrow part of Roanoke Sound was about 100 yards wide and safely assumes that it could not have been more than a ditch 350 years ago. Hence, it could not with any degree of understanding have passed for the river in question in Barlowe's account. The map, referred to above, shows the Indian town "Pomeiock" on Neuse River. According to Barlowe's narrative it was on the river "Occam" and "Secotan" adjoined it. We cannot place the two localities on Roanoke Sound even though such could pass for the river, since that would be placing them on the Island when they were far from it to the Southwest. According to Hawks, the historian,

Roanoke Sound is the only body of water in that territory that can possibly be considered as the river "Occam". 

However, as the land lies today, this is impossible of acceptance. Relying on the mathematical axiom that a body cannot occupy two spaces at the same time, the two Indian towns mentioned above could not have been on that body of water without being on the Island, and they could not have been on the Island and be away from it to the Southwest at the same time. Yet, Roanoke was on the river "Occam". This fact alone would eliminate 

Roanoke Island as the birthplace of Virginia Dare.

Fortunately we do have a body of water that the Englishmen would as a matter of course have called a river. 

This body of water is about 30 miles long. Entering this

from the spacious waters of Pamlico Sound, they would naturally believe they were coming into a mighty river. 

Even today Core Sound could be mistaken for a great river. This also fits in with the description of the island extending 20 miles to the west along which Barlowe had sailed en route from the West Indies. Since there has never been any record of the closing of Okracoke Inlet, this has always been the principal gateway to Pamlico Sound and its tributary sounds and rivers, and was unmistakably the inlet entered on the Carolina coast by those Englishmen.

Amadas and Barlowe remained on this outer bank or island two days before seeing any of the natives. After their friendly meetings with the Indians Barlowe later went with his men by boats from their anchorage "20 miles into the river that runneth toward the City of Skicoak which river they call Occam and the following evening we came to an Island which they call Roanoke distant from the harbor by which we entered seven leagues." 

He continues, "Into this great river falls another great river called 'Cipo' in which there is found a great store of muscles. Likewise there descendeth into the Occam another river called Nomopana on the one side whereof stands a great town called Chowanook. Beyond this island there is the main land and over against this island falls into this spacious water the great river called Occam by the inhabitants".

The spacious water mentioned is universally understood to be Pamlico Sound. A modern map will show that 

Core Sound, that 30 mile stretch of water identified by the author as the river "Occam", enters Pamlico Sound "over . against" Cedar Island. In other words Cedar Island is on one side of the entrance into Core Sound. Barlowe could not have given a better description of Core Sound and Cedar Island when he wrote that "the river fell into the spacious water over against the Island." "The evening following", that is after going 20 miles up the river, "We came to an island which they call Roanoke". At first the reader would be inclined to think that Barlowe found this

island 20 miles up the river, but he could not have meant that because it is expressly the island where the river opens into Pamlico Sound. This means without question that he had passed the island when going up the river and stopped on his return. According to Barlowe this is the first island in North Carolina called Roanoke.

Cedar Island is here given to include Hog Island which is separated from it for the most part by a bay except a neck of land which storms cut into some thirty years ago.

An old deed about 1765 refers to the head of the bay, which is a mile from its present head. Less than a century ago people could talk to each other across the bay as stated by old people. Marsh land, submerged only during storms with an isthmus of higher ground, held the two islands as one. It is reasonable to suppose that Barlowe recognized the whole mass of land, marsh and high ground, as one island, as it was and substantially still is. 

It is to be noted from White's narrative that George Howe, a member of the Lost Colony, was killed while wading from Roanoke to Desamongapeuc. This is to say that the two places had a common shore line, as is the case with Hog Island and Cedar Island. Manteo was christened Lord of Roanoke and Desamongapeuc. Thus we observe that this land mass was recognized as two localities by John White, while it was spoken of as one 

Island by Arthur Barlowe.

When the white man asked the name of the place, the native, observing his outstretched hand and not knowing what he had said, probably replied that this land was surrounded by water. One can naturally infer that the word 

Roanoke was the Indian word for island. Each had not the slightest knowledge of the language of the other, which fact should ever be kept in mind. 

1. Disraeli in his "Amenities of Literature," writes: "The actual names given to those places in America which they still retain, are nothing more than the blunders of the first Europeans, demanding by signs and catching at words by which neither party were intelligible to one another."

It is impossible to locate on a modern map the rivers "Cipo" and "Nomopana" falling into Core Sound, but one's attention will be arrested when crossing the bridges in eastern Carteret County. Nelson's Bay, between Sea

Level and Stacy, could easily be mistaken for a river by Englishmen who were not used to great rivers in England.

Sailing in Core Sound passing the mouth of Nelson's Bay, which extends far inland, an exploring navigator might well take it to be a river even if he had gone a mile upon it, since it is navigable for two miles in ship's boats, and extending farther in a narrow stream. Another body of water that is easily mistaken for a river is Jarrot's 

Bay between Marshallberg and Williston. Navigable for two miles or more, North River opens into the headwaters of Core Sound.

All these waters are natural oyster beds which correspond with that part of Barlowe's narrative, "in which there is found great store of muscles in which there are pearls." Pearls are sometimes found in North Carolina oysters but are of no value.

Again quoting Barlowe, "We came to an island which they call Roanoke distant from the harbor by which we entered seven leagues; and at the north end thereof was a village of nine houses built of cedar." To be sure the 

Indians on Cedar Island would have ample cedar for building purposes since the island was so named for its cedar growth. If we bear in mind the first part of this quotation and examine a modern map, we find Cedar Island seven leagues from Okracoke Inlet, the harbor of entrance. It is noted that Hawks designated the New 

Inlet as the harbor of entrance which is stated to be seven leagues from Roanoke Island. This supposition cannot possibly be reconciled to Barlowe's description of the surrounding terrain and waters, his compass reading and his subsequent navigation and exploration of surrounding waters.

If therefore we have definitely fixed upon Okracoke Inlet as Barlowe's harbor of entrance, we have found the

key to the whole drama of the Raleigh colonists and the subsequent establishment of John White's colony. 

Quoting from Martin's history, "Amadas and Barlowe at first judged themselves on the continent; by taking advantage of an eminence they discovered that the sea surrounded them. The island that lay between Cape Hatteras and Cape Fear is concluded to be that of Okracoke or some other near it along the coast row in the County of Carteret." Martin was not far wrong. If he had observed the DeBry map of Lane's expedition, that Hatteras Inlet was not open at the time; if he had noted that a great body of water lay to the north of this outer island and that Barlowe entered the inlet with the island on his left, he would certainly have recognized the place of his first landing. As it is, we are able to locate Barlowe's first landing from his description alone.

Barlowe writes of numerous islands. Entering Okra coke Inlet and sailing from Portsmouth to Core Sound the following islands or shoals will be passed: Royal 

Shoal, Old Castle, Beacon Island, Wainwright, Shell Island, Harbor Island, and Cedar Island, covering a distance of about 15 miles.

On his first expedition he observes that some children were seen with auburn and chestnut hair. The natives explained to the sailors that "26 years before in 1541 a ship was cast away near Sequotan manned by white people; that some of the crew were saved and preserved by the natives; that after remaining ten days at Wocokon, an out island uninhabited, they with the help of some of the dwellers of Sequotan fastened two boats together, etc., and these aforesaid were seen only of the inhabitants of Sequotan." It is observed that Wocokon is a part of the beach, being an out island and must certainly have been farther to the southwest on Core Sound. It was far enough that the natives in the vicinity of Cedar Island had not seen them, but near enough for them to have heard of the wrecked crew from wandering natives. From the above account we must fix the location of Wocokon between Portsmouth Island and Cape Lookout. Indeed

this is its location according to the map of DeBry. It follows that Sequotan is on the mainland opposite Wocokon. We will not be far wrong in designating either Marshallberg or Davis on Core Sound as Sequotan. Note that 

Sequotan was "towards the southwest" from the island of Roanoke, and this is the direction from Cedar Island. 

Although Barlowe did not state specifically the course or direction of the waterway, he did indirectly when he mentioned the other Indian localities lying to the southwest of Roanoke, all on the same river. From this description we determine that the waterway held the southwest course. And Core Sound is the only body of water of consequence between Cape Lookout and Cape Henry or tributary to Pamlico Sound, that stretches to the Southwest. Since this waterway borders the outer banks, it could not have changed its course, unless Cape Lookout and Cape Hatteras had shifted their positions; and we would suppose that they have held their present points of latitude since the last geological epoch. The earliest maps of this territory bear such evidence. (This is given in answer to those who have raised the question of the probable change in North Carolina coast line.)

In Williamson's History of North Carolina, Vol. I, it is stated that Sequotan was near the present site of Beaufort. This location fits the narrative like a glove when it is considered that Beaufort and the Neuse River country adjoin. As Barlowe writes, "Also adjoining to this country called Sequotan begins a country called Pomonik belonging to another King whom they call Piamocum and their King is in league with the next King adjoining towards the setting of the sun and the country Newsiok situated upon a goodly river called Neus."

In August, 1584, Barlowe sailed for England taking with him two Indians, Manteo and Wanchese, who returned with the expedition of Sir Richard Grenville, which will be mentioned in the next chapter. The only other native of whom Barlowe writes is one Wingina, a king or chief on the island at the mouth of the "Occam" where it flows into that spacious body of water i.e. Pam-

lico Sound. It is evident that Grenville had full particulars of Barlowe's explorations on the North Carolina coast either directly or from Sir Walter Raleigh who was apparently director-general of expeditions to the coast of "Virginia" between 1584 and 1590.

It is evident that Barlowe explored many of the sounds in eastern North Carolina. From his narrative we can conclude that he spent most of the time in the Core Sound area between Okracoke and Beaufort. It was on Portsmouth Island, the locality called by the Indians "Croatan” and so shown on DeBry's map, and on Cedar Island that he writes mostly of his experiences, places where he traded and palavered with the natives. 

It was at Portsmouth or Croatan that Manteo was first met, the DeBry map showing its location, and the native habitat of Manteo is further confirmed by John White, leader of the so-called Lost Colony. A map of Carolina made in 1666 and reproduced in Hawks history, Vol. 2, shows Crotan, spelled Croatoan, approximately in the same location far to the south of Roanoke Island as known today.

The principal places described in Barlowe's narrative have now been definitely located not only from his own account but from the configuration of the coastal islands and mainland of North Carolina. No great distances separate these localities visited, namely, Croatan (Portsmouth), Cedar Island which they called Roanoke, the river Occam (Core Sound) and the villages of Wocokon and Sequotan.

This is to be kept in mind as the later expeditions of English colonists and explorers to the same coast in the following years are described.

CHAPTER II 

In the spring of 1585, Sir Richard Grenville, under instructions of Sir Walter Raleigh, sailed from Plymouth with a fleet of seven vessels. After a time spent in the 

West Indies he sailed up the coast and on June 23rd was in "great danger of a wreck on a beach called the Cape of Fear." On the 24th they came to anchor in a harbor where they caught fish, evidently between Wilmington and Beaufort, both in North Carolina. On the 26th "we came to anchor at Wokokon." Taking into consideration time and distance, this place of anchorage is the same Wokokon mentioned by Barlowe, about 15 miles from the present Cape Lookout.

On the 3rd of July Grenville sent word of his arrival at Wokokon to Wingina on so-called Roanoke, the same chief who was friendly to Barlowe, who wrote of him as on the "island they called Roanoke" at the mouth of the river Occam flowing in Pamlico Sound. Wokokon, as located, is about seven miles from Cedar Island, not too far to have sent a message to Wingina. The island known today as Roanoke is distant 80 to 90 miles, too far away to be considered the home of Wingina and the destination of the messenger.

Grenville's ships entered the inlet at Wokokon. This inlet was later known as Old Cedar Inlet and is shown in the English Pilot published in 1820, and was frequently used as a harbor of refuge. It is now closed to passage. 

The inlet at Wokokon is about seven miles from Drum 

Inlet at the west end of Croatan or Portsmouth Island.

From Drum Inlet to Okracoke Inlet the distance is about 20 miles, making the distance from Wokokon to Croatan, or Portsmouth, about 27 miles. Grenville sent two of his captains to Croatan on July 6th to pick up two of his men left there by Captain Raymond twenty days before, Raymond evidently having sailed on ahead of the fleet as far as Okracoke Inlet. The two captains and the two men return to the fleet at Wokokon on the 8th, which squares with the distances given above. 

Between the 11th and 16th of July some of Grenville's

fellow adventurers, including Ralph Lane and John

White, visit the villages of Sequotan, Aquascogoc, Pomeiok and the great lake Paquipe. Captain Amadas, companion of Barlowe on his expedition the year previous, was also of this party and would be the proper guide to those places where he had tarried before. Leaving their vessels at anchor in Wocokon harbor and crossing in ship's boats to the main, they proceeded to the localities already fixed by Barlowe's description and DeBry's map. 

Therefore it is incontestible that Grenville's party crossed 

Core Sound and that the "main" was between Beaufort and Cedar Island.

There remains but two points to be cleared up, the location of Aquascogoc and the identity of Lake Paquipe. The former is shown by Geo. Schroeter on a map of Indian settlements 1584 up on the Neuse River on the south side of Club Foot Creek. This is perhaps too far but it is an honest guess and does not place the settlement outside the itinerary of the visitors from Grenville's ships. 

On a map made by Geo. Schroeter by order of the Lords 

Proprietors, 1671, we find near Mattamuskeet Lake the 

Aromuskkeet marshes. The "Muskkeet" in the two places adjoining might well mean that the lake has always born its present name. Paquipe and Mattamuskeet are both Indian names. It is unreasonable to suppose that the Indians changed the names of their localities. 

Consequently, if the big lake in Hyde County had ever been called "Paquipe" by the Indians it would have retained the same name, unless given an English name by white people. This lake is some 50 miles from Lane's place of anchorage. It is not accessible by water; a land journey through the wilderness was necessary. Time would not permit. Hence Mattamuskeet Lake must be ignored as the great Lake Paquipe.

Neither Aquascogoc nor Lake Paquipe is mentioned in the Barlowe or John White narratives. And it is a safe guess that the lake was discovered in going to the village. 

This is good and admissable evidence that Cedar Island Bay was the Lake Paquipe seen on this journey. This

bay was practically land locked 350 years ago. Reference is made to the U. S. Geodetic survey of 1870 and that of 

1920 which reveal the gradual breaking up of the long strip of land between Cedar Island Bay and Pamlico Sound which to cursory observation enclosed the Bay 350 years ago and made it appear as a "great lake". From statements handed down in the families of settlers nearby there was but a single strip of land seperating the Bay from the Sound in 1810 which shows the action of wind and tide in breaking through this barrier. The survey of 1920 showed this barrier or island, called Swan Island, divided into three sections; while today, in 1939, there are but two small sections left.

In Lane's story, they were entertained on the 15th by the natives at Secotan, and returned to the fleet at the inlet. On the 16th they made a return trip to Aquascogoc to demand a silver cup that had been stolen on the 13th.

There is an Indian shell mound 200 yards long only a half mile distant from Long Bay, an arm of the great bay pointed out as the great lake "Paquipe". These places can be visited in a day from Wocokon harbor.

These points disposed of, it is now certain that the Grenville party followed and located the same settlements and islands formerly discovered and mentioned by Barlowe, namely, Croatan (Portsmouth), the Occam River (Core Sound), Roanoke (Cedar Island) and Wokokon. 

On August 21st Sir Richard Grenville sailed from Wokokon to "Haterask" where it is recorded that Granganimeo and Manteo came aboard the Admiral, Sir Richard's ship. Since the former was brother to the Chief 

Wingina on Cedar Island it is fairly assumed that he crossed over to what is now known as Drum Inlet, called

Haterask, met Manteo and together with him paid their respects to Grenville on the Admiral. The DeBry map places Haterask at New Inlet or perhaps Loggerhead Inlet, now closed, but this is in error since it is wholly improbable that Granganimeo would have met the Admiral at so great a distance from his home. Manteo likely came by boat to Drum Inlet and met the Chief's brother at the place of anchorage.

On August 25th, 1585, Grenville sailed for England, leaving behind Ralph Lane with a colony of men on Cedar Island. From this base Lane made journeys of exploration in and about Albemarle Sound with a number of his colonists and admittedly stayed for a time on what is known today as Roanoke Island. While there, he records the visits of Chiefs or Kings, "All came to visit the colony of the English which I had for a time appointed to be resident there." The fact that it was only "for a time" means that he had chosen the island merely as a temporary base for further exploration in that section of the country. The fact that Lane was accompanied by only a part of his men is attested by that part of Hariot's narrative wherein he writes of the critics of the expedition to Virginia, "The cause of their ignorance was in that they were of that many that were never out of the island where we were seated or not far or in the leastwise in few places else during the time of our abode in the country." 

Quoting again from the same source, "The Weroance (Chief) with whom we dwelt, called Wingina, and many of his people would be glad many times to be with us at our prayers and many times call upon us to pray and sing psalms, etc." This chief we rightly take it to be the same 

Wingina on the island over against which the Occam River issues into Pamlico Sound. Wingina was the most prominent Indian with whom the English had dwelt the year before 1584.

Reference is made here to Schroeter's map of Indian localities. Barlowe states that Newsiok was upon a goodly river called Neus and west of the "Island" with Sequotan southwest (on Core Sound) which definitely identifies this "Island" with Cedar Island. Newsiok and Sequotan are both in the same general direction from Roanoke Island according to the map, that is, southwest. Neither locality is west on the Schroeter map. In order to reconcile the map with Barlowe's description, Newsiok would have to be up on the Chowan River to be west from Roanoke Island.

It must be understood that these various narratives

must be interpreted in the light of expert knowledge of the terrain and waters of the North Carolina coast. Distances are guessed and hence are not always accurate. 

Ralph Lane makes such errors and yet, while he is on his Albemarle Sound expedition, he gives his distance from "home" as 160 miles, a fair guess, from Cedar Island to the headwaters of Albemarle Sound. 

On the return trip from Chowan River Lane lodged upon an island which is easily identified as Roanoke Island as we know it today, and there they spent the day fasting on account of rough weather. The next day they started across the "broad sound" and arrived at their home the next morning. This is a normal and usual description of a journey from Roanoke Island to Cedar Island across Pamlico Sound.

Lane, being now hard pressed for food and expecting the supply ship of Grenville for their relief, sent Captain Stafford with 20 men to Croatan to watch for the ship. 

If Grenville had not entered Okracoke Inlet the time before, why would Lane have sent Stafford there to be on the lookout for him? It was at Croatan that the fleet of 

Sir Francis Drake of 23 ships, returning from adventures in the West Indies, was first sighted by the same Stafford. 

The fleet having anchored off shore, Stafford set out to inform Lane of Drake's arrival. As Lane writes, "Captain Stafford came unto me having that night before and that same day travelled by land twenty miles." Now

Stafford was at Portsmouth when the fleet was sighted and thereupon he goes twenty miles by land to report to Lane. He could not have made the journey to Roanoke 

Island in the time mentioned, and it would not have been undertaken at night. Therefore, it must be assumed that he travelled southwest along the beach to Drum Inlet, about twenty miles from Okracoke Inlet, where Lane and some of his men were stationed. Cedar Island is across 

Core Sound from Drum Inlet.

An account written by Sir Francis Drake is included in Lane's and is quoted, "But some of our ships, being of great draught and unable to enter, we anchored all

without the harbor in a wide road at sea about two miles from shore whereupon there arose a great storm which they said was extraordinary and very strange that lasted three days together and put all our fleet in great danger to be driven from their anchoring upon the coast. For we broke many cables and lost many anchors and some of our fleet which had lost all was driven to put to sea in great danger in avoiding the coast."

It is noted that Drake's fleet came to anchor off Okracoke Inlet June 8th, 1586. From what direction was this wind or great storm which lasted three days and obliged 

Drake's ships to leave their moorings? Coast guardsmen and boat captains on the Carolina coast state that three day storms during the first half of June are almost invariably northeasters. This is confirmed by the station log at the Core Banks Coast Guard Station accurately kept for 42 years. This record will be found in the Addenda. 

If Drake's ships had been anchored at Oregon Inlet, as some claim, this storm would have driven and wrecked them on the beach. The contention is that weather conditions are fairly uniform over the years and that the storm that hit Drake was a northeaster. If this is true, then he must have been south of Cape Hatteras. A wind blowing more or less parallel with the beach would have given the fleet a fair chance to clear themselves when breaking their cables. However strong this evidence appears, we would not consider it of such weight, were it not for the story given before in connection with Captain Stafford. 

On the 9th of June, the day after Drake's arrival ("Sir 

Francis Drake Revived", printed in London, 1653)- "upon sight of one special great fire the General sent his skiff to the shore where they found some of our English countrymen and brought one aboard by whose direction we proceeded along to the place which they make their port." Lane writes, "the 10th day Drake arrived in the road of our bad harbor." "From whence," according to

Drake, "the general wrote letters to Master Ralph Lane then at his fort about 6 leagues from the road in an island

they call Roanoke". Then Lane, "The 11th day I came to him." The question here arises, could Drake have gone to New Inlet or Oregon Inlet around Cape Hatteras in that time? An experienced sea captain has stated that everything would have had to be very favorable to have made the trip from Okracoke to Roanoke Island in 24 hours in the clumsy craft of that day. This would have included the night during which Drake would hardly have undertaken the voyage around Hatteras shoals. The most reasonable thing to have done was to wait until the next day. It cannot be assumed that Drake would have navigated in the night time looking for an inlet on a coast with which he was unfamiliar. The distance from Portsmouth Island to Oregon Inlet, sailing around the Cape, is almost 100 miles if he was not obliged to tack. 

Upon receiving Drake's letter Lane proceeded to Wocokon Inlet which had become his harbor, and met the commander of the fleet with whom he discussed the colony's needs for continuing its settlement and explorations. It was then agreed that Drake would leave behind three ships fully manned and would take back with him the weak and sick. On the 13th the storm arose and obliged 

Drake to put to sea where he rode out the storm returning to his anchorage on the 16th. In the meantime the colonists, perhaps considering more fully their plight and suffering from nostalgia at the sight of the English fleet homeward bound, persuaded Lane to embark them all for their return in Drake's ships. The request of Lane accordingly having been granted by Sir Francis, the men went aboard "and the rest sent for out of the country and shipped, we departed from that coast the 18th of June". In other words they sent for the men then at Cedar Island and Drum Inlet, shipped them and departed from Wocokon harbor.

In order to relate more closely the expeditions of Barlowe and Grenville respectively in their explorations and common experiences, a translation from the Latin of De- Bry is quoted from Hawks' history: "The sea coast of 

Virginia abounds in islands which cause the entrance into

that country to be very difficult; for although there are breaches at frequent intervals, which seem to promise convenient entrance, nevertheless we found them to our great loss to be shallow and troubled with breakers; nor were we able to ever penetrate into the interior until we had made trial at various places with our small craft. At length we found an entrance at a certain place well known to our Englishmen. (He means those of the Barlowe expedition). Entering therefore and pursuing our navigation a little way, we observed a great river making its way out from this region of islands, which however we could not ascend because of its narrowness and the heaps of sand which obstructed its mouth. Therefore, prosecuting our navigation further, we came to a large island and then to a town called Roanoke." Note his statement, "a great river making its way out from this region of islands." Certainly no river in North Carolina makes its way out of a region of islands. The very fact that the body of water did so make its way, is evidence that it was a sound bordering the beach. Because of the islands already observed by the two explorers, we must conclude that this body of water or river was no other than Core Sound.

Though we cannot rely wholly on any one old original map, that of DeBry shows the beach bordering Core Sound broken into four or five islands. The great hurricane of September, 1933, cut the banks in the same places all of which are now closed again with the exception of 

Drum Inlet. Sloughs near the beach indicate the location of former inlets now closed. Both the Schroeter and John 

Lawson maps show this part of the beach cut into islands. 

Therefore at times the beach between Okracoke and Cape Lookout could plausibly be described as a “region of islands". The size of Core Sound makes it possible for inlets to be closed over long periods between bad storms and to take care of enough water in heavy storms to burst through the beach at intervals, cutting through at practically the same places on every occasion. 

In addition to the banks islands bordering Core Sound

and the numerous islands about its mouth, the land between the Bays of the mainland appears as islands to one sailing up the sound.

Grenville and Lane entered the "well known" inlet, namely Okracoke. After entering and "pursuing their navigation a little way" they observed the "great river" described above, "a little way" being only a matter of ten miles from Okracoke Inlet to Core Sound, in fact they used both Okracoke and Old Cedar Inlets. (See addenda.)

Its distance from Okracoke and the various descriptions given of water and land nearby, as already mentioned, indisputably mark Core Sound as the river on which lay the island Roanoke.

In his history Williamson states, "Haterask was the name of a small inlet to the westward of Cape Hatteras. 

The second inlet to the westward of the Cape was called 'Oceam'; and there was a third inlet a few miles from the eastward (misprint-he means westward) of the present 

Okracoke that was called 'Wokokon' ".

That section of the banks between Oceam and Wokokon was called "Croatan". The principal fact established is that Croatan is west of Okracoke, east of Wokokon and partly on Core Sound. "Haterask" was between Cape

Hatteras and Cape Lookout. The DeBry map, as stated before, shows Oregon Inlet or New Inlet as Haterask.

Evidently Williamson did not rely on that part of the DeBry map, but formed his deductions from the narratives of the expeditions. If so, he was close to the truth. 

It is not likely that DeBry would have left out so prominent feature as an inlet. According to his map there were three inlets as Williamson stated, the first being Okracoke, the second Drum and the third Old Cedar, Wokokon on the map and so called by Williamson. 

One reason some students of the DeBry map do not agree that the first inlet west of Cape Hatteras is Okracoke is because the two points of beach at the entrance jut out to sea. They pretend to believe that the entrance is otherwise but they are in error, as every fisherman knows who enters the inlet. While DeBry has not drawn

his map to an accurate scale, it is a fact that the guiding sand shoals at the entrance do jut out into the sea. 

It seems that none of the expeditions gave names to the inlets themselves, but on the contrary, the names were given to the banks islands near the inlets. The place west of Okracoke was called Croatan, that near Old Cedar 

Inlet Wokokon.

About two weeks after the departure of Lane and his men in Drake's ships Sir Richard Grenville in the looked for relief ships arrived on the coast and started his vain search for the Lane colonists. Not finding them or any news of them and finding the places which they inhabited desolate, he was yet unwilling to lose possession of the country by abandonment. He therefore landed fifteen men with provisions for two years and departed for England.

CHAPTER III 

In 1587 Sir Walter Raleigh, intending to persevere in the planting of colonies in Virginia, prepared a new colony to be sent there under the charge of John White who had returned to England the year before in Drake's ship. 

Three ships were fitted out, the Admiral, a fly-boat and a pinnace, in command of Simon Ferdinando between whom and White relations were hostile from the very beginning. They departed from Plymouth for Virginia on 

May 8th.

White relates, "About the 16th of July we fell with the main of Virginia which Simon Ferdinando took to be the island of Croatan where we came to anchor and rode there two or three days, but finding himself deceived, bore along the coast where in the night-we had been all cast away upon the beach called the Cape of Fear."

Evidently Ferdinando was looking for Croatan. He had piloted two other expeditions to that place and he would likely transport White and his colony to the same place whatever were the intentions of White to go elsewhere. 

After spending about three days anchored at the place first taken for Croatan, they must have been at Cape Fear the night of the 20th. On the 22nd they "arrived safe at 

Haterask". Sailing cautiously along the coast looking for inlets with which he was familiar, he could not have covered the distance from Cape Fear around Cape Lookout and Cape Hatteras up to Oregon Inlet in that time, about two days. In that time he could not have advanced beyond the inlets entered by the former expeditions both piloted by himself.

It is in order now to fix upon the locality of Haterask. 

It is recalled that Ferdinando lost the Tiger which grounded on a sand bar at Wokokon or Cedar Inlet, when he commanded the Lane expedition. He was aware of the risk in attempting to sail in with the Admiral, a ship of 120 tons; and since it was his intention to leave her anchored at sea, he went to a smaller inlet close by where the fly-boat and pinnace could enter. It is correctly assumed from White's account that these two boats did

enter the sound. "Our two ships, the Lion and the flyboat almost ready to start the 21st of August, there arose such a tempest at the northeast that our admiral, then riding out of the harbor, was forced to cut his cables and put to sea." It is not recorded that the Lion and the flyboat put to sea. If they had their experience would have been similar to that of the Admiral, probably worse. If they had been at Okracoke on this particular occasion, regardless of where White and his colony were landed, except on the beach, the colony could not have seen the Admiral put to sea at the time the other two boats were ready to leave while they were in the sound. From this narrative it is certain that these two ships were in sight of the colony. Hence it must be concluded that the inlet entered by the colonists was Drum Inlet almost directly across the sound from Cedar Island, that it was called 

Haterask and so known to the earlier explorers.

Since Ferdinando was in great haste to rid himself of this human cargo he must be as near as possible the place where he was determined to leave them. 

Sir Walter Raleigh had instructed White to ascertain the welfare of the fifteen men left behind by Grenville and these were his first concern. He writes, "In relating what happened to Grenville's fifteen men, as told by the Indians, some of them, some having been killed, left Roanoke in their boat and landed on a little island on the right hand of our entrance into the harbor of Haterask where they remained a little while, but afterwards departed whither as yet we know not." There is still a little island on the right hand entrance of Drum Inlet. It has always been there in the memory of the oldest inhabitants nearby. When the inlet was closed at times the little island still remained. To see it, one would think that even a moderate storm would take it away but it has withstood all the storms and hurricanes that are still remembered. This little island, called Horse Island, still remains there as mute evidence of its resistance to wind and tide and, equally, as evidence supporting the identification of Haterask.

According to Raleigh's instructions it was White's plan, after ascertaining the welfare of the fifteen men, the state of the country and Indians, to proceed to Chesepiok where the colony was to be established. 

Ferdinando could easily deceive White by stopping as near as possible to the place of former English settlements, to permit him to inquire into the welfare of the fifteen men and allow part of the colonists shore leave. 

Then he could deposit the colonists and their belongings on the beach and after refusing the return of those first landed to the ship, with relief he departed on his return to England. He had placed them at the inlet and coast which were most familiar to him. He had no time to look for another landing, say, at Chesepiok. The sailors were anxious to start on their return voyage and "it booted not the Governor to contend with them." It was a quick landing, the whole operation being completed on the 22nd, the same day of their arrival. Ferdinando would not allow the colonists to return to the ships. Going to Chesepiok was ruled out. 

Upon the arrival of White and his colony at the island called Roanoke, that is, Cedar Island across the sound where they landed, they found the fort razed, the fifteen men missing and all the houses standing unhurt. That would be like the Indians to leave the houses standing, since they would eventually use them provided no more whites came. White people would have done the same thing, preserved the homes in a similar case where they might fall to their gain. 

Quoting White further: "On the 30th day of July, Master Stafford and twenty others passed by water to the island of Croatan with Manteo who had his mother and many of his kindred dwelling in that island." Now Manteo was a native of Croatan which has been definitely established as Portsmouth Island. The idea of passing over the water gives us a sense of nearness. The south end of Cedar Island from which Stafford passed over the water is about fifteen miles from the east end of Croatan and only three miles from the west end. Roanoke Island,

as now known, is 60 to 70 miles away, too far to speak of passing over the water in small boats. A special reason for passing over the water to Croatan was to hold a conference with the Indians "concerning the people of Sequotan, Aquascogoc and Pomeiok, willing of them of Croatan to certify the people of those towns." As clearly brought out before, in discussing the Barlowe and Lane (Grenville) expeditions, these places were on or near Core Sound not a great distance from Croatan, part of which is also on Core Sound. There was reason in White wanting to know if the Indians in those places could be relied upon as friends. The natives of Croatan informed him that they would endeavor to arrange a conference of the chiefs of such places which was duly held. It is significant that this business was dispatched in one day, not possible if the colony had been on the large island at the mouth of Albemarle Sound, Roanoke Island now called. 

The question arises, why would White call for a conference with the Indians from places known to be on or near Core Sound if he had been on Roanoke Island? In such case he would be wanting a powwow with the Indians of what are now Dare and Tyrrell Counties. The natives of Croatan had alleged that the Indians of Sequotan, Aquascogoc and Dasomonquepeuc had killed eleven of the fifteen Englishmen; natives from the latter place had slain George Howe of White's colony shortly after its arrival. Therefore White had fears that must be dispelled. He must bring the natives together for a common understanding for that part of the country where his people were to live.

Going through Lane's account when he was exploring in and about Albemarle Sound, we do not find names of localities already well known to him and previous explorers. We find instead such names as Tripanieks, Opossians, Mandoags, Moratocs and Mangoaks, all localities 160 miles from his "home" to which he returns across the broad water to Cedar Island and Core Sound. 

The colony having been settled there, the colonists persuaded John White to return to England in their inter-

ests. On August 27th he boarded the fly-boat and started seaward, where he met the Admiral which had been riding out a storm for six days and was now coming in again.

Nine days before White left the colony, an important event occurred. "Eleanor, daughter to the governor and wife to Ananias Dare, one of the assistants, was delivered of a daughter in Roanoke and the same was christened there the Sunday following, and, because this child was the first Christian born in Virginia, she was named Virginia." 

Thus wrote the proud grandfather.

In John White's account of the settlement of the colonists and ending with his return and arrival in England, he states that shortly before his departure, the ship Admiral was obliged to cut her cables and put to sea, where she was obliged to stay six days while the storm lasted. 

If White had not stated that the wind was northeast, we would have surely guessed it, because storms of such duration at that time of year are always northeasterns on the Carolina Coast. White leaves us in no uncertainty. 

The beach north of Cape Hatteras extends to the north, and bears still further to the north from Oregon Inlet past that part of the beach which is adjacent to Roanoke Island. Every experienced boatman whom the writer has approached for an opinion on this matter is emphatic in his assertion that the Admiral, had it been anchored at sea off the beach north of Oregon Inlet, could not have put to sea in a northeast storm when she had cut her cables in order to get away, but would have been wrecked on the beach. 

An old sea captain who served 22 years in sail vessels was blown ashore on the beach between Okracoke and 

Hatteras Inlets, the wind being south by southeast. North of Cape Hatteras he would have been blown ashore with the wind northeast. In the vicinity of Oregon Inlet there is what is called an eddy tide occasioned by northeasters and the Gulf Stream which drives vessels on the beach.

On account of this condition ships have to steer well off

shore. Sailors not taking this precaution lose their ships. 

Hence more vessels have been lost on that part of the coast than anywhere else in the world, due to the conditions mentioned above. The small ships of the Elizabethan period would more likely become the prey of winds blowing about Hatteras shoals than the streamlined fore and aft schooners of the present century. Therefore the ship Admiral had to be somewhere between Cape Hatteras and Cape Lookout, in order to have escaped wreck. 

And, following White's account, the ship must have been off "Haterask", known now as Drum Inlet. Here the beach lies a little east of northeast. The wind in that storm was blowing about parallel with the beach, so that when the Admiral cut her cables she could wear off shore with even a little jib and mainsail. This coast has lured the careless navigator on to its shoals when the storms have come out of the southeast, which is a rare occurrence. Consequently it has never known but a small fraction of the wrecks that have been the toll north of Hatteras.

From this evidence alone, recorded at the time, and the direction of the wind carefully noted, we can positively deduce the fact that White's colony could not have been on Roanoke Island, as known today, or near it.

CHAPTER IV 

At the end of February, 1590, Sir Walter Raleigh fitted out three ships to supply, comfort and relieve the people of Virginia, and after many delays the fleet sailed from 

Plymouth the 20th of March with John White the only passenger. White once again was at odds with the commander and was not allowed a servant to attend him. 

Like all the former expeditions they came up the coast from Florida to Virginia. According to White's letter to Hakluyt, the commander was not interested in the colony, but spent most of the summer in plundering the 

Spaniards in the south. At noon, August 3rd, over four and one-half months since beginning the voyage, they were in the 34th latitude, a few miles north of Cape Fear. 

Towards night they were "within three leagues of the low sandy islands west of Wocokon." The second latitude recorded is 35 degrees which he stated was also west of Wocokon. The third latitude given, which he writes was at the very northeast end of Croatan, is 3512. This is one-half degree too high. That gives as the latitude of 

Roanoke Island 36%, also one-half degree too high. Going back to the second reading of 35 degrees and subtracting one-half degree as error, we find the ships west of Wocokon as White stated. Observing again the latitude given for the "very northeast end of Croatan" and knowing the latitude of Okracoke Inlet, we here again establish definitely and beyond doubt that Portsmouth Island and Croatan are one and the same. Since Wocokon is 25 miles southwest of Okracoke it is not possible that these two names are those of the same place as some historians have attempted to show.

Did White know where he was on this his last voyage to Virginia? We would suppose that he did or he would not have been writing so familiarly of the places. The commander of the ships evidently did not know the part that Croatan had played in the Barlowe expedition and those that followed it.

These could have been read from the ship's log even after reaching England. He might have known when he passed Croatan and he might not. One of the inlets which he had known might have been closed, thus putting him off his guard, until he had sailed by. 

White states that on his last voyage they came to anchor the 15th of August toward evening at Hatorask.

The morning of the 16th boats from the ships went on shore. It is to be noted that when the landing was made he seemed to be indecisive about going at once to the place where he claims to have left his colony. He went to a "second smoke" first, beyond the place and then back, thus consuming so much time that he did not succeed in arriving at the desired spot before nightfall, and returned to the ship. They deferred going to "Roanoke” until the next morning, the 17th. It was ten o'clock before leaving the ships and nightfall before reaching the island. Then they did not land until the morning of the 18th at daybreak. The time consumed on this occasion is great in contrast to that in which Simon Ferdinando effected his landing of White's colony. White relates "from hence we went through the woods to that part of the island, directly over against Dasamonguepeuk, and from thence we returned by the waterside round about the north point of the island, until we came to the place where I left the colony in the year 1586." He left the colony in the year 

1587; the first date is that when Roanoke Island was used "only for a time." Using the date 1586 might be significant and it might not. But why would White go first toward the west part of the island in the direction away from the fort and the old houses? When he put his colony out at the place where Ralph Lane had spent a year, and where he himself had spent a year, he was not so indecisive. He went at once to that place. But on this his last voyage, he lands near the place and goes farther

away from it and then returns. This is what we would expect him to do when trying to hide something from those about him, that he had make a mistake in going to the present Roanoke Island.

He found "divers chests"; five were found "in the end of an old trench, made two years past by Captain Amadas", three of which belonged to White; also his coat of armor. It had been three years since White left his colony. Philip Amadas was prominently mentioned in the Lane expedition as he was with Barlowe; but his name does not appear at all in the narrative of the Lost Colony. Hence it is rightly presumed that he made the trench and buried the chests when Roanoke Island was used as a base in the spring of 1586, when Lane explored 

Albemarle Sound. 

They "found many bars of iron, two pigs of lead, . . iron locker shot, and such like heavy things thrown here and there", etc. They did not find "any of the last falcons or small ordinance which were left at my departure from them." Why mention "last falcons or small ordinance"?

The assertion that some things were left "last" is admission that other things were left previously. Our deduction here is that Lane left the heavy material there; and the light material referred to was not left there at all. 

In this connection we recall that Lane was delayed by a storm before crossing the broad sound on his return to Cedar Island. The rough weather would have caused him to leave those heavy things; and he might have had intentions of later making that island his principal habitat, expecting to return shortly. Whatever his plans, the sudden appearance of Sir Francis Drake put an end to them. 

This the year before the arrival of the Lost Colony. 

Accepting the theory advanced that White's colony was on Cedar Island and that what White found on Roanoke Island was left there on Lane's expedition, it must be wondered why White did not proceed there to recover his goods after he arrivad with his colony in the summer of 1587. He did not have time because of his short stay, preparations to be made for the needs of the colony and

his sudden departure. Once this was decided, Ferdinando was not one to wait on a passenger's convenience. He had suffered a bad storm and was anxious to leave.

The theory that the colonists on "Roanoke" saw a Spanish ship at Hatteras, cut "C-R-O-A-T-A-N" on a tree and hastened south is pure fiction. In the first place they would have been more exposed in so doing than by staying on the island because they would have gone by the place where the Spanish ship was supposed to be. Making their way over the water at night was out of the question. 

The probability of the appearance of a Spanish ship is almost nil. The Spanish Armada was destroyed only a year after White's colony was established. Of course a ship at the present Hatteras Inlet could not have been seen from Roanoke Island. If Oregon Inlet is meant for

Hatteras, the colony would not have ventured south in plain view of the Spanish ship.

The markings on the trees were very probably carved casually by Lane's men, when they made Roanoke Island their temporary headquarters. It is one way of passing the time. We might well expect them to have written 

C-R-O-A-T-A-N, since they had gone from there or its vicinity. People would not be leaving Roanoke Island to go to Portsmouth, when they were not forced to go. The absence of the distress sign shows that they were not compelled to leave. Portsmouth is the sand banks, and the colony would not be leaving a place like Roanoke Island to go where they certainly could not make a living. 

Viewing the matter in this light, we are forced to the conclusion that White's colony did not write Croatan on Roanoke Island. White could have invented that part of the story in order to direct anyone who might follow him later to proceed to Croatan.

If White's colony had been placed on Roanoke Island and, on his departure, it had prepared to remove from there 50 miles into the main, as he said, it is not at all likely that they would have changed their minds and gone to Croatan in the absence of distress of which there was no sign. We cannot imagine a situation that would have

caused them to depart for Croatan without a signal of distress, and to have left behind the chests, etc., which 

White states were buried there. The presence of the fort might suggest the idea of distress which is lacking. From

White's description of the fort we are forced to assume, by reason of his very words, that it is the first time that he had seen it, his expression being "very fort-like".

White must have realized the importance of building a fort before leaving his colony. And it is unreasonable to suppose that he did not. If there had been a secret understanding with the governor that they should leave a cross in case of distress, it is unreasonable to suppose that they would have forgotten it when supposedly giving the name of their destination. The distress sign would have been the most prominent feature of their tree carving. Leaving under no distress, why would they leave all those possessions which White alleges that he discovered dug up and scattered about? If there had been no reason for haste they would certainly have carried those goods with them, goods of inestimable value to those people, absolutely not to be ignored.

From Lane's narrative it is remembered that it was his intention to build forts on his journey of exploration "every two days march". It is claimed that he occupied 

Roanoke Island as a base for further exploration in the Albemarle Sound country. And our deduction is that he built at that time the fort found by White. Certainly he did not build many houses, no more than a few makeshifts for his exploring party. The description "very fort-like" would just about fit temporary forts "every two days march", presumed not to be as permanent as his main fort on his home island.

From the narrative of the establishment of White's colony, we learn that the fort built by Lane the year before on "Roanoke" was torn down, but that the homes were unhurt. From the account of White's return in 

1590 we note that the fort is standing but the houses torn down. Would this mean in the first instance that the Indians would go to more trouble to destroy a fort than in

the second? If the English had occupied Lane's fort for a year and the other fort temporarily the Indians would have a motive. Accepting the fact that Lane built a fort on Roanoke Island to use only for a short while, the Indians would not, as a matter of course, have attached as much importance to it as the former. 

In Hawks' history is a letter from Francis Yeardly to John Farrior, in which a young trader visited Roanoke Island in 1653, sixty-six years after White's colony was established, and was shown by the Indians the ruins of 

Fort Raleigh. Would we not expect the Indians to have some knowledge of the colony? They were sufficiently interested in what the white people had done there to point out the ruins of a fort, but they had nothing to say. about the colonists. Did they know anything about them?

Those who were supposed to have established homes there?

It is too bad that the narratives of the Barlowe and the Grenville-Lane expeditions do not mention latitudes even approximately since they would, even allowing for error, have resolved all doubt. John White was on the latter expedition but evidently knew little about navigation. It is probable that he did not observe Drum Inlet and Old 

Cedar Inlet on his last journey up the coast. Drum Inlet might have been closed at the time. After three years' absence from wooded coast, inland waters and islands, his recollection of inlets and landmarks might well be imperfect. He might have been lost until he was near the landing place made on his last expedition. Thus arriving contrary to his expectations, near Roanoke Island, after passing the inlet where his colony had landed three years before, he would naturally proceed to that island to discover if there were any Englishmen there, and to find out what had become of his goods buried there when he and Lane had been on the island in 1586. It would be easy to recover those things and return to Croatan and 

Cedar Island. The captain naturally would not object to that, when it was "discovered" that the colony had moved. 

White relates that "what we had seen in this place so

much as we could, we returned to our boats." Hence he secured his chests, books, etc., and his armor. Following this accomplishment, and "greatly joyed that he had found a certain token of their being safe at Croatan", "it was agreed by the captain and myself, with the master and others, to weigh anchor, and go for the place at Croatan, where our planters were; for that the wind was good for that place." They had left a water cask on the beach, and it was also agreed to recover that on the return from Croatan. A water cask was a necessity. 

It is suggested to historians to read again the chronicle of John White, written two and a half years after the events he narrates, and to realize that no existing documents can confirm or deny his statements on which alone the legend has been built. 

His character can be read in his own writing, captious, complaining and ever in revolt against the ship's navigator. The captain of the ship on his last voyage was intending to stop on the shores of Virginia only as an accommodation to White, to get it over with as quickly as possible in order that he might proceed south where booty and fame would reward him. Consequently White was put ashore where it pleased the Captain of the ship and likely informed that shore leave was limited. He had been before on Roanoke Island in 1586, and naturally thought the Captain could be persuaded to go elsewhere. 

His description of the place is likely authentic, but his later account of the establishment of his colony there and finding it gone is just fanciful, what is known today as an "alibi".

White must have known where his colony had been established, but the ship's Captain was not concerned. As a matter of fact, the colony had never emigrated from Cedar Island and would likely have been found if the Captain of the ship had been more interested in White's quest than in his more alluring promises of spoils in the 

Caribbean Sea.

Instead of going to Croatan, it was further purposed that they should go to some islands in the West Indies

and secure water and victuals, spend the winter there, capture Spanish prizes, and visit Croatan the next summer. They most certainly had other casks to hold water, and they could have obtained it at Croatan. They sailed for two days with a northeast wind, which took them by the coast of Croatan. And making a stop on that coast would not have been dangerous with the wind blowing parallel with it. Why did he not stop? He knew the location. He said the wind was good for that place. It was out of all reason not to have gone there. No one in his right mind would have missed the opportunity. Hence we must look for the answer out of an extraordinary circumstance. 

White had made a mistake in going to the wrong island.

But after making it, things would have ended well but for a serious incident. Without such an incident, he could easily save face; with trouble he also had to save face.

It is noteworthy that White shares in the agreement not to go to Croatan, but pass on by. Stopping would have been an easy matter. He could not assume full responsibility for that, neither could he afford to hold the Captain responsible for it. The first would have made a case against him with Richard Hakluyt, the second would have made a case against him with the Captain. These conditions considered, it must be concluded that White did not want to go to Croatan on this particular voyage. 

Something out of the ordinary had happened, unpredictable. 

Seven men were drowned, "the chiefest" of eleven that were in the boat, Edward Spicer, Ralph Skinner, Edward 

Kelly, Thomas Bevis, Hance, the surgeon, Edward Kilborne, Robert Coleman. Skinner was the master's mate,

Hance the surgeon, and Spicer were important figures on the voyage of the Lost Colony, and probably the only ones who shared the secret of White's mistake. This was a calamity, and "so much discomforted the sailors that they were all of one mind not to seek the planters". Although they were persuaded to make the landing, which we have described, they could not have been in a pleasant mood.

In a large measure White was responsible for the loss of these men, in that he had made the mistake in going to that part of the coast. Considering that the sailors were in a threatening frame of mind, we question the advisability of the stop at Croatan. What would have been their attitude towards the Governor when they learned that the colony had never been where their shipmates were lost? This secret could not have been withheld from them once they were with the colony. White's choice in not going to his colony under the circumstances is the same that most men would have made. It is human to cover our mistakes at the discomfiture of others. With most people the price is high in admitting mistakes, and added to this most likely, was the fear of bodily harm. 

White was alone; he could not endure the taunts of an angry crew. He would defer going to Croatan on another voyage. If the colony had been established on the present Roanoke Island, White would have had no fear of going to Croatan, since no mistake would have been made, which may be entered with other evidence that the colony was established on the island at the entrance to Core Sound. Hence, one of the most important incidents connected with the whole story is the loss of seven men; for that reason it is most probable that the colony was "lost". 

The DeBry map of Lane's expedition, a drawing of John White, shows the outline of the outer banks; also 

Roanoke Island. The question arises, why did White not give a better description of Core Sound and Cedar Island, that being the location where the colony was established? 

Since he was forced to deceive us in the account of his last voyage, in order to make that deception leak-proof, it was necessary to draw his map accordingly. He gives us the correct location of Croatan. The direction hither of a future attempt to find the colony would have pointed from there to nearby Cedar Island.

White wrote the account of the voyage of the Lost Colony the same year of that expedition. It is very interesting that he did not write the account of his last voyage until well over two years afterwards, to Richard Hakluyt, one of the principal promoters in planting the colony.

CHAPTER V 

The following is quoted from Martin's history: "Sir

Walter, although he had no longer any particular interest in the colony of Virginia, made a further effort for the discovery and relief of the men left there by Governor White. He purchased and fitted out a bark and despatched Samuel Mace, an able seaman from Wymouth who left that port in the month of March, fell on the American coast in about the 34th degree of latitude and proceeded along it but returned home without effecting the object of his mission." This was the fifth attempt of Sir 

Walter Raleigh to succor his colonists since 1587. At the last time, to avoid all excuse, he bought a bark and hired all the company for wages by the month, but they fell 40 leagues to the southward of Hatorask in the 34th parallel or thereabouts; and having there spent a month, when they came along the coast to seek the people, they did it not, pretending that extremity of weather and loss of some principal tackle forced them from the object of searching for the fort of Hatorask to which they were sent."

Like White when he was at Roanoke Island in 1590, they gave up the search when they were so near their goal. They were working for wages and had no special interest in these colonists.

White's colony was never found by their fellow countrymen. In that sense only they were "lost".

CHAPTER VI 

In this chapter there will be considered various circumstances and events which point to the survival of White's colony.

On Schroeter's map we find "Old Field" on the north side of Pamlico River between Belhaven and Bath. The name at that time could not have indicated a long period of settlement by the English. Unless there were earlier colonists of whom we have no record, White's colonists are the only Whites to whom the name "Old Field" is traceable. It has been impossible to find any historical source or explanation of the name. Later on, when deeds accompanied the transfer of real property, a section of 

Cedar Island described as "Indian Old Field" is mentioned in a deed. This was intended to distinguish it from other Old Fields. It was sufficiently well known in Cedar 

Island to have been preserved in a deed of conveyance of land and shows that whites and Indians were there together for a long time. The very name signifies that it is an inheritance from the Indians. The fact that the map maker gave it such a prominent place means that it was outstanding. If he was correct in his location why does the name not have some historical reference in Beaufort County? Finding that very condition on Cedar Island brings us to wonder if those responsible for locating “Old

Field" in Beaufort County, hearing that it was on the north side of a sound and having scant knowledge of Core Sound, put it on the north side of Pamlico Sound. It was important enough to be put somewhere on the map. Did he place it correctly? There is a suspicion that he did not. 

Some 30 or 40 years after Jamestown was settled (1607) the Governor of that colony, hearing a report that white people were to the south, sent a party to investigate, but they did not go south of Albemarle Sound. Travel was hazardous and they returned without learning anything. These rumors must have been carried by the 

Indians and not without foundation. If there had been

no White's colony, and nothing else had ever suggested the existence of white people in eastern North Carolina, then we could truly say that there was no foundation for such reports reaching Jamestown. Happily there are other circumstances that confirm such rumor. 

John Lederer, a German explorer in eastern North

Carolina reported a settlement of bearded men two and a half days' journey to the southwest which he supposed to be Spaniards. At the time, he had been somewhere between Pamlico Sound and Albemarle Sound. There is no record of a Spanish colony that distance, and it could not have been the settlement on the Cape Fear River which was disbanded in 1667, two or three years before 

Lederer's report.

In 1709, about 120 years after the date of White's colony, John Lawson, while making explorations southwest of Pamlico Sound, met a body of Indians who had been to their hunting grounds in what is now Sampson and Robeson Counties. The territory southwest of Pamlico Sound is Carteret County. The following is what Lawson wrote as a result of his interview with the hunting party: "The Indians who lived on Roanoke Island (that is, Cedar Island) or much frequented this locality tell us that several of their ancestors were white people and could talk in a book as we do; the truth of which is confirmed by gray eyes being frequently found among these 

Indians and others. They value themselves extremely for their affinity to the English and are ready to do them all friendly offices, etc." The writer has not chosen to refer to the Indians in Robeson as Croatans, because no such tribe ever existed. Croatan was only an Indian locality, as previously shown and proved. The Indians of Carteret County, the seat of John White's colony, were Coronines or Cores; and as reported by the Smithsonian 

Institute, from a study of potsherds and skeletal remains, belonged to the Algonquin nations, extending from New 

York to Florida on the Atlantic Seaboard.

In "Genesis of the United States" it is stated that there is a chart or map, published by Alexander Brown of Vir-

ginia, which was sent from England by the Spanish Ambassador in London to the King of Spain in 1608. On this map is drawn a river supposed to be the Neuse on the south side of which is located Passorapuick and on the north side Ohanohawan, with this inscribed legend:

Here remaineth four men that came from Roanoke to Ohanohawan.

Strachey says in his writings: "At Peccorecamek and 

Ochanahawan the people have houses built with stone walls and one story above another so taught them by the English, etc." There is a chart or map by Captain Francis Nelson who left Virginia in 1608 with this recorded legend: Here remaineth 11 men clothed that came from 

Roanoke to Ohanahawan." Again this last place was supposed to be on the Neuse. In Smith's relations one of the 

Indian Kings told him "of certain men clothed at a place called Ocanahawan, clothed like me." Take note then that it was reported to the English that white people were living on Neuse River about 20 years after White had failed to find his colony. If there had been only one report we might have reason to doubt; or if in the several reports different places or sections of the country had been mentioned, we still might doubt. If there had been no "Lost Colony" we could reasonably doubt these stories but, since these reports came from different sources reporting the same thing, that there were English people in these parts, we must surely conclude that White's colony survived. 

Hon. Hamilton McMillan of North Carolina in his pamphlet on the "Lost Colony" writes that in 1888 he interviewed a woman about 90 years old of an Indian tribe in Robeson County who related to him that her “Faythers came from Roanoke in Virginia" and upon inquiry as to the exodus of Governor White's colony from Roanoke Island she stated that a chief named Wyanoke conducted them to a settlement on the Neuse. According to her story they went by land and gradually went westward. 

According to William Edward Fitch on the "Lost Colony" 

Chief Wyanoke is mentioned in "Genesis of the United

States" and appears on the map left by Captain Francis Nelson in 1608 as Eyanoke. Hence it follows that the 

Indian woman must have been right inasmuch as she gave the name of the chief as above. If the colony had been settled on the present Roanoke Island by White and subsequently moved to Croatan with Manteo's people, the old woman would have said that her "faythers" came from Croatan. Accepting the story as having weight we must conclude that "Roanoke" is the colony's place of abode before its migration to the Neuse River. Not only does this story attest the survival of the colony but also to the claim that Cedar Island is the original Roanoke, since it is in the neighborhood of the Neuse. 

John M. Phillip, a public school teacher born in 1860, taught Indians for several years in Richmond County, North Carolina, and in Marlboro County, South Carolina.

He was at one time a traveling photographer and boarded with George Washington Lowery, an old man. Knowing that Mr. Phillip was from the eastern part of the 

State, Lowery asked him if he knew where Hunting Quarters was, and then related this story as told him by his parents: "An old Indian woman named Delilah Cumbo used to visit Hunting Quarters, she said, to see her people. This took place before the Revolutionary War. Of course, it is presumed that these visits were in her young days. Hunting Quarters was the name of Atlantic in eastern Carteret County until a Post Office was established about 1885. The oldest deeds carry that name, sometimes, The Quarters. No one knows how long this section has borne the name. It is not Indian, but has reference to the Indians and suggests that whites and Indians lived there together for a considerable time. 

A man of 70 years of age says that his grandmother used to tell him of a family in Hunting Quarters who was part Indian and bore the name of one of White's colonists, Bishop. It has always been rumored that certain families are of Indian extraction. It is true that various parts of the country contain people of Indian ancestry, but the above shows that the Robeson County woman was

visiting the eastern section of Carteret to see her kinfolks who were part English. This might be questioned on the ground that visits could have taken place in other places. 

While this is true, there is no argument supporting the existence of John White's colony anywhere else. 

Hon. Hamilton McMillan has shown in his pamphlet on "The Lost Colony" that 43 percent of their names exist among the Indians of Robeson County and that they were using the English language when first seen by white people. This is a remarkable percentage even if no names had died out, and considering that seventeen of the colonists were women. The only argument that the writer has ever heard disputing the connection between the colonists and the Robeson County Indians is that they came up from South Carolina, a mere statement without mention of similarity of names to support it. In respect to 

White's colony we have the names, the language, the migration, the visits and the rumors which amount to a certainty of Indian ancestry among the people in that section of the country where the writer claims the colony to have been established. 

Together with Old Field and Hunting Quarters we may mention another item of interest along the same line. We point to these two names as suggesting a period of association of whites and Indians, the two places practically adjoining. As stated, they are English names with an 

Indian meaning. There is a body of water between Hunting Quarters and Cedar Island called Berry's Bay. How long it has been so called is unknown. The first deed of land west of it, a Dixon grant in 1760, refers to Berry's Bay, evidently its name for a long time before. Nelson's 

Bay, four miles away, took its name from the man who first occupied the land adjoining it. Berry's Bay was surely named for a settler who occupied land on the bay. 

Where did the name come from? To answer that question truthfully is to prove that the "Lost Colony" did survive and whose blood stream flows all over the country. 

It is a very reasonable assumption that a descendant of Henry Berry of White's colony was a squatter on the west

shore of Berry's Bay and hence the name. We have no record of the original Berry ever having lived in that territory. The man who lived there either had no sons to carry on his name or he moved away to better his condition. We do not actually know that Berry was a descendant of the colonist Berry, but the fact that the bay has borne the name since the memory of man runneth not to the contrary gives good reason to believe so. It is adjacent to the island claimed to be the original Roanoke. 

And so, like Old Field and Hunting Quarters, Berry's Bay appears in the oldest land grants, but was never put on the map as an Indian locality. The old shell mounds in eastern Carteret County bear witness that this section was surely Indian territory. The map which marks Old 

Field on the north of Pamlico Sound should have placed it on Core Sound, where Hunting Quarters was an important Indian settlement. The reason why these names do not appear on Schroeter's map is because they were isolated localities. For example, Core Sound near Beau fort Inlet on the map of 1796 published in the first official census of 1790. On the same map there is a long narrow island extending half the length of Core Sound which is not named. The same, is located on a map of 1738 by 

Wimble. We have no knowledge of such an island unless it is intended to be a small island near Harker's Island, near Beaufort. The earlier map referred to is 150 years after White's colony was located. However, the map of the province of Carolina divided into parishes according to the latest acts published in 1730 by H. Moll, famous map maker, gives Core Sound its name as derived from the Coranine Indians. Hunting Quarters is given the name Coranine even though it was called by the former name in old deeds. Apparently the old map makers were doing their work mostly by hearsay. Certainly the name of the Indian tribe was better known than any other geographical designation in that section. These facts are brought out to show that this particular territory was, because of its isolation, little known to explorers and map makers.

As further evidence of isolation, found in the colonial records and mentioned in Ashe's History of North Carolina, Vol. I, we quote: "In August 1722 the Council had established Carteret Precinct extending southward to the bounds of government. The Town of Beaufort was incorporated into a seaport entitled to a Collector of Customs. 

And a road was directed to be opened from 'Core Point' to New Bern." From this we would not assume that Core 

Point was any place in eastern Carteret County because, had the Council known that territory, they would not have contemplated opening a road from there to New Bern inasmuch as it was cut off by water in many places. 

It is said on good authority that there was a fortification at Beaufort or nearby in 1712. As shown in the colonial records a fort was erected at Core Point on Core Sound in the vicinity of the Core Indians called Core Town Fort about the time that forts were built on the Neuse and Pamlico Rivers for defense in the Tuscarora wars in which Colonel Barnwell figured in 1713 above New Bern. 

We do not know exactly the site of Core Point but it may be correctly inferred that the Core Point from where the road was to extend to New Bern is the same place where the fort was erected. A later Act established a ferry from Core Point to Bath and hence it must have been on Pamlico River. The Core Sound on which the fort was built was not the present Core Sound. The waters about 

Beaufort were called Core Sound on early maps. Newport River on the map of 1796 was called Coranine River. 

Therefore, it is certain that Core Point where the fort was erected was not in eastern Carteret County. Then it follows that the Indian war in which Barnwell commanded the English was not carried on near the place claimed as the site of the "Lost Colony." This suggests that the whites and Indians were peacable here, having lived together and intermarried.

The Indians did not all disappear from eastern Carteret County when their memorable defeat occurred at 

Fort Barnwell above New Bern in 1711. One Robert 

Hamilton, who appears in the first United States Official 

Census of 1790, took an Indian baby girl as his slave who

was left behind in her mother's haste to get away. Whether she intended to return and pick up her child we know not. Forced to cohabit with a colored man in later life this Indian child gave birth to a daughter who was known as Black Nann. Later she became the wife of a negro victor in a fist fight with another of his race, the victor winning the girl. This couple was seen by an old man of 87 years in his youth as he stated in 1938. Robert 

Hamilton was his wife's grandfather. If he saw these people 80 years ago then the woman must have been born about 1788. Subtract 20 to 25 years from this date to arrive at the age of her mother when Black Nann was born and we go back to 1750 allowing a couple of years for the age of the Indian child when captured. It is reasonable to suppose that by 1750 more white people had come into this region and stirred the animosity of the Indians.

A story of two Indians living on the north end of Cedar 

Island at North Bay going at daybreak to the home of

George Styron, living on the south end of the island, to go hunting with him, always giving him the better part of the game, dates from 1750. A woman of Cedar Island, now about 60 years old (1938), relates that, when play ended in their childhood quarrels, other children would speak of her family as "Old Injuns", a term not understood but explained by the grandmother, saying that they should not get mad but should rather be proud that they were akin to the Indians and that they were the only true Americans. The family admits that a forebearer was a native Indian woman. These incidents show that white people and Indians lived as neighbors in concord and peace long after the Indian wars north of Pamlico Sound. 

In his history Williamson writes: "The Indians who lived upon the coast in Carolina were divided into small tribes without any powerful confederacy. Upon every section of the banks there was a tribe; and there were other small tribes within the sound. Those Indians, having plentiful supplies of fish, depended less upon bear's

flesh and other wild game than their brethren who lived further from the coast. This seems to have been one of the reasons why so much land was taken up and settlements formed more than 60 years before the first Indian war. Another reason for the long continuance of peace may be traced from the situation and temper of the first settlers. They were not under the protection of government; they came among the Indians as suppliants who asked favors, not as masters who claimed rights. Their conduct was as inoffensive as their language. They purchased the soil, paid the stipulated price and shunned every cause of hostility. The conduct of their successors for many years was equally inoffensive."

From the above we would reasonably surmise that White's colony had an excellent chance of survival. We cannot conceive of a people occupying a position of suppliants more than they. They had no bargains to drive with the Indians. The two races could never have been placed in a more equal social equality. The old adage of the lion and the lamb living together in the flood was here given the human touch. In the first place they were of the poor class in England; it was not in their psychology to expect to lord it over the Indians. It was indeed more natural for them to accept advice than to give it. Living with the Indians was a boon to them. The colony brought edged tools; in cooperation with the Indians they could build better homes and boats and could clear more land for corn and vegetables. Where is the argument that they did not survive? The Indians were human; why should they have wanted to destroy them? The Indians needed the colony as much as the colony needed them. 

Then there was Manteo who had lived with white people and who certainly occupied an important position of liaison between them and his race.

One outstanding element of Indian nature was his hospitality, going even as far as sacrifice. He did not stop at sharing his food, his comforts and his enjoyments. 

The ratio of men to women in the colony was six to one. 

This situation would hardly invite jealousy on the part

of the Indians who had a moral code of their own. However it is quite proper and reasonable to suppose that the Indians did not live long with the white people without intermmarriage, an implication of their generous hospitality. After this, which did not wait long, who will claim that White's colony was destroyed when there was a mixed race, a tie between the pure bloods of both races? 

If we lacked all else that pointed to their survival, we could not be better satisfied that they did not perish.

If descendants of the colonists were here when later settlers came, is it at all likely that they would have looked upon themselves as "lost"? The probability that the newcomers had ever heard of White's colony or had speculated on the disappearance of any settlers is nil. 

Using the first official census of the United States in 1790, we note there is evidence showing the existence of White's colonists and descendants in that part of the State claime dto be the original settlement. In Carteret 

County there was a white population of 3,019. Of these 

578 were heads of families, 60 of whom bore names of the colonists, a percentage of almost 12 percent. Among the 69 were 24 familiar names of the original colonists. 

Among these original colonists of White there were 

88 different names. Craven County had 30 of them which appear 139 times among 1440 heads of families. This is a percentage of 9.65. Among the 1290 families of Beaufort County 19 of these names appearing 78 times, or 

10 percent. Hyde County had 20 of the names occurring 

95 times among 625 heads of families, a percentage of 15 percent, larger than in the other Counties. Onslow 

County claims 15 names showing 54 times in 721 families, 7.5 percent derived from the colonists. Wilmington furnished 20 names repeated 49 times among 628 heads of families, or 7.8 percent. Fayetteville had 5 names shown 8 times in 281 heads of families, or 3 percent. 

Considering the lapse of time from 1587 to 1790, a period of 200 years, it appears there is a slight margin in favor of the theory that White's colony survived in the

area where established and spread out to adjoining territory with the other settlers.

As gathered from the records in the Federal Building in New Bern, North Carolina, the original land grants with about one-third of them in Craven and Carteret Counties, made in connection with condemnation proceedings for setting aside national forest reserves, show less than seven percent of the grantees to bear the names of the original colonists. There were 261 grants in that area up to 1800. The number of original grants in Carteret County up to 1750 as recorded in the land grant office in Raleigh, North Carolina, is 229, six of the grantees bearing the names found among the original colonists found in the census of 1790. If the number of land grants from 1750 to 1790 had been added they would not have increased the percentage of the familiar names for they rarely appeared. Up to 1795 there was not one original grant of land on Cedar Island to a grantee bearing the name of one of White's colonists, yet such names are to be found scattered about in this same territory since we know not when.

If there were descendants of White's colonists in Carteret County, we would hardly expect them to apply for land grants as readily as those who came after the establishment of government and law, unfamiliar to the first settlers. They would have been squatters, since in no other way was title secured except by occupation and use. 

Consequently later settlers would be asking the grant of larger acreage before the others saw the necessity of it. 

It has been shown that eastern Carteret County was little known in that no mention is made of it in connection

with the Indian wars. In writing of places of white settlements, Hawks the historian of New Bern, states most of the white inhabitants were north of Albemarle Sound in and about New Bern and in the county between the town and Washington, North Carolina, and up the Pamlico River in and about Bath. In Jones County and in 

Carteret between New Bern and Beaufort were to be found most of the other white settlers. Most of the Swiss and German Palatines were in New Bern and vicinity.

Here were their leaders deGraffenried and Mitchell. This was shortly after New Bern was founded in 1710. Eastern Carteret was hardly known, yet some white people were there. The colonial records give an account of a preacher, Ebenezer Taylor, who came from Bath to preach on Core Sound in 1719-1720. It was reported that he owned his boat and had considerable money. The four men who accompanied him said that he had frozen to death at Harbor Island at the entrance to Core Sound.

He was buried in a sand bank on the southwest end of 

Cedar Island. It is presumed that he was killed, that the secret was well kept among the four. From this account it follows that there were white residents of eastern Carteret at that early date. The earliest land grant of property on Cedar Island is dated 1763. The earliest land grant of acreage nearest Cedar Island was in 1740 in 

Hunting Quarters or Atlantic, North Carolina.

Granting that White's colony survived, then why did someone not discover its identity long ago? When a people preserves no records of its existence from year to year, its births and deaths, its records of war and peace, it is very difficult for an outsider to piece together its principal claim to fame. Unless there are written records the average family cannot trace its ancestry for more than a few generations, and in their ignorance lose all interest. Twelve generations have come and gone since 

White's colony arrived in America and there is likely not a single geneology in existence by which a family can trace its forebears back to those colonists. They themselves had no pride of ancestry nor land or chattels to inherit. The only duty of parents was to feed and clothe

their children, and prepare them for the struggle of life and pioneering in a wilderness. About the only way their identity could have survived would have been by inscriptions in the cemetery. There is but one family whose name appears in the first United States Census in this area that is marked by a cemetery inscription and, while the name exists no longer in Atlantic, North Carolina, there are descendants of that family still living there. 

There was formerly a cemetery in Atlantic, long since washed away by storms, with ballast rock for markers.

All that is known is that a man born in 1812 had a brother buried there who died in early manhood. This is mentioned not as settling the question of White's colony, but as showing how easily a people may lose completely its identity after a long lapse of time. There is no record of the first white people who resided in eastern Carteret County. This is enshrouded in the dim past. Are there people today the descendants of White's colonists? We do not have the most meager proof that they are not. No evidence is found that the colony perished. After 1590 no one made a serious effort to find them. After that any interest in them was confined to a few humble folks, and that soon evaporated with time and distance. While the present laws may declare a man dead after he has disappeared for seven years, the assumption is that a man lives until he is known to be dead and certainly that a people numbering over a hundred does not perish except in the natural course.

For several years during the early life of the Jamestown colony, established on the James River in Virginia in 1607, there was a continuous correspondence between the King of Spain and his Ambassador in London on the ventures of the English in Virginia with the design of opposing them. The following quoted from the "Genesis of the United States", an excerpt from the General Archives of Simanacas, Department of State, Vol. 2587, folio 

59, includes a letter from Don Pedro de Zuniaga to the 

King of Spain, dated December 31st, 1609: "Sire, I have reported to Y. M. that two (three?) vessels had come from Virginia and that they did not come well satisfied.

Since then four others have come in and in a storm the two others have been lost on the French coast with which I think they will have to be quiet for the present. But now they hasten the Lord de la Ware to take his departure and they tell me he will do so in a month or a month and a half and they have assured him that after him they will send this summer a thousand men. In like manner there will sail for Guiana two small vessels with small crews, but I fear that if any of the people which Watawole (Sir Walter Raleigh) left there should be found, they will send more because they praise that country very much and say that gold and silver are found there and it is thought that they will take Watawole out of the Tower that he may go there. May our Lord preserve and guard the Catholic person of Y. M. as all Christendom needeth. From Highgate December the last, 1609. Don 

Pedro de Cuniga."

It will be observed that this letter was written over 22 years after John White had left his colony in America which indicates that the Spanish Government knew nothing of any Spanish vessel ever attacking the colony, so that the rumor of such and duly included in a contemporary narrative may be wholly dismissed.

Having already brought out the pleasant association between the whites and the Indians of the earliest days and the mixing of their blood streams respectively, note what McMillan writes in his "Sir Walter's Lost Colony": "In the latter part of 1864 three young men of the Croatan tribe who had been drafted to work on the fortifications at Fort Fisher were killed, it is supposed by a white man who had them in custody. An inquest was held and at its conclusion an old Indian, named George Lowrie, addressed the people assembled in substance as follows: 'We have always been the friends of white men.

We were a free people long before the white men came to our land. Our tribe was always free. They lived in 

Roanoke in Virginia. When the English came to Roanoke our tribe treated them kindly. One of our tribe went to

England in an English ship and saw that great country.

We took the English to live with us. There is the white man's blood in these veins as that of the Indians. In order to be great like the English we took the white man's language and religion, for our people were told they would prosper if they would take the white man's laws. In the wars between white men and Indians we always fought on the side of the white men. We moved to this land and fought for liberty for white men, yet white men have treated us as negroes. Here are our young men shot down by a white man and we get no justice and this is a land where our people were always free." "

Quoting further, "They assert that the English colony became incorporated with the tribe which soon after emigrated westward to what is now Sampson County and they had probably settled on the Lumber River in Robeson County as early as 1650 where they were found by the Huguenots in 1709, having farms and roads and other evidences of civilized life. Their language is almost pure 

Anglo-Saxon. Many of the words have long been obsolete in English speaking countries.”

In addition to the above, McMillan has shown that 43 percent of the names of the original colonists have been preserved among the Indians of Sampson and Robeson Counties. 

In 1704 the Rev. John St. Claire, traveling as a missionary through the Albemarle settlements, reported that a powerful Indian tribe was living south, that "Many live among the English, a very civilized people." This report is evidence that these Indians had lived with the English a long time and furthers the conclusion that White's colony survived in that part of the territory first occupied by it. 

A letter from Rev. Morgan Jones written in 1686 and published in 1840 in the Gentlemen's Gazette is in part as follows: "After being captured by the Tuscarora Indians in 1660 and sentenced to die, he spoke in the British tongue: 'Have I escaped so many dangers and must I be knocked on the head like a dog?' Whereupon a war captain told him in the same tongue that he should not die

and agreed for his ransom and the five men with him. "They (the Doegs) there welcomed me to their town and entertained us very civilly and cordially for months during which time I had the opportunity of conversing with them familiarly in the British language and did preach to them three times a week and they would confess with me about everything that was difficult therein and at our departure they abundantly supplied us with whatever was necessary to our support and well doing." He had made this journey from Port Royal, South Carolina, and it is presumed that he was in the vicinity of Robeson County, North Carolina, or counties adjoining. We have no record of any Englishmen except White's colony who could have taught the Indians to use the English language at that early date.

General Archives of Simancas, Department of State, Vol. 2588, folio 82 (inclosed in folio 8) from a copy of a document inclosed in the letter of the Duke of Lerma to the Secretary Antonio de Arostegui, dated November 

13th, 1611. "Thus having made all diligence all along the coast and examining it to trace the ship they were in search of, they continued their voyage coasting along, sounding and seeking the land at 37 degrees, they came to Puerto and Bahia de Santa Elena which is at 33 degrees where they entered with the sloop and also sounded, finding that the bay has at the mouth about three fathoms and farther in a much greater depth from which the pilot of the Carovela conjectured that there is a channel in said bay of greater depth. From there they went on following the coast until they met another river called River of the Cross which is at 34 degrees at the mouth of which they anchored.

This was done at night and in the day, having sounded the harbor and found that at the mouth it may have been two or three fathoms depth and within 15, they continued their voyage sailing in search of the Cape of San Roman which is at 34 degrees until where from Sant Augustin of la Florida the coast runs to the Northeast a quarter to

east which in the ship's course makes 110 leagues. Here they keep away from land, from the coast which makes a small bay of about 25 leagues and likewise there is a shallow at the south side which stretches out into the sea for 8 or 9 leagues. From there you sail to the northeast some 

10 or 12 leagues from land and at the end of 15 leagues there is a shallow which stretches out into the sea 6 or 7 leagues and ends at the northeast. From the shallow to the Cape of Trafalgar to Virginia the coast runs to the north quarter to northwest; the distance may be about 

45 leagues and here you can go hugging the coast at the distance of an arquebus shot from the land, because there are always 7 to 10 fathoms and there is no shallow at all till you come to the point of the Bay of Virginia where there is a shallow before you come to the entrance which stretches out into the sea less than half a league. This point of bay on the south side is at 37 degrees and 10 minutes north latitude, etc.”

This letter indicates that the Spaniards did not risk entrance into small inlets, making anchorage at sea necessary, which further proves that the colonists were wise in settling on the coast that offered poor harbors. In this case they would be more secure from attack. It would seem that the Spaniards did not have the latitude of 

Roanoke but did have that of Jamestown.

There are many Indian shell mounds of various sizes on the marshes of Hog Island adjoining Cedar Island as well as mounds of shells on high ground. Many years ago the shells from the largest mounds were carried away in boats for soil improvement in other coastal counties in the State. In 1938 and 1939 shells from the mound known locally as Cedar Hill because of its cluster of large cedars, have, for the most part, been used on Cedar Island roads. This mound, illustrated herein, is 75 yards long, 50 yards wide and 10 feet deep. It concealed many human skeletons, the teeth showing no decay and pointing to a substantial diet; also there were found animal, fowl and fish bones, Indian relics such as bone arrow points, earthen ware, pots and stone axes and, at different

levels great heaps of ashes, another great kitchen midden. 

It was thought that the contents of this mound might throw some light on the early white settlers there.

Looking so intently for traces of iron implements, the various animal bones were overlooked. Some of the workmen suggested from time to time that certain bones uncovered were those of hogs and none were kept. Large bones that were thought to be those of the American buffalo, thrown aside, are now believed to have been cattle bones. A lower jaw, herein illustrated from a photograph, has been identified by the Smithsonian Institute as that of a cow. There were no domestic cattle or hogs in this territory prior to the coming of Europeans as stated by the Smithsonian Institute. Such animals were brought over by White's colonists. This is not offered as proof of the existence of White's colony in this neighborhood but it does offer an interesting speculation and is not to be dismissed as of no value.

This bone was found four feet below the surface. The cow could not have been buried there in later years by white people since it was not attached to the skeleton. 

We can reach one conclusion, namely, that the cow was consumed by those who contributed the material to raise the mound. There is nothing to prove that the mound might have been made in the early part of the 18th century and, besides, white people would not have been living then according to the Indian customs and practices. 

The mound is of earlier origin but subsequent to the arrival of White's colony who were the first to bring in cattle according to record. Therefore the mound itself was reared after the coming of the White settlers. It may be said that a cow might have been brought from a distant part of the banks by an Indian. This is not impossible but very improbable since, to date, no cattle bones have been uncovered in Indian mounds on other parts of the coast.

The generous debater will ever admit that there are two sides to a question, even though insisting that the far greater load of evidence rests with his own. In the end

it is the listeners who determine the weight of evidence and reach what is commonly called the "general consensus of opinion," and this continues until the weight of evidence shifts to the other side. 

Accepting the bare statement that White's colony was established on the present Roanoke Island without consideration of the evidence, historians and students have ceased to seek any other.

If the writer has accomplished no other purpose in presenting the facts upon which his reasonable deductions are based, he will have at least stimulated historians, students of American history, and many others to review this interesting episode in the founding of our Country. 

It is the reader who will decide whether the facts stated and the argument are sufficient to change a "general consensus of opinion".

As a lawyer addresses the jury at the end of the case— With confidence in your intelligence and integrity, I submit this case to you, feeling certain that you will render justice according to the facts.
