"THE GREATEST SUFFERER" AND TO ALL WHO SUFFER WITH HIM

Foreword 

This comforting little booklet first appeared as a series of sermons preached in the First Presbyterian Church, of Hendersonville, North Carolina, to ever increasing and more deeply appreciative congregations. I had the privilege of hearing these sermons and seeing their effect. 

The writer takes up the age-long problem of pain, suffering, and sorrow, and illumines it with faith, hope, and courage. As he speaks, one is reminded of Shannon's wonderful prayer: "My Father, for Christ's sake make me unafraid of pain; make me fearless of the dark. If it be Thy holy will for me to suffer, now in health grant me faith to realize that Thou art lovingly seeking an opportunity to manifest Thy divinest works, as Thou canst not in a life unacquainted with pain and sorrow." 

Dark days lie ahead of us. Trouble, pain, sorrow, and tears may be God's challenge to us. When they come to your friends, put this little booklet into their hands. And always remember in reading that the writer would point you to these things:

First, God's precious promise that "all things work together for good to them that love God." Have faith to trust that promise. 

Second, His assurance that neither tribulation nor distress "shall separate us from the love of Christ."

And finally, that these light afflictions, which are but for a moment, work out for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory.

JOHN MILLER WELLS, D.D., LL.D.

Introduction 

We are all one in life's experience of suffering. All belong to "the common brotherhood of pain." This is life's most perplexing problem and there is no greater need or yearning than for light, truth, comfort, peace, hope, when our time of suffering comes.

The purpose of this little book is to help meet that need or yearning by focusing light from our Father's Word upon our cross as it is focused upon His cross. It is not a book for theologians, though they will find it true to the Word and therefore the word of truth. It is for the humblest child of 

God, or one who may become His child, in home or hospital; for the sick, the crippled, the blind, the distressed, the bereaved, the tempted.

If you find help within its pages, will you not think of a fellow sufferer to whom you may "pass it on"?

L. T. W.

An Example of Suffering 

This is a vital, practical, and perplexing subject, for often the very best people are the greatest sufferers and there is no other subject on which they more eagerly seek and welcome light. Our heavenly Father has given us considerable light on this dark problem-and what He felt to be sufficient light.

We shall consider the subject under the following six main headings: "An Example of Suffering," "The Cause of Suffering," "The Use of Suffering," "The Attitude Toward Suffering," "The Sufferer and the Broken Pieces,” and "The Greatest Sufferer." 

First, then, “An Example of Suffering." And to this example God gave an entire one of the sixty-six books of His Word, a book of forty-two chapters, the book of Job. Someone has said: "Great poetry, great music, great philanthropy, have come out of souls 'heated hot with burning fears and plunged in baths of hissing tears."" So has this book of Job come to us, and so, indeed, has the entire Word of God. 

We shall have this example of Job before us throughout the series; and ask that you read just here the first two chapters of the book. Indeed, it would be well if you would read the entire book.

LET US LOOK AT JOB'S CIRCUMSTANCES 

There is no doubt that Job was a good man, one of the very best who ever lived. That fact is upon the face of the record, though we find human imperfections sticking out here and there. And God Himself testified to his goodness: "None like him in the earth"; "a perfect and an upright

II

man," evidently so far as was possible for a mere man to be; "one that feareth God, and escheweth evil."

And he was a man whom God had richly blessed with everything that heart could desire, so that he was "sitting on top of the world." He had abounding material prosperity or wealth, so that he was "the greatest of all the men of the east." He had a fine family, consisting of a wife and ten children, seven sons and three daughters, who now had grown to manhood and womanhood, for we read that they had their own houses or homes. Also he enjoyed good health and had devoted friends; and, crowning it all, he had God, who had given him all. 

But then the sledge-hammer blows of adversity began to fall upon him in rapid succession. First, he lost all of his property, one servant after another coming in to inform him of his losses. And then all of his children were snatched away from him at one time and by tragic death, the house in which they were gathered being crumbled to the ground by a cyclone. Then he lost his health, his body becoming covered with aching boils or sores. And then he lost his wife. Perhaps you had never thought of that. He did not actually lose her, as by desertion or death. But he did virtually lose her in that he lost her wifely interest and sympathy and love that he so greatly needed, which is revealed in the fact that she coldly and cruelly bade him "curse 

God, and die." And finally, he lost his friends. No, not actually so, for they came to him and sat with him, and so great was their sympathy for him that they sat speechless for an entire week. But then they began to talk; and their talk was such that they have come down to us as "Job's comforters," who are in fact tormentors pouring salt into one's wounds.

Let us note the substance of the conversation of Job and his three friends, to which most of the book is given. First, they suggested to him that all of his trials were evidence of

They said in effect: "Now, our very dear friend, you know that you have been a very wealthy man and you know the common sins of the rich; their sin of lack of charity toward the poor and their sin of even robbing them and taking the very bread out of their mouths. Are you very certain that you have not been guilty of these sins of the rich?" And to this final charge Job declared most emphatically that he had never robbed the poor and that his heart and his hand had ever been open to their cries. (Read his fine passage on eating one's morsel alone: Job 31:16-22.) Thus we say that he virtually lost his friends; for to have friends of such a distrusting, accusing, and heartless attitude is far worse than having no friends at all.

Thus Job lost all that he had-his property and his chil- Idren and his health and his wife and his friends. All that he had left was his God. Would he lose Him also? Let us leave that for the moment and proceed to ask a question.

WHO OR WHAT WAS BACK OF THE SUFFERINGS OF THIS GOOD MAN? 

We have often thought that if Job had only known the reason for his sufferings, what a help and an inspiration it would have been to him! In and through all of his trials, a battle was being fought between the powers of good and evil, between God and Satan.

And mark this! The man Job was to decide the issue, was to gain the victory for one or the other, was to glorify one or the other. And is not that true of a Christian's every temptation or trial? Is it not a battle or contest between 

God who would save him and Satan who would destroy him? Will he not decide the issue and give the victory and the glory to the one or the other? Let us see who and what were behind Job's trials, keeping in mind that this was something about which he knew absolutely nothing. That takes us back to those strange scenes in which God and Satan appear together and converse with one another, strongly reminding us of our Lord's conversation with Satan in His wilderness temptation centuries later. 

In the first scene, God calls Satan's attention to His servant Job, as if to say: "Here is one man My grace has produced with whom you have been unable to do anything-neither to enslave with sin, to tempt away from 

Me, nor to destroy!" But Satan's sneering reply was in effect: "Humph! And there's a very good reason, a purely selfish reason, and therefore an entirely sinful reason. 'Doth Job fear God for naught?' Do you think that he serves you out of pure devotion to you? I should say not! He knows on which side his bread is buttered. He serves you because you have so richly blessed him. Just take your blessings away from him now and you'll see! He will curse you to your face!" God gave him permission, and Satan brought about

Job's loss of his property and his children.

15 We should notice here one or two important things. 

Even Satan is in God's power and cannot touch one of God's servants without God's permission, for here he had to get God's permission, and in giving that permission God put a limit on him; he could touch all that Job had but not 

Job himself. And it was said after those first trials, "In all this Job sinned not, nor charged God foolishly."

In the second scene, God chidingly calls Satan's attention to His servant, saying in effect: "You see how grossly you have slandered my servant, who has remained faithful to me despite all of the trials that you have brought upon him!" 

But to that Satan whiningly replied: "Your supposedly faithful servant just hasn't been sorely enough tried. There is one thing that a man cannot stand up under and that is physical pain. Just let me touch his body now and cause him excruciating pain or suffering, and you'll see. He will curse you to your face!"

That has been a method of the Devil all down the centuries. He used it on the Christian martyrs to move them to deny Christ. He has moved the Nazis and the Japs to use it today on their helpless victims. And doubtless he seeks to use all of our ordinary physical sufferings to that diabolical end: to move us to doubt God and His love and care or to move us to other sins.

Again God gave Satan permission, and again He put a limit on him: "Behold, he is in thine hand; but save his life." Then Satan proceeded to afflict Job's body with the painful disease that gave him no rest by day or by night. 

The result of it all was that, notwithstanding Job's trials and the dark perplexities of his situation and his long unanswered cries to God for light, he kept his faith-hold upon God, as he had expressed it in his declaration to his friends, "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him.”

We naturally expect a third scene between God and Satan. But there is no third scene. Satan had done his worst

in trying and tempting God's servant. The battle between 

God and Satan for a man was ended; the man had won the victory for God and given Him the glory! In the end God blessed His servant more than He had ever done before. 

The personal word for each of us is this. Suppose that I had been in Job's place. To which-God or Satan—would 

I have given the victory and the glory? Or let us put it another way. To which do I give the victory and the glory in my every trial and temptation?

II 

The Cause of Suffering 

We have said that God's conversation with Satan concerning Job strongly reminds us of our Lord's conversation with Satan in His wilderness temptation centuries later. 

We ask that you stop here and read that account. (Matthew 4:1-11.) 

The subject "Why Good People Suffer" is one that touches every one of us. A true statement of one of Job's friends was, "Man is born unto trouble, as the sparks fly upward." And as one has said: "Men die but sorrow never dies;

The crowding years divide in vain, And the wide world is knit with ties 

Of common brotherhood in pain."

Our first main division of the subject was "An Example of Suffering," that of God's ancient servant Job. This time it is "The Cause of Suffering," which is a most important consideration. Let us begin by asking:

WHAT WAS THE CAUSE OF JOB'S SUFFERINGS? 

The answer to that question is just one word: Satan. He was the cause of every trial or loss that came to God's servant-the loss of his property, his children, and his health; the virtual loss of his wife by her coldness and lack of sympathy; and the loss of his friends by their misjudgments of him and their false accusations against him. This is not a treatise on Satan; but it seems that he was an archangel of heaven who rebelled against God and who with his fellow 17

rebels was cast out, who continued his opposition to God here on earth by introducing and promoting sin in the hearts and lives of humans, who sought the destruction of the Son of God Himself as in the wilderness temptation, and who is finally to be cast into "the lake of fire" forever. 

We say that Satan was the cause of all of Job's sufferings; and that in a twofold way, as you will see by reading the first two chapters of the book of Job. 

First, he sought and gained God's permission (and never forget that he had to get that permission) to try and to tempt God's servant (and never forget that a trial is always a temptation to doubt God and His love and care) with the hope of breaking Job's faith-hold on God and of winning him from God and of destroying him forever, as he does with every human.

But far more than that, it was Satan who actually brought Job's trials upon him. It is upon the face of the record that 

Satan went forth from the presence of God and moved the bandits to rob Job of his property and slay his servants, and that Satan used the destructive powers of nature, the lightning and the cyclone, in the further destruction of Job's property and his servants and to cause the tragic death of all of his children. And read Job 2:7: "So went Satan forth from the presence of the Lord, and smote Job with sore boils from the sole of his foot unto his crown."

And in that connection we might read the words of our 

Lord concerning an invalid woman of His time: "And ought not this woman, being a daughter of Abraham, whom Satan hath bound, lo, these eighteen years, be loosed from this bond on the sabbath day?" And Paul called his "thorn in the flesh" "the messenger of Satan." No! We are not going to put the blame for our trials and troubles upon our gracious and loving heavenly Father, but where it belongsupon His enemy and ours: Satan, the deceiver and tempter and destroyer of mankind.

LET US PROCEED FURTHER WITH OUR INDICTMENT OF SATAN AS THE ULTIMATE CAUSE OF HUMAN SUFFERINGS 

According to the Word of God and of truth, it was Satan who introduced sin into the hearts and lives of humans and who has been busily promoting it down the ages. And sin is the direct or immediate cause of all of our trials and troubles. Dr. J. H. Thornwell, noted theologian of our 

Church, said: "Never a pang would have been felt, never a sigh would have been heaved, never a groan would have been uttered, never a tear would have been shed-had not sin invaded the race."

And the entire Word of God bears out the truth of that. 

Immediately upon the sin of the first pair, God in sorrow had to tell His children of the trials that their sin would bring upon them and theirs. The whole Bible is a record of sin and consequent suffering, and its all-comprehensive and oft-used word for such suffering is "death." "The wages of sin is death."

Human experience also bears out the fact that sin is the direct cause of suffering. Look at sin operating in the individual, even some sin of the heart, such as ill will, envy, hate, and the mother sin of selfishness-the inevitable effect is always suffering and sorrow. Look at the homes whose peace and happiness are marred and destroyed by the sins of their members. Look out upon this world of war and destruction and death and suffering and sorrow-the sins of greed and revenge and hate are the direct cause of it all. "No! We are not going to put the blame upon our God of righteousness and holiness, but where it belongs-upon Satan who introduced sin, and upon sin which is the direct cause of our sufferings, and upon ourselves in so far as we yield ourselves to Satan and to sin. Both the Word of God and human experience bear out this fact.

THE SUFFERING OF GOOD PEOPLE 

But now we think you would like to say to us: "I thought we were talking about the suffering of good people!" 

In answer to that, we would say, first, that there are no good people in the strict sense of the word and we can use it only in a comparative sense. As Jesus said to the rich young ruler, "There is none good but one, that is, God." You will search the Bible in vain for even one good, in the sense of sinless or perfect, human. When God spoke of Job as “a perfect and an upright man," we think that He meant it only in a comparative sense. The very best of people are the quickest to acknowledge their sins and their daily need to pray God's forgiveness and cleansing. Therefore, since all have sins, all suffer the consequences of their sins. 

However, we should note this caution just here: be very careful how you attribute your own or another's particular suffering to particular sin on your or his part. The book of 

Job presents the need for that caution. Job's ignorant friends attributed his trials to specific sins on his part; but we know that in doing so they were utterly mistaken. 

One day Jesus' disciples asked Him concerning a man born blind, which had sinned, the man or his parents, that he had been born blind? Jesus replied that the man's affliction was not due to sin, "but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." We remember a grief-stricken mother who had lost her child by tragic death and who cried out to us, "What have I done, that this should come upon me?" She had done nothing but be as fine and faithful and devoted a Christian mother as anyone ever had. God simply had something better for her child than this world of sin and suffering and sorrow.

Again, sin brings suffering upon good people in that they are often made to suffer for the sins of others; and to us that is one of the saddest things about sin, that the innocent

THE CAUSE OF SUFFERING 

21 must often suffer for the sins of the guilty. The whole family suffers from the sin or sins of one of its members. 

And millions of good people in the world today are having to suffer because of the sins of a few men in power who plunged the world into war.

Once again, sin has caused the suffering of many of the very best in the from-sin-saving service of God, in bearing their witness to Him and to His saving truth. The Bible is full of them, over and above all of whom stands Christ the Son of God Himself. And there is the long line of Christian martyrs, a line more crowded today than ever before in the history of the world. "Yea, and all that will live godly in Christ Jesus shall suffer persecution."

And finally, sin causes the suffering of God's children in that He has to use it at times as a means of grace in the process of their own salvation from sin, as we shall see in our next consideration, "The Use of Suffering."

Our attempt at this time has been to show that the blame for all human suffering rests squarely and solely upon Satan as the ultimate cause and upon sin as the direct cause and upon ourselves in so far as we yield ourselves to Satan and sin; and that the blame rests not at all upon our gracious and loving God, who is "of purer eyes than to behold evil," who made us in His own holy likeness, who was grieved at His heart when His children became the sad victims of Satan and sin, and who did the biggest and costliest thing possible for Him to do to save us: "... gave his only begotten

Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life."

III The Use of Suffering 

Having considered an example of suffering, that of God's ancient servant Job, and the fact that sin, introduced and promoted by Satan, is the cause of suffering, we come now to the use of suffering-the use that God graciously makes of the very suffering that sin has brought upon us. And we ask that you stop here and read Hebrews 12:1-11.

OUR SALVATION FROM SIN 

In a word, we would say that God uses our sufferings for our salvation from sin. 

That fact is set forth in the word, "chasten," which occurs no less than seven times in that passage. We do not like the idea of chastening any more than we do that of taking medicine or having an operation. But the declared purpose of this chastening is correction, which is the very word that is used-correcting what is wrong or evil or sinful in our hearts and lives. And that surely is an important and essential part of our salvation. Suffering, chastening, correction, salvation!

That God uses our sufferings for this purpose is proof of two closely related facts, according to this passage of Hebrews. 

First, that God is a real Father to us. What son is there, asks the apostle, whose father, supposedly wise and devoted, does not chasten him and so correct him and save him from becoming the victim of evil and maybe a criminal before the law? No father! Then surely we can expect no less of 

God if He is our Father.

Second, and closely related to the other fact, God's use 23

of our sufferings is proof of His love for us. Some deluded parents adopt the idea that they love their child too much to correct him by chastening or painful discipline. But God loves His children too much not to do it. "For whom the 

Lord loveth he chasteneth." 

You remember, in the story The Sky Pilot, the fourteenyear-old mountain girl who had a fall from her horse, injuring her spine so that she had to be put into a cast. The sky pilot, a young missionary, came to see her and talked to her about the love of God. But she was very bitter and expressed her hatred of God for allowing her to have the accident. The missionary asked her if her own father had not allowed the doctor to hurt her dreadfully by putting her into the cast. "Yes," she said, "but he did it for my good and because he loves me." Even so, we may be very certain that our Father God allows no hurt to come to His children except for their good and because He loves them. 

MOVING US TO SEEK SALVATION 

Let us go on now to consider that God uses our suffering to move us to seek salvation. 

He uses it to open our eyes to our need of salvation. What does a severe pain in your body do for you? Thank God for that pain! Without it you would probably be a dead person in a short time. It is a warning that you are ill and must have a doctor and medicine and perhaps an operation, if you would live. So does God use the suffering caused by sin to warn us that it is a destroying power; that we are serving the cruelest of masters, whose "wages is death"; and that we are having a foretaste of the hell of sin. Some people smile tolerantly when that is spoken of, but they need only look at the suffering millions in Nazi-enslaved countries to see hell seeping up from below.

Thus seeing our need of salvation, may our eyes be opened to the salvation that God has graciously provided in Christ

25 the Saviour; and may we be moved to avail ourselves of it by repentance of sin that can only curse us, and by faith in 

Christ-acceptance of whom alone can save us.

THE PROCESS OF OUR SALVATION 

Let us proceed now to see how God, in the process of our salvation, uses the suffering caused by sin. 

First of all, let us make it clear that no suffering of any Christian is in any wise punishment for sin or the payment of the divine penalty of sin. According to God's own Word, Christ did that fully once and for all time by His sacrificial death for us upon the cross. And now to say that our punishment must be added to His is to minimize and disparage the divine sacrifice, as though it were not enough. No, the old hymn is right: "Jesus paid it all!" "The blood of Jesus

Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin." "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus." The suffering of a Christian is never punishment for his sin.

But there is a great deal more to salvation than deliverance from the punishment, penalty, and hell of sin. There is deliverance from sin itself, from its presence and power and slavery. And one thing that God uses to that end is the suffering that sin always causes, even as the sting of a hornet and the burn of fire teach us to leave them alone. God thus uses suffering in the lives of even His dearest redeemed children to teach them obedience and that they must be watchful and prayerful against temptation and sin. 

We are reminded of an incident in the mountains of Switzerland. A visitor came to a sheepfold and saw there a sheep with a broken leg. The shepherd told her that he himself had purposely broken its leg. But as he went on to explain it, she saw that it had been an act of kindness instead of cruelty. The sheep would not heed the shepherd's voice but would stray off into dangerous places, leading other

sheep astray. “And so,” he said, “I had to break its leg as

I have done with others. It is the only way; soon its leg will be well again, and it will be the most obedient sheep in my flock." Even so some of God's children will learn obedience only by the suffering that disobedience or sin causes them.

But there is much more to salvation. There is the important matter of supplanting sins of the heart or traits of evil with godly graces or virtues that are becoming to children of God and that beautify, enrich, and ennoble their characters. This Hebrews passage emphasizes especially this purpose of God in our chastening by suffering. "But he for our profit, that we might be partakers of his holiness." "Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby." These Christly graces are "the peaceable fruit." May we glance at the choicest of them. 

Faith-instead of the sin of doubt, unbelief, infidelity, atheism. Faith that is more precious than all the gold of the earth. Faith that is purified and strengthened only by exercise, or being put to the test. Faith like that of a young girl who was a Christian and a deaf-mute, to whom a thoughtless (or worse) visitor to the institution put the question, "If God is good and loves you, why did He make you without speech and hearing?” And she answered out of her golden faith, "Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight." Such faith is worth more than all speech and hearing and sight. 

Patience instead of the sin of impatience. Patience is from the Latin word that means to suffer; and it is produced only in the soil of suffering. Job was the most patient man and he was made so by his trials mingled with grace. “We glory in tribulations also," says the apostle, "knowing that tribulation worketh patience."

27 Humility-instead of the sin of arrogance or pride. 

Humility, which is a prime condition of receiving God's blessings. "God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace unto the humble." Sometimes it takes a severe blow of adversity or a critical illness to take the starch of arrogance or pride out of some people.

Gratitude-instead of the sin of ingratitude. Sometimes the only way that we are made grateful for some of God's blessings is to have them taken away from us for a while. 

Spurgeon said: "Discontented persons need a course of the bread of adversity and the water of affliction to cure them of the wretched habit of murmuring."

A tender heart of sympathy for others-instead of a heart that is hard and cold and selfish. Suffering is grace's usual way of producing this heart of sympathy. "I sat where they sat," said one of the prophets in his sympathy for a suffering people. "So out of commonest stuff 

God made him a man 

To stand in the steep roads and rough, As only he can; 

Eagerly lending an ear 

To each tale of woe, Able to answer sincere, I know, yes, I know."

And usually God's children who have suffered most have borne these precious fruits most abundantly. A potter was asked why a special piece of his ware was more valuable than the others. His answer was: "Because it has passed more often through the furnace." Usually the most tried 

Christians are the most Christly. McCheyne says: "There is a great want about all Christians who have not suffered. 

Some flowers must be broken or bruised before they emit

fragrance. All the wounds of Christ send out fragrance; all the sorrows of Christians do the same. Commend me to a bruised brother-'a broken reed'-one like the Son of Man. 

The Man of Sorrows is never far from him." 

Such Christians, revealing God's transforming grace and power, do more than any others in glorifying Him, which is what we are here for. We know a Christian woman who has been a helpless invalid for years; yet she is ever bright and cheerful and grateful, a ray of spiritual sunshine to all who know her. She is more glorifying to God than many a dozen other Christians whose trials are small and few.

IV The Attitude Toward Suffering 

Our attitude toward suffering is the way that we take itthe way we look upon it, feel about it, respond to it. Robert 

Quillen gives us this homely illustration. He causes one of his characters to say, "My pa raised mules and broke the colts before he sold 'em. The harness never hurt a young mule, but he'd fight it and lather himself and kick like he was sufferin' dreadful. But as soon as he got reconciled to it and quit strugglin', he got along easy. It wasn't the harness or load that hurt him, but just the way he looked at it." And the way we look at our trials is of very great importance for two or three reasons.

OUR ATTITUDE REVEALS TO OURSELVES AND TO OTHERS WHAT WE REALLY ARE 

It reveals the kind of character that we have or are; whether or not we have such fine traits as faith and courage and patience and humility, enabling us to bear or to stand up to and under trials, even as gold is seen to be gold when it is put into the white heat of the fire. Or as when a diamond is seen to be a diamond when it is put to the water test. The jeweler tells us that only a real diamond will continue to shine or sparkle when it is submerged in water. 

Likewise does our true character show itself when it is put into the fire of trial or under the water of sorrow. 

Our attitude reveals also our true relationship to God the Father. It indicates whether we are truly His children in the gospel sense of the word, having toward Him the spirit of true children, the trust in Him and the love for Him that make us submissive to His wise and holy and loving will, 29

which is the spirit of every regenerated, born again child of God. Not all are children of God in that sense, and there is no surer or severer test of it than our attitude toward our trials.

In the twelfth chapter of Hebrews the writer presents the fact that God shows Himself a true Father when in love He chastens us to correct us and so to save us from sin and its woeful consequences. And in that connection he brings out equally clearly and emphatically the fact that we show whether or not we are His true children by our response to His chastening. He says that we reverenced our "fathers of our flesh" for chastening us when we needed it; and just as surely will we have the same attitude toward the chastening of the Father God-if we are His children. 

Your own son, if he trusts and loves you, will submit to your discipline; but you had better not try it on somebody else's son whom you have no right to discipline—you will be apt to have a fight on your hands. Some people, even professed Christians, under trial act like somebody else's children, not God's.

Contrast the attitudes of Job and his wife. His attitude was expressed in his, "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." It revealed the gold of his character and the fact that he was a true son of the Almighty. But what of his wife? Her attitude was expressed in her cold and cruel word to him: "Curse God, and die." The poet represents her as chiding him thus: "Dost thou not see that thy devotion's vain? 

What hast thy prayers procured but woe and pain? 

Hast thou not yet thy interest understood, Perversely righteous, absurdly good? 

Those painful sores and all thy losses show 

How Heaven regards the foolish saint below."

Thus we say that our attitude toward our suffering reveals to ourselves and to others our true character and true relationship to God the Father.

OUR ATTITUDE DETERMINES THE EFFECT OF OUR TRIALS UPON US 

As we have seen previously, God's gracious design or purpose is to use our trials as a means to our salvation from the sin which has brought them upon us; to awaken us to our need of salvation and to the salvation which He has provided; and to enrich and ennoble our characters with such graces as faith and patience and humility and gratitude, and give us hearts of tenderness and sympathy. 

But trials do not so work in everybody. On some they are utterly wasted, the greatest wastage in human life. On others they are far worse than wasted; instead of producing these graces, the trials produce their very oppositesunbelief, impatience, arrogance, ingratitude, and hardness and coldness of heart. The medicine, instead of curing, poisons; the surgeon's knife, instead of saving, kills; or, as someone has said, "The same sunshine that melts ice hardens clay." What is it that determines the effect, good or bad? It is our attitude; whether it be that of God's children, accepting our trials as His disciplines for our good; or as somebody else's children, rebelling against them and against Him. 

Let us take the Bible illustration of the potter and the clay, in which He is the potter and we are the clay and His design is to make us as vessels of service and beauty. If we lie quietly in His hands, even when He places us in the fire, He will make of us that kind of vessel. But suppose that the

clay, like ourselves, could move and that it squirmed and resisted and even jumped out of the potter's hands. Would it ever become any kind of a vessel at all?

Job reveals the attitude necessary to a good effect of our trials upon us in his, “Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." And so does the Apostle Paul-severely tried as he was and the finest product of Christianity, the Gospel, and the Great Potter-in such statements as, "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love 

God"; and "we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience," etc.; and his statement concerning his "thorn in the flesh" in II Corinthians 12:5-9. 

Dr. Joseph Fort Newton has said: "The natural reaction to pain, suffering, and defeat is resentment, rebellion, but that turns defeat into disaster. Look into the lives of those whom you most admire for their worth and beauty of character, and you will find that they faced loss, difficulty, despair, and won out. When we resist events, two tragedies occur-the event and our rebellion against it. When we ask 

God to help us accept the event, there is no tragedy, but a triumph. The cross so accepted always leads to a resurrection."

We came across this nugget while rambling through Tolstoi's voluminous War and Peace. He tells of a man, once rich and selfish, who was taken prisoner and had to suffer the bitter cold and live on horse-flesh. And he quotes him as saying: "People speak of misfortunes and sufferings; but if at this moment I were asked, 'Would you rather be what you were before you were taken prisoner or go through all of this again? then for heaven's sake let me again have captivity and horse-flesh." And Tolstoi adds this significant statement: "We imagine that when we are thrown out of our usual ruts, all is lost; but it is only then that what is new and good begins." May our trials begin something new and good in us.

OUR ATTITUDE DETERMINES WHETHER WE SHALL USE OUR TRIALS FOR GOD OR AGAINST HIM 

Never forget it, our attitude for or against Him means for or against ourselves and others, even those who are nearest and dearest to us.

Job used his trials for God and for himself and for countless others by showing in himself the power of religion, the power of faith, the power of God, to give victory over temptation, sin, and Satan at their strongest; to enable others to be true and loyal and faithful to God and the right under the most adverse circumstances; to sustain and give comfort, peace, and hope amid the fiercest trials imaginable.

Jesus assigned witnessing unto Him as the life mission of every Christian; and the witness that counts most is that which is borne in the fire of trial or from a cross of one's own. Today is the biggest opportunity that many Christians will have to bear such witness to Him, and many are doing it. For instance, an army officer who censors the letters of the men in the service wrote this: "So many men writing to their fathers, mothers, or wives have shown a faith that was a living and real thing, that I began to feel that I was missing the biggest thing in life. It changed me for good.”

Elsie Robinson, who writes a daily column for the newspapers, had an only child, a son, who was taken by death; and for a time her light went out, drowned in sorrow, but she recovered and found a new peace and even joy. Others in bereavement, knowing of her experience, wrote to her for counsel and consolation, asking her what to do, what she had done. And she said that in her sorrow the thought had come to her that now that she hadn't her own son to bestow her mother-love upon, she could bestow it upon sons of others who need a mother's love. And thus has she turned her cross into an instrument of service to others and to Him whose own Cross is the hope of the world.

The Sufferer and the Broken Pieces 

Good people, even the best, have received smashing blows of adversity, loss, bereavement. But God has enabled them to get up out of the wreckage, make the most of the broken pieces, and go on to live lives inspiring to others and glorifying to Him. Even as finest art-glass windows have been made of broken pieces good for nothing else. Or as Michaelangelo shaped his "Statue of David" out of a shaft of marble that others had cast aside as of no value. Or, as someone has put it: "God's greatest victories are won on the fields of our greatest defeats."

In the twenty-seventh chapter of The Acts, we have one of the finest descriptions in all literature of a storm at sea. The point in the account we wish to stress is that those men, when their ship was wrecked, did not quit or give up in despair; but, with heroic courage and determination, made the best of what they had left, even the "broken pieces of the ship," and, by their own efforts and God's providence, reached the shore in safety. There is a fine message here for those who have suffered severe losses from the storms of life and have been left with nothing but the broken pieces of what they once had or were—a message of making the best of what is left, of going on in faith and hope and courage, with the assurance that with God's help they may reach the shore of their heart's desire.

A FINANCIAL STORM 

We may have suffered a financial storm, and our ship, instead of "coming in," is driven and broken to pieces on 35

the rocks of adversity. Are we going to give up in despair?

Or are we going to trust in God and do the best we can with what we have left?

Dr. Archibald Rutledge tells of a one-armed man who made a living for his family by making and selling oars. 

One day his humble little home was burned to the ground, and there was no insurance. He was found with his wife and children sorrowfully surveying the ruins. But his first words were: "She's gone; but I will start to rebuild tomorrow." Says Dr. Rutledge, “He had lost every material thing he owned, even the simple tools of his trade. But he had not lost his heart." That is the spirit that everyone needs in the day of adversity-the heart to start to build again with the "broken pieces" of a wrecked fortune.

A STORM OF PHYSICAL AFFLICTION 

Now and then we see a man or a woman, a boy or a girl, walking haltingly but bravely along the street, evidently the victim of infantile paralysis or of accident. And the thought comes to us: there is one who is making the most of the broken pieces, is making shore on the "broken pieces of the ship." Bartlett's book, They Dared to Live, tells of thirtyseven men and women who, though severely handicapped, lived greatly and courageously: scientists, explorers, educators, artists, musicians, authors, statesmen, and missionaries.

A STORM OF TEMPTATION 

This is a storm that from time to time strikes even the best with terrific force. And when they fall before it, they awake to find themselves in the midst of moral wreckage. 

But those who know the Lord and His mercy and grace turn at once to Him in penitence and prayer. They receive

The Bible is full of instances like that-instances in the lives of even its greatest and its best. There was David, “a man after God's own heart," who committed adultery and then virtually murder to cover it up. There was Peter, who thrice denied his Lord with cursing. And there was Saul, the self-esteemed moral paragon who fell from his moral pedestal to the heinous sins of blaspheming Christ and of persecuting Christians to the death, but who became Paul the apostle. We remember how our Lord pictures all such sinners in His matchless parable of the Prodigal Son. 

And this is the common experience of Christian life. When not watchful and prayerful, the best are tempted and fall into sin of one kind or another. But God's grace enables them to make the most of the "broken pieces" of their moral selves.

TWO REFLECTIONS 

May we conclude this phase of the subject with two reflections. One is that nature itself sets us the example of making the most of the "broken pieces." Dr. Archibald

Rutledge strikingly presents this fact in his article, "You Can't Put Out the Sun." And what a significant title that is! He bids us go out and view the desolation that marks the pathway of a storm: bent and bruised and broken and mud-covered foliage and flowers. But nature does not give up and quit. She sets to work at once restoring, renovating, re-creating; washing away the stains with her gentle showers, lifting up the fallen with the drawing rays of the sun, reviving and mending everywhere, until ere long you would never know that there had been any storm at all. He makes us see the same spirit in God's dumb creatures, telling us of two hummingbirds that built their little nest on the tip end of a dead limb. And then came the storm that sent a higher limb crashing down upon the little nest home, bringing it to the ground. But the very next morning the little birds were up bright and early, building another nest on the broken tip of the same branch. "Tiny creatures," says Dr. 

Rutledge; "how brave and sound was their philosophy: that a night of disaster is always followed by a dawn of redemption."

Our other reflection is that God Himself sets us the example. His cherished enterprise there in Eden, of human beings to love Him and be loved by Him, was wrecked and smashed by sin. But God did not give up. With marvelous grace and love, He at once set to work redeeming the "broken pieces" of sin-wrecked humanity. And to this end

He gave the best He had, even His only begotten Son. “And the vessel that He made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter: so He made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter to make it." Of Him wrote George Mathe-

The Greatest Sufferer 

Thus far we have considered the outstanding Bible example of suffering; sin, introduced and promoted by Satan, as the direct cause of suffering; God's use of suffering as one of His means unto our salvation; the importance of the attitude toward suffering, revealing as it does our true character and relationship to God and determining the effect of our suffering upon us, whether we shall use it for God or against Him; and the sufferer making the most of his sufferings.

Now have you not had the feeling all the way through that God Himself is on trial in this matter, that He is not altogether blameless for the tragedy that has befallen His children, that He needs to be defended, and that the evidence in His favor is not wholly satisfactory? We ourselves confess to such a feeling and such a judgment. We might ask, who is the creature to sit in judgment on the Creator? And yet God has given us minds and reason and judgment; and 

He evidently expects us to use them even with reference to Himself. Surely He has nothing to hide if He is the God of righteousness and justice and love that He declares Himself to be. Let us consider what has been presented in God's favor, and the rebuttal or reply that His accuser would surely make to it.

In God's favor, is His absolute hatred of all sin; the salvation from sin that He has graciously provided and offers to everyone; and His use of the suffering caused by sin as a means to awaken us to our need of salvation and to move us to accept His salvation. What would God's accuser answer 43

to that? He would doubtless say: "That is all very well. But why did not God prevent any need of salvation in the first place by keeping sin out of His world and away from His children? You say that He is all-powerful. Then why did He not do that?"

In reply to that, God's defender would say that God in creating mankind wanted, not puppets or dolls, but children or persons even as He Himself is a person; that as such He created them free agents, with wills of their own to make their own choices; that, when good and evil were put before them, they chose evil instead of good; and that therefore, not God, but they are responsible. But would not God's accuser come back with this rejoinder: "Could not God have made man both a free agent and yet morally strong enough to resist temptation—even as 'the man Christ Jesus,' who was surely a free agent and yet 'was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin'? Why did He not do that?"

God's defender would then bring Satan, the tempter, into the case, saying that Satan was too strong for man, even the moral creature that God had made him, to resist. But the accuser would have a double-barreled reply to that. He would say: "Surely God is stronger than Satan and He could have enabled man to resist him! And more than that, does not God tell us that in the end of time He is going to confine Satan to 'the lake of fire'? Then why did He not confine him in the beginning and keep him away from His world and His children altogether?"

Do you not see that, as strong as all such arguments in God's favor may be, a strong reply can be made to every one of them? That they are not sufficient? That God does not yet have an air-tight case of defense in this matter? And we are led to wonder if there is anything more that can be presented in His favor, or if He must stand condemned at the bar of human judgment.

There is one other fact about God that has brought us

THE GREATEST SUFFERER 

45 considerable satisfaction as we have thought about God's case and have sought to defend Him to our own minds. 

And that fact is this: God allowed Satan and sin and suffering, although He knew beforehand that He Himself would be the greatest sufferer from it. Now if that be true, it would seem to throw a new light upon God's case. It would have been different if He had stood off in His heaven safe and secure and untouched by the tragedy that befell His children.

But when we say that He was the greatest sufferer from it, is that true? Let us remember that He is infinite in His being or nature, and therefore everything about Him is infinite; for instance, His capacity for suffering. Therefore any suffering that He endures is infinite, boundless, beyond estimation or imagination.

With that in mind, let us consider something of His suffering. There was the suffering of grief, the deepest sorrow of the heart. His Word tells us that as He looked upon the sinfulness of His children, "it grieved him at his heart." (Genesis 6:6.) He was like a good and devoted father of a godless, wicked, prodigal son given to drunkenness and gambling and other immoralities and forever in the toils of the law, a shame and a disgrace to the family. Nothing, not even the death of his son, could cause a devoted father such grief as that. So it was with God the Father: "it grieved him at his heart."

But His chief suffering was the sacrifice that He made for us, for our salvation in and through Christ. We cannot present that suffering here. It is throughout His Word, as blood is throughout your body. Its symbol is the Cross. And never forget that His chief suffering was not of the body but of the heart, soul, spirit; as expressed in Gethsemane, “My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death"; and as expressed on the cross, "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" The prophecy of His Word was, "Thou shalt make

his soul an offering for sin." Let God's accuser never forget it, that when he makes any charge against God, he must go out to God on the Cross to do it!

The fact that God was the greatest sufferer and that He knew that He would be, should seal our lips and keep us from advancing any accusation against Him for allowing the human tragedy of sin. We should say, "There is mystery here, much that we do not understand, and we must seek further light." And then we should proceed to inquire why

He allowed it when He knew that He would suffer most from it. His Word seems to give a twofold answer to that. 

First, He did it because He knew that through it the love of His heart would be made known to His universe for all eternity as it never would have been made known otherwise. "God is love," His Word tells us. But how do we know it? How does love make itself known? Chiefly by sacrifice. Love of country. A mother's love. And God's love.

He has made His love known by the varied loves that He has implanted in human hearts and by His blessings of many kinds. These, however, cost Him nothing. It is from Calvary, the Cross, His sacrifice of Himself for us, that His love shines forth in its splendor and glory and will continue to shine on down the endless years of eternity. (John 3:16 and I John 3:16.) There never would have been the knowledge of His redeeming love without the Cross; and there never would have been the Cross without sin and our consequent need of salvation or redemption.

And second, He did it because He knew that thereby He would win our love for Him as He never would or could have otherwise. It is love that begets love. A mother's love, revealed in her sacrifice of herself, wins the love of her child. And God's love, revealed in His sacrifice of Himself, wins the love of His children. "We love him," says the apostle of love, "because he first loved us."

And thus has He wrought much good even out of evil,

47 good that never would have been wrought otherwise: the revealing of His love for us and the winning of our love for Him. In heaven there would have been angels, and doubtless human souls; but there would not have been a single redeemed soul, had there been no need of redemption. Christ the Lord would be there; but not Christ the Saviour whom we love and adore, had there been no need of salvation. 

Many sinners will go down to the hell of sin accusing, blaming, cursing God. But other sinners, a host of them which no man can number, with believing and penitent and grateful and loving and joyful hearts, sing here upon earth, "Amazing grace! how sweet the sound, That saved a wretch like me!" 

And in heaven above, throughout eternity, they will join in the song of the redeemed "Unto him that loved us, and washed us from our sins in his own blood."
