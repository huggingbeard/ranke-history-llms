FOREWORD 

ERHAPS the most significant feature of this informal study and life story of Dr. Francis E. Townsend is the fact that it is the work of a man of independent and undeclared views regarding the merits of Old Age Revolving Pensions, who has never in any way been affiliated with the Townsend organization.

The author, a newspaperman whose writings have been featured by a great metropolitan newspaper for fourteen years, was assigned by his paper to talk at length with Dr. Townsend and to write a series of articles which would be entirely without bias.

He has, in providing the printer with the manuscript for this booklet, retained the form in which the series was originally written, although he freely confesses that in so doing he violates all life history traditions. To recast the story would, he believes, be to lose much of the flavor of Dr. Townsend's simple, frank, and winning personality.

Decided to Become a Doctor 

And so, after a few years of wandering, Francis Townsend pulled his chaotic spirit back into one piece and decided that it was high time he headed himself for somewhere definite. I guess, in the spot he was in, one ambition seemed to be about as unattainable as another. He decided that he would take a course in medicine and become a doctor. "By saving, and not eating what I should have eaten, and not living as I should have lived, I'd managed to scratch together savings of exactly one hundred dollars over a period of two years. 

Nothing to shout paeans of joy about. I knew that hundred would never buy me a diploma. But I made up my mind flatly that it would be a first payment on one. "I went to the dean of the medical school in Omaha and I said, 'I want to study medicine.' "He knew by my appearance and my tone that I was no scion of comfortable family. He knew without asking what my problem would be and that I'd come to ask his help to find a solution. "'How much money have you got?' "'One hundred dollars.' "Well, it turned out that baled hay was worth $40 a ton in gold, the medium of exchange, and that looked good to us. We agreed on the idea of leasing a big tract of land and raising wild oats and barley to get hay for the market. "We bought two teams, a hay rake and a mower, and a tent for living. We grew our hay and cut and cured it, and piled it, so that it wouldn't bleach out too much. We bought a hay presser; it had a box lid at the top and a plunger that lifted from the bottom, squeezing upwards to make the bales. Hay bales were two by four by three feet. "We labored on our hay all summer, and in the fall we did team hauling to Los Angeles, 25 miles away. We made the round trip in 24 hours. Yes, we sweated to make this a real start in life; lived simple and cooked our own food. "And then, suddenly, when we had several hundred tons of hay in the barn, the real estate boom collapsed in Los Angeles. 

Far from selling our hay, we couldn't give it away, and we had to sell our mules for what we could get. From being independent farmers we had to start out again as hired hands." 

That, as Doc admitted, was not so good. Still, it was better than the plight of an independent business man of today who, when he goes bankrupt, has no easy time finding even a hiredman's job for himself.

It's the waste of honest labor that hurts Doc, as he thinks back to those old days; the labor that was hardest when it came to piling the bales of hay in the barn, liſting them with tensed muscles, sweat dripping from the forehead and chin and moist-hot body soaking your shirt as you climbed from bale to bale to put this one at the top, then later to climb again and put another above it. 

And, as he says, it's waste of honest labor that's hurting the world today; men wandering the streets, lining up for a pittance, looking longingly toward mills whose whistles seldom blow. [ 42 ]

CHAPTER VII 

T IS AS good a place as any to leave the career of Doc Townsend. Not but what there are a few more things to say, but the rest is modern stuff. The story of the boy who set out to make his mark in the world is told. He had his hired hand days in California; he had ticklish work driving a hard-to-handle team, before he was given the easier task of driving the family carriage. 

And then, as the beauties of California began to dwindle under the pressure of hard-cash realities, he turned his eyes back toward the Middle West; he went back, and homesteaded, and taught school, and failed in finances, and wandered about for hundreds of miles, from State to State, a vagabond. Till, at last, determination flared and he made of himself a doctor who was to give years of precious service to humanity.

Of his own flesh and blood Doc Townsend has only the one son. The daughter is an adopted daughter, and the twins that came to bless the lives of Doc and Mrs. Townsend died after a few months of life. But in his son Robert, Doc takes keen pride.

His Slim Diet "You know," remarked a friend, "Doc only eats twice a day: breakfast, and dinner at 4."

AFTERWORD 

In the foreword to this booklet I referred to myself as a "man of independent and undeclared views regarding the merits of Old 

Age Revolving Pensions." I think that that is the best way to leave the matter. The study of Dr. Townsend which you have read is an objective piece of work, and no writer who permits himself to be involved one way or the other in a controversy can succeed in maintaining an objective point of view. The neutral attitude, of course, is never popular with those engaged in controversy, but it has its value, even to them. 

At any rate, there is one matter on which I am not at all neutral, as the reader may have discovered, for I have said it several times: my liking for Doc Townsend. Undeclared though 

I remain with regard to the Plan itself—and no intelligent person will assume that my neutral position indicates opposition, for an honest man, when he is against a thing, says so, and I make some small claim to honesty-I come right out on the line for its father. 

I should be unafraid to trust him with all my moneys, with my honor, with the safeguarding of all I hold dear. I believe in his integrity.

I still remain neutral, because I have an interest in Doc's work, and believe that a neutrality that reports the pros and cons on the issue is far more worthwhile (certainly more interesting to me) than a taking of sides. Propaganda is propaganda; the very word has taken on an insidious meaning in this country, because propaganda has been insidious, has been half-truth-telling, has been a deliberate effort to arrive at an end whatever the means. 

In what I have written about Doc, in what I am writing in this personal afterword, there is no shadow, even, of propagandanothing but observation and impression. That means that within the limits of the human mind and conscience to recognize such, it is truth.

And so I think I am now at the end, even for an afterword. [46]
