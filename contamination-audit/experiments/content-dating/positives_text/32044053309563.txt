INTRODUCTION 

One hundred and fifteen years ago, a blond, robust, mustachioed American established a consulate in a temple in the village of Shimoda and thus became the first foreign official accredited to Japan. His name, of course, was 

Townsend Harris-a distinguished citizen of this city. Most of our knowledge of the first years of official contact with Japan is found in his diary. What makes it most appropriate to invoke his memory in this setting tonight is the fact that the publication of the diary of Townsend Harris was one of the first and most important contributions of this Society. We are here tonight in support of its latest and perhaps most ambitious project-an expanded program to be carried out from the new Japan House.

The occasion was his call on the Shogun, the first by any foreign official. This, therefore, may be the first flag of any foreign nation ever displayed in Tokyo. It may not surprise some of you who are familiar with the pattern of our textile trade to learn that it was made in Japan-reportedly in Shimoda. 

In the years since Townsend Harris carried that flag to Tokyo our relations with Japan have undergone vast changes. I will not dwell tonight on this wellknown history. But one thing is clear. As we move into the decade of the 1970's, Americans recognize that a close and friendly relationship between Japan and the United States is vital to the kind of world both of us want. In the Pacific in particular we recognize that the relationship between our countries will be the most important single factor bearing on future events in the area. 

The maintenance of peace and the course of political events in Asia and the Pacific are likely to concern both our countries for the rest of this century. The problems involved will have to be resolved in concrete situations and in differing circumstances. 

But if Japan and the United 

States go separate ways, there can be no doubt that those problems would be magnified for each of us and for the area as a whole. Working together, however, and in cooperation with others, Japan and the United States can make a decisive contribution toward stability and strength of purpose in the Pacific and toward the growth of economically healthy and politically representative societies. 

OKINAWA REVERSION 

President Nixon has therefore sought to put our relationship with Japan on a stable and durable basis. The historic ceremony which took place just 2 weeks ago, on June 17, was a major contribution to that process. On that day, Foreign Minister Aichi in Tokyo and I in Washington signed a treaty which, when ratified, will restore the administration of Okinawa to Japan. 

The formalities of concluding that treaty were uniquely suitable to the importance of the occasion. By means of a flawless

live television production carried to both nations via the Pacific satellite, our peoples for the first time could watch their representatives-separated by thousands of miles-simultaneously sign a major international agreement. I understand that because of the intense interest in Japan well over half the Japanese population watched this event as it occurred; millions did so here as well. 

The treaty symbolizes a reaffirmation of common political and security interests. It thus confirms the agreement reached. 

by President Nixon and Prime Minister Sato in November 1969. 

With that agreement, the United States recognized that it was in neither our national character nor our national interest to continue to administer a territory which has been historically associated with Japan and whose people desired to rejoin it. Japan in turn recognized that a U.S. military presence in Okinawa was necessary for the defense of the Far East, including Japan and neighboring areas such as Korea and Taiwan, whose security

Japan regards as important to its own. 

The Okinawa agreement, historic as it is, is but an evolution in a close relationship defined in a broader and even more basic document-the 1960 Treaty of 

Mutual Cooperation and Secur ity. That treaty is not, as is sometimes supposed, merely the basis for a military alliance. It was no accident that the word "cooperation" preceded "security" in the title. The men who framed the treaty felt-as we do today-that our two governments should strengthen our bonds of peace and friendship and cooperate to bring economic benefits to both of our peoples.

MUTUAL ECONOMIC PROBLEMS The resolution of the Okinawa problem, the single greatest source of political tension between Japan and the United 

States over the past 20 years, highlights the fact that, for the rest of this decade at least, our bilateral relations are likely to be dominated not only by matters of mutual security but also by economic affairs. Our bilateral trade-over $10.5 billion, the second largest in the world-is the most dramatic example of our economic interdependence. It is perhaps inevitable that, with a trade of such magnitude, there should be economic difficulties such as those we are facing on synthetic textiles. In most respects such problems arise from the success rather than failure of our growing economic association. Even so, it is no less important to find prompt, effective solutions to them before they become even greater political problems. 

We also need to be candid in realizing that it is not simply the size of our economic relationship which underlies our difficulties. 

There are other problems. We have many things in common-a democratic society, freedom of expression, a broad spectrum of political parties and views, universal suffrage, and open elections. But it is also true that we are not bound by a common culture, a common language, a common religion, or a common history. These cultural differences require both of us to devote special energy to our cooperation.

Obsolete Stereotypes 

There is, moreover, a kind of perception gap arising from the phenomenally rapid growth of the Japanese economy. Among some Japanese there may be a tendency to view Japan as still struggling to make its way in a world of more more economically powerful nations and thus requiring close government support of industry. Among some Americans the tendency may be just the reverse-to see Japan's economic strength as growing so massively that it has become a major threat to our own economic position.

Both these stereotypes are obsolete. On the one hand, Japan no longer needs the panoply of economic protection that seemed appropriate only a decade ago.

For in that decade its gross national product tripled until today it is the third largest in the world, after the United States and the Soviet Union. Japan's per capita output has already exceeded that of the Soviet Union and is comparable to that of Western Europe. Also during the decade, Japan's exports quadrupled. Its trade balance shifted from deficit to substantial surplus. And its foreign exchange reserves have more than tripled. 

Japan has become a major power, both politically and economically, and we must and do treat it as one. At the same time, it goes without saying that Japan must act with the sense of responsibility appropriate to its new position. Its economic policies, for example, must be subject to the same criteria as those of the other major developed countries. In our bilateral eco-

nomic relationships Japan cannot expect privileges in the United States which it is not itself prepared to extend. 

Fortunately, Japan is coming to recognize that policies of economic nationalism are no longer compatible with its own interests, with those of its major partners, or with the requirements of the international economic system.

Americans, on the other hand, need to realize that Japan has not waged and won a war of trade on the United States and that the American economy is strong and soundly based. With only twice the population of Japan we have five times the gross national product. While both are low, our investments in Japan are over five times Japan's investments in the United States. 

And Japanese goods are 15 percent of total imports, whereas U.S.goods constitute 29 percent of Japan's total imports. our

Americans also need to recognize more clearly the major benefits of our economic relations with Japan. Japan is, after Canada, our largest market. It is also the largest importer of U.S. farm products, buying $1 billion worth in 1970. Moreover, it is by far our fastest growing market; during the 1960's our exports to Japan rose almost three times as fast as our exports to the rest of the world.

Finally, it is too little understood in this country that Japan's economy is a major source of strength, and hence of stability, in the rest of Asia. Japan is the largest supplier of goods to Asia and is fast becoming the largest market for the exports of 

Asian countries.

If, in both our countries, we can eliminate the stereotyped attitudes I have referred to, then the job of resolving the real issues that confront us will be much easier. I am glad to hear that the recent Washington meeting of Japanese and American business leaders, the Eighth

Japan-U.S. Businessmen's Con ference, already is contributing to a greater understanding of our mutual economic problems. The newly established private economic advisory councils on each side are continuing this important dialogue in industrial diplomacy. And we are looking forward to welcoming our Japanese colleagues to Washington in September for the eighth Cabinetlevel meeting on trade and economic affairs.

Trade Imbalance 

A major reflection of our economic problems from the U.S. point of view is our recent persistent trade deficit-$1.2 billion last year and running at more than twice that rate so far this year.

The causes of this imbalance are diverse and complex. They include differences in labor costs, higher rates of productivity in many Japanese industries, the effect on our competitive trade position of recent U.S. rates of inflation, the effect on its im ports of the current slowdown in Japan's economic growth, and Japanese restrictions on imports. 

The American consumer, of course, benefits from low-cost, high-quality imports from Japan. 

While it may be questioned whether anybody in this august assemblage owns a Japanese motorcycle, there are probably few of us here who do not own a 

Japanese tape recorder, bicycle, transistor radio, or camera.

Nevertheless, when Japanese imports increase at a rapid rate in a short period of time-as they have in a number of fields-they can raise difficulties for particular American firms, workers, and communities. And while we recognize that Japan has placed restraints on increases of some of these imports, we cannot be indifferent to the industrial and employment problems which they continue to cause us. 

We do not look for exact balances in bilateral trade nor to restrictive trade patterns as the solution. In fact our desire is for expanded two-way trade. We are prepared to compete on equal terms.

We are, however, concerned about the degree to which our competitive position in Japan is weakened by a variety of Government restrictions. While Japan is making efforts, the fact is that the rate of progress toward their removal has not been sufficient to affect the balance of trade. 

Quotas are maintained, for example, on such sophisticated products as large computers and integrated circuits, on which free competition might be expected in the light of highly developed 

American and Japanese technological capabilities.

The U.S. Government has made an intensive effort to develop a better degree of reciprocity in our economic relations with Japan. We have sought the reduction and removal of Japan's trade and capital restrictions, including those on direct investment. The response of the Japanese Government to these efforts has been encouraging. Programs to ease restrictions on trade and investment have been started. 1205093

Japan has reduced the number of commodities subject to import quotas. A further reduction takes effect today, and more reductions are planned for later this year. We have asked for, and expect, remaining quotas to be removed. Japan has opened new domestic industries to foreign investment. Such steps to liberalize investment have helped pave the way for Chrysler's recently announced joint venture with

Mitsubishi Motors Corporation. 

Japan has also liberalized outward capital flows and tourism expenditures to the point where more Japanese visitors come to the United States than visitors from any other overseas country. 

Unfortunately, the pace of

Japan's economic achievements abroad has continued to outstrip the effect of its liberalization programs in opening its markets at home; and the imbalance of trade, instead of lessening, is continuing to grow. A more rapid movement toward truly reciprocal policies is therefore needed. 

JAPAN'S POSITIVE ACTIONS 

We believe that the Japanese Government is itself recognizing this. For example, early this month the Government announced an eight-point program to liberalize imports, reduce tax support for exports, permit more foreign investment in Japan and more Japanese investment abroad, and expand economic aid. We welcome this program as a hopeful sign that Japan believes, as we do, that a nation's interests are best served by expanding economic opportunities rather than by contracting them. The results will be of vital interest not only to our Government officials but also to our business, labor, and legislative leaders. The early and substantial implementation of the program would do much to demonstrate Japan's intention to meet its responsibilities as a major economic power. 

In a related area, we applaud Japan's resolve to expand economic assistance to underdeveloped countries and to increase contributions for this purpose to international organizations. The 

Japanese Government is publicly committed to a target by 1975 of 1 percent of its gross national product to public and private financial flows to developing countries. This could amount to about $4 billion. But only a quarter of Japan's current flows is in official development assistance; most of the rest is in commercial-type loans and guarantees. Many Japanese and other 

Asians hope that Japan's expanded efforts will include a substantial increase in the proportion of Government assistance. We share that hope. 

The emphasis I have given in these remarks to our economic relations with Japan attests to my belief in their importance in the years to come. The continued health of those relations is vital to the free international trading system which the United

States led in building and from which Japan has benefited materially. It is also vital to the stable and peaceful Pacific toward which the policies of both the United States and Japan are directed. The task before us during the 1970's is to resolve our economic problems in the same creative spirit in which we have resolved our political problems. 

Two years ago, on my first visit to Japan as Secretary of

State, both delegations to our 

Cabinet-level meeting on trade and economic affairs agreed that our trade problems "could be overcome in a spirit of mutual understanding and common interest." Nothing since then has caused me to change my mind, because the essential fact of our relationship is that our transitory differences are far outweighed by our common interests. 

I leave you with a a final thought When Townsend Harris stepped ashore in 1856, he carried a letter from President 

Pierce to the Emperor of Japan which stated:

It has occurred to me that the existing Treaty between the United States and Japan might be soon amended so as to secure greater facilities for the exchange of the rich and varied productions of Your Majesty's vast Empire for those of the United States.

From that beginning we have today a yearly flow of over $10 billion of "rich and varied productions" between our countries.

There is no valid reason why this exchange should not continue to grow. The United States, with the world's largest and most productive economy, and Japan, with the world's most dynamic, have much to offer each other.

But the exchange must be conducted on a basis of harmony and mutual accommodation. 

That is the mutual challenge of of our diplomacy. I believe-and

I trust-that we can meet it.
