INTRODUCTION 

A compact is an agreement between two states, approved by the legislatures thereof and by the Congress of the United States, whereby the signatory states consent to certain procedures affecting interstate relations. 

The legal basis of interstate compacts is found in Article I, Section 10 of the Constitution of the United States.

In the early history of our country the interstate compact was used mostly to settle boundary disputes between states. In more recent years compacts have been negotiated for interstate seaports and harbors, for bridges, for forest fire protection, for the control of stream pollution, for the supervision of parolees, for higher education and for the settlement of other interstate problems.

There is presently in effect a compact between the states of Washington and Oregon dealing with fishery problems in the Columbia River, one on higher education, for criminal identification, an interstate oil compact and others.

BRIEF HISTORY OF THE COLUMBIA INTERSTATE COMPACT 

1909-The Washington State legislature passed a resolution providing for a compact between the states of Washington and Oregon relative to the waters of the Columbia River. 

1925-Congress enacted a consent bill for a compact for the states of Washington, Oregon and Montana. No agreements were made in conformity with these actions. 

1943-The governors of five Pacific Northwest states, meeting in Boise, Idaho, adopted a resolution recommending an interstate compact relating to the division and use of waters of interstate streams of the Pacific Northwest. 

1949-The governors of the several Pacific Northwest states discussed the interstate compact idea and Governor Langlie was asked to take the lead in the development of a compact. At the Governor's request J. V. Rogers, director of the Department of Conservation and Development, named a committee to make preliminary studies and recommendations as to procedure. 

January 31, 1950-The committee composed of Donald Webster and Joshua 

Vogel of the University of Washington, Fred Cunningham, Spokane attorney,

Lars Langloe and Geo. R. Thompson of the Department of Conservation and Development, and Holland Houston of the Public Service Commission, Olym pia, met at the University of Washington and adopted a resolution advising that the Governor request the governors of Idaho, Montana, Oregon and Wyoming, name representatives of their respective states to meet with a delegation from this state to consider the negotiation of a compact. 

July 10, 1950-The first meeting of representatives of the Pacific Northwest

Third Biennial Report states was held in Spokane. Progress of the negotiations from this first meeting to June 30, 1954, have been recorded in the First and Second Biennial Reports. 

This report will have to do with the progress of Compact negotiations since July 1, 1954 and will be confined as nearly as possible to activities of the Washington State Commission. The Columbia Interstate group (seven Pacific 

Northwest states) publishes an annual report covering progress of the full commission.

ENABLING LEGISLATION 

The 1951 legislature enacted House Bill No. 217 which provided for a state compact commission composed of one member appointed by the Governor, the chairman of the legislative council and three members to be nominated by the chairman of the legislative council. 

In 1953 the legislature amended the 1951 act. The amended consent legis lation is now Chapter 43.57 RCW and reads as follows: 

43.57.010 Commission created-Appointment of members-Purpose. There is created the interstate compact commission to consist of five members, no more than three of which shall have the same political party affiliation, to be appointed as follows: One member, appointed by the governor, who shall be the chairman and who shall serve at the pleasure of the governor, and four members of the state legislature, two of whom shall be members of the house of representatives and shall be appointed by the speaker of the house, and two of whom shall be members of the senate and shall be appointed by the president of the senate. The commission shall represent the state on a joint commission to be composed of commissioners representing the states of Idaho, Montana, Nevada, Oregon, Utah, Washington and Wyoming, and one or more commissioners representing the United States, should they be appointed to said joint commission by the president of the United States, which joint commission shall be organized for the purpose of considering, negotiating and entering into an agreement or compact between not less than five of said states, with the consent of the congress of the United States respecting the division, apportionment and use of the waters of the Columbia river and of its tributaries and the determination of rights in connection therewith and incidental thereto. (1953 c 130 p. 1; 1951 c 113 p. 1.) 

43.57.020 Powers and duties-Per diem and expenses-Term of office. 

The commission representing the state on said joint commission shall have full authority to consider and carry on negotiations for such agreement or compact, to attend meetings of the joint commission convening in or out of the state, to employ clerical, legal and engineering assistance and generally to perform such duties as shall be required of the members thereof in carrying out the purpose and intent of this chapter; the term of office of said commissioners shall be from June 11, 1953, until an agreement or compact binding on the state of Washington under the provisions of RCW 43.57.030 has been entered into. Any vacancies occurring in the membership of said commission shall be filled by the appointive power shown in RCW 43.57.010. Members of the commission representing the state who are not in the regular employ of the state shall receive a per diem of fifteen dollars for the time actually spent on the work of the commission, and reimbursement for subsistence and traveling expenses incurred while away from their respective places of abode. 

Members of the commission who are in the regular employ of the state shall receive no per diem, but shall receive reimbursement for subsistence and

Interstate Compact Commission traveling expenses incurred while away from their respective places of abode, in lieu of other provisions made by law for reimbursement of their expenses as such state employees. Payment of all expenses incurred by the interstate compact commission, including the per diem and expenses of its members, shall be made on vouchers approved by its chairman. (1953 c 130 p 2; 1951 c 113 p 2.) 

43.57.030 When agreement or compact is binding upon states. Any agreement or compact approved by said joint commission on behalf of said states shall not be binding or obligatory upon any of said states or the citizens thereof, until and unless the same shall have been ratified and approved by the congress of the United States: Provided, That said agreement or compact shall not be binding upon any state the legislature of which fails to ratify or approve the same. (1951 c 113 p 3.)

SCOPE OF PROPOSED COMPACT 

1. The proposed compact would establish a permanent commission composed of representatives of the signatory states and the United States to carry out the provisions of the interstate agreement. 

2. The commission would be a recommendatory group rather than an operative body empowered to finance, construct and manage projects. It would be charged with authority to make basin-wide studies relating to all phases of Columbia River basin development and reviewing plans of river development proposed by federal or other agencies if plans involved either federal financing or federal licensing. It could also initiate project planning. 

3. Priority of consumptive uses of water (such as irrigation, domestic and industrial uses) in the upstream area including the portions of Oregon and Washington east of the Cascade Mountains, over nonconsumptive uses (such as power, navigation and flood control) in the downstream states is provided for all existing consumptive uses and those established by the year 2000 or a later date, but not beyond the year 2050, if the Commission by unanimous action extends the date. Any then unappropriated waters are to be left for fair and equitable division between the states. The compact will not become effective unless Congress waives the priority rights the United States may have under the Commerce Clause at downstream plants as it has done in other instances. 

4. With respect to proposed hydroelectric developments, the Commission shall, unless found impracticable, make recommendations that will lead to a fair and equitable apportionment of hydroelectric power among the member states. This is to be done by recommending the reservation for the states where a project is to be located of an allocation equal to the prime power and energy for that project that would result from the coordinated release of water therefrom through existing downstream projects and the coordinated operation of all projects. Such recommended reserved power may be used elsewhere in the Basin until actual need is shown in the states where the project is located. 

5. The Commission is specifically authorized to make recommendations concerning pollution control, and concerning the methods and facilities of protecting fish, wildlife and recreation features in all proposed projects. 

6. All water rights acquired under state law are fully protected and each state retains control of waters within its own boundaries, subject to the pro-

7. Commissioners will be appointed under the laws of the state they represent. Their terms of service and compensation will be established by each state in legislation approving and ratifying the Compact. Financing of the 

Commission's activities will be divided among the states with all of Commission's budgets subject to approval by state legislatures. 

8. The terms of the Columbia Interstate Compact have been negotiated and agreed upon by Commissioners appointed by each of the seven states. All problems have been carefully studied and all interests given opportunity to present their views. 

9. The primary purpose of the Columbia Interstate Compact, as stated in the Compact itself, is the promotion of the greatest ultimate economical justifiable development of the water resources of the Columbia River Basin. The 

Compact provides a means whereby that development can and will be achieved for the benefit of and under the guidance of the people of the Colum bia River Basin. 

Most compacts dealing with natural resources, which are presently in effect in western states deal primarily with the appropriation of waters of interstate streams for the use of the states involved. The Columbia Interstate Compact is much more comprehensive and complex than such contracts. 

Naturally each state is zealous to protect its own rights and should do so. 

With seven states and the federal government involved the negotiations so far have been subject to many compromises and adjustments. For example, the representatives of the state of Washington have insisted that, in the voting arrangement for the permanent commission, Washington and Oregon should have sufficient strength to prevent any combination of votes of upstream states from passing an action detrimental to the two downstream states. Upstream states do not want an infringement upon their right to use all the water needed for irrigation and other consumptive uses. The United 

States, on the other hand, does not want downstream power plants to suffer from lack of water caused by depletion of stream flows by upstream states. 

The allocation of power produced by plants constructed in upstream states and the increased power produced by plants in downstream states due to release of stored water in upstream states was the cause of much argument and many compromises.

One representative on the Washington commission, Charles Savage, has promoted the idea that the permanent commission should be authorized to become a financing, constructive and operative group when (1) other agencies, public or private, would not construct a needed project or (2) when federal projects are fully amortized the Commission should be in a position to take them over and operate them as area-wide projects. 

December 29, 1954, the Interstate Commission came to an agreement on a compact draft. January 15, 1955, the draft was officially signed by the representatives of the seven Pacific Northwest states with reservations by the Washington delegation. Legislation for the states' approval was presented to the 1955 legislatures of the seven states. Idaho, Nevada and Utah approved the Compact. Montana, Oregon and Washington legislatures requested more time to study the instrument as the members of the legislatures and people in general had not had time for study and analysis of the draft after final approval of the same on December 29, 1954.

Several meetings of the commission and advisory council members were held at which the views of the council were presented to the commission. 

Two provisions of the compact draft were the subjects of considerable discussion. (1) The organization of the permanent commission and the voting strength of the states. Indications are that satisfactory adjustments will be made in the compact on this subject. (2) The provisions for the allocation of power as between upstream and downstream states was opposed by several members of the advisory council and suggestions were made for changes in the provisions.

The commission endeavored to have the compact draft written to cover all phases of resource development in the Columbia Basin as completely as possible at this time due to probable difficulties for future amendment. 

Plans to have a revised draft of the compact approved by the Interstate Commission, with points of conflict between the states and federal government adjusted, in time to acquaint the people of the several states with the compact are now under way. APPENDIX 

Following the June 25, 1956 meeting of the seven state commission the compact executive committee of which J. V. Rogers is a member, conferred with several federal agencies in Washington, D. C. Practically all items in the compact were cleared with all United States agencies concerned with the exception of the articles dealing with stream flow depletions. The Department of 

Justice questioned the provisions of the compact draft on this subject. 

A subcommittee of the Columbia Basin Interagency Committee is presently making a study of possible future depletion for the Corps of Engineers as a part of the review report of House Document No. 531, 81st Congress, 2nd

Session. 

In brief the problem involved is-Will future demand for water for consumptive uses in upstream areas endanger the supply of water for down stream nonconsumptive uses to the extent of limiting power production in federally financed plants? When this problem is solved as between the states and the United States the negotiations of the compact should be completed and a draft ready for presentation to the several state legislatures.
