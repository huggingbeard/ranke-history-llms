IN the parade of life she was one of the wistful ones who lag in the rear. The last of a line of aristocrats she inherited only their unfitness for life, a strange otherworldly beauty and an oversensitive soul. Fate taught her to fear men, to shudder from the love that men can offer. In her loneliness she poured out her longings in a book that swept the literary world. But, too unworldly to take advantage of her success, when her royalty money was stolen she was penniless, drifted through the streets until she was picked up in complete collapse, a victim of pneumonia..

Bryan Bramley, the doctor who saved her life, knew that he had to cure more than her body; he had to teach her the necessity and the beauty of physical love. Out of love for her he risked earning her hatred; risked dragging her through the darkness of shame. How he taught her the real meanings of love, and how he won his reward, make a magically enthralling story that stirs the reader long after the book is closed.

A SLIM girl in a shabby black costume was standing on the platform of a crowded Paris railway terminal. She looked about sixteen-a lovely child with thick plaits of pale golden hair and wide hazel eyes. In a bewildered manner she was scanning the surge of people, obviously looking for somebody. 

Lorraine D'Or Sylvester was English, but she knew very little of her native land, having left it at the age of seven. She was an orphan, quite alone in the world, her only brother, Ralph, having been killed during the War.

In England the Sylvesters had once owned large estates, but they had fallen on evil times. The final remnant of their property her father had been forced to sell to his life-long enemy, a wealthy brewer of the name of Bramley, a man whose grandfather had once been a groom.

With a pittance the Sylvester family had settled in 

Switzerland. There Lorraine's parents had been killed in a climbing accident. The European disaster had robbed the girl not only of her brother but also of her tiny fortune. When the War was over there had been nothing left to pay her school fees. She had been sent to Finland, to a Russian refugee family who had fled to their estate there. For nearly five years Lorraine 

D'Or Sylvester had lived au pair with Princess Marie 13

Straight from the wilds of Finland she had come to the French capital. Prince Boris had been told to meet her and see her safely to her new post.

Lorraine D'Or inherited her imposing name from a remote ancestress, Lorraine de la Valmontry who had come from France with Mary, Queen of Scots. Lorraine, whom men had called "The Golden" and had worshiped because of her goodness and beauty, and who, herself, had worshiped truth and honor and virtue. Lorraine D'Or, who had been wooed by kings and princes and who had given herself, for love, to a simple English squire.

The name stayed in the family, and occasionally the type cropped up in a golden-haired, golden-eyed, golden-voiced maiden, with golden laughter in her throat. 

Like her remote ancestress Lorraine worshiped truth and honor and virtue, and in her possession she had an old amber necklace that had once belonged to the first Lorraine D'Or. 

But she was not thinking of her long-dead namesake as she stood in the Paris terminal, but of Prince Boris Barvoneski, hoping that he, in his casual, careless way, had not forgotten to come and meet her.

II 

LORRAINE'S presentiment about her new situation proved only too true, but she felt she could not afford to quarrel with it. 

The school lay not very far from the Quai d'Orsay, in a narrow back street. A wooden gate in a straight high wall gave entry to the playground. On three sides of the latter were blank walls; on the fourth the house; an unfriendly place with stiff short blinds at the windows. Behind the house was a further blank wall; only one side showed a glimpse of the world. There, a couple of windows on each floor looked on the road, on the opposite side of which was a row of small shops.

There were thirty boarders in the school, and about the same number of day pupils. Besides Lorraine and 

Madame Verrain there were only two other mistresses, and an elderly spinster whose activities were confined to housekeeping, and with whom the child had to share a bedroom.

There were other things she had to share with the old maid. At meals the staff joined Madame Verrain and several parlor boarders, and at lunch and dinner the more tasty of the dishes very often did not go round. Lorraine and the elderly spinster were always the last to be served. It was quite an ordinary thing for only one egg, or one cutlet, or one sardine to be left 22

23 for the two of them, and there was no such thing as bringing on a further supply. The child, whose turn came first, generally refused the scrap which fell to her portion; if she took it there would be none left for the old woman, and the latter always watched the dishes with such sharp, hungry, anxious eyes.

In Finland Lorraine had had nothing to do; in Paris it seemed she had everything. Besides having a class of her own of little children whom she had to instruct in all elementary subjects, she taught English and German and music to the elder girls, and she was expected to superintend the pupils' wardrobes, their meals, their dormitories, and head the procession when the school took its occasional walks. 

From half-past six in the morning until nine at night she was on the mill; from week-end to week-end there was not an hour she could call her own. Her delicate face grew pinched and shadowed, and the frightened look that had always lurked at the bottom of the grave golden eyes deepened. 

In her diary Lorraine wrote: "I'd no idea that getting a living for oneself was going to be so hard. I'm afraid I do things very badly. 

However much I try Madame never gives me a word of praise. If I dared I would leave. But I'm afraid, both of her and the world outside. I'm between the upper and nether millstones. I've discovered I'm not a courageous person at all. I wish I had a friend. There is no one here I like, or care to talk to, only my diary.

III 

WITHIN half an hour of the fatal laughter that had resulted in Lorraine's being bundled bag and baggage out into the street by her irate employer, the girl was sitting alone in a fireless little bedroom at the top of the hôtel meublé. 

In all the world she had only just over five pounds. 

Madame Verrain had omitted to pay her the quarter's salary due in a week or so's time-it being then nearly 

Christmas-and Lorraine had been too proud to ask for it.

In hat and coat the child sat pondering on the problem of the future. Having by a fluke, as it were, escaped from Madame Verrain, which, left to herself, she felt she could never have done, she had no wish to go back. The ten months spent there had made her in no way disposed to try for another post in Paris. 

Out of her predicament arose a desire to be once more with her own people, to get back to England, though she knew not a soul there.

The idea no sooner presented itself than it was grasped at. And she must get back to England at once, lest Madame Verrain came and claimed her and put her back on her treadmill.

Early next morning Lorraine was at the Gare du 

Nord. The third-class fare to London made sore in- 30

31 roads on her pitiful store, but the child's heart ached for her own country; it spelt home.

A little after midday, through a driving mist, the cliffs of her native land rose up before her. 

To Lorraine standing on the wet and sloppy steamer's deck, they appeared to frown at her in a sullen, unfriendly manner, as if demanding why she was going to a city where already there were seven million people. 

Wasn't that more than enough without her adding her drop to the ocean?

The seven million people obsessed her during the journey up to London. She saw them all as wrestling together in some arena where the weak were trampled underfoot; as a whirlpool swirling round and round, those not strong enough to battle with its current sucked into its awful mouth; as a rushing, headlong river, each individual clutching frantically at the Rocks of Opportunity, the less fortunate ones whirled on with wild, anxious faces to be poured with the thundering flood over some bottomless abyss. 

With these despairing visions the push and rush and clatter and noise of Victoria Station did not tend to cheer her. She stood bewildered; there was not even the rock of Barvoneski to clutch at now.

But the stream caught her up and bore her on towards the outer platform, and left her stranded there with her box and handbag, wondering, in a confused way, how she had managed to get so far.

With a word to his chauffeur, he dismissed the waiting motor. Holding her arm more firmly in his, he started towards her lodgings. But he did not talk of himself and his desires, but of her play.

He knew that he had won, and Lorraine knew that he knew it too; and what was more, that he intended to push his victory to the fullest and make her say she loved him.

On reaching the door of the house, he did not wait for her to issue any invitation, nor did he say "May I come in?" He just came.

Following her upstairs to the first floor where she had a suite a sitting-room and two bedrooms-he switched on the light of the former and then stood watching her, saying nothing. "Would you like to see Ralph?" Lorraine asked, her face crimson, unable to think of anything else. "I should, very much."

Crossing to one of the two doors leading from the room, she opened it. The light showed a small boy with a tousled mane, asleep on a little bed.

Had not one's mother come to London hoping to make her fortune? This was the first man she had ever been seen with, and he had not only been brought to the house, but right into one's bedchamber. If one had a mind that thought and connected things, it was not difficult to guess who this desirable-looking stranger was. "I know who you are," a child's voice said. 

Bramley cast a teasing glance at Lorraine. "It's a wise child, etc.," he said in an aside. 

Then he turned to the youngster. "Who am I then?" he went on. "You're the father mother's going to get for me when she's rich."

A further glance was cast at Lorraine, but she was looking at her son. "So you've heard of me before," Bramley said with amusement. "But you take me very casually, and quite as a matter of course." "I knew you'd come. Mother promised," was the reply, said with an air as if the voice whose promises were never broken had spoken. "Are you satisfied with your mother's choice?"

Bramley's own eyes looked at him, with his own cool critical gaze, and the tousled little head nodded. "Ralph, you must go to sleep," Lorraine put in quickly, as if fearing what the conversation might lead to, and knowing she had had very little choice in the matter of the father of her child. "You mustn't sit up talking now."

To get away from Bramley's teasing glances, she stooped to kiss her treasure.

He was standing as she had so often seen him in the old days, half-lounging against the chimney-piece, with hands deep in his pockets, and the look that she once could not fathom was now alight and alive and growing in his eyes.
