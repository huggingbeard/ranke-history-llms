CHAPTER I 

"We are of the opinion, Mr. Dare, that you are not a citizen of the United States and, hence, not entitled to a passport."

To have lived in the United States practically all one's life, exercising all the rights of citizenship, even to the holding of a federal office, and then, at a most critical moment, to be informed that one was not a citizen was, to say the least, rather startling information.

That the reader may, in some measure, appreciate the feelings of John Dare and also understand something of the situation in which he was placed, by the decision quoted above, the following facts are given, in brief, just here.

The Board of Managers of the Corporation, of which Dare was Executive Secretary, had elected him a delegate to a world conference of kindred interests to be held in Stockholm, Sweden, and he had agreed to go. To go to that conference, however, a passport was necessary. But, when he made application for a passport, at the Federal Office, located in the city

where he lived, it was denied on the grounds that he was not a citizen of the United States!

That Dare was rather dazed by the decision will be readily understood. Perhaps, "bewildered" would be a better word than, "dazed" to express his condition. 

Among the many things to intensify his "bewilderment," as he left the Federal office and walked slowly back to his own office, just two blocks distant, might be mentioned the following: First, his own father was an American citizen. Second, all his forebears, back to 

Revolutionary Days were citizens of the United States. 

Third, his paternal grandfather, lived and died in the

United States and was a pensioner of the wars of 

1811-12. Moreover his only son had "done his bit," with regiment on the fields of France during the 

World War. 

"Not a citizen of my own country," Dare said to himself, as he entered his own office, "the thing is absolutely absurd!" 

Feeling sure that the local office was in error, Dare determined to take the matter up direct with the Chief of Passport Control, in Washington. And, not wishing his office force to know anything about the matter, he wrote the letter himself, placing all the facts before the department in Washington, and waited. Within a few days a reply came from the Chief of Passport Control confirming the decision of the local office. 

While this was very disappointing it did not satisfy Dare. He felt sure that both the local office and the 

Chief of Passport Control, were in error in their interpretation of the law and he determined to take the

matter up with the Secretary of State, which he did. 

And, since the time was getting short before he must sail for Europe, if he did sail, he asked for a reply by wire and within a short time he received the following telegram from the Secretary of State: 

"UNDER SECTION NINETEEN NINETY THREE REVISED STATUTES, YOU WERE NOT BORN A CITIZEN." 

Dare was fairly stunned by the import of the message! The words, "not born a citizen," struck with bludgeon-like force!

The highest authority having spoken, Dare was now face to face with the fact that, the accident of birth, under the statute quoted, deprived him of citizenship, “in my own country," as he had put it before. Dare's use of the statement, "my own country,' will be better understood by the reader, when it is stated that, before he cast his first vote, eminent legal authority had assured him that, he was a citizen! 99 

However, with the decision of the Secretary of

State went all hope of obtaining a passport. "I am going to that conference," Dare said to himself, as he left his office that evening and started for home.

CHAPTER II 

The time had now come when Dare must reveal the situation in which he found himself to his wife. Up until now only he, himself, was aware of the dilemma, not to say embarrassment, in which he was placed. 

His wife knew, of course, that application for passport had been made. Just why it had not arrived she did not know. To her inquiries Dare had given excuses of various sorts. However, further excuses were futile. She must now be made acquainted with the facts. And these "facts" he had made up his mind to give her on his arrival home that evening. The task before him, however, was growing more and more unpleasant.

His wife, an Illinois girl, no doubt about her citizenship, must now be told that she had married a foreigner! and by her marriage, had, in all probability, lost her American citizenship! "Did the passport come?", was his wife's greeting as Dare entered his home that evening. "No, my dear, not yet," he replied, a forced laugh following the answer. 

Mrs. Dare looked rather sharply at her husband for that sort of a laugh was not one of his characteristics. "Anything the matter, John?" she inquired. "Just a bit hungry," he parried.

"Well, dinner is ready and waiting," she said.

During the meal there was little conversation. The questions asked by his wife were answered by "yes," or "no," or by "what did you say?"

Dare knew that his wife was eyeing him closely and he began to feel even more uncomfortable than when he arrived home.

When dinner was over and they had risen from the table Dare said, "My dear, when you have a little time

I have something I wish to say to you" and, turning abruptly, he left the room. 

A little later Mrs. Dare entered the room where her husband was sitting. She noted that, notwithstanding the beauty of the June evening, instead of going out onto the wide, screened-in, porch, which he called, "the best room in the house," as was his custom, he had remained inside! As she sat down near him he said, "I thought we would be less likely to be disturbed here than on the porch." 

Mrs. Dare, now a bit disturbed, drew her chair closer to her husband's and waited. That something out of the ordinary was on his mind she sensed very clearly. Seldom, if ever, had she seen him in as strange a mood. However, she remained silent. She knew that, presently, she would learn what was troubling him. For, during all the years of their married life each had been the other's confidant. 

Just now, however, she had a feeling that, had she expressed it in words, "something different" was troubling him. And she was the more puzzled because, so far as she knew, there was every reason why

he should feel free from care or worry of any sort! 

Was he not about to start on a trip that would take him over most of Europe? Had he not been greatly honored in being chosen a delegate to such conference? 

The challenge of such a privilege and such an opportunity should keep one bubbling over with enthusiasm! 

Why, then, the present seriousness? 

Mrs. Dare, knew, of course, that for her husband to be gone for three months from the business which had occupied his time for many years, required careful planning. She knew that this had caused him much concern. She wondered whether some of these plans had miscarried? "John, is the care of the business while you are away, worrying you?" she finally inquired. "No, my dear. I am not thinking about 'business' just now but about a passport." "Passport," she repeated, "don't let that worry you. It will arrive in plenty of time." "I wish I could believe it as matters now stand," he answered. 66 6 'As matters now stand?' What 'matters' John?" and her voice indicated the anxiety she was beginning to feel. "I mean," my dear, "that I have been refused a passport!" "Refused a passport'," she exclaimed, "refused a passport," she repeated. "Why?", came the inquiry in a most emphatic manner. 

As he did not answer at once and discovering a glimmer of a smile he sometimes wore, she exclaimed,

"John Dare, you gave me a scare! You looked so sober and spoke so seriously that, for a moment, I believed you! I have a good notion to . . ." "Just a moment, my dear," he interrupted, "I never spoke more seriously in my life; indeed, never had reason to. When I said that I had been refused a passport I meant just what I said. Let me tell you about it," and, drawing his chair closer to hers he told her the whole story. When he had concluded she said, "It is surely an unusual situation in which to be placed. And to think, John, that it should have come up at such a time as this!" "Don't worry, my dear," said Dare, his own voice a bit tense, "I'm going just the same!" "But, John!" she protested, "you cannot go without a passport!" "I know it," he replied, "but I'm going. Just how

I don't quite see at this moment. But, passport or no passport, I'm going to that conference!" 

But, is he?

CHAPTER III 

While John Dare slept little, the night following the events recorded in the previous chapter, he did a lot of thinking with the result that, at the breakfast table the following morning, he said to his wife, "My dear I am leaving for Washington this evening." "Leaving for Washington this evening'," she repeated in great surprise. "Yes" he replied, "I did some thinking last night," he went on, "and I am going to see what a personal visit will do. I, someway, cannot bring myself to believe but that, if all the facts were really understood, by either the Chief of Passport Control or the Secre tary of State, that I would still be denied a passport.' "Well, John," his wife said, as he paused, "do as you think best." 99 "You see," continued Dare, "I find myself in an almost unbearable position. Indeed, the more I think of it there enters into the situation not only embarrassment but that which is much harder to bear, the sting of personal humiliation!" "I can see the 'embarrassment' John," said his wife, "But as for 'humiliation,' I don't quite see it. You've done nothing dishonorable, unless making a foreigner of me, without my knowledge or consent, might be deemed to be such," and she smiled broadly, as she went on, "I might, of course, apply for a divorce on

the grounds of deception! And, come to think of it,

I am not so sure but that this is my oppor .," but the smile broke into a laugh, as Dare interrupted with, "Oh, I say, my dear, this is no laughing matter I assure · ? 

"So I see," she interrupted.

Dare glanced at his wife, their eyes met and the explosion came. It was the first real, hearty laugh Dare had had in a long time. And a long, hearty laugh, was the need of the moment. 

What a treasure some wives are? John Dare had such a wife!

In the city where John Dare lived there lived also one of the foremost members of the United States Senate. With a telegram from the latter to his private 

Secretary to meet Dare on his arrival in Washington, and, with a letter of introduction from the Senator to the Chief of Passport Control, Dare left for the nation's capitol. 

We might just whisper to Mr. Dare that not every one who has gone to the nation's capitol has come away with the things he desired.

CHAPTER IV 

Upon his arrival in Washington Dare was met by the Senator's secretary, whom we shall call Windom.

After going over the situation with Windom, the latter suggested, first of all, a conference with the 

Chief of Passport Control. 

The Chief received Dare and Windom very graciously. All the correspondence Dare had had with the Department was gone over very carefully. However, after about an hour's consideration of the matter, the Chief said, in substance: "Mr. Dare, under Section Nineteen Ninety-Three,

Revised Statutes, you were not born a citizen, as the telegram of the Secretary of State informed you, and, much as I regret it, that decision must stand. Hence, to grant you a passport would be impossible!" 

As Dare listened to the Chief's decision he wondered whether he might not be experiencing a touch of the feelings a prisoner might feel whose final appeal for freedom was denied? That the Chief was sympathetic there could be no doubt. But law was law! "Let's go to my office, Mr. Dare," suggested Windom, as they reached the street after the interview. 

Arriving at Windom's office the latter, seating himself at a desk and pulling a typewriter toward him,

turned to Dare and asked, "Mr. Dare, are you willing to take a chance?" "Well, that depends", Dare parried. "As I understand it," Windom went on, "to you, personally, there is much at stake in this matter. The question is, is that stake of sufficient importance to you to warrant your taking a real chance?" "Just what is on your mind, Mr. Windom?" inquired Dare. "In substance it is this," Windom replied, "I'll draw up a document setting forth that you wish to visit certain foreign countries, and Stockholm in particular. 

Of course, it will not be a passport. Of course, you may not be able to get out of the United States with it and, getting out, you might have difficulty in getting back. Is the stake you are playing for, Mr. Dare, worth the chance?

Dare sat for some time in deep thought. Finally he said, "Mr. Windom, I am not afraid to try anything once!" "Good," commented Windom, and he began writing.

While it is not our purpose to anticipate yet, we wish to say just here, that if John Dare had known what was ahead of him he would not have taken the "chance" he was about to take. 

When Windom had finished writing and Dare had subscribed to it, (Windom being a Notary), they went to the office of the Secretary of State. Here 

Windom secured a formidable looking document, containing the seal of the United States and the signature of the Secretary of State, which document affirmed;

"That the document hereunto annexed is under the Seal of the Department of Justice of the United States, and that such Seal is entitled to full faith and credit." 

It will be noted that, in the foregoing quotation, it is the "Seal" that is emphasized. Down in the lower, left-hand corner, in small type, occurred the following, "For the contents of the annexed document the Department assumes no responsibility!"

From the office of the Attorney General of the United States, Windom secured another formidable looking legal document, with the great Seal of the Department and the signature of the Attorney General attached, which, when carefully examined, was simply an affirmation of the right of Windom to act as a 

Notary Public. 

The statement prepared by Windom, with these two state documents attached, gave it the appearance of a most formidable, official state paper! "Now for the Visés," said Windom and he and 

Dare started for the British Embassy. "Why the man's a British subject!" declared the

British official, when Windom asked for a Visé on the document he presented. "No he isn't," Windom replied, "but there isn't time to prove his American citizenship, before he must sail." "I'll give him a British passport," said the official, but I'll not visé this." 

Windom reported to Dare. "Tell him," said Dare, "that I'd see him hung and the British Empire sunk first. I'll cut England and take the northern tour."

"Fine!" commented Windom, "we'll go to the

Swedish Embassy. No doubt about obtaining their visé. They are anxious for a large attendance of 

Americans at their World's Fair, held this year at Gothenburg, (Gottenborg) and with their visé all others will follow."

And so it proved. Without opening the document, the German, French, Danish, Norwegian and Italian Embassies attached their visés. "Goodbye and good luck," was Windom's parting words as Dare left Washington for home.

It has been well said that "luck" was just good, oldfashioned "pluck" beheaded! Some folks find this out a bit late!

There are other things that are found out, or recalled, a "bit late" and which if, "re-called" in moments of crisis might have great and far-reaching influences. 

Among such things might be mentioned the following, namely, that Dare, neither in his correspondence with the Secretary of State nor in his personal conference with the Chief of Passport Control, mentioned the fact that his parents brought him to the United States when he was about seven years of age! However, since his father and mother were both dead, at the time of which we are writing, and no others, so far as he knew were alive, who could verify that fact, he said nothing about it, determining to face the chance he was taking, win or lose! But John Dare did not yet know the chance he was taking! Had he known, this story would not have been written!

CHAPTER V 

On the way home from Washington John Dare did a lot of thinking. The "chance" he was about to take began to assume sizable proportions! As he reviewed the matter, menacing aspects appeared.

First: To try any irregularities with Uncle Sam might lead to most serious consequences. While the document he was carrying as a passport contained but a simple statement of facts and was open for the inspection of officials, yet, might there not be deemed to be an irregularity in one's attempt to use such a document? For, Dare's only hope of "getting by" the examiners of passports in New York and getting aboard ship, was that, while the paper he carried looked nothing at all like a regulation passport yet, folded as it was, with the Secretary of State's most formidable looking document with the great red Seal stamped upon it and the bold signature of the 

Secretary in plain sight, with Dare's photograph and the Swedish visé showing just below, it would not be opened!

A second most disturbing question was as to what might be the attitude of the examiners of passports in foreign lands? All kinds of complications might, and doubtless would, follow, should such examiners not accept as carte blanche their own visé and pass the bearer without further thought! Should they not do

so, however, and Dare found himself in the "clutches" of the law on foreign soil, to whom could he appeal? 

Since it had been declared he was not a citizen of the United States, and, since he himself had refused to be deemed to be a citizen of the country which had offered him a passport as a citizen, he would really be, "a man without a country!"

There were moments when Dare's courage all but failed! The, "chance" seemed too great! And yet as he thought of all that was at stake he would determine to accept the challenge of the chance!

And so it came about that by the time Dare reached home the question of his taking the "chance" was settled. 

And yet as he bade goodbye to those he loved and boarded the train for New York, the shadow of the chance he was taking reappeared. Happily for John 

Dare the portentousness of the "shadow" of this chance he was taking was unknown to him at the time.

CHAPTER VI 

With one last wave of his hand in the direction, where fluttering handkerchiefs were last seen waving farewell in the gathering twilight, Dare entered the observation car, made his way slowly to the pullman where he had a reservation and sat down. The sun had run its course of the long June day and the train, speeding eastward, seemed to be racing swiftly into the gathering darkness ahead. The last edition of an evening paper lay on Dare's baggage close by. He picked it up, and adjusted his glasses, settled back into his seat, turned to the front page, glanced at a headline or two, folded the paper and threw it on the seat before him. For suddenly and with great vividness, it dawned upon him that he had really started for 

Europe! And then, with great clearness, there recurred to his memory, his reply to Windom, that he was, "not afraid to try anything once!"

Now John Dare was not accustomed to climb a hill before he got to it. He was, naturally, optimistic and disposed to look on the bright side of life. Moreover he had cultivated the habit of seeing the good in those with whom he was brought into association. To Dare, life was, on the whole, a tremendously interesting and enjoyable adventure!

Just now, however, there was something about the

deep rumble of the train, as it rushed on through the enveloping darkness, that affected him as it had never affected him before. It was not that he was unaccustomed to the "rumble" of trains, for the work in which he was engaged made it necessary for him to travel many thousands of miles every year. But somehow the rumble of the train tonight was different! It was not the noise of the train that affected him for, in a pullman, there is little of the noise of the day coach. 

No, tonight, the rumble of the train was different! 

There seemed to be something portentous about it! 

It did not occur to Dare that he had been going at exceedingly high pressure for the past few weeks and that his nerves were at high tension. For, not only had his work to be gotten into shape for an absence of three months, but the exasperating situation in which he had been placed in the matter of passport, had worn his nerves down to a pretty thin edge! As a matter of fact, though he would not have admitted it, when he had thrown the evening paper down, slipped his glasses back into his pocket, stretched his legs out onto the seat opposite, folded his arms, closed his eyes and leaned back heavily against the cushioned seat, he was an exceedingly tired man! And, as he sat thus, the rumble of the train sounded farther and farther away until it was finally lost and he was sound asleep. Then, suddenly, a tremendous crash brought him to his feet! Had there been a wreck? No. It was the porter pulling down the upper berth across the aisle!

"Yes, porter, you may make up my berth also, I believe I'll retire," said Dare. 

And so John Dare has started for New York, the port of embarkation. But, will he embark?

CHAPTER VII 

Sick or well one usually feels more like facing the realities of life in the day-time rather than at night. 

It may be that this is one reason why more people die at night than in the day-time. At all events, John 

Dare was feeling fine, on this beautiful June morning, when his train pulled into New York City. 

However, on the way to the pier, where he hoped to embark, the shadow of the document he carried in lieu of a passport reappeared, darker and more ominous than ever before!

One other matter added to his burden. He was no "lover of the sea." He had a feeling that if ever a ship went down it would be when he was on board! 

And this feeling was not helped any, by a letter he had received, from an enterprising firm in New York, a part of which read as follows: "While sea travel is now comparatively safe, yet no one knows when an accident might occur. Hence, all wise travellers provide themselves with one of our 'non-sinkable suits!' and while Dare had ordered one of these suits, he had doubts whether a shark might respect such a suit! 99 When he arrived at the pier and joined the crowd of waiting passengers he noticed a number of rather large policemen mingling with the crowd. Might they be on the look-out for such as he? Just now, however, certain other officials were announcing, "Have

your passports ready, and also asking the passengers to form in line preparatory to going aboard. 

The passengers were formed into two lines. Dare joined the one nearest him. He noticed, not far from the point where one boarded ship, two officers, standing upon platforms about a foot high, each with a desk before him. The two lines of passengers were to pass between these two officers, showing their passports for examination. Presently the lines began to move. 

To Dare those two officers appeared like two executioners awaiting victims! For a moment he was minded to leave the line and head for home!

And now the last one in the line ahead of him had shown his passport and had moved on and Dare stood before that august looking official. As unconcerned as possible Dare placed his "passport" before the examiner. "What's this?" the examiner inquired.

Dare was about to make some sort of a reply, when he discovered that the examiner had addressed the question to his fellow officer. "That's a special," was the reply. And the Inspector was right. "Oh, yes," said the other examiner, "I see," and without opening it he returned the document and Dare hurried on board. 

Well, Dare is on board ship!

CHAPTER VIII 

While Dare had successfully ran the gauntlet of Passport Examiners, at New York, and the good ship was bearing him steadily and delightfully, farther and farther from home officials it was bearing him just as steadily, and surely, nearer to the foreign scrutinizers of the document he was carrying as a passport. And, even though he was not yet one-fourth the way to 

Plymouth, England, the first port of call, and not one-fifth the distance to Bremer-Haven, his hoped-for port of debarkation in Europe and, even though there was much to interest the "land-lubber" on his first trip, "across" and, notwithstanding the pleasantness of the entire environment, nevertheless he could not quite forget the sort of a passport he was carrying! 

However, the fourth day out was so beautiful, the atmosphere on board so pleasant, and the fine democracy of the passengers, the delightful music of band and orchestra, and the excellent menus so faultlessly served, caused Dare, for the time being, to throw off the thought of what might happen when he attempted to land.

Furthermore, at least six days remained before he would have to face the problem, so why not enjoy them to the fullest? The ship was proving a delightful place to be and should he not be permitted to land, worse things might happen than a return trip on this

same good ship! The thought of not being permitted to re-enter the United States brought, of course, its harrassing moments! But, since that "hill of difficulty" was, at least, sixteen days away, why worry just now? He determined, therefore, to begin a letter to his wife. He would mail it if permitted to land and if not he would carry it back with him as a memento of the way some things have of happening in this life!

Will Dare get into Germany? If so, how?

CHAPTER IX 

To one who has never been out of sight of land for more than a few hours at a time, to see land once more, after eight days and nights entirely surrounded by water! Well, only those who have had the experience can have any idea of the feelings of John Dare as, standing in the bow of the ship, crowding the rail, glasses in hand, he brought those far-away specks close enough to be assured that he was really looking at land, solid land!

An extract, from a letter Dare wrote his wife under a July date runs as follows: "My dear Wife: "The last 24 hours brought some thrills. First was the sight of land! Oh, boy! wasn't it a 'grand and glorious feelin'?' Away off in the distance, by the use of my glasses, I saw the lighthouse on the Cilly

Islands. Shortly after the rocky shores were dimly seen through the fog. In passing let me say that it would add mightily to the pleasure of a trip like this were there no fogs. Moreover it would help one's patience. Later the coast of Cornwall came into view and I was, at least, catching a glimpse of England's coastline. Then in the distance, a great distance away, another lighthouse appeared dead ahead. I was a little concerned for a time lest our helmsman had not

seen it, for in addition to the lighthouse were huge, dark, menacing rocks, some distance beyond the lighthouse and, as we were headed, should we chance to miss the one there was no possibility, seemingly, of escaping the other! However, someone must have put the helmsman wise to our danger, or, he might have noticed it himself, at any rate we missed hitting either lighthouse or rocks by (as Harry Lauder would put it), 'roughly speaking, about a quarter of a mile!'

I was greatly relieved. For, you must bear in mind that the waters of this ocean seem obsessed to occupy all space possible. There does not seem to be an opening in the shore-line any place that the water does not feel free to enter. Why, even here at Plymouth, it was noticeable. For when the ship ran in between two points of land and then made a sharp turn in behind those points, as if seeking a dry place to land the passengers, it was no use, the water was there also and plenty of it. Makes it real awkward don't you know." 

About midnight, Sunday, the ship left Plymouth and headed for Cherbourg, France, arriving early the following morning.

What would be the reaction of the German officials to the document he carried as a passport? Would he be permitted to land? And, if not, then what?

CHAPTER XI 

The German officials, knowing that the United States ship had lost the race with the tide, had started out to meet her before she came to anchor, and was presently made fast. While there was no confusion on the 

American ship everyone and everything, seemed to be in a hurry. All baggage, heavy and light, including mail sacks, having been ready for some time, was now being transferred to the German boat. All passengers had been notified to have passports ready for examination. 

Dare had watched the approach of the German boat with feelings which we shall not attempt to describe. The uncertainty was becoming acute. His fellow-travellers, carefree and light-hearted, were exchanging jokes and making comments on this, that and the other thing, in happy anticipation of experiences to be had in Germany. And, while Dare did his best to join in the merriment, it was something like a man trying to smile while awaiting judgment! 

However, Dare was to have a short respite before coming to judgment. For, the officers of the American ship had invited the German officials to lunch with them, and all were now on the way to the dining room.

There is, generally speaking, nothing so conducive

to goodfellowship, as a hearty meal of good wholesome food midst congenial surroundings. And, it may be noted in passing, that not everyone in Germany, at this particular time, enjoyed such meals as were served on board the United States Lines. 

But now, the moment had come. Everyone was in line, Dare, about the center, and moving toward the point where sat two German officers, each the possessor of a bristling military moustache.

With the paper he carried folded so as to show the official document of the Secretary of State, with his signature and the great Seal of the United States attached, his own photograph just below and the German visé in plain view, Dare, with as much dignity as he could command, reached the document toward the officer nearest him.

Then a strange thing happened. Both officers glanced at Dare, a peculiar, though not unkindly twinkle in their eyes, then looked inquiringly at each other, said something in German, touched their caps in salute, and Dare was permitted to proceed without their having so much as touched the paper he carried.

When they landed in Bremer-Haven, some of Dare's fellow-travellers, having caught a glimpse of the document, and having noted the German officials salute, without examining the paper Dare presented, became curious to know just who Dare was, the mission he was on. Later Dare understood that there were those who believed he was on some sort of a secret

mission for the United States Government. And,

Dare, rightly or wrongly, did nothing to correct this impression!

However, to get into a country without a passport does not by any means insure a safe departure!

What a happy thing it is when one is so constituted that, "tomorrow" being the day of "execution" they yet enjoy "today!"

CHAPTER XIII 

Travelling in Europe and travelling in the United States are two altogether different propositions, as Dare was presently to find out. In the United States one boards a sleeper in New York and goes straight through to San Francisco, some 3,500 miles, without change or interference, by any government official. 

Not so, however, in Europe. All Germany could be dropped down in Texas and still leave enough territory in which to place a number of the New England States! And, as for such countries as Denmark, Norway and Sweden, each could be slipped into some counties in the United States with room to spare for a number of good farms! As Dare put it, "one is scarcely into a country in Europe before he is out of it!" Such a situation is annoying enough when one is carrying a regular passport, but when carrying a document such as Dare was carrying the annoyances, plus misgivings are raised to the "nth power," as he was presently to find.

Dare was of the opinion when he entrained at Hamburg that he was going direct to Gottenborg, Sweden. 

He was not a little surprised, and disturbed also, when he found that he was to go by the way of Denmark! 

For some reason, (mayhap accounted for telepathically?) when Dare found that he was on his way to the Danish border, the shadow of the passport he car-

ried, grew suddenly very dark and apprehensions of a most disagreeable sort beset him. And, we might add, just here, that this "shadow" was to grow, suddenly, into a cloud of such density, at a certain point, as to bring him to a standstill, four thousand miles from home! But, we must not anticipate further. 

As Dare approached the Danish border, he wondered just what one would do, since an imaginary line formed the boundary between Germany and Denmark, at the point where he expected to cross it, if Denmark refused him entrance and Germany would not let him remain? Just what would happen Dare was to know shortly, for the train was now coming to a stop at the 

Danish border. 

After inspection of baggage, etc., on the German side of the frontier, all passengers were searched. 

Men and women were taken, one at a time, into separate rooms for this searching process. When Dare's turn came he entered the room with many misgivings. 

And these misgivings increased mightily, as he heard the door closed carefully behind him and he found himself face to face with a large, much bewhiskered, fierce looking official.

Dare knew, of course, that at that time in particular, the Germans were in great need of gold. And it was commonly understood that one purpose, at least, for the searching of individuals, was to discover hidden gold! Should gold be found on any one it was taken and German marks given in exchange. While Dare carried no gold specie, he did carry that which, when endorsed, was as good as gold to the holder!

"Haff you money got?" inquired the official in broken English and surliness of manner. He 

The question confirmed Dare's suspicions. managed, however, to answer, as indifferently as possible, "Yah."

As the official stood in an attitude of expectancy Dare took from his pocket a sizable roll of German marks, the outermost one being for 20,000. At sight of the money a remarkable change came over the official. The sternness of his attitude relaxed, a smile seemed to wrinkle his whiskers, while his blue eyes twinkled with friendliness as he said, "Yust a leetle tipe!" 

Peeling off a 20,000 mark Dare handed it to the official.

As the officer took it, he smiled broadly, touched his cap and with a "das is alles," opened the door on the opposite side of the room, and bowed Dare out.

Whatever other interpretation might be placed upon the incident, to John Dare it was another evidence of the tragedies of war! 

But Dare was not yet in Denmark!

CHAPTER XIV 

Dare, with others, having passed through the German Custom house, now boarded a diminutive looking train for the Danish border. They had hardly gotten seated when the train stopped and the order "all out," was heard. "Now, what?" said Dare to himself.

When Dare placed the document he carried, folded now with the Danish visé showing, before the inspector that official glanced at it a moment then, calling an official from an adjoining room, handed it to him.

Dare, being under high tension, wondered, for a moment, whether he had heard correctly? For, under the circumstances, to hear a man, an official, upon whose decision so much depended, speak as he did and in such good English was, to say the least, most unexpected! "You see," said the Inspector, as Dare approached, "the trouble is this: Your visé permits you to enter 

Denmark once. But your route, as planned, by the way of Gottenburg, will bring you into Denmark twice!

After some further thought, the Inspector said, as if speaking to himself "Now, let me see. Maybe we can fix it." 

Just what the Inspector really did, Dare did not know. What he did know was that his document was returned to him without further comment and he was permitted to continue his journey. 

To the numerous inquiries, from his fellow-travellers, as to why he had been called into the private office of the Inspector, he being the only one so invited, Dare tried to look wise but offered no explanation. This increased the suspicion that he was on some sort of a secret mission. 

Much to Dare's satisfaction and peace of mind,

after the incident at the Danish frontier, just mentioned, no other untoward experience was had while travelling in Denmark, Norway or Sweden, though each time the "line was crossed" from one country into another, the "shadow" of the passport hovered around, nemisis-like, causing many disagreeable moments. 

Happily for John Dare while touring Denmark,

Norway and Sweden, the growing darkness of the passport "shadow," was not seen!

The taxies, however, both in these countries and in the parts of Germany I have thus far visited, go most any old way to get there! If they drove in Des 

Moines, as here, every last taxi driver would be arrested forthwith. Now understand, this is not to say that some taxi drivers in Des Moines should not be arrested! Then here in Stockholm, the street cars where there are double tracks, run just the opposite to ours. That is to say, they run to the left instead of the right. To me, of course, the thing is all wrong. However, I suppose I'll have to get along with it. When once people get a good start in the wrong direction, it is hard to stop them. For example, in the few hours 

I have been in Stockholm-about 36-I have seen more drunken men than I have seen in Des Moines in a whole year. This may have been accidental or unusual, but it is a most solemn fact!

"Another peculiarity about the street cars is, they ring the bell twice to stop and once to start. And that reminds me, in these Scandinavian railroads, I have seen no bells on locomotives. In Denmark and Norway I saw men, when a train was pulling into a station walk along beside, what we would call the pilot or "cow-catcher" and with bell in hand ring it if people got too near the edge of the platform. I saw the same thing in Christiania when a yard or switch engine, was crossing street intersections, a man riding on the front of the engine, bell in hand, would ring it if pedestrian or vehicle approached too near! But of course, I happened just now to be a foreigner, but even to a foreigner some things look a bit antiquated, not to say, funny. "However, there are many good things to be said for the railroads in these parts. Particularly is this true of the sleeping car service. I deem them in some respects to be far and away superior to our ordinary pullmans. And their first and second class day coaches are in certain respects, and in particular for parties travelling together, superior to our own. And the uniform kindness and courtesy of employees is marked. "One is constantly seeing 'new things under the sun.' 

For example, I saw a load of square milk-cans today.

Also instead of hitching horses by tie to posts, or heavy metal weights along the streets, they hobble about the knees with straps!"

And, we might add, that humans as well as animals have found themselves "hobbled"!

CHAPTER XIX 

To mingle with a bunch of people coming from the four quarters of the globe, all engaged in the same line of work, acts like a tonic, or should, upon the individual and has a tendency to cause certain cares and perplexities to give place to a buoyancy of spirit which thrusts aside such things. There are certain things, however, like Banquo's Ghost, which, seemingly, will not down and which, moreover, have an uncanny habit of reappearing at the most unexpected times and places. Dare found this to be true regarding the passport question. For instance: about nine o'clock in the morning of the day the meeting he was attending was to adjourn, the chairman announced the names of three or four for whom cablegrams had arrived and among the names mentioned was Dare's. 

When his name was read Dare leaned back a bit heavily in his seat and, turning toward an acquaintance sitting near and who could speak Swedish, asked him if he would go to the secretary's desk and bring him the message. 

When Dare opened the envelope he found the message was in "code" and his Code Book was at the hotel.

On the way to the hotel Dare did a lot of thinking. 

And as he "thought" his anxiety increased. And, with increased anxiety his pace quickened until, when he reached the hotel he was almost running.

He could think of but two reasons for the cablegram. One, a sudden death in his family; the other, some warning, doubtless, regarding the document he carried as a passport! "Believe me!" Dare commented to himself as he hurried to the hotel, "if ever I come to Europe again there'll be no question about the kind of a passport I shall carry!"

By the time he reached his room he was fairly out of breath. Hurriedly pulling his Code Book from his travelling bag, he took a seat by the writing desk, which stood near a window, and, hands trembling a little, he began turning the pages of the Code Book in quest of the word "faith," which was the message in code. Having found the word, he discovered its 

"BE SURE AND HAVE A GOOD TIME AND DO NOT LET MATTERS WORRY YOU!" 

For a moment or two Dare was not sure whether he felt weak or strong! What he did know was that he began to laugh! Not a little laugh but a great, big, hearty, wholesome laugh, such as he had not had for some time! Whether he kicked the chair on which he had been sitting across the room, or, whether the suddenness with which he had gotten to his feet did it, needs no explanation here! At all events he found himself presently standing by a window, Code Book in hand, reading the translation again. Then, with considerable emphasis he commented, "What fools some of us are, climbing hills before we get to them!"

Turning from the window he crossed the room, opened his travelling bag, dropped the message and Code Book in, and was about to close it when the document he was carrying as a passport slipped from one of the pockets of the bag and landed squarely upon the message and Code Book, completely covering them! 

While Dare, like many others, would deny, most vehemently, that there was the least element of the "superstitious" about him, yet, the incident gave him a rather uncomfortable feeling that unpleasant experiences were awaiting him in connection with "that passport business," as he called it. And, we might add, that, someway, somehow, "coming events," at times, seem to have a way of "casting their shadows before!" as Dare was to find out.

CHAPTER XX 

When Dare arrived in Stockholm he found a great religious gathering in session. It was called "The 

Baptist World Alliance." It seemed to derive its name from the fact that representatives of the Baptist persuasion, from every nation in the world, meet once every five years for the purpose of considering matters of common interest. What the leaders called the "fellowship of kindred spirits" engaged in common tasks, appeared to have large place in the gathering, as well as better acquaintance and mutual understanding through personal contact.

It was Dare's privilege to attend a number of the sessions of this body. In a letter to his wife Dare gave some of his impressions of that gathering. From that letter we shall quote a few paragraphs. "I confess, he wrote, there are many things about such a gathering to challenge the attention. Among such things might be mentioned the fact that they claim a membership, which this World Alliance represents, of fourteen million actual church members! The claim was made that there are, in the United States, nine million members of Baptist churches! "You know, my dear, that there is an old saying running something like this: 'One must needs go away from home to hear the news.' Well, listen to

this: One speaker affirmed that Thomas Jefferson obtained his idea of 'Democratic Government' from a small Baptist congregation not far from his home in the South. Now, that's 'going some' in the way of a claim, isn't it? Of course, it is well known that, theoretically, at least, a local Baptist church is the most democratically governed body to be found anywhere. However, as one mingled a bit with those at this gathering, he gained the idea at least, that not all churches were thus governed! "Another thing about that gathering that challenged attention, was the driving power of its emphasis on the rights of the individual in both religious and political matters! If I interpret what I heard correctly, in matters of religion a Baptist allows no individual or organization to come between him and God, save, as he says, 'Jesus Christ the Mediator between God and man!' And, politically, his position seems to be summed up in the following: 'All men were and are created free and equal and are endowed with certain inalienable rights, among which are, life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. And that all governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed.' They hold that many of their forebears laid down their lives for these principles long before they were written into the Declaration of Independence! "At one of the sessions of this body I saw, what was, to me, a 'new thing under the sun.' It was this: 

I saw, Africans, Americans, Chineses, English, Dutch,

French, Germans, Italians, Danes, Norwegians, Armenians, Jews, Russians, Swedes, Roumanians, Aus-

tralians, indeed, 'devout men from every nation under heaven,' as one reads, does he not, in the Book of the Acts? Well, I saw all these representatives rise and sing, each in his own tongue, 'all hail the power of Jesus name,' and, when, during the singing, I saw men, recently enemies on the field of battle, with tear-filled eyes, grip each others hands! Well, my dear, I could tell you of a fellow in whose eyes tears might have been seen also!" 

Dare closed that letter with the following: "There must be a cause other than human for such a manisfestation!"

CHAPTER XXI 

The Conference, which took him to Stockholm, having come to an end, Dare started for Berlin, for a second visit to Germany. He travelled by train from 

Stockholm to Trelleborg and thence, by the "Deutchland" across the Baltic Sea to Sassnitz, Germany. It took about four hours to cross. On the way over the remains of a wreck were seen. Arriving in Berlin about II p.m., he went directly to the "Central Hotel." 

When he arrived at the Hotel, Dare, was in fine spirits. The document he carried as a passport had, thus far, been accepted by all German officials as it had been when he first entered Germany at Bremer- Haven. He determined, therefore, to give no further thought to the matter of passports while in the land once dominated by the Holland Exile. 

But to come to a decision to banish all worry concerning a given matter, is one thing but to actually do it is quite another thing, as Dare was presently to find out; and as many before him have found out, and, as many others, doubtless, to the end of time, will find out! It is the constant reminders, from one source or another, of things disagreeable, that prevent absolute forgetfullness of matters that worry, as Dare was just now to find out.

The hour being late when he arrived at the hotel and anxious to get to bed as quickly as possible, he

opened his travelling bag and drew from it, with a bit of a jerk, his night-dress. As he did so, he heard something hit the floor of his room with a sort of a slipping muffled sound, like stealthily approaching footsteps, ominous in their suggestiveness. Glancing quickly in the direction of the sound he had heard, he saw the document he was carrying as a passport, come to rest on the highly polished floor, before a writing desk on the farther side of the room. Hastily crossing the room he picked it up and was about to place it upon the writing table when he noticed a blank, bearing all the ear-marks of officialdom, lying in plain sight. 

Scanning it carefully he found it was, indeed, an official blank. In fact it was from the Police Department! 

And it required the occupant of the room, without delay, to fill in the blank, being sure to answer every question, etc., etc.

Among the questions asked was, "Of what country are you a citizen?” 

Dare was tired, the light in the room none to bright, the print on the blank fine and difficult to read! He yawned wearily. The bed looked exceedingly inviting. 

Finally, with an emphatic, "Not for the whole Police force of the German Republic would I fill out any blankety, blank blank tonight!" he turned out the light, crawled into bed and straightaway fell asleep. 

The following morning Dare faced the matter of filling in the blank. The question "Of what country are you a citizen?" stood out with rather menacing aspects! Dare had something of a conscience! Even without the compulsion of an oath, he was disposed to

tell the truth. What answer, therefore, should he give to this question?

As the Department of State had interpreted the law, from the evidence, misunderstood and misinterpreted, as Dare belived, and as high legal authorities whom he had consulted also believed, he was not a citizen of the United States! (This decision, Dare proposed to try in the courts, should he be permitted to return to the United States). Luckily, for Dare's piece of mind, he did not see, at this time, the two obstacles to be raised in the way of his return!

Much has been said and written on the question as to whether a "Lie is ever justifiable?" There is another question, however, which has never been satisfactorily answered, at least to the satisfaction of every one, and that is "What, really, constitutes a lie all things considered?" 

Dare filled in the blank. And, since he spent some time in Berlin and vicinity and at other points in Germany and, since he was not molested by the authorities, he is of the opinion the blank, as he filled it in, must have been satisfactory.

 CHAPTER XXII 

"Central Hotel, Berlin, Germany, "My dear wife: "The Central Hotel is a very fine hotel. Indeed, I am not sure whether I have ever been in a finer Dining Room than the one in which I have been taking my meals in this hotel. Ceiling and walls are vast mirrors-plate glass, I judge-paneled in massive, gilt scroll work! As I walked through the lobby, happening to glance ceilingwards, I had the rather unique experience of seeing people walking head downwards, myself among them! The service throughout the hotel is all that could be desired. "There is one thing, however, which I should like to change and that is, the so-called, 'Continental Breakfasts l' 

I know some real, strong language I should like to use in anathematizing them! "From Strasland to Berlin there is a fine stretch of farming country. The farm buildings, both houses and barns, are excellent. I saw many large herds of fine cattle. The crops, rye, wheat, oats, barley, hay, sugar-beets and all garden truck, were very good. The towns were many and substantial. The people seemed well-to-do, busy and, apparently, happy. So far as outward appearances go, 'business as usual' would seem to be true. And yet, there is an indefinable something

The tremendous spring of the arches, the naves, the chancel, the balconies, the great organ, the statuary, the paintings, the whole scheme of decoration, the very heavy gilding, the three magnificent windows behind the chancel, depicting the Nativity, Crucifixion, and the Ascension, the great dome, high, massive, ornate, all made a strong appeal to one. "Just across the way stands the, now deserted save as a museum or curio, great city palace of the Ex- Kaiser. One of its drives, lined with superb marble statues on either side, excells, in sheer beauty and charm, any drive I have yet been over. "And then one must needs go to Potsdam. This is located on the Heval River. The trip by auto and boat from Berlin is delightful. Many wealthy Germans occupy fine cottages on either side of the Heval. 

Potsdam was the summer residence of Wilhelm, one time Emperor of Germany, now resident in Holland.

Here is also the Palace of Sansouci. This was the summer residence of Frederick the Great. One should not fail to visit this palace."

CHAPTER XXIII 

The atmosphere which Dare found in Munich, (Munchen) was wholly different from that experienced in Berlin, or, for that matter, in other parts of Germany. In Berlin the most cordial attitude toward 

Americans, so far as Dare was concerned, was manifested. Not so, however, in Munich, this famous, old 

Bavarian town.

The Bavarians are, of course, noted for their Bavarianism. It seemed to Dare as if they hated 

Berlin and all the Berlinites stood for. The Bavarians seemed to be keenly conscious of the fact that the United States was largely responsible for their defeat in the World War and the sting of defeat still rankled deeply.

During a conversation with a scion of the late nobility, Dare casually observed, "All Bavarians are, I suppose, solidly behind the present government?"

The man addressed turned a cold, hard stare upon Dare and, with a curl of the lip and an annoyed expression on his face, said: "We Bavarians do not believe that Germany was, nor is yet, ready for a Republic! The people need to be educated first!" And then in a tense voice, with a strong note of finality in it, concluded by saying, "But it will not, cannot, last!"

In view of this attitude Dare began to have very serious misgivings about how the Bavarian officials might treat the document he carried as a passport

when he attempted to leave Bavaria! Would it give them a chance to "get back" at, at least, one of their former enemies? The thought of being held up in such an environment gave Dare no small concern.

It is possible, however, that two items might have had something to do with softening Bavarian boorishness, if it may be so called. One was money; real money, looked good to them at the time; not as a bribe, be it remembered, but as something mightily needed! The other the appearance of the document 

Dare was carrying! 

From Munich Dare proposed going into Italy with

Venice as the first objective. But to get into Italy from 

Germany one must needs get out of Germany! And

Italy was also a recent enemy as well as the United States.

Dare, while awaiting his turn for the Munich's official o. k., assumed, as best he could, an independent, yet benevolent, attitude, as became one charged with high responsibilities of State! When his turn came, the official glanced at the document, then at Dare, who, with a raise of the eyebrows, causing a facial expression which seemed to pass important information, silently, of course, from one government official to another, and, in such a way as to prevent any bystander from noticing it, the Bavarian official, without opening it, attached the coveted o. k. and, with fine deference, returned the paper to Dare!

What a happy thing it would have been for John Dare if that same, "fine deference" could have been experienced at a most crucial point some distance ahead!

CHAPTER XXIV 

"Hotel Rheinisher Hof, Munchen (Munich) Germany. "My dear wife: "We had a delightful trip, by rail, from Berlin to this city. We ran into or through, a number of showers on the way, and saw two or three rainbows, one of which was exceedingly brilliant. "For about half-way from Berlin the country was very level and somewhat low. All the way, however, every foot seemed to be under cultivation. Better crops I have rarely, if ever, seen. "It is harvest time. Men, women and children, are in the fields. The tools, for the most part, are exceedingly primitive. I saw harvesting being done with a sickle, as in Bible times. Others were using ordinary scythes. Some had scythes with a half-fan attachment and a very few had old-fashioned cradles. I saw one old-fashioned self-rake reaper and one mowing machine at work. After reaching Bavaria I saw few horses, but many oxen and cows hitched to carts and wagons, singly and in pairs, doing the work of horses. "I also saw, in real life, what I had seen only in pictures, namely, children and adults herding and driving geese! Saw many women, old and young, carrying immense bundles of hay and fagots on their backs. It seems to me that women and children were doing the

major part of outside work! It looked a bit odd to 'a man from Iowa !' "A very interesting thing to the 'Auslander' (foreigner), is the way the land is farmed. I saw thousands of acres of grain and not a fence to be seen! It seems that those who till the land, in many sections of the country, live in small villages. In the morning they go out to their respective holdings which are, I should judge, from one to five miles distant, work all day and return to the village at nightfall. As a result you see one of the most charming landscapes imaginable. Consider two or three thousand acres of land farmed by a group of people, living in a common center. Each landhoder sows the kind of grain he desires. Different kinds of grain, some winter wheat, some spring, including wheat, rye, barley, oats, hay, sugar-beets, potatotes, and so forth, are sowed side by side, some ripen early, some late, the result being a variety of colors, in squares, and rectangles, giving the effect as of a vast checker-board! Then add the busy harvesters and what a unique and charming landscape results! "On the way from Berlin to Munich, we crossed what is called, 'Thuringer-Wald,' or 'Woods of Thuringa.' These are wooded hills, which rise to quite a height and derive their picturesqueness from the fact that the Germans have learned, and practice, the art of re-forestation. As soon as a piece of timber is cut, either for wood or lumber, young shoots are immediately set out. Hence, you find trees in all stages of growth and development.

"A tour of Munich is really worth while. I shall mention but two things however. One is the 'Dance of the Coopers,' which occurs each day about noon. 

The other is the 'Chamber of Horrors!' Here instru ments of torture of the middle ages are on display. 

If one doubted the existence of a Devil, here is abundant evidence of the existence of a multitude of devils! 

Only such could invent and use such instruments of torture on their fellowmen and women!"

CHAPTER XXV 

From Munich to Venice Dare crossed a part of the

Austrian Tyrol. It was up this valley that the Austrians made such a desperate effort to break through into Italy during the World War only to suffer one of the major defeats of that world tragedy. It was while crossing the Tyrol that Dare heard through the car window, as the train halted at Trento, a newsboy shouting, "La Morte Del Presidente degli Stati Uniti." 

Hurrying out Dare purchased a copy of a paper called, "Il Giornale Di Trento." In this, with the help of a native, Dare read the announcement of the death of President Harding! An odd place to receive such news!

Better and much abler writers than I have written at much length: yes, at very 'much length,' about Venice; much of it true and much of it, to my mind—well, I am not going to say what was on my mind! CHAPTER XXVI

Practically all tourists are agreed that there is a thrill experienced, upon approaching Rome, for the first time, such as is experienced on the approach to no other city save, perhaps, Jerusalem. That John Dare found this to be true, in his own experience, is evident from the following:

The shadow of the passport gone and, having won in his "Adventure With Chance," Dare hurried from the ship to the nearest telegraph office and sent the following message to his wife: 

"ARRIVED SAFELY. EXPECT ME WEDNESDAY EVENING ON NUMBER SEVEN." THE END

Dare, having made up his mind to test the matter of his citizenship in the courts, immediately on his arrival home, wrote the St. Louis office. In a conference with an official from that office, it was agreed that, while he could, doubtless, establish his citizenship, yet, because of the time it would take and the complications, it was advised that, application be made for citizenship, under the law provided in such cases, and that he would obtain full citizenship in three months. 

This Dare did.
