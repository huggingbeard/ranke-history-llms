THE GOLDEN WALL WHEN Valerie Hale mar ried Joe Merriweather she chose money instead of love. When an accident widowed her she guarded her fortune closely. Brett loved her but she mistook love for fortune hunting. Upon the advice of her friend, Marcia Bennett, she adopted a baby. The joy that this brought her was great but what Valerie needed was a partner in managing her life and home. Before she was to know this joy she had to endure tragedy and sorrow.

MARGERY AND LAWRENCE

CHAPTER ONE ROSALIE BENNETT tripped down the curving stairway into the living room. With her came youth and life and gayety, and the drab brown and tan room was transformed by the brightness of her yellow frock and shining golden hair.

As she picked up the receiver of the pealing telephone, there was laughter in the voice which said: "Hello.-No, Joe, Marcie's not here. She's over at 

Val's. I can call her or I—All right, I'll tell her.—Soon as she comes in. Bye-eye."

Rosalie was delighted that, for her sister at least, the all important question of youth in the busy North Carolina town of Midboro should be solved temporarily. It was thought absorbing query-: A date tonight or no date tonight? It gave color and meaning to the lives of Rosalie and Marcia Bennett.

There was the subordinate question as to whether the date should include Brett or Joe, or J. T. or Ronnie, or perhaps Web, or some newcomer or visitor; whether it would be a single, a double or the crowd. 

The telephone had much to do with deciding such

ΙΟ 

matters. Dad Bennett called it the sixth member of his family. "And the most spoiled," he insisted. "There it sits in the very center of things, only waiting for a chance to break into conversations, interrupt a bit of quiet thought or a nap; demanding immediate attention the moment it makes itself heard." 

But both Marcia and her younger sister considered that it had every right to a bit of spoiling. It brought imagination and a touch of mystery into their innermost lives. One could never be certain what the next call might bring. At any moment the welcome ring might summon them to answer, bringing them the voice of a prince or a pauper; Sir Galahad or the gas man.

Rosalie was so pleased with the nice message that she had received for her sister that she was tempted to run next door to deliver it at once, and would have done so had she not been so sure that the phone would ring again, perhaps for her this time, the moment she was out of the door. Yes, it was somewhat of a tyrant. 

So the girl contented herself with a magazine on the back porch, where she made a very pretty picture indeed, a composition in several shades of yellow, framed in the green of the wicker chair. 

It was too bad that such a picture had to be wasted on a rear porch; unfortunately, the sun took full possession of the front of the house during these midsummer afternoons.

II The Bennett house was on the East side of First Avenue. To those of you who do not know Midboro, Mother's philosophical description of their location, opposite so many handsome dwellings, sums up the family's situation in a nutshell. "There are more houses on our side, but we certainly have the best view," was the way she phrased it. 

Footsteps on the path at last. Marcia had scarcely opened the screen door when Rosalie began; "Joe Meriweather called. Said he'd phone again and to tell you not to make a date for this evening."

Marcia looked pleased, but she did not show surprise. "Yes I knew he would. The crowd are going over to the pool for a swim." "Oh, grand! How'd you know about it? See him somewhere? Aren't you just wild about his new car? 

Gee! mustn't it be simply thrilling to be able to get one when you don't even begin to need it?" Rosalie feigned a throaty tone as she continued: "Hello. This the auto show rooms? Please send over a few roadsters. Anything but blue will do. Had blue last month." She laughed. "Don't you wish once that—” 

Marcia interrupted to answer the first question. "I've been over at Val's. I was there when he phoned." She said it as though that explained everything. Rosalie looked mystified. "Well that's funny. Why, he called here just a few minutes ago. He told me to be sure and tell you the moment I saw you and not to—”

"Nothing funny about it, Rosalie. He called to ask 

Val, but he isn't going to take her. You see, she already had a date with Brett, so of course I'll go with Joe." 

Her soft brown eyes showed a certain pleasure, but no excitement. 

Rosalie snapped to a sitting posture and faced her sister, who had settled herself in a wicker chair opposite. "Of course? Nothing funny about it?" She repeated Marcia's words with a rising inflection, then gave a sniff and shrug that said volumes. "Well, I'd think it funny if Ronny Scott acted that way with me. And I'd think it even funnier if he got a date with me. Moreover, I—” 

Marcia laughed. "Oh, Rosalie, you're only sixteen. 

Ronny isn't much more and-" "And are you trying to tell me that when I reach the great age of nineteen, like my reverend sister, I'll look upon such things as a big joke and perfectly all right?" Indignation made her grey eyes glisten.

Marcia continued to smile. "Not a joke exactly but perfectly all right. Everyone in the crowd knows that Joe and Brett go with Val and me. They always have. Always will. And that's all there is to it."

She continued to tell about the party that was being planned for the evening. It was a last minute affair being arranged by Nancy Boyd for her cousin from

Winston-Salem. The Cavalier club boys had each been invited to get a girl and meet at the pool at sundown. There would be a supper served, and later all would drive back to the club for a dance. 

But Rosalie was not listening. The conversation she had started held dramatic possibilities. She returned to it. "I'll agree you always have and maybe you always will, but Valerie Hale can't marry both of them, and you'll-"

The sudden ringing of the telephone interrupted, as it so often did.... Marcia hurried in to answer. 

Of course it was Joe calling. They talked for a few minutes as to what time he and Brett might be expected.

When Marcia returned to the porch her sister had disappeared. Yet her unfinished sentence still seemed to hang in the air, faintly but as surely there as the perfume from the four-o'clocks opening their crimson and white blossoms in the flower border by the steps.

She stood looking down the winding flag-stone path and giving the words of Rosalie a few moments thought. But there was no time for more than that. 

The boys would be calling for Val and herself in an hour and another merry evening be under way.

This summer had been like that. Every week had brought visitors and unexpected pleasures to add to the usual round. Only two more weeks of it remained

before the boys would be going back to colleges and universities. Then she and Val would be back at their studies at the Academy. 

But that, too, seemed a matter for consideration in the distant future. The immediate present was all that mattered now.

She must wave her hair, press her blue linen, collect her bathing outfit, and above all hurry. This was no time for porch conversations, even if her sister were there to take part. Probably Rosalie had entirely forgotten about what she had been going to say, for when Marcia saw her five minutes later she was quite absorbed in her own affairs. The girl was standing in front of a full length mirror being fitted in a new dress of a lovely pale green that, with her yellow hair, made one think of swaying golden-glow.

Mother sat at the sewing machine busily buzzing down a seam while Aunt Laura-Lindsay, who was visiting, sat upon the floor pinning up the dress hem. "I wanted it longer," said Rosalie. "It looks as if you never will remember I'm taller than Marcia. I'm getting to be a big girl now." Her laugh rippled.

It was very evident that she was eagerly anticipating the future.

Marcia, though, would have held time back by the forelock. Now was so pleasant. The past few months had been just about perfect. Never had a season been so full of pleasure. It had been possible for her to forget that she was not what people called popular.

At present she was having as good a time as any girl in Midboro. She wasn't missing a thing. 

She had ceased to give a thought to the fact that she was not as pretty as her chum. For after all, who was? Certainly no girl in this town. Juanita Kane had hair almost as golden and wavy. Nathalie had as good a figure. Judy's complexion was as flawless. 

Fay Crawford's violet-blue eyes and charming smile were as perfect; but Valerie Hale had all these rolled into her lovely young self. 

And with it all she had remained unspoiled. "She's pretty enough for both of us," Marcia often said.

Not that Val needed to be, for Marcia had her own attractions. But never in all her life had she heard them so extolled, as everyone was always talking about Val's. In fact, the first time she had ever heard of the girl her own parents had been discussing the child's picture-like beauty.

Marcia could still recall having slipped out of the room and stood for several moments in front of her mother's mirror studying her brown eyes, too big for her little face, her sleek dark hair. 

She had felt cheated. Why should Val have everything? Why should anyone have to wear dark hair?

She had given it a pull and decided, then and there, never to look into a mirror again. 

Naturally she did not live up to that promise for long, and her love and admiration for her new playmate soon made her forget her jealousy.

To tell the truth, Marcia was not nearly as hopeless a case as that four year old child had supposed, and as she grew older she made the best of what she had. 

Individually her features were not perfect, but the general ensemble had a definite charm. Her creamy skin was clear and toned well with the dark hair. 

People usually spoke of her as "the girl with the lovely brown eyes." There was a certain sureness about her walk; one felt that she would be perfectly able to take care of herself or others in an emergency. 

And though she was not tall, neither was she overly small. "A nice comfortable size,” Dad called her. 

Marcia could not remember a time when she hadn't known Val. It was inevitable that the two girls should be closer than sisters. They were almost the same age and had grown up together. 

Valerie Hale had been brought to the brown bungalow next door when she was less than three years old. 

Though she could not remember her parents, she had never lacked love or comfort.

As a matter of fact, Marcia had often thought that the little golden haired girl had gained many things by being an orphan. From the very first she had been favored with special attentions. Extra privileges were granted her. It was as if the whole busy little city had adopted Val. She had called Marcia's parents Uncle 

Jerry and Aunt Grace, and Marcia had been given the role of big sister.

"Take care of Val," was the phrase so often repeated by Aunt Ellen that Marcia felt it her bounden duty. 

However, it is doubtful if Val really needed the little brown eyed girl's protection and guidance, for it was always Val who led.

Others always followed willingly enough, but Val was the girl who first marked up the sidewalk for hopscotch, appeared on the school grounds with the first pocket full of jacks, or the first skipping rope.

As they went through school together it was usually Val who had the first new hat of the season, the latest thing in wraps and beads. 

And now, although they were almost nineteen, they seldom started out together without Aunt Ellen's: "Take care of Val, Marcia dear."

Val was never by any chance left out of a game or overlooked at a dance. From the very earliest days she had an appeal for the opposite sex. When she had been a pretty baby, fatherly men would pick her up and make childish love to her. It was nothing for the boys to bring her shining apples and the prettiest of the season's flowers, which she always shared with her chum.

Brett and Joe had shared her favors for several years. 

The one who first asked for Val's date was the lucky boy. The other took Marcia, and as they usually went to the same places it didn't make much difference. 

The two boys were also inseparable pals. They lived

opposite to one another and nearer to the heart of the city. Joe was the only child in the Meriweather home. 

Everybody in Midboro knew the Meriweather mansion, as it was called, and every visitor to the city was taken to see it. If you were lucky enough to be there in Spring you saw the tulip tree in bloom and so did not give much thought to the large somber looking stone house behind its high brick wall. 

After you were told; "And this is the Meriweather mansion," it was explained that the widow who lived there was one of the richest women in the state. You were informed that she owned the cotton mills on the West side, and they were pointed out from the hill top. 

If a woman were showing you around she was pretty sure to mention that Mrs. Meriweather was very lovely, and many wondered why she had never re-married. 

But if you were a native of Midboro you knew all this except the answer to the "why," and probably just continued to wonder. Perhaps a few even knew the answer.

Brett Ellwood's father was in a position to know. 

He had been legal advisor to the family for many years and lived opposite in a large white colonial house. 

Joe's mother had always been very fond of Brett. 

He was just the age of her own boy, and then, too, she felt sorry for him. Not that Brett wanted her to. He hated sympathy, just as many do who have a right to it. 

An illness in his baby days had made Brett a cripple for many years and left him with one leg slightly

shorter than the other. His friends knew better than ever to let him think that his slight deformity was noticeable.

Joe and Brett had gone to Kindergarten and grade school together, graduated from High, and now shared a room at the State University. 

The fact that Joe Meriweather was one of the wealthiest boys in the state seemed to be forgotten. He was just one of the crowd, and it was evident that his greatest pleasure was to share everything with them. 

He was seldom seen alone in his roadster. The 

Cavalier Club was his idea. Each boy paid dues, but the fine old house they used for a meeting place was part of the Meriweather estate. 

Of course there were other crowds, many of them in the flourishing little business center of the southern state, but none that had quite so much fun as the 

Cavaliers and their lucky girls.

As Marcia had drifted through the years with this happy group she had often listened to the love affairs of one and another. She was a wonderful listener. 

Her interest seldom wavered. Naturally, at times when she heard a story, there came the thought: What about my own?

She liked the boys, enjoyed their companionship, but had never felt that any one of them meant a great deal more to her than any other.

Joe Meriweather was one of the finest fellows she had ever known, but she knew it would be foolish to

call her feeling for him love. Even more foolish to be in love with him.

He treated her like a sister, was always telling her of some new interest or of his affairs with other girls up in the college town. This summer he had been telling her of his love for Val.

Marcia knew that this would be at least a part of the conversation during their engagement this evening. 

But what did it matter? He was such fun that any girl in Midboro, or in the State, would have given her eye teeth to change places with Marcia Bennett tonight.

CHAPTER TWO 

AT THE musical call from the horn of Joe's shining new roadster Marcia and Val both flitted from their homes like bright butterflies, their yellow and blue dresses meeting beside the green car, whose doors opened for a moment and shut almost noiselessly. Their color added the finishing touch of beauty; even the motor purred with satisfaction.

After the first exchange of greetings, they sat quietly content while the machine skimmed along the center of the broad highway, leaving the city behind; passing now a stretch of woods, now a farm home surrounded by green fields of cotton, where a few open bolls waved a white flag as if to announce that autumn was very near. 

Marcia was humming a popular tune. "You must be happy," Joe suggested. "Who wouldn't be?" She gave a glance around her. "Isn't everything just perfectly perfect?" "Well, yes and no." Joe's bright eyes twinkled and he gave her a meaningful smile. “Not complaining, of course." 

"But I suppose I'm to infer that it could be better?" 

She lifted her eyes knowingly. 21

"Slightly, yes, but then it could be a lot worse. At least we are all here together." "Just what I was saying to Val this morning, Joe.

This summer has been grand. I hate to have the next two weeks end." "It must have been in the air this morning. I'd never so much as given it a thought, until Mums began to talk about it being first of September and time to plan things, and now I don't seem to be able to get the idea off the road. It's blocking traffic." Joe frowned. "Of course I suppose it's grand for you boys, going back to your friends up there and the team and frats and dances and things. You don't know anything about being a day pupil at the Academy," said Marcia. "No, I agree it looks as if that's one of the things old Brett and I will have to miss, but I think we can forego it." He smiled. His jollying tone always added a note of fun to his conversation. Now suddenly he was serious as if in afterthought. "Yes, I suppose things really could be worse than having to go back to the Hill." "Worse?" She turned and looked at him in surprise. "Why Joe! What's come over you? I thought you were always just wild to get back." "I want to have my pie and eat it, I guess. I'd want to go back right enough if only I could take what I want along with me." This was a serious Joe talking. 

One whom few knew. "You know how 'tis." he said.

But evidently Marcia did not, for she answered with an affirmative nod. "Of course, I'd forgotten about it though. The new ruling goes into effect this year. 

They won't let you take the roadster with you." "I wish that was all I wanted to take," said Joe. 

Then he corrected himself hurriedly. "No I don't, but I wish-" He had stopped and was shaking his head. She smiled at him. "Do you know what you really do wish, Joe Meriweather?"

Several minutes passed before he answered, then he spoke slowly. "Yes. I wish I was sure about everything, I guess." "That wouldn't be much fun, though." Marcia's mood was still merry; she was not sure she understood him even now. No one expected Joe to remain serious more than a moment. He had blue eyes that twinkled and a quick smile seldom absent, but it was still missing when he spoke again. "Not fun perhaps, but mighty restful. This; she didn't say yes-she didn't say no; business isn't so good at a distance.”

Marcia's usual nod of understanding, that her confidants knew so well, was his only answer. She turned for a word with Val and Brett, but they had taken up the song she had dropped. She joined her voice with theirs, and all were harmonizing in a song of the day as they turned off the main thoroughfare to the narrow lane that led to the pool.

"I suppose Brett feels the same way," she was thinking as she watched the occupants of the back seat. 

There was a merry party of about twenty boys and girls, which was reinforced by almost as many more boys later, come to cut in on the dances at the country club. 

No girl ever rested for long, but during a “no break" dance which she sat out with Brett on the porch, gazing into a starlit sky, she again introduced the subject on her mind. 

To her surprise Brett disagreed with her. "I couldn't stand it here much longer," he said. "Ten days more will be plenty for me. I'll be glad to get back. A regular diet of this would choke me." "Why, Brett Elwood, I thought you were having a grand time!" "So I am, some of the time, but what's a fellow to do? You know how I feel about Joe and always have. Well, I want to go on feeling that way, but this other thing is getting a bit too strong. I thought you could see how things are. You always seem to understand." He stopped and waited for her to go on, but evidently she was not sure of his meaning, for she offered no help.

She shifted her position and took her gaze from the stars to look closely at him. After some moments of silence he began again. “Don't you see, Marcia, this thing is nothing new

with me? I've always felt this way about Val, I mean. It's not just a summer affair.” "And do you think that getting away is going to help?" she asked. "I don't think anything is ever going to help―much. 

I've tried to think that I had a chance with her." He hesitated. "But how could I have?" The last words were blurted out as if they hurt. "So you see it's no place for the two of us. First thing you know I'll have old Joe hating me. Any one can see he's got a thorough-going case of it, too. Can't get away from it with us all here together. You know how it is, don't you?" "I'm not so sure, Brett. I don't believe I ever had a serious case like yours. Sometimes I wonder if I ever shall." She tried to recall the few boys who had ever interested her more than Brett and Joe, but decided that none of them had mattered greatly; and she remembered her words of a few hours earlier to 

Rosalie; "It's always been like this. Always will." 

As if it could be.

The dreamy waltz ended and the lights of the dance floor were turned on to their full again. It seemed too much sudden light. A feeling almost of fright clutched her.

It was a relief to be joined by the others. Through every door the happy, laughing crowd was coming out for intermission. Marcia and Brett rose and fol-

lowed them down the drive to the parked cars, where they piled in gayly, talking. Cigarettes were lighted, horns were honked, and they were all off for a cooling drive in the breeze as they sped over the roads and down to a favorite stand in a nearby shopping center. 

There Negro boys, dressed in white, darted about with trays of cold, foamy drinks. There was much merry chatter from car to car. Then back they dashed to the club, refreshed, cooled somewhat, and ready for the second half. Dancing began, boys were breaking in, every girl was on her toes in every sense of the words.

No time to think of complicating situations. The present was the paramount issue. "Why bother about tomorrow?" Marcia asked herself. For she realized that, whether she bothered or not, she could neither stop the march of time nor see into the future. Life went on, inexorable and not subject to her will.

Tomorrow they were driving over to Matthews for a barbecue and visit to the County fair. Saturday they would be going up in a crowd to the baseball game at Statesville. Sundays were always the same.

The girls grouped in threes and fours at one another's homes, and the boys came in bunches to call and drink punch. Monday there was to be another party for this visiting girl, who was already as much one of them as though she had been there for weeks instead of a few hours.

So the days flew by. Sandwiched in between these

larger pleasures were visits with the girls, drives to barbecue stands for a round of something to eat and drink, or even to the drug store two blocks from home, for no one ever thought of walking in Midboro, unless we count such folks as Callie.

She was the coffee colored maid who came in twice a week; "to holp Miz Bennett rid up a bit," is the way she expressed her usefulness.

Marcia seldom had a moment alone during her waking hours. Naturally she had given some thought to the boys' problem. It was nothing new. She had always known of their feeling for Val. It had seemed just a happy game, but suddenly it was getting serious. 

The boys were a few years older than herself. Joe just twenty one, Brett almost a year his senior. One more year at the University, and then what?

She decided to have a quiet talk with Val first chance that offered, but that didn't come for several days and then lasted but a few minutes. 

She began it as they drove down town in the Bennett car to bring Dad home for lunch. “Just think, Val. Only six more days and the boys will be leaving, and then it's back to St. Mary's for us," she began. "Oh, don't let's talk about it. As Callie says; "Time enough to think 'bout ol'man trouble after he done gits you, chile. Don't believe in cou'tin' him"." "But when he's just around the corner-" "Turn the other way," Val interrupted, and her pretty laugh rang out.

Marcia joined in; then, as they waited at the corner for a red light to fade, there was a well known whistle from the sidewalk, and a moment later they had old 

Toby and J. T., otherwise James Tobias Frazier and 

J. T. Gregg, as back seat passengers.

Serious conversation was out of the question as they stopped at a drug store for crackers and drinks, with much laughing and chatter.

By the time the boys left them at the town square their minds were far from what autumn days would bring and had turned to the subject ever dear to a woman's heart. "Aunt Ellen has my new green almost finished.

I'm going to wear it this evening. It's almost exactly the color of Joe's roadster. What're you going to wear?" Val asked; and while they sat outside the town's principal machinery house, where Mr. Bennett spent a greater part of every day, they were soon deep in the discussion that never bothers lilies of the field. "Discussing the affairs of the nation, I suppose?"

Dad interrupted in his usual smiling way a few minutes later. He was a tall, slim man with greying hair, and though the passing years had taught him that there should be a few "maybe's" and "perhapses" in the psalm of life, they had not taken the twinkle from his kindly grey eyes. He had learned to labor and grown used to waiting.

Val and Marcie were not very well versed in either subject. "Let us then be up and doing" was more in

their line; and the following few days were a perfect example of the quotation.

The next "quiet talk" took place on Val's porch, to the accompaniment of a clattering lawn mower that 

Fred Bennett was pushing in order to earn his weekly spending money. The girls were looking their prettiest, waiting for the boys who were taking them to a watermelon feast, and who were already half an hour late. "Maybe they decided not to come," Val suggested. "I declare Brett acts so queerly lately I don't know just what to expect next. If I give him a date he seems almost apologetic for asking because we go in Joe's car. 

What's got into him all of a sudden, I ask you? We never used to pay any attention to whose car. We could use ours or yours for that matter. Whichever is empty does for me. Then he gets moody and-" "I suppose you never gave a thought as to what's the real trouble," Marcia interrupted. 99 "Of course I have. I always try to be as much with him as with Joe. Always have. I couldn't to save me tell you which one I like best. I guess I've known them both too long, and anyway, why get serious all of a sudden? It's fun this way. Why change it?"

Marcia took a long whiff of air perfumed with newly cut grass, and settled more comfortably into a camp chair. "My idea exactly, Val."

Then suddenly they both jumped up and ran to the

front of the house, where Joe's car was just stopping. "Enjoy yourselves, my dears. Take care of Val, Marcia." Aunt Ellen said as they passed.

So the last days of their happiest summer ambled away in true Carolina fashion.

Joe and Brett were to be among several boys who were leaving on the eleven o'clock train that fateful morning. Val and Marcia would be at the station to give them a "send off", and had planned to drive down in the Hale's car. When Marcia ran over to hurry her chum, she was met by Aunt Ellen, who seemed puzzled. "But I thought she was with you, Marcie. She left over an hour ago."

There was no time to discuss the matter. Marcia hurried to the street car, which took its own time and all of her patience before it finally landed her at the dingy grey depot where most of the crowd were gathered. "Where's Joe?" all were asking, for Mrs. Meriweather had also just arrived. "Isn't he here? He left sometime ago in the roadster." His mother looked in a startled way from one to the other. "Where's Brett? He will know."

But Brett didn't know. He had waited for Joe to call for him, then had decided to come down alone.

He thought it all a great joke, and was laughingly taking all the boxes of home made candy and cake that the girls were bringing.

"It looks like I'll have a busy week eating all this sweet stuff myself if he misses the train. I'll have to watch my figure." Any reference to his slimness always called for many more, and so the moments passed until ten minutes had slipped away. 

The train was at the station, puffing impatiently while the express cars were being loaded. It would leave in two minutes. But still Joe Meriweather was not in sight.

It was Marcia who first saw the cloud of dust down the sand-clay road at the side of the station, and a moment later she recognized the green roadster. "There he is. Just in time." She pointed and called to Brett.

The engine bell was ringing. The green car was flying toward them, its musical horn tooting as it came, but already the long white arms that guarded the rail-road tracks were being lowered. 

Joe tried to stop, but his speed was too high. The car skidded, swerved and threw him against the arms which had met to protect him.

Every one ran toward the wreck. It was not until then that Marcia saw Val. She was dressed in her green linen that matched the car from which she had been thrown.

If she were hurt she did not know it. She had rushed to Joe and was trying to drag him from the overturned car. "Joe! Joe! Don't you know me?" she cried hysteri-

cally. "It's Val! Don't you hear me, Joe? Val, your wife!" 

Marcia supposed that her chum was out of her head.

Mrs. Meriweather, who was down on her knees by her son, had not heard. She was too frightened by the look she saw in her boy's eyes, which had opened for a moment. "Take care of Val, Mums. I'll be all right. We just got married down in York. Almost missed the train." He had managed to smile before he slipped into unconsciousness.

Those were the last words Joe Meriweather ever spoke.

The train pulled away, its bell tolling.

CHAPTER THREE 

ALTHOUGH the year that followed was in many ways the same for Marcia and Brett, it seemed like an entirely different life.

At first they had decided that things could not go on, but as usual they found that things must. Brett returned for his fourth and last year at the university, Marcia for her second and final year at St. Mary's 

Academy for Girls. Both felt horribly alone, though at all times surrounded by young people. 

But to Val, life was entirely changed. She had gone to live at the Meriweather home. However, it was not Joe's loving mother who was caring for the girl. Val's bruises had soon healed. Mrs. Meriweather's injury had touched a more vital spot; so it was Val who was trying to take Joe's place and comfort the broken-hearted woman. 

If Marcia had not felt that she must still "take care of Val," Aunt Ellen would not have allowed her to forget it. "She's so alone. She needs you more than ever, dear," the elderly woman was constantly telling her.

Seldom a day passed without the girls spending part of it together, but how differently from in the past. 33

Sitting about the large dull house they talked in undertones, lest they disturb Joe's mother.

Marcia brought Val news from the school girls or the crowd. It was a rainy fall. The elms sent down a shower of leaves at every gust of wind. 

The great magnolias dropped their rain-washed leaves. There was a steady tattoo of water falling on the tin roof of the porte cochere. The nearest approach to happiness about the place was black Aunty Lou crooning about it being "Bout time to pass over de' rivah Jordan.”

Their only escape from the present lay in the past. "Do you remember the party over at Frazier's when we all dressed up? Remember the trip down the river when I fell in? Remember the dances at the country club when we had the orchestra from New

"I can see it all now, Marcia. I've been playing follow the leader. All the girls I liked best at school were taking the commercial course and pounding the typewriter each day, and I just went along, like the boys did tonight. I never thought of being a nurse. 

I knew I could never teach." She shrugged and continued. "Mother and Dad both agreed that office work was the only thing for me. I just followed along. That's why I'm making such a hash of it. Fred's fall woke me up." "Or was it Dr. Hume's discovery?" Marcia asked. 

He shook his head. "No, I can't take much credit. 

Someone would have found her if I hadn't. I've been trying to warn her of the hard years of training and the very real work there is to it, but she isn't the least bit discouraged. Remember what he said? 'Folks just nacherally know some things.' This is one of them." There was the same expression in her eyes as in those of the little chap in the garden. Marcia speaking. Trees whispered in a passing breeze, katydids exchanged their usual line of summer evening conversation.

It was the first time in months that she had been absolutely alone with him. Marcia was not at all sure she could control her voice if she spoke. It was pleasantly painful to be so near him. He was at her feet. She could reach over and touch his hair by a slight movement. Her hand almost ached in its longing to caress him. But she dared not.

He looked up finally, and as though Rosalie had just spoken, he was carrying on her thought. “I quite agree with her. I believe if I could enlist your help on my side, I'd never worry again, either.”

There was a tired, troubled note in his voice that 

Marcia had never heard before. She had her usual impulse to offer comfort. What could be the matter?

Dr. Dave turned and was looking squarely at her.

She felt very proud of the way she had made that last statement. She felt she was facing facts. 

Dr. Dave stood up, took a look at the seat beside her, then thought better of it and went into the house for a look at his patient.

Marcia moved over on the porch swing, buried her face in the crook of her arm. And she had told him she was happy. "I won't cry. I won't. I won't," she repeated.

Brett since baby days. It nearly killed him when Val married Joe Meriweather and tied her up with that fortune. He felt he hadn't the right to ask her.

Marcia thought for a few moments before replying. “Well, no one ever came right out and was rude as that; but when I've mentioned my feelings on the subject no one has ever disagreed with me, so I know it must be so."
