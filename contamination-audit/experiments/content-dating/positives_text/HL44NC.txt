ADULT PAROLE AUTHORITY ANNUAL REPORT Introduction 

On June 30, 1969, Ohio's seven penal institutions held 10,056 inmates. On the same day over 4,800 parolees were free throughout the state, most of them working, supporting their families and paying taxes. Every parolee, working or not, is always under the supervision of a trained parole officer. 

Thus, parole has become a part of our system of criminal justice. It allows us to control an offender's behavior in the community where he will live and work. Eventually 98 percent of all 

10,000 inmates now in Ohio's prisons will be released. Some will be released when their terms expire; some will be released to begin another term in another institution; some will be released through legal writ. Most of them - over 95 percent — will be released through parole. When they are so released, they become the responsibility of the Adult Parole Authority and its 212 employees.

Parole Supervision, however, is only one function of the Adult 

Parole Authority Agency. Probation Development is another. Headed by a superintendent, this section strives to develop the state probation system so that probation services can be applied more fruitfully throughout the state. Probation saves the state a great deal of money by permitting an offender to remain free and work rather than spend time in prison. Probation is seldom granted to dangerous or assaultive offenders; rather, it is usually for the first time offender convicted of such crimes as theft, forgery, or for offenses against property. In any case, at the first sign of further criminal activity, Adult Parole Authority probation officers act fast to revoke the probation.

A third section of the Adult Parole Authority is the Administration and Research section. Also headed by a superintendent, this section has the responsibility for research, record keeping, and recruiting. In recent months it has assumed the added responsibility for staff development, federal grant acquisition and coordination, and program planning.

The fourth section in the Adult Parole Authority is the Parole Board. Seven men strong, this board has the grave responsibility of determining when to release a prisoner to the community on parole. 

Each month they make a circuit of all seven of Ohio's institutions conducting interviews with inmates eligible for parole. 

All four sections in the Adult Parole Authority Parole Supervision, Probation Development, Administration and Research,

and the Parole Board - are supervised by the Chief of the Authority, George F. Denton. As chief, he is the appointed authority who has the responsibility for personnel, policy and practices in the Authority. An added responsibility was acquired this year through his creation and supervision of the position of Review Officer. 

Through the use of these review officers he has provided a method for reviewing and granting meritorious consideration to selected inmates. This review officer device has added immeasureably to inmate morale and provided incentive for achievement.

The Adult Parole Authority was established in March of 1965. 

Since its inception it has maintained a spirit of innovation, scholarship and inquiry. These attributes plus the application of the latest concepts in corrections have produced another year of memorable achievement. This annual report is a brief chronicle of these achievements.

Parole Supervision 

The Parole Supervision section maintains custody, control, and supervision of paroled persons. Parole units are organized in ten offices throughout the state (see map). Each unit has five or six officers, and is headed by a unit supervisor who is accountable to a regional supervisor. Altogether, there are 19 parole and three probation units distributed within the three regions of the State. (For a listing, see ADMIMISTRATIVE OFFICERS OF THE ADULT PAROLE AUTHORITY). 

Parole supervision is a complex job. It requires the parole officer to demonstrate a variety of skills ranging from that of employment counselor to investigator. 

The call is to those who have a rare combination of interest, skills, and dedication of purpose; to those who are looking for a career in a progressive agency; and to those who want to make a professional contribution to the protection and safety of the community by reconstructing lives of parolees. 

Parole Officers supervise persons released on parole from one of Ohio's seven penal institutions. The supervision process begins with a development of a parole plan by the parole officer and an evaluation is made of the plan's merits. If the plan is acceptable, the parolee is released and placed under supervision. Continuing supervision is then carried on in the field by the Parole Officer. In this work the officer functions under the premise that each person has a basic worth and dignity. He further believes that criminal behavior can be changed. 

By helping each parolee to understand himself better, he enables the

parolee to find acceptable and satisfying ways of meeting life's problems.

To accomplish this difficult task, a specified type of case work is employed which, though sometimes aggressive in its approach, considers each situation objectively, with faith in human nature, and with an understanding of inter-personal dynamics. The officer establishes a professional relationship with each parolee and strives to produce a behavior modification that neutralizes anti-social and criminal inclinations. Investigations 

Contacts 

On June 30, 1969, Adult Parole Authority records show that our officers were supervising 4, 832 active cases (see Table 3). The average case load on that date was 53.1. In addition to their supervisory duties, these officers performed investigations of various kinds: for example in 1969 our officers completed 5,478 placement and 7,782 miscellaneous type investigations. Placement investigations require that the parole officer examine the conditions and environment of a proposed residence for a emerging parolee. 

Miscellaneous investigations include such activities as gathering information for other states, employment candidate investigations, etc. Every officer is required to keep an accurate record of his contacts with or about any parolees under his supervision. In their supervisory role officers maintain a constant dialog with the parolee, his family, his employer, with community welfare agencies, with the police, and with any other entity having impact on the parolee or his adjustment. During the year, our officers had contacts with parolees directly, at least 52,733 times or for an average of 4,394 personal contacts per month. Contacts with others, that is, family, neighbors, employers, etc. came to 88,397 or 7,366 per month.

Parole Activity 

In the course of the year total of 8,013 men and women were on parole from Ohio's seven prisons. In addition, there are 1,375 parolees from other states supervised by Ohio parolee officers. All told, during the course of the year over 9,000 different parolees were handled by the Adult Parole Authority (see Table 4).

Ohio is signatory to the interstate compact and agrees to supervise offenders from other states. Similarly, offenders from 

Ohio's institutions who choose to live in another state may do so under a reciprocal agreement. In such cases, the state agreeing to assume supervision is known as the receiving state and the state supplying the offender is known as the sending state. 

During fiscal 1969, Ohio sent 453 parolees out of the state to be supervised elsewhere and received 448 parolees in return. Thus, Ohio sent out five more cases than it received. Violations

Nine out of ten parolees released to the community succeed on parole. In the year just elapsed, only 809 parolees were returned to prison or sentenced on new charges. When this figure is compared to the 8,013 handled during the course of the year, it shows that only 10.1 percent of all the offenders handled during the year failed on parole.

When a man is released from a penal institution to parole, he is still deprived of certain basic citizenship rights forfeited by conviction; namely, the right to vote, to serve on jurys, and to hold public office. He may, however, engage in the normal community activities enjoyed by everyone else. If his parole behavior is satisfactory for at least one year (in most cases), he is awarded a final release certificate. When he receives this certificate his forfeited rights are restored and he is no longer under the jurisdiction of the 

Adult Parole Authority. 

Thus, contrary to popular belief, most released offenders succeed on parole, become wage earners and tax payers, and earn final releases with the attendant restoration of rights. 

Probation Development 

Typically, in the course of a year, there are around 9,000 felony convictions in Ohio. Of all these convictions, about 33 percent go to prison. Twenty percent are jailed for short terms, fined, or receive suspended sentences. The balance 47 percent 

47 percent - receive probation.

Thus, over 60 percent of all felony convictions are disposed of through probation, fines, jail terms, or suspended sentences. 

When a man is sentenced to a penal institution and is subsequently released after serving a minimal period of time he is known as a parolee. If instead of prison, the offender is released to the community and placed under the supervision of a probation 10

officer, he is known as a probationer. The only difference between the probationer and parolee is that in the case of probation, the sentence is suspended as long as there is good behavior. 

For over 40 years probation in Ohio was strictly a county responsibility. The quality of probation services varied in the counties from the inadequate to the near professional. Many counties lacked even a rudimentary probation system. 

In 1965, however, the state legislature, recognizing the deficiencies in probation services throughout the state, created a 

State Probation Development Section within the Adult Parole Authority. Beginning with a staff of two, the Probation Development section increased its services each year until today the staff numbers 

18. Through its Probation Development section the Adult Parole 

Authority supervises active cases and dispenses advice and direction. throughout the state to probation systems requesting it. Active cases today total 417, compared to the 276 supervised last year. In addition, the section maintains control over 111 inactive cases compared to 79 similar cases last year. The number of counties served by the Adult Parole Authority in the area of probation is now 

23 compared to 16 served last year. (See map)

The Adult Parole Authority is empowered to institute a probation system in those counties where the courts request such services. 

The counties that have requested and received such services are shown on the map below. Case activity in both the active and inactive category is shown in Table 5. 

One other function of the Probation Development section worthy of note is the pre-sentence investigation. This report is written by a well-trained probation officer who exhaustively examines records for every pertinent fact about the offender's life and behavior. This vital information enables supervising authorities to make evaluations and predictions regarding the offenders behavior.

The report is also helpful in selecting offenders for probation since it serves as a guide for a judge in his decision whether to imprison or to put on probation. The report is also used extensively by institution staff, the Parole Board, and parole officers. 

When conducted by a well trained probation officer, a presentence report is a major control in the administration of criminal justice. Although it is a judge's most valuable guide and it is required by law where probation is granted (section 2951.03 O.R.C.) 42 percent of the defendants appearing before Common Pleas courts in Ohio are sentenced without the benefit of such a report. Parole Board 

Ohio has a seven man parole board one of whom serves as chairman. Regularly each month the Board visits every one of the seven penal institutions conducting interviews of "parole eligible" cases. Usually, they "hear" cases in three panels of two men each.

The remaining board member handles office and administrative duties as they arise.

This year the Parole Board interviewed 7,034 cases. Not all, however, were parole hearings. Broken down into various categories they represented 6,561 parole hearings; 430 involved technical parole violations and the remaining 43 were clemency cases. If the Parole 

Board, in examining a case, decides to grant a parole, arrangements are made for the inmate to be released, usually thirty days after the "hearing." If, for some reason, the board finds that an inmate is a threat to the community or that he lacks sufficient maturation, it may deny parole.

Most of the men and women selected for parole by the Parole Board succeed on parole (see Table 4). Some about 10 percent are returned to the institution because they committed another crime or because of an inability to meet the requirements of parole supervision. Because of the Parole Board's careful discrimination in the selection process and because of the intensive treatment orientation of our parole officers, the number of technical violators returned each year has steadily decreased. 

In 1966, for example, Ohio parole authorities returned 416 technical parole violators for an average of 34.7 per month. Returns have declined steadily since then. In 1967 the monthly average was 

30.7; and in 1968, it was 25.5. Last year only 306 technical parole violators were returned.

When an inmate is denied parole or continued (in prison jargon "flopped"), he is informed of the Board's decision within a few days. together with a reason as to why. Administrative Releases 

From time to time a parolee will abscond supervision or be committed to an institution for another crime. If the absconded parolee is not heard from for ten years or more he may be given an administrative release. Similarly, if he is reconvicted, he may also be granted such a release. This is not leniency, however. Rather, it permits the Adult Parole Authority to terminate jurisdiction over a case for reasons of administrative convenience. Such a release does not restore the civil rights of the parolee. 

When the administrative release procedure was first conceived and applied in 1965, such releases reached a peak of 1,918. This 14

activity represented the disposition of accumulated cases that had been inactive for ten years or more or cases where additional long-term incarceration was demonstrable. This year, by comparison, only 402 administrative releases were granted. Since the law's inception, 3,877 administrative releases have been issued by the Adult Parole Authority. Enormous time and expense have been saved by the exercise of this law. 

Review Officers 

This year, for the first time in Ohio correctional history, a position of review officer was established by the Adult Parole 

Authority. Three qualified and experienced Parole Authority personnel were appointed to these positions. A novel and imaginative approach to a pressing problem, the review process provides prisoners the incentive for good behavior by motivating them to undertake programs for self-improvement.

The Review Officers gather and evaluate as much information as is possible about each case they review. This information is then incorporated in the Parole Authority's file for the final analysis decision by the Parole Board. To make the process more understandable, we will cite a specific example. Suppose an inmate appeared before the Parole Board in January of 1968 and for various valid reasons the Parole Board denied parole and set a future hearing date of January, 1970. In such cases the inmate is said to be continued (in prison jargon “flopped"). Consequently, he would not normally be eligible for a parole hearing until January 1970 arrives. 

Under the review process however, after the inmate has served only one-half of his continued time he would be eligible for parole consideration providing the Review Officer's report is favorable and providing the Chief of the Adult Parole Authority renders a favorable preliminary judgment. Thus, if the inmate can demonstrate by word and deed that he has made commendable efforts to improve his attitude, his work record, or his behavioral record, he can in effect diminish the continued time originally affixed by the Board. 

There is one other benefit from the review process and this benefit involves the institutional operation. Before the Review Officer gathers information for his report, he conducts a preliminary meeting with two staff persons appointed by the Warden or Superintendent of each institution. These three people - the two institutional people and the Review Officer function as a screening committee to examine each case within the following criteria: 

15

1) Each prisoner must have served at least twelve months since his last parole board hearing and at least one-half of any continuance ordered by the 

Parole Board. 

2) The prisoner has served at least seven years on a sentence on a mandatory 10 year minimum; twelve years on a sentence with a 20 year minimum or seventeen years on a sentence with a 

25 year minimum. 

3) The prisoner has served one-half his minimum sentence where such sentence is more than two years and less than life.

After each case has been examined, the Review Officer prepares a report showing previous Parole Board actions and summarizes the entire case history. He will include special information obtained through interviews made with the prisoner and through contacts with the screening committee. He will then make an evaluation as to whether his information argues for or against a new hearing for parole.

The Chief of the Adult Parole Authority receives every report prepared by the Review Officers. He then indicates his concurrence or disagreement with the recommendation therein. Recommendations approved by him are sent to the Parole Board so that a parole hearing can be arranged. In every case, the final decision to parole or not to parole rests with the Parole Board. 

The review process as described began in February of last year (see Table 1-A). Parole Authority records show that since that date 

269 inmates appeared before the Parole Board after being screened by the institution, the Review Officers and the Chief. The result was that 78.8 percent or 212 of these were paroled. Twenty-four others had their "continuance time" modified and/or reduced. This reduction in time occurred in 8.9 percent of the cases. Only 33 inmates who had experienced the review process were denied consideration of any kind. This is only 12.3 percent of the total cases. Even in these cases, the fact that the inmates received this "second chance" caused a manifestly beneficial effect on the institution and on the other inmates. The rejected inmate now recognizes the reasons for his continuance and is better prepared to deal with them. 

As a result of the Review Officer procedure, institution administrators are quick to admit that inmate morale is at an all time high. As evidence, they cite the decline in the percentage of behavior problems in each institution. 16

Jail Time Credit 

In some instances the Parole Authority may reduce the time the offender must spend in prison by granting jail time credit. The jail time is that period of time the prisoner spent in jail between his conviction and his commitment. For example, he might have pleaded guilty in December, and for a number of reasons, been kept in a city or county jail until January, when he was formally admitted to begin his sentence. The Parole Authority is authorized to give credit for this time if a judge so recommends. We had 332 such recommendations in 1969. Of these, 268 were granted in total, 36 were partially granted, and 28 were denied. (See Table 2).

Cost of Probation and Parole 

Inflation and the additional programming institutions now provide makes the cost of keeping a man in prison for one year rise steadily. For example in 1967 the cost of keeping a man in prison for one year was $1,521. This year the cost of keeping a man in prison for one year has risen to $1,864. 

Determining the cost of probation or parole is a difficult problem. For one thing it is difficult to isolate each service applied to each parolee and to affix a price tag to that service. For another, it is not an easy task to record and evaluate such items as tax contributions and welfare allotments. However, notwithstanding these difficulties, we decided that one way of illustrating costs of probation and parole would be to divide the average monthly parole and probation caseload into the amount spent by the Adult Parole Authority. Since probation and parole cases averaged 4,952 per month, the average cost of maintaining a man under probation or parole supervision by the Adult Parole Authority comes to $398 per man for the year. It is readily apparent then that the cost of keeping a man in prison is 4½ times the cost of keeping him on probation or parole (see Table 6). 

Earnings of Parolees and Probationers 

Most offenders work! A survey by the Parole Authority showed that 90 percent of Ohio parolees are employed and earn an average of $100 per week. Together, they earned over $23 million last year, and paid an estimated $3 million plus in taxes. The paid income taxes alone are about double the entire budget of the Adult Parole 

Authority! And this does not even include social security payments, state sales taxes, or county and city taxes. In addition to earnings, tax contributions, and savings in imprisonment costs, there is an 17

added savings through the removal of dependents from welfare roles. 

An estimated 30 percent of the parolees support dependents who would otherwise be recipients of Public Welfare. This data applies only to parolees and probationers under direct supervision of the Adult Parole Authority. As previously stated, most probationers are still supervised by County Departments of Probation. If the output of all these probationers would be added to our computations, earnings by probationers and parolees in Ohio would be at least doubled.

Innovated Projects 

The Adult Parole Authority encourages its staff to institute innovated and imaginary planning. As an impetus to such innovation and planning, scholarship and intellectual inquiry are encouraged and various seminars, meetings and institutes are organized and presented in the course of the year. As a consequence, the Adult Parole 

Authority conceives and implements various projects and programs, some of which are described below. 

Halfway House Specifications - Many citizens call our agency requesting advice and guidance in the establishment of a halfway house for parolees. Frequently enthusiasm tends to diminish the importance of dealing with the practicability of such a venture. As a consequence, hastily organized and insufficient halfway houses flounder and the result is a disheartened and disillusioned group of citizens. To preclude or to minimize the occurrence of such incidents, the Adult Parole Authority has written and published specifications for a halfway house. These specifications are available on request and represent the first time in the nation that such a publication has been made available. It describes guide lines for the organization of a halfway house plus the minimum standards required by the Adult Parole Authority to insure success and productive results. 

Operation Prevention - Last year, throughout Ohio, hundreds of children packed high school assembly halls to hear young ex-convicts tell it like it is. "Yes, I'm an ex-convict" they relate, "And believe me, its no bed of roses.' 

Working in concert with the Ohio Youth Commission, the Adult Parole Authority arranges for parolees to address high school assemblies in hopes that the students will come to realize the folly of a criminal career by hearing about the consequences 18

of such a career. Despite their lack of speaking skills, volunteer parolees who are selected to participate in this "Operation Prevention program" invariably hold young audiences spellbound as they recite their stories of arrest and imprisonment. 

The natural curiosity of students and the presence of a "real live criminal" causes everyone to pay rapt attention. 

After the parolee has described the trials and tribulations he experienced, he receives questions from the audience. School officials are always amazed at the intensive interest of students during this question and answer period. Many feel that the program nips budding criminal careers.

While the results of this program are difficult to evaluate, authorities and school officials agree that it has an undeniable positive effect. Some of the glamour is stripped from crime as the offender tells of weeks or months spent in lonely isolation in a jail cell; or when he tells of prison regimentation and the restriction of his liberties. Almost always audiences murmur in disbelief as the offender describes how it feels to spend 16 hours a day in a six by eight cell.

Operation Fact - The Adult Parole Authority has long been aware that there is a vast reservoir of untapped professional counseling talent in the form of Ohio ministers willing to volunteer their services. With this premise our agency conceived and implemented a program using ministers called "Operation Fact." 

Standing for Friend, Advisor, Counselor, Treatment, it permits ministers from various faiths to give counsel and guidance to certain offenders in the last half of their parole periods. 

Ministers selected to participate in this program are first carefully screened. Then, working with seasoned parole officers, these volunteer ministers give supportive counseling to selected parolees within guidelines established by the Adult Parole Authority.

Before cases are assigned to ministers, however, they are carefully screened to minimize the possibility of danger. The ministers are given orientation lectures and a review of each offender's record. They then exercise their pastoral skills accordingly. While this program has not been in existence long enough for an evaluation, first indications are that it will aid parolees in the reinforcement of positive values acquired 19

through the parole supervision process. So far, "Operation

Fact" has only been applied on a pilot basis; when it is instituted state-wide, it may well involve hundreds of ministers each capable of administering in-depth professional counseling. 

A Plan for Action - Working in concert with the public sector of the community, the Adult Parole Authority has devised a program for identifying hard core parolees, provide training for them, and then arranging for their employment. In cooperation with the Bureau of Vocational Rehabilitation, these offenders are provided with a job readiness program prior to employment and within two or three weeks, a job. 

The job readiness program itself is of two weeks duration during which time the parolee receives orientation and training about how to find, hold and maintain a job. He is also provided with sufficient funds by the Bureau of Vocational Rehabilitation to maintain himself until he is placed on the job. After he finishes the two weeks training program, he is placed on a job by previous arrangement with a cooperating employer. Jobs range in pay from $2.10 an hour to $3.61 per hour.

This project is an attempt by the Parole Authority to deal with that segment of the offender population which consistently demonstrates an inability to find or hold suitable employment. 

These are the offenders who have no previous working experience of any consequence or those who have been employed for less than one year or those who earned less than $70 a week. Special consideration is given to the under employed offender whose family is or has been on welfare. So far the wholehearted support of the public and the Bureau of 

Vocational Rehabilitation leads us to believe that “A Plan for

Action" augurs well for aiding hard-core unemployed in the coming years. Although at the present time the plan is. in operation as a pilot project in Columbus only, the initial success has been beyond expectations. There is every reason to believe that next year it will be applied throughout the state. 

Operation Redcoat - It often happens that a parolee is released to the community with only a few dollars in his pockets. As a result he can't support himself for the week or two required before he locates a job and draws his first paycheck. Sometimes it also happens that his job requires him to have tools or special equipment. Likely as not, he is unable to acquire the needed 20

S 1 equipment. In many such cases there might well be a reversion. to criminal activity or welfare. In coping with this problem, the 

Adult Parole Authority has encouraged various agencies to make loans to parolees to enable them to get started. So far the 

Lions Clubs of Ohio have responded most enthusiastically and working together we have fashioned a program to loan money to parolees certified by parole officers as being worthy and being in need.

Called Operation Redcoat (because Lions Club members sport red blazers) in honor of the Lions Clubs, this program enables a parolee to borrow money without interest in time of need. By doing so he can maintain his dignity in the first trying weeks following release.

Penologists and correction workers have long noted that a man functions well only as long as his self image is satisfactory. 

Newly released men especially have to have every psychological support if a return to criminal activity is to be avoided. One way to provide this support is to enable him to make a life through his own labor. “Operation Redcoat" does just that.

Staff Development 

The Adult Parole Authority plans to continue and intensify its program of staff development. In addition to the regular parole officer training school, there are seminars, institutes, workshops, and special projects for correctional personnel. Some of this in-service training comes about through federal grants and much more is financed by the State. Many of our officers and administrators are using state tuition reimbursement programs to take graduate coursework at universities. Others have taken advantage of the many free training courses sponsored by the State Department of Personnel and the Department of Education. Still others attended programs outside the state. This year, for example, four expense-paid scholarships were awarded Adult Parole Authority officers to attend. the three-week course at the Rutgers School for Alcoholic Studies at 

Brunswick, New Jersey.

Some Plans for the Future 

With funds to be made available to us under the Omnibus Crime Control bill, we intend to improve the quality of our established Parole Officers School by bringing in various specialists. We also plan intensive seminars for our middle managers and line officers. 21

In this area of in-service training, we will organize and conduct two 3-day training seminars in cooperation with the Bowling Green 

State University and the office of Law Enforcement Assistance 

Administration. This is the second phase of an in-service training grant put into operation in 1968. The objective of this training is to increase the effectiveness of community based corrections personnel. 

During the coming year, we plan to revise our Parole Officers Manual to conform to recent changes in practice and policy. 

With the acquisition of automatic filing cabinets our record keeping operation is now excellent. We expect to improve it still more, however, by updating the contents of records and exploring the feasibility of computerizing and data processing. 

The Adult Parole Authority will devote a great deal of effort to the American Correction Association's self-evaluation and accreditation program. Originally financed by the Ford Foundation with a grant of one quarter million dollars, this program enables correctional systems to evaluate their operations in order to make improvements.

Other plans include the organization of a speakers bureau; the development of a practicum program in cooperation with various universities in order to nurture interest in corrections; development of a system for the inspection and certification of Ohio halfway houses; and added emphasis on research and the continuation of new programs and procedures for dealing with the offender in the community.

Conclusion 

This report of the Adult Parole Authority has high-lighted the major achievements of the past year. This is not an exhaustive summary, however, but a comprehensive report. While we are proud of our many accomplishments, we realize there is still much to be done in meeting our objectives of protecting the public, eliminating criminal careers, and making citizens of offenders. 22 22
