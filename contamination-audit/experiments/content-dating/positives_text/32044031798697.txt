CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

Section 1. 

History 

A guardian is a person lawfully invested with the power, and charged with the duty, of taking care of the person and managing the property of another, who, due to some peculiarity of status, or defect of age, understanding, or self-control, is considered incapable of administering his 1 own affairs.

In some form or other guardianships have existed from very early times. Under the feudal system in England the baronial lords, by means of an arrangement called guardianship in chivalry, imposed certain restrictions on the vassals who were required to give military service. 

If a male heir was under twenty-one or a female heir was under sixteen at the death of the father, the lord was entitled to take the property into his possession and appropriate to himself all the profits of it during the minority of the ward on the ground that such was a reasonable compensation for the services which the minor, because of his age, could not render. The lord also had the right to bring about a suitable marriage and appropriate whatever he could for this service. If the ward refused to pay him for this service, he could recover from the ward's property; if the ward married without the lord's consent, he could demand a still larger forfeiture than if he brought about the marriage. This form of guardianship applied to military tenure only and was abolished by statute in 1660.2 

Guardianship in socage was another incident of the feudal system. 

It arose when an infant acquired lands by descent. The tenants of the land were obliged to give a certain portion of the crops to the lord, or to plow the lord's land. If the infant was under fourteen when he

2 became heir to the land it was given to his next of kin who could not possibly inherit from him, and this relative had authority over the person of the heir, his land, and such property as crops and cut timber. 

When the infant became fourteen he could choose his guardian.3 

After guardianship in chivalry was abolished, a father, who was the natural guardian, was permitted, either by deed or will, to name a person to act as a guardian of his child during the child's minority. 

Such a person was called a testamentary guardian.4 In North Carolina a father is still allowed to appoint a guardian by will for his unmarried minor children.5

These guardians were designated by the common law itself. Since the court of chancery had jurisdiction over the person and property of infants, the chancellor could appoint guardians of infants who were without guardians by the common law. In the United States the courts of equity took over this function quite early. Today special courts such as probate, orphan or juvenile courts have the power to appoint guardians over the person and estate of infants.6 In North Carolina this duty falls upon the clerk of the superior court.7 

Section 2. Nature and Scope 

The relationship of guardian and ward is a fiduciary one which requires the utmost good faith in all dealings and transactions by the guardian in connection with his ward and the ward's estate. A guardian is, in effect, an arm of the court, and is subject to the control of the court in the discharge of his duties as guardian.9 

A guardian may be appointed to care for the person or the property of his ward. Generally the same person is appointed to act as guardian

of the person and estate, but in North Carolina the clerk has authority to make separate appointments.10

A guardian of the person is responsible for the care, maintenance and education of the ward in a manner suitable to the ward's financial 11 and social station in life.

But where he is not a parent of the ward, 12 the guardian is not personally liable for the ward's expenses. The ward is supported out of his own estate, and the guardian's duty is to see that the estate is judiciously applied to the ward's maintenance and education.13

The legal title to the property of the ward is in the ward rather than in the guardian. The guardian is merely the custodian and the manager or conservator of the ward's estate, and has no beneficial title thereto.14 The relation of guardian and ward should not be confused with that of trustee and cestui que trust. A trustee holds legal title to property for the benefit of the cestui que trust, who has equitable title. The guardian is held to the same strict rules that govern trustees, however, and the term "trustee" is sometimes used when speaking of the guardian.15

In addition to the powers and duties of the guardian which are discussed in the following chapters, some miscellaneous statutes pertaining to the guardian's authority are summarized below (these statutes generally provide that the specified duties are to be performed by a parent, guardian, or person in loco parentis to the child): (1) G.S. 115-166 requires the guardian to keep a child between the ages of seven and sixteen years in school (unless the child has been assigned against the guardian's wishes to a public school attended by a child of another race, and it is neither practicable for the child

to be reassigned to a public school not attended by a child of another race nor for the child to attend a private nonsecretarial school). 

G.S. 115-172 requires the guardian of a blind or deaf child between the ages of six and eighteen years to send the child to a school for the blind or deaf. (2) G.S. 35-37 allows a guardian to petition for the sterilization of a mentally defective ward, and G.S. 90-272 allows a guardian to petition the juvenile court for an order authorizing the sterilization of an unmarried minor ward (the ward's consent is also needed). (3) G.S. 30-2 authorizes the guardian of the person or estate of a minor or incompetent ward to file a dissent from the will of the ward's deceased spouse. Under G.S. 30-17 and 30-27 a guardian may apply for the year's allowance to which a child under the age of eighteen years is entitled from the estate of a deceased parent. (4) G.S. 130-88 makes it the duty of a guardian to see that his ward is immunized against diptheria, tetanus, whooping cough (required before the child is a year old), and smallpox (required before the child attends school). ward. (5) Under G.S. 48-9 the guardian may consent to the adoption of his (6) Under G.S. 51-2 a guardian or other person standing in loco parentis to a ward between the ages of sixteen and eighteen years may consent to the ward's marriage, and a guardian of the person may consent to the marriage of a pregnant female ward who is between twelve and sixteen years old. (7) G.S. 94-8 authorizes the guardian to sign an apprenticeship agreement of his minor ward where both of the ward's parents are dead or incapable of giving consent.

Section 3 - Types of Guardianship 

A. Guardian by nature 

To all practical intents and purposes, natural guardianship is but another term for the parent-child relation. 

It carries the same legal authority and responsibility for the person of the child. In the absence of appointment by the court as guardian of the ward's estate, the natural guardian has no control over such estate.16 

In North Carolina the father is the natural guardian of the person of the child.17 If the father abandons the child, or if there is a divorce proceeding and the custody is awarded to one other than the father, he is no longer the natural guardian. By statute the mother immediately becomes the natural guardian of the person upon the death of the father. But the statute also provides that this shall not abridge the powers of courts over minors and their estates and over the appointment of guardians.18

G.S. 33-5 authorizes the clerk to appoint a guardian of the estate of any minor although the father of the minor is living. B.

Testamentary guardian 

A testamentary guardian is one appointed by will. Such guardian may be designated to act in relation to the child's person, property, or both. 

C. General guardian 

A general guardian has control of both the person and estate of the ward. D. 

Special guardian 

This is a guardian who is appointed for a special purpose. He has only limited powers and duties with respect to the ward. E. 

Next friend 

The next friend is a special guardian appointed for the temporary and limited purpose of prosecuting an action for and in the name of a person non sui juris (under a legal disability to act for himself).

CHAPTER II THE APPOINTMENT 

Section 1. - Who Appoints? 

Under G.S. 33-1 the clerks of the superior courts have, within their respective counties, power to take cognizance of all matters concerning orphans and their estates and to appoint guardians of infants, idiots, lunatics, inebriates, and inmates of Caswell School. G.S. 2-16(16) also authorizes clerks of the superior courts to appoint guardians for infants, idiots, inebriates and lunatics.

In Duffy v. Williamsl the court stated that the powers which a court of equity formerly exercised in regard to orphans and their estates are now conferred upon the clerk of the superior court. 

Section 2.- Where is the Guardian Appointed?

A. Basic rule The basic rule provided by G.S. 33-1 is that guardians shall be appointed by the clerks of the superior court in the county in which the ward resides. The word "reside" as used in this statute has been interpreted to mean the ward's domicile. 2 To establish a domicile there must be a residence at some place and the intention to 3 make it a home or to live there permanently. 

The case of In re Hall4 discusses the general rules relating to a minor's domicile: (1) A legitimate child takes at birth and retains during minority the domicile of his father, following it as it changes. The father's domicile at his death continues to be the domicile of the minor child until it is legally changed, (2) An unemancipated infant, being non sui juris, cannot of his own volition select, acquire or change his domicile.

(3) After the father's death, the domicile of the infant follows that of its mother during her widowhood, and ordinarily may be changed by the mother in changing her own. It is held by some states that the domicile of an infant will not follow that of its mother after her remarriage, since by remarrying her domicile is again fixed by that of her husband. There is, however, other authority to the effect that a widow does not, by remarrying, lose her power to change the domicile of her children of a former marriage. North Carolina apparently has never decided this question. (4) On the death of both parents, the domicile last derived from the parents, or from either of them, continues to be the domicile of the infant during minority until it is legally changed. (5) A guardian by nature may change the domicile of the infant. 

Within this rule, In re Hall held that when both parents of a minor child are dead, the grandparent who takes the child to his home and actually stands in loco parentis to the child becomes the natural guardian, and the domicile of the grandparent thereupon becomes the domicile of the child. The court stated that it would seem that the doctrine of natural guardianship has never been extended to an uncle or aunt who stands as next of kin to the minor.

The Attorney General has expressed the opinion that when both parents of an infant have died and the infant's married sister takes him into her home as a member of her family, the sister becomes a natural guardian to the extent of determining the child's domicile. Therefore the clerk of court of the county in which the sister lives has jurisdiction to appoint a guardian for the infant even though the infant's parents were domiciled in another county at their death.5

A. General 

The procedures for appointing guardians are set out in 

Chapter 33 (Guardian and Ward) and Chapter 35 (Persons with Mental Diseases and Incompetents). G.S. 33-7 refers generally to the appointment of guardians for infants, idiots, lunatics, inebriates and inmates of Caswell School, but it is the complete procedure only for the appointment of guardians for infants. G.S. 35-2 requires an inquisition by a jury into the mental capacity of persons alleged to be mentally incompetent

before a guardian may be appointed. A guardian may also be appointed under G.S. 35-2.1 when a person is found to be insane by a jury in a criminal or civil trial, or upon the certificate of the superintendent of a hospital for the insane as provided by G.S. 35-3. It is not clear how the procedure set out in G.S, 33-7 relates to the procedures for appointment in Chapter 35. In one case an inquisition of lunacy under G.S. 35-2 was held to be irregular where no notice was given to relatives as required by G.S. 33-7. It is suggested that in appointing guardians for mental incompetents, the clerk should follow the steps outlined in G.S. 33-7 as well as those in the appropriate section of Chapter 35. 

(1) General procedure upon application for guardianship (G.S. 33-7) 

On the application to the clerk of the superior court for the custody and guardianship of any infant, idiot, lunatic, inebriate, or inmate of Caswell School, it is the duty of the clerk to inform himself of the circumstances of the case on the oath of the applicant or any other person having knowledge. If none of the relatives of the prospective ward are present at such application, the clerk must assign, or for any other good cause he may assign, a day for the hearing. In such case the clerk must notify such relatives or other persons, if any, as he deems it proper to notify. On the hearing he ascertains, on oath, the amount of the real and personal property of the ward and the value of the rents and profits of the real estate. The clerk may grant or refuse the application or commit the guardianship to some other person, as he deems best for the interest of the ward. In Cheshire v. Howard' it was contended that the defendant had not qualified as guardian because she did not sign the application for her

appointment as guardian or the oath appearing in the record. 

This was held to be an irregularity which did not make the appointment void. 

Failure to give notice of the hearing to the ward's relatives does not make the appointment void, but it is not conclusive upon such relatives. In In re Parker' a guardian of the person of an infant was appointed upon an ex parte application. Notice was not given to an 

It was held that the appointment aunt who then had custody of the infant. was not void, but that it was not binding on the aunt and did not dispose of the custody of the child as against her. She had no opportunity to oppose the order appointing the guardian, nor to appeal from it. The guardian would have to bring an action to assert his right to custody of the infant, while the aunt could take steps to set aside the guardianship. (2) Inquisition of lunacy and appointment of a guardian A guardian may be appointed under G.S. 35-2 for any person found to be a mental defective, inebriate, mentally disordered, or incompetent from want of understanding to manage his own affairs because of the excessive use of intoxicating drinks or from any other cause. (a) Definitions

An inebriate is any person who 

habitually, whether continuously or periodically, indulges in the use of intoxicating liquors, narcotics or drugs to such an extent as to stupify his mind and to render him incompetent to transact ordinary business with safety to his estate, or who renders himself, by reason of the use of intoxicating liquors, narcotics or drugs, dangerous to persons or property, or who, by the frequent use of liquor, narcotics or drugs, renders himself cruel and intolerable to his family, or fails from such cause to provide his family with reasonable necessities of life. The

habit of indulging in such use must be of at least one year's standing at the time of the inquisition.9 

A mental disorder, mental illness or mental disease is an illness which so lessens the capacity of the person to use his customary selfcontrol, judgment, and discretion in the conduct of his affairs and social relations as to make it necessary or advisable for him to be under treatment, care, supervision, guidance, or control. These terms are construed to include "lunacy," "unsoundness of mind," and "insanity,10 A mental defective is a person who is not mentally ill but whose mental development is so retarded that he has not acquired enough selfcontrol, judgment, and discretion to manage himself and his affairs, and for whose own welfare or that of others, supervision, guidance, care, or control is necessary or advisable. This term is construed to include "feeble-minded," "idiot," and "imbecile. "11 (b) The inquisition of lunacy 

Anyone may file a petition before the clerk of the county where the supposed mental incompetent resides setting forth the facts and asking for the appointment of a guardian or trustee to manage the property of the respondent. Notice must be served upon the alleged incompetent to appear at a time and place named to present evidence and show cause, if any, why he should not be declared incompetent. This notice serves the function of a summons. The clerk 12 then issues an order to the sheriff of the county commanding him to summon a jury of twelve men for the hearing. The clerk swears the jury and presides over the hearing. The jury's return is made to and filed and recorded by the clerk. If the person is found to be a mental defective, inebriate, mentally disordered or incompetent by the jury, the clerk must proceed to appoint a guardian as in cases of orphans.

If the person is found to be incompetent from want of understanding to manage his affairs, by reason of physical and mental weakness on account of old age and/or disease and/or other like infirmities, the clerk may appoint a trustee instead of a guardian. 

The trustee or guardian has all the powers of a guardian administering an estate for any person and is subject to all the laws governing the administration of estates of minors and incompetents. 

The finding of the jury may be appealed by the applicant or the alleged mental incompetent to the superior court where it is tried de novo before a jury. Pending an appeal the clerk may not appoint a guardian, but the judge may appoint a temporary receiver. 

In Bethea v. McLennon¹3 it was held that the alleged lunatic has a right to be present at the inquest, and if no notice is given to him, it is good cause for setting aside the inquisition. 

In re Worsley involved a proceeding for the appointment of a guardian on the ground that the respondent was incompetent for "want of understanding to manage his own affairs." Section 4. 

Who Should be Appointed as Guardian?

Selection of the person to be appointed guardian is a matter which is largely in the discretion of the appointing clerk of court. The statutes offer little assistance, except for the use of general terms such as a "fit and suitable person." The clerk should, however, keep in mind the following factors when selecting the guardian: 

A. Best interest of the ward The interest of the ward is the para- mount consideration,26 meaning that which is best for his lasting good. 

In selecting a general guardian of a minor, the court should be guided by what appears to be for the best interest of the minor's physical, mental, and moral welfare. The court may want to consider the financial situation, physical condition, morals, character, conduct, and present and past history of a prospective appointee, as well as the probability of his being able to exercise the powers and duties of guardian for the 27 full period of a guardianship. 

B. Conflict of interest of proposed guardian and ward The guardian must be free from any interest or motive that could possibly interfere with the faithful performance of his duties. In In re Dixon28 the guardian father improperly claimed as tenancy by curtesy the rents and profits of lands which had descended to his infant ward. This was held to be such an adverse claim to the rights of the ward as to justify his removal. 

A guardian may not represent the ward in any action in which the guardian has or may have an interest antagonistic to the ward, but a disinterested next friend or guardian ad litem must be appointed. 

For example, where the action involved a suit on the bond of a former guardian and the plaintiff guardian was a surety on the former guardian's bond, the court held that a next friend should have been appointed to protect the ward's interests,30 29

Because of possible adverse interest, some jurisdictions do not allow the appointment of the same person as executor or administrator and guardian. 

North Carolina merely requires that when the duties of an executor or administrator appear to be in conflict with his duties as guardian, a disinterested party must be appointed to protect the ward's interests.32 

In Carraway v. Lassiter33 the clerk properly appointed a guardian ad litem for an infant defendant in a special proceeding by an executor to sell lands, when the executor was also the general guardian of the infant. 34 C. Wishes of a parent The wishes of a parent should be considered in appointing a guardian, but they are not necessarily controlling and do not outweigh what the court considers the best interest of the ward. 

D. Wishes of an infant By statute or judicial decision, most jurisdictions allow an infant who has reached a certain age, usually fourteen years, to nominate a guardian who must be appointed by the court unless there are reasons justifying the court, in its discretion, in 35 refusing to appoint this nominee. North Carolina has no such statute, and it was held in Grant v. Whitaker36 that the court was not bound to confirm the choice of a guardian made by an infant of fourteen years of age and over, but could exercise its discretion in making the appointment. 

However, the preference of the child always has weight with the court according to the age and intelligence of the child. In Spears v. Snell 37 Snell,38 the court stated that a minor, age thirteen, had a right to testify as to his preference of a guardian.

E. Religious considerations 

The religious faith or affiliation of the parents, more particularly that of the father in the event that the parents differ as to religious preference, should be considered in selecting a guardian of the person.39 A North Carolina statute requires 31

Section 5. 

Testamentary Appointment by a Parent 

G.S. 33-2 permits the father, if the mother is dead, to provide for the custody and tuition of his unmarried minor children during their minority or for a less time to a guardian appointed by his written will. 

If the father is dead and has not exercised his right of appointment, or has willfully abandoned his wife, then the mother may do so, Every such appointment shall be good and effectual against any person claiming the custody and tuition of such child or children. The statute also provides that every such guardian shall have the same powers and rights and be subject to the same liabilities and regulations as other guardians. 

If specifically directed by the will, the guardian named will be permitted to qualify and serve without giving bond unless the clerk having jurisdiction over the guardianship determines that the best interest of the minor requires such bond.

Although the statute only authorizes the father to dispose of the "custody and tuition" of his minor children, which would seem to contemplate only the appointment of a personal guardian, the courts have recognized the appointment by will of general guardians of both persons and estates. 

In In re Young45 the court referred to the "duly appointed testamentary guardians of the persons and estates of these children." In pointing out that no particular language is necessary to create a testamentary

(emphasis added)

In Sanderson v. Sanderson47 the plaintiff's father by his last will and testament appointed defendants as the testamentary guardians of the plaintiff. As such, they took charge of the plaintiff's estate. The plaintiff, by next friend, brought an action to have them removed. Section 6. Duration of the Guardianship 

A. Termination 

Unless expressly limited as to time, or shortened by an order of removal, or by the resignation of the guardian a guardianship continues until terminated in one of the following ways: 

(1) Majority of the ward When the guardianship was created because of the ward's infancy, it terminates upon the majority of the ward.53 

In In re Simmons54 x was appointed the guardian of a minor in 1937. In 1942, after the ward had reached his majority, X was authorized by the court to sell land of the ward. In 1961 the ward, by his present guardian, moved to have the sale set aside. It was shown that the ward had been non compos mentis since birth. It was held that X had no authority to act for his ward after the ward had reached his majority, even though the ward was at that time mentally incompetent, and the sale was set aside. (2) Death of the guardian or ward

Death of either the guardian or ward terminates the guardianship.55 When the guardian dies, the deceased guardian's executor or administrator may pay any money belonging to the ward into the office of the clerk of the county where the guardian was appointed. The payment will discharge the deceased guardian's estate to the extent of the amount paid.56

57 In Alexander v. Wriston the court held that the administrator of a deceased guardian could not maintain an action on notes payable to the deceased as guardian. The guardianship had terminated upon the death of the guardian, and it was the duty of his administrator to turn over any guardianship property to the succeeding guardian, or to the ward he had arrived at age. 58 In Davis v. Fox the ward's lands were sold by court order and the money was paid into the office of the clerk of court. The money was never paid to the guardian or to anyone else for the ward's benefit. 

Upon the guardian's death, his administrator brought action on the clerk's bond to recover the funds. It was held that the action should have been brought by the ward if she were of age or by a second guardian if she were still a minor.

Lowder v. Hathcock59 held that upon the death of the ward, his distributees may have letters of administration taken out and call for an accounting from the guardian. (3) Marriage of the ward 

It is generally held that marriage of a male ward terminates the guardianship of his person but not of his estate.60 

Marriage of a female ward terminates the guardianship of her person,61 but the authorities are divided as to whether the guardianship of her estate also ceases. 

62 In Shutt v. Carloss63 and Fowler v. McLaughlin64 the North

Carolina court held that marriage of a female ward to an adult husband terminated the guardianship entirely because at that time the husband became the owner of her personal property in the hands of the guardian and was entitled to the possession, rents and profits of her real estate. 

The Constitution of 186865 and G.S. 52-1 now guarantee a married woman the absolute ownership of her separate estate. Since the reason for the rule

laid down in the Shutt and Fowler cases no longer exists, it is believed that the marriage of a minor female ward now revokes the guardianship of her person but not of her property.66

CHAPTER IV NEXT FRIEND AND GUARDIAN AD LITEM 

Section 1. General 

The case of Johnston County v. Ellisl discusses the distinction between a next friend and a guardian ad litem: "There is, we are aware, a lack of uniformity in judicial decision in the several jurisdictions respecting the duties and authority of a next friend and the extent of his representation. These differences are largely due to the variation in pertinent statutes of the particular jurisdiction. We think it essential to orderly procedure, and to the better protection of the rights of infants and others non sui juis, to adhere to the distinctions between next friends and guardians ad litem or general guardians traditional in our practice and formally recognized and implied in our statutes

These distinctions stem mainly from the circumstances that a next friend is appointed to bring or prosecute some proceeding in which the infant suitor is plaintiff, or at least where some right is positively asserted; while a guardian ad litem is appointed to defend. 

In legal effect, the distinctions are substantial and not merely formal." 

The next friend or guardian ad litem is not required to give bond as a guardian, does not take charge of property, and is not liable for costs if he acts in good faith.2

In the case of infant parties, the next friend, guardian ad litem, or guardian cannot consent to a judgment against the infant without an investigation and approval by the court.3

CHAPTER V 

CUSTODY AND MANAGEMENT OF THE WARD'S ESTATE Section 1. 

Investments

A. General 

It is the duty of the guardian to keep his ward's money and property separate from his own, to keep an account thereof, to make investments, not in his own name, but as guardian, and to keep those 1 investments separate from his own.

Throughout the statutes can be found lists of investments which the guardian may make. However, the guardian may make investments outside these lists provided he exercises good faith and due diligence in selecting the particular securities or other investments.

A guardian has not performed his duty merely because he has made an investment which is authorized by statute. A blind following of the list does not absolutely protect the guardian against liability. A dictum in a North Carolina case says that if the investment was made under "statutory authority," there will be no liability resulting therefrom "in the absence of fraud or gross negligence." The fact that the investment is on the statutory list raises a strong presumption that it is sound, but the guardian should make at least minimum inquiries to learn whether any important changes have occurred which should cause him to avoid the investment at least temporarily, and he should use the care of a reasonably prudent man in acting upon knowledge which actually comes to his notice with regard to legal investments.2 

The statutes allow the following investments: (1) United States bonds or any securities for which the United States is responsible;3 (2) farm loan bonds issued by federal land banks;4 (3) bonds, debentures, consolidated bonds or other obligations of any federal home loan bank;5

(4) bonds and notes secured by a mortgage or deed of trust insured or guaranteed by the Veterans' Administrator or by the Federal Housing Administrator, mortgages on real estate which have been accepted for insurance by the Federal Housing Administrator or Veterans' Administrator, and obligations of national mortgage associations;6 (5) bonds of the 

State of North Carolina issued since 1872;7 (6) drainage bonds issued in accordance with Chapter 156 of the General Statutes;8 (7) bonds issued by any North Carolina county, city, town or school district subsequent to January 1, 1915, provided the net debt of the school district does not exceed 10 per cent of the assessed valuation of the property therein subject to taxation;9 (8) savings accounts or certificates of deposit of any federally insured bank in North Carolina; 10 (9) stock of building. and loan associations organized and licensed in this state, provided that no such funds may be invested until authorized by the Insurance Commissioner to the extent that the funds are not insured by the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation;¹ and (10) stock in federal savings and loan associations on approval of an officer of the Home Loan Bank at Winston-Salem or such other governmental agency having supervision of such associations, provided such approval is not required to the extent that such funds are insured by the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation.12 11 By G.S. 36-41 the clerk of the superior court in which the guardian qualified, with the approval of the resident judge or judge of the superior court in term time, may make an order authorizing the guardian to invest funds in single premium life, endowment or annuity contracts. 

The clerk may also order the investment of the income of the ward's estate in any annual premium life, endowment or annuity contracts of legal reserve life insurance companies qualified to transact business in

The leading case in North Carolina on the subject of guardianship 13 investments is Sheets v. Tobacco Co.. In this case a guardian invested $9,000 in the preferred stock of the tobacco company. The stock depreciated, and the wards sued the company for the $9,000 plus interest less dividends paid. The judgment for the plaintiffs was reversed and a new trial ordered, because of the trial court's refusal to submit to the jury defendant's issue as to the good faith and diligence of the guardian in making the investment. The Supreme Court said that if there was any liability, it was primarily that of the guardian and the sureties on his bond. "A guardian is generally authorized to make any investment of funds in his hands, belonging to his ward, which, in his best judgment, arrived at in good faith and after the exercise of due diligence, will secure the principal of said fund, and yield a reasonable income therefrom for the benefit of his ward's estate. All that can be, and all that should be required of him, in making any investment is that he shall conduct himself faithfully, and shall, at the time, exercise a sound discretion. Section 3. Commissions 

The payment of commissions to the guardian for the management of the ward's estate is governed by G.S. 33-43 and 28-170. The clerk has discretion to fix the commissions at an amount not to exceed five per cent of the receipts, including the value of all personalty when received, and of expenditures lawfully made. If the gross value of the estate if $2000 or less, the clerk may fix the commissions in any amount. In determining the amount of commissions, the clerk must consider the time, responsibility, trouble and skill involved in the management of the estate. 

If separate appointments are made for guardians of the person and estate, as provided for in G.S. 33-6, no commissions are allowed to either guardian for payments made by the estate guardian to the guardian of the person for the ward's maintenance and education. But the guardian of the person is allowed commissions on the disbursement of these amounts. 

Any interested party may appeal the clerk's order for commissions to the judge of the superior court. But an appellate court will review the matter of commissions allowed by the clerk only when they have been given on a wrong principle of law or when the allowance appears to be clearly inadequate or excessive.32
