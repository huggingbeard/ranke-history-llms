Biography of GEORGE MONROE CAMPBELL Author 

George M. Campbell was born a mile east of the Old Crow 

Wing Reservation on June 29, 1859. He lived there most of the time, with the exception of one winter at Red Lake in 1867. He moved to the new reservation at White Earth, Minnesota the, winter of 1868.

The Chippewa Indians were moved from the Gull Lake Reservation to the White Earth Reservation in the early part of the summer of 1868, and they first arrived on June 14, 1868. June 14 is celebrated each year at White Earth, Minnesota George M. Campbell began to interpret for the Government of the U. S. in 1874 for Agent E. P. Smith. He interpreted for a time for U. S. courts in different places: St. Paul, Minneapolis, Fergus Falls and Crookston, under Judge Nelson Judge 

Losebon, Judge Edgerton, Judge Thomas, Judge Amadon, and

Judge Page Morris of Duluth. He was a court interpreter for over thirty-five years. 

Mr. Campbell died on June 11, 1936, at the White Earth 

Hospital and was buried on June 13, 1936. He was 76 years old.

FOREWORD 

I am writing the Chippewa language as they talk it today and as they did fifty years ago. I am writing it as nearly as it can be written, mostly by sound. As the Indians never had an alphabet, they are borrowing the English alphabet, and using the English letters to write Indian words. The Indian tongue is not a language; it is nothing but a gibberish, a dead language.

The Indian words cannot be interpreted correctly. I have omitted many words as they cannot be translated into English by sound or otherwise. There are among the Indians many brilliant orators, but no interpreter can do justice to any of their orations. The best of interpreters have failed to give answer to any of the great and beautiful orations. The late Paul 

H. Beaulieu was second to no one in interpretation, but he, too, failed to give justice to the interpretations. Mr. Beaulieu told me personally, that it could not be done, and I have failed also to give detailed interpretation. I cannot do justice to the Indian language.

The time has come when the Indian must give way to civilization. His habits, customs, legends, stories, grand medicine, and writs have passed away. His language is also vanishing; in another decade it will be obliterated entirely. The purpose of this dictionary is to preserve the Chippewa Indian language.

The reader will note the absence of Chippewa words for many common objects, places and intangible things. It should be remembered that the Chippewa language encompassed only those things and thoughts as the Indian of many years ago met in his daily experiences. Hence you will find no Chippewa word or phrase for such items as silverware, autos, radios, pavement and the like. In certain cases words for other objects of some relationship of appearance or use are transferred to the new objects. 

-The Author

THE INDIANS' DRESS 

WOMEN: The Indian women dressed similar to the dress worn today. They wore broadcloth. This is not the broadcloth of today, but a heavier material and generally blue or red in color. The skirt came up under the armpits and down just below the knee cap with a strap over each shoulder. The strap was fastened in front and in back to hold up the skirt. This left bare both the front and back of the neck. They also wore leggings, red or blue in color, and of the same material as the dress. Mocassins were also worn. All the women had long hair which they wore in braids. The braids were tied very neatly in bow knots of broadcloth and hung five or six inches down the woman's back. This was the dress worn by an Indian woman from about 1850 to 1870, when they began to dress in long dresses. Now, they dress as their white sister in the modern styles.

MEN: The men dressed in leggings, a shirt, a belt around the waist line to hold up the leggings, mocassins, and a blanket. 

No hat or cap was worn unless it was cold weather, when they wore the pelt of some fur-bearing animal. The hair was worn long, as was the women's, braided and hanging down in front on each side of the breast. The braids were wrapped with some nice fur, generally mink or weasel. The face was painted with fast colors, although red was generally used. Feathers were worn in the hair. When his headgear had nice eagle feathers in it, and when he carried a tomahawk, a butcher knife, a war club, and a bow and arrows, the Indian was ready for the war path.

The Indian in his primitive state, while yet living in his birch-bark house (wigwam) was always very careful about his blanket. When going outdoors, he never forgot the blanket. 

It was always wrapped about his person. There was a motive for whether sitting, standing, walking or otherwise the blanket was always the same. The writer was lost for a long time to bring to mind what the motive was and after living among them for many years finally came to this conclusion. The Indian was ashamed to put off his blanket, his only wardrobe which covered his person, as he was dressed always sparingly, very poorly, and thinly, the same during winter or summer. His costume might be made of fur during the winter months, but the style was always the same. Leggings, breech-cloth, or skin from some animal. Whenever an Indian laid aside his blanket, he was

fearful of exposing his nudeness, while he was not nude altogether, he was nude enough to shame him in his half-dressed condition.

USES OF BIRCH BARK: The dwelling of the Indian was made of birch bark and rushes. "We-gie-waun" was his habitation, his home, his castle. Birch bark was used among the Indians for everything; drinking cups, all kinds of baskets, dishes of all description, canoes, and, in fact, almost everything they used.

The Indians were always busy. After their sugar making they were ready for the gathering of birch bark which they peeled from the birch tree. The bark was used to make wigwams for the coming winter; to make birch pans to catch the sap; to make canoes, baskets, and many other things.

Next came berry picking which they smoked and preserved for winter. Then came the summer hunt which brought them to the latter part of the summer. They smoked and dried the meat for winter and then harvested the wild rice. After the rice harvest they fished, setting nets and catching thousands of fish, only the tulibee and white fish. The fish were racked by tens and then hung up on very large racks above the ground to freeze, as the fishing usually began just before winter. 

Then the men were ready for the winter hunt and trapping and for fishing through the ice. They cut a hole in the ice, took a two-tined spear, a decoy made of birch wood and other soft wood, covered themselves with a blanket and lay there upon the ice for hours fishing. 

The men never bothered themselves about getting wood and water for the camp. That was left for the women to do, as it was a woman's place to supply the camp with wood and water and do all the other drudgery. 

TRADITIONS, A CODE OF INDIAN LAWS MARRIAGES: According to the primitive laws of the Indi ans, when a man and a woman are in love the man goes often to her home, and finally spends the night with her. If he accepts the invitation to breakfast, he remains in the family circle, and this constitutes a marriage. Common law marriages were the rule. 

The reverse also constituted a marriage. If a girl was in love with a man, she spent the night with him in his parents' lodge or wigwam. There were no matrimonial ceremonies among the

Indians. Sometimes the daughter of a chief was given to her lover by her father. A big feast, and sometimes a dance, followed when the girl's father made a speech fitting to the occasion.

INDIAN DIVORCES: Sometimes the man was to blame for the separation, and sometimes the woman. A man would leave his wife and take up with another woman; the woman would do the same if she left her husband. Sometimes this would result in murder as the man would kill his wife. However, the woman would seldom kill her husband, although there have been a few cases where she would. When a man left his wife he would pack his belongings and go, never to return. The woman would do likewise. This constitutes a divorce according to the law and custom among the Indians. 

INDIAN JUSTICE: The Indians had no trial courts, no courts of justice, in fact no courts of any kind. They had an organization or council. It was the unwritten law with the Indians at all times that, if a man was killed, the deceased's brother would kill the murdered of his brother, and a feud would follow. I have seen this practiced here on this reservation (White Earth, Minnesota) not many years ago.

The Indians were very honest and truthful; there was never much thieving. As to murdering or killing a man, a family feud, "If you kill my brother, I will kill yours," was the unwritten law among these wild children of Nature. I believe that was the same law the white men practiced in Tennessee and Kentucky in the early days.

The Indians had their war dances, their Grand Medicine dances, and one dance which they called "shin dance." They had their games-mocassin games, hand games in which a player would hold in one hand a bullet, a small stone, a hazel nut or some kind of a berry or stone. A second person would try to find the object by pointing to the hand. They had a song and they would go through all kinds of motions. 

OWNERSHIP OF PROPERTY: The Indian in his primi tive way of living never had any amount of property. When a man died whatever property he left reverted to his oldest son. 

The wife had nothing to say, neither did the rest of the family. 

That was the custom among the Indians. 

The woman was not recognized in any matters. She had nothing to say regarding her property rights-she had no rights. 10

She was only a slave; recognized as such by the customs. Being regarded as a slave at all times, she was not supposed to dine with the men but was to wait till the men were through and then to eat whatever was left, if any. She had to be satisfied with her lot and had to do all the work and drudgery while the men hunted and trapped.

MONETARY SYSTEM: There has never been any monetary system among the Indians. As they had no money, they bartered and traded with one another, using fur, fish, meats of animals and game, wild rice, maple sugar, etc. Although at that time they never had any established price on these articles of trade, the price was whatever they would give, or whatever they would take. It has been known back in primitive times. that men did most of the trading to show their bravery. 

SUCCESSION OF CHIEFS: The chief was recognized as the executive head or the ruler of his tribe. He is not an elected officer, but he inherits the chieftanship. The son always succeeds the father; if the chief has no sons, the daughter becomes Queen of her tribe. If the chief has no son or daughter, the chief's wife becomes the Queen and ruler of her tribe. This was the custom in olden times. The Indians had a code of laws which was very good.

FAMILY DEATHS: 

Formerly, when a man lost his wife or child, he would get up a war party and kill some of his enemies to assuage his grief.

SUPERSTITIONS 

Indians are very superstitious. A bird flying into a house is very bad luck-there is going to be a death in that house or in that family. 

Indians believe in witch craft. Indians are also ardent believers in ghosts. A ghost is a night prowler looking for people.

During the daytime ghosts are not often seen, except in a secluded place or far out in the forest, in some old deserted house, or near some old grave. The ghost will hiss and whistle at the people; some are seen running. The Indian is afraid of the ghost.

The Indians believe in dreams and in ghosts. When they see a ghost at night they are afraid of the ghost. They are also afraid of a snake; they say the snake is an evil spirit. They do not kill the snake but coax it in a friendly way to gain its 11

friendship for if they should kill the reptile it would do something awful to them or someone would die among the Indians. 

They believe in juggling and think that the juggler is infallible.

They also believe that a ventriloquist is a god and absolutely fear him. In

They believe that tobacco is divine and has the powers of a god. The Indians use tobacco for everything. It acts as a god. every walk of life they cannot do a thing without the tobacco. 

If a hunter goes out hunting in the forest, meets a red fox, and the fox runs quietly away, nothing will hapen. But if the fox should stop and howl on seeing the hunter, then someone in the hunter's family is going to die in a short time. 

When a hunter is out hunting rabbits, wounds one, and is bitten on his fingers, hand, etc., someone in the hunter's family is going to die in a short time.

The object of this small piece of Indian lore is to show, explain, and inform the reader of the customs and beliefs of the Indian, and to impress upon the mind of the reader that the Indians absolutely believe all this. Furthermore they believe that their leader is a spirit, and infallible. They believe in dreams; that ghosts travel in villages at night; that the ghost talks, can talk, and does talk to people. 

INDIAN CUSTOM 

Indians always mourned a long time for their dead children and relatives. The writer has seen men and women smear their faces with charcoal, a sign of love or sorrow for their dead.

Sometimes they would apply the charcoal when one of their relatives or neighbors was going away on a journey and would be gone for a long time. 12

TOTEM-DO-DAIM 

ne-do-daim is the present pronunciation as the language changes as time goes on. Just the word do-daim, a clan; an organization. Ne do - daim, meaning I belong to that clan; I belong to that organization; this is the way it is spoken now days, as many 

Indian words are changing with the times. The Chippewa language has changed within the two past decades or more to a large extent. Zha-wah-ne-gesbig. Skies. o-be-sah-gah-kee-zaun. To peel with fire, an old custom among the Indians just as far back as time will let you go. Whenever it was cloudy or threatening rain, it was the duty of the medicine man to fill his pipe and smoke. 

The smoke peeling off the clouds from the fire of his pipe would invoke the air Gods to that end: to affect a clear sky by smoking his pipe.

Note: 

In general, sound is the key to the pronunciation of the Chippewa language. Keep in mind the Chippewas were an outdoor people, children of the elements, and every breeze in the trees, every swish of the waves, every murmur of the streams created sounds which formed the foundation for their own audible expressions. Their words are composed of syllables, each syllable with its own meaning to create the meaning of the entire word. Each syllable is pronounced separately, but the accent generally appears on the last syllable. When spoken fluently, the language may be likened to the rise and fall of the wind.
