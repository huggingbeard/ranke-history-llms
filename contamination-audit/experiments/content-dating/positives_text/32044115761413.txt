WASHINGTON STATE LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL 

The Council

The first Washington State Legislative Council, in 1947, had twenty-one members, of whom eleven were from the House and ten from the Senate. The statute has been amended twice since then and the present law provides for thirty-one regular members, of whom sixteen are from the House and fifteen from the Senate. The law also provides that the membership must be bipartisan and neither party may have more than seventeen members. The Speaker of the House and the President Pro Tem of the 

Senate are members by virtue of their offices and the Speaker appoints the remaining representatives while the Senate President appoints the remaining Senators. In each case the appointments must be confirmed by - 2 -

the respective house. The vacancies of Senate members are filled by appointment by the remaining Senate members and the same procedure is followed by House members. Vacancies must be filled from the same political party as that of the members whose seat was vacated. The term of office of all members is from the time of confirmation or election until their successors have been confirmed or elected, or until they cease to be members of the Legislature. 1. 2. 3. 4. 5. 6. 

The Council's powers and duties include: 

Interim Studies. To perform through the Council as a whole, or through committees, duties and functions customarily delegated to special interim legislative committees.

Improve State Government. To study administrative organization and procedure of all agencies of state government and to recommend improvements.

Hearings. To make other studies of state government and to hear complaints, hold hearings, gather information, and make findings of fact. Prior approval of two-thirds of the membership is required to authorize public hearings. 

Legislative Matters. To attend generally to business addressed to, or affecting the Legislature during the interim between regular sessions.

Reports. To make periodic reports to members of the Legislature and a biennial report ten days prior to each regular session. 

Interstate Cooperation. To cooperate and function with similar Councils in other states and with the Council of State Governments. 

The Council has operated through committees, numbering during the last few years approximately twelve, and paralleling the major standing committees of the sessions. It has a permanent research and clerical staff which prepares materials and reports for the Council and its committees and places all recommendations in actual bill form for introduction in the Legislature. The Council frequently utilizes other members of the 

Legislature as special committee members, and has on occasion established large advisory committees representing various organizations in the state to assist in its major projects. Regular meetings are held at least every three months, and committee and other meetings number in the hundreds during a biennium. As in the other forty-three states which now have Legislative Councils, it is recognized that a sixty day session, once every two years, is inadequate for the scope and complexity of present day government, and the Legislative Council, which has continuity and time to study and appraise these major problems, is one method of assisting the Legislature. In addition to the long-range development of proposed legislation, the members and special members of the Council become extremely well-informed on matters of public assistance, education, natural resources and other areas in which they work. - 3

Upon reviewing the recommendations of the Legislative Council that were enacted by the Legislatures in 1969 and 1970, it is readily apparent as to what can be accomplished by the continuous work of legislators during the interim, along with public participation and a professional research staff.

In addition to the recommendations which were approved by the Legislature and listed above, it is essential to point out some of the other critical services that are performed by the Legislative Council which do not always culminate in legislation. For example, the legislation which is often referred to as the "open space" legislation had been under consideration by the Legislative Council for five years before finally becoming law. The final bill that passed the Legislature was not a Legislative Council request bill in and of itself. However, the Legislative Council had proposed legislation in prior sessions. 

Each biennium that the Council worked on this legislation, there was further refinement. If a comparison were to be made between the legislation that was enacted by the Legislature and the recommendation that was proposed in the 1969 session by the Legislative Council, it would become readily apparent that the efforts of the Legislative Council were very fruitful in informing legislators as to the need for such legislation. 

Through this process, the Legislative Council serves a very vital function in providing information to the legislators. 

The fact-finding ability of the Legislative Council was also demonstrated in the 1970 session with the enactment of the local government sales tax. The 1969 Legislature requested the Legislative Council to study the possible problems that would be encumbered if the Legislature were to grant such authorization and report back to the next regular legislative session. Immediately after the adjournment of the 1969 session, the Local Government Committee of the Legislative Council began pursuing the subject of extending the sales tax to local units of government. After intensive study of this subject, the Legislative Council had acquired a considerable amount of data and made it available to the Legislature. Because of the fiscal crisis at the local levels of government and the efforts of the local units of government, themselves, to acquire the right to levy a local sales tax, the 1970 Legislature prepared legislation. In the preparation of this legislation, the Legislative 

Council staff assisted the legislators with facts and figures and details as to how such legislation could be enacted within the constitutional framework of the state of Washington. Today a majority of the cities and counties have enacted the 1/2% sales tax.

In some instances, recommendations identical to those of the Legislative Council will be introduced by the non-Council members of the - 6-

Legislature. These bills sometimes pass, rather than the bills proposed by the Legislative Council; however, the Legislative Council does assist those legislators in their endeavors, since the Council is not concerned in only passing legislation it proposed, but is concerned, rather, with achieving the end objective. Therefore, Legislative Council staff members work with all legislators, as well as Council members, in accomplishing its objective. 

Some Legislative Council recommendations will also be included in other bills, such as:

Funds to pay for keypunching the Revised Code of Washington for the Legislature was a Legislative Council recommendation. However, the recommendation did not pass but was incorporated into the appropriation that the Legislature makes for the House and Senate in the opening days of the session.

In other instances, Legislative Council bills will be attached to other bills in the closing days of the session and thereby lose their Council identification. The reason for pointing this out is that quite often Council members may have a tendency to only compare the success of the Legislative Council by the amount of legislation that has been approved by the Legislature, while in fact it may be argued that the fact-finding and information dissemination functions of the Legislative Council are as important as its ability to submit recommendations to the Legislature.

FINANCIAL STATEMENT 

The appropriation of the Legislative Council for the last biennium was $423,893. Of this amount, $20,000 was specifically earmarked for the Legislative Council to hire consultants for the purpose of conducting a health care cost study. This $20,000 was used by the 

Public Assistance and Public Health Committee to enter into a contract with Laventhol, Krekstein, Horwath and Horwath. Funds appropriated for that purpose were not used for Legislative Council staff or office expenses. The 1970 Legislature appropriated $13,200 to the Legislative 

Council for the purpose of paying for previous unanticipated expenses. 

The Legislative Council was also directed to use $15,000 for the purpose of conducting the Capitol Museum Study. The State Government Committee of the Legislative Council contracted with Frank & Stein and Associates to perform that study. During the 1969-1971 biennium, the Legislative 

Council also provided staff to the Public Employees Collective Bargaining Committee and the Joint Board of Legislative Ethics. The Public 

Employees Collective Bargaining Committee was created in the 1969 Legislature and received an appropriation of $25,000. From this appropriation, the Collective Bargaining Committee paid the Legislative Council for that portion of the staff and secretarial time and materials used by the Collective Bargaining Committee operation. As required by law, the Legislative Council provides the staff support for the Joint Board of Legislative Ethics. Travel expenses for the Council staff are equally shared by the Senate and House Boards. The Legislative Council, from its own appropriation, pays for the staff work that is required by the Joint Board of Legislative Ethics. During a portion of the biennium, the Legislative Council provided staff to the Temporary Special Levy Commission. The Legislative Council was reimbursed for the time spent by the staff for that Committee.

INTERSTATE COOPERATION 

During the biennium, the Council continued to function as the Commission on Interstate Cooperation. The Council continued its very successful program of interstate exchange of reports with other states who have joined in the agreement to exchange the knowledge and activities of the legislative interim committees or councils of other states. 

One result is the acquisition of numerous current reports from other states which assist the individual committees of the Council in carrying out their functions. Senator William Gissberg, Vice-Chairman of the 

Council, continued as the Washington State representative on the drafting committee for Suggested State Legislation. Cooperation still exists between the Legislative Council and the Advisory Commission on Intergovern mental Relations.

EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE The announcement of the retirement of Mr. Donald C. Sampson as executive secretary of the Legislative Council came as a shock and a disappointment to the legislators and staff who had benefited by the knowledgeable, informative, and reliable service that they had received from Mr. Sampson. Mr. Sampson had served as the first executive secretary of the Legislative Council, and in this capacity devoted a considerable amount of time and energy in assisting legislators in developing the type of agency they desired. In recognition of his abilities and the service that he had provided for so many years to the Legislature, the Executive Committee of the Legislative Council introduced a proposed concurrent resolution, which was adopted by the full Council and submitted as House Concurrent Resolution No. 1. The loss of the service provided by Mr. Sampson will be severely noticed by the Legislature, members of the executive branch, judicial branch, and the citizens of the state of Washington, to whom he was so eager and willing to offer services. A copy of House Concurrent Resolution No. 1 is encompassed in this report as the first page, in recognition of Mr. Donald C. Sampson, Past Executive Secretary of the Legislative Council.

The Executive Committee, serving as an administrative, fiscal control unit in regard to Council operations, performed other functions during the 1969-1971 biennium. During this biennium, the Executive Committee recommended the following actions to the Legislative Council for their approval:

In addition to these recommendations, the Executive Committee, during the course of the biennium, also considered legislation which would create an interstate cooperation committee, wherein the executive, judicial and legislative branches would have served with the executive secretary of the Legislative Council acting as staff. Rec. No. 23 

Bill to revise the Washington state criminal code. (Senate Bill No. 384). With the preparation of this proposed revised criminal code, Washington joins the ranks of the many other states which have undertaken to rewrite their criminal statutes in the past several years.

The need for a new criminal code is particularly strong in view of the fact that not since 1909 has the Legislature enacted a comprehensive revision of the Washington code. 

The proposed code is based mainly upon, and is very similar to, the Model Penal Code adopted by the American Law Institute, and the recently revised criminal codes of New York, Connecticut and Illinois. 

Among the central objectives of the code are: (1) a code which is better suited than present law to provide a basis for a more efficient 

· 22 -

use of the criminal law to protect society from criminal conduct; (2) a code which takes account of the enormous changes in social values and in the circumstances of life itself which have occurred in the six decades since the last comprehensive revision of the criminal code; (3) a code which utilizes a more orderly structure based on related topics rather than the alphabetical listing of offenses found in present law; (4) a code which replaces the archaic language of present law with a drafting style that is more modern and direct; (5) a code which includes within its provisions many of the general principles of criminal law presently not codified at all and which now must be discovered by time-consuming research into the case law of the courts; and (6) a code which has greater precision and uniformity in authorized sentences and penalty provisions than present law.

Any real discussion of the many changes proposed would be impossible in this report. The primary object of the commentaries to each proposed section is to point out these changes and to explain them. However, several general observations can usefully be made: 

The more precise treatment of mental responsibility helps clarify an area of criminal law which presently is rather confusing and uncertain. The general provisions of the code concerning such matters as defenses of justification afford greater certainty and predictability than the present maze of uncodified and sometimes inconsistent case law. The more extensive and detailed coverage of homicidal, assaultive and kidnapping-restraining conduct should provide more efficient and useful tools for the criminal justice system to deal with such serious acts of violence. The discarding of many anachronistic technical distinctions derived from a much older time should be particularly useful in the context of property crimes. The enactment of the provisions concerning sexual conduct would preserve necessary protection against harmful and intolerable sexual behavior such as forcible sexual acts or acts directed at young and immature persons, while dispensing with many of those present provisions which simply bear little or no relation to the social values of contemporary society and which consequently are so conducive to widespread disrespect for the law. Finally, the introduction of a structure of degrees of felonies should allow for more rational grading of individual offenses because of the greater ease with which comparative judgments about the relative severity of different offenses may be made. Consequently, this proposed code provides a fairer and more consistent treatment of various similar activities than does present law. 

It bears emphasis that in preparing this proposed code, the basic and essential goal of protecting society from criminal conduct has been given constant attention, and it is the belief of the Committee that this goal would be made more attainable by adoption of this revised criminal code.

At a highly attended public meeting the Committee held on the code, it heard testimony from judges, prosecutors, police officers and a host of other interested parties, and this testimony was overwhelmingly in favor of the code's enactment. - 23 -

Senate Resolution 69-67EX. is identical to House

In connection with the latter pair of resolutions, the issue of the extension of the coverage of industrial insurance, administered by the state Department of Labor and Industries, to agricultural workers was especially current. Agricultural workers being excluded from coverage by law, the director of the Department of Labor and Industries decreed, through administrative rule, the coverage of agricultural workers engaged in the hops and tree fruit industries, effective April 1, 1969. - 24

While it is estimated that there are over 110,000 workers engaged in agricultural employment in Washington, the hop and tree fruit workers make up approximately 40% of all agricultural workers. 

The Joint-Committees held several public meetings, eliciting testimony on the two study resolutions dealing with industrial insurance, and the Committees' proposal for revising the Act. The Joint-Committees heard mostly from farm employer organizations, who did not address themselves to the Committees' proposal but, instead, spoke in favor of the concept of the so-called "3-way option" method of industrial insurance. Under this system the exclusive state fund would be replaced by the addition of private insurance companies in the field, or permit employers to self-insure against hazards to their workers. Among these employer organizations, the consensus was in favor of the extension of workmen's compensation benefits to all agricultural workers, but not without replacement of the exclusive state fund with the "3-way option" system.

The proposal being considered by the Joint-Committees involves the following: (a) mandatory universal coverage of agricultural workers; revised rate-making procedures; (b) (c) revised experience rating methods; (d) revised classifications of employment under compulsory coverage; (e) (f) (g) (h) revised method of calculation and collection of premiums; substantial death benefit increases; chances in the catastrophe loss formula; and substantial changes in temporary total disability [time loss] benefits.

The "3-way option" system was urged as a possible improvement to the system in Washington where workmen's compensation is handled exclusively by the state through the Department of Labor and Industries. In Oregon, an employer has an option to ensure his employees either through the State Compensation Department, through private insurance carriers, or through self-insurance. The Workmen's Compensation Board, an independent agency, has administrative and regulatory authority over all of these. 

Organized labor opposed the introduction of the "3-way option" in Washington while employers generally are among its proponents. There still remain serious questions regarding the cost of such a system to the employer and the processing of claims and appeals. 

Those advocating the "3-way option" system generally cite "private enterprise" and the freedom of choice for an employer of the insurance system he wishes to ensure under. Employers also generally believe that this system will mean lower premiums for them. Apart from "3-way option", itself, but included in the package proposal for the introduction of the system into Washington, the extension of coverage of all workers is offered (in place of the coverage now in effect in only "extrahazardous employment") and an increase in the benefit rate structure - 25.

now in effect. Proponents also assail the present system as too slow in the processing of claims, too cumbersome, and find fault in the appeals structure and other details in the operation of workmen's compensation as it exists. 

Opponents of "3-way option" are convinced that handing a social insurance system over to the "free enterprise" market will work to the detriment of injured workmen, not only because of the profit motive involved, but because the insurance company will be the agent of the employer, whose interests are inimical to the injured workman for whom the workmen's compensation act exists. They also say that "3-way option" is litiginous and will inherently mean delays brought by crowded dockets and the over-involvement of lawyers, which the conditions of the system will make necessary. 

Labor is also of the opinion that "3-way option" will mean higher, rather than lower premiums, a view which the Oregon experience is now beginning to indicate. 

The Committee arrived at no conclusions.

LEGISLATIVE PROCEDURE AND FACILITIES COMMITTEE

SEACOAST MANAGEMENT LEGISLATION 

The members of the Legislative Council believe that it is necessary for comprehensive shoreline management to have the following provisions: 

1. The primary and most important prerequisite should be adequate provisions for local planning. The state should establish broad, comprehensive guidelines for local governments to follow. 

Provision should be made so that local governments must comply with plans within these guidelines within a two or three year period. 

Local jurisdictions should be given the authority to adopt provisions more stringent than the state guidelines, if they so choose. They must be given enough time to draft the provisions and funds from some source to pay the cost of such planning. The statewide zoning proposed by Initiative 43 is not the answer. Local areas better understand local problems. 

2. Present construction on seacoast and shoreline areas must be grandfathered in. Initiative 43 does not do this and it does not answer all the questions posed by the Supreme Court in the Lake 

Chelan decision of Wilbour vs. Gallagher. 

3. Following approval of the local plans by the state, control over implementation of the plans should be at the local level. 

The state should only have a review power on variances which are granted by local government for new developments. After the local plans are approved by the state, the state should not have the authority to review all new developments. After the local plans are approved by the state, the state should not have the authority to review all new developments. All applications for development should be approved, if they come within the local plans as approved by the state. 

4. Private persons should be allowed to institute class actions for damages and injunctive relief only if developments are in violation of the local plan. All civil penalties should be instituted by the local or state authorities and not be individuals. 

Shoreline management legislation must be specific enough so that it does not conflict with the state's laws on air and water pollution, nuclear energy siting, and other legislation. 

5. Provision must be made to acquire private property by condemnation either upon recommendation of local governments to state agencies, or by the state agency which administers the act. The definition of what is and what is not navigable is a question which must be defined by the Legislature and the courts and the institutions. 

Attempt to re-define navigability without adequate research is futile. 

6. If at all possible, decisions by the Department of 

Ecology and local governments should be reviewed by the existing ecological commission rather than creating a new board or commission. 

7. Legislation must provide for control of both clear cutting of timber and other types of vegetation if they damage the environment. Simply prohibiting the clear cut of timber is not sufficient. It is imperative that existing private property rights - 36 -

be maintained and simply stating that shoreline areas are "held in the public trust" does not necessarily take care of this. Enforcement should be through the superior court via the Administrative Procedure Act. The attorney general and the prosecuting attorneys of the counties should be the enforcement agencies.

Once guidelines are approved by local governments and the state there should be some assurances that those plans will be the guiding ones and that only where a variance is requested should there be state review over local plans. The methods and procedures of court action must be clear enough so that cases cannot be tied up with years and years of litigation on every contested proposal. 

The initiative has placed the Legislature in a difficult situation since the Environmental Council's proposal has, in effect, delayed any adequate seacoast management by precluding a resolution of the problem until the 1972 general session. "Enrichment" of breads and cereals in the United States means addition of certain B vitamins, niacin, riboflavin and thiamine and iron in specific amounts. When wheat is processed into white flour, which the American public has long preferred, most of the nutrients are removed. Today, there is an almost universal enrichment of bread and flour and certain other cereal items in the United States. Some bakery products and mixes available to the baker and the housewife, however, are not enriched. These products include many bread substitutes, such as sweet rolls, English muffins, doughnuts and crackers. Despite this far-reaching program of enrichment, iron deficiency anemia among women of child-bearing age is frequently observed in the United States. There is every reason to conclude that additional enrichment of flour and bakery products would be beneficial to the entire population regardless of income. It is desirable that this enrichment be undertaken. 

The milling industry should attack this problem in two phases. First, all wheat and corn flour should be universally enriched, preferably at the mill, to the present limits of the regulations, the only exceptions being where the end use of enriched flour

2. Statement of the problem: Nationally, malpractice suits filed by patients against physicians are on the rise, and rates for malpractice insurance have consequently soared. Statistics from news sources indicate that one doctor out of every six of the 300,000 in the United States has been, or is being, sued for malpractice. Last year, physicians paid $75 million in malpractice insurance premiums and, in addition, some $18 million was paid in awards to patients in 1968, according to the general counsel of the American Medical Association. Legal fees would be added to these costs. Higher insurance rates are being laid, attributedly, to the increased number of malpractice suits filed by disgruntled patients, and the larger settlements being made both in and out of court. According to the Insurance Information Institute in New York, premiums on malpractice insurance generally are up 47% over a year ago. From 

1959 through 1963, insurance companies have had to pay out $140 for every $100 received in premiums from physicians. For surgeons, it was $151 for every $100 paid in. Malpractice insurance rates were increased in 27 states and decreased in two. The increases ranged from 10% in four states to 75% in Montana and Ohio, and 100% in 

Vermont. Rates in six states went up from 50% to 60%. Ironically, rates were reduced 15% in Oregon and 10% in Ohio. 

In Washington: Washington has experienced an increase in malpractice insurance rates since November of 1967, averaging over 

40% per year. Specialties have been reclassified and a fifth category added, paying the top premium rates, these being gynecology and obstetrics, anesthesiology, neurology, orthopedics and plastic surgery. 

The Aetna Casualty and Surety Company in 1967 paid out an actual $1.38 on every $1.00 of premium income, the permissible loss ratio being a 55¢ pay out in order to break even. The Aetna Company handles about 75% of the malpractice insurance business in this state. In the first six months of 1968, in spite of the premium increase, the incurred loss paid out was $1.25. The increase in insurance premiums is laid principally to the increase in amounts of judgment awards and settlements. In Washington, the actual number of cases has not increased significantly, according to a report of the Washington Medical Association. The Aetna Company estimates an increase in cases of only 10% per year. But, the increase in awards is more significant. 

In 1966 the average malpractice claim was $3,028. In 1967, it jumped to $4,410. The estimate for 1968 is a $5,000 average for each claim paid.

Conclusions: It was the conclusion of the Committee to await the results of a special medical-legal malpractice review panel, established in and for Spokane County, composed of an equal number of physicians and lawyers. The panel will pass on the merits of potential malpractice claims, with a view toward eliminating frivolous claims, as well as assuring the prosecution of valid ones. If a majority of the panel determine that there is a reasonable probability that the - 42 -

acts complained of constitute professional negligence, and that there is reasonable medical probability the claimant was injured thereby, the Spokane Medical Society will make available expert medical testimony to the attorney for the claimant in cases where settlement could not be made and where the panel has made a finding of probable cause that there was medical malpractice. On the other hand, if the panel should determine that there are no grounds for imputing negligence, the Bar Association will use all efforts and moral persuasion to have the claim dropped, and the attorney bringing the matter for review shall thereafter refrain from filing any court action on it. 

The prospect for pre-trial malpractice review holds hope for eliminating abuses. Certainly, it would infringe upon constitutional prerogatives if such review were mandatory, and if the decisions of the panel were final and binding. The screening process will call upon the utmost professionalism of lawyers and physicians to accomplish its goal. The idea of such a medical malpractice review panel for Spokane 

County, if successful, holds promise for solutions to the problem for other counties, as well. Pending the results of this innovative experiment, the notion of legislative tampering with the interpreted rules of court, such as the statute of limitations and res ipsa loquitur, would be most premature.

During the 1969-1971 interim, the Revenue and Regulatory Agencies Committee concentrated its attention on seven major study These areas were: steam plant taxation, open space taxation, local government financing, the tax exempt status of retirement homes, proposed technical amendments to the enacted income tax statute, mobile home taxation, and camper taxation.

Steam plant taxation. The 1957 Legislature enacted legisla tion providing for the creation of joint operating agencies by PUD's and/or cities for the purpose of acquiring, constructing, operating and owning facilities for the generation and/or transmission of electrical energy. That legislation (RCW 43.52) further specified that when operating a steam generated plant, the joint operating agency 

- 47

would be exempt from taxation. The initial basis for this exemption was to provide an incentive for the construction of a coal-fired steam plant in the then depressed Cle Elum area. Although that plant was never constructed, the exemption is being utilized by the Washington Public Power Supply System on electricity being produced by the nuclear powered Hanford facility.

The Committee felt that continued existence of this exemption was not warranted but that the existing facility be required to negotiate its taxes (in the same manner as city-owned power producing facilities) with the affected cities, counties, and school districts. 

Open space taxation. In November of 1968, the voters of the state overwhelmingly approved HJR 1, a constitutional amendment to allow for the taxation of timber, agricultural and open space lands on the basis of current use as opposed to highest and best use. The impetus behind the enabling legislation was two-fold. First, there was a growing recognition that without preferential tax treatment, it would be virtually impossible to continue in existence adequate open space lands in urban and suburban environs. There was the conscious determination that a social benefit resulted from the maintaining of "green belts" near the heavily populated metropolitan areas of the state. The second factor was the necessity of encouraging continued optimal timber and agricultural land mangement. Statistical evidence had indicated that urban sprawl, coupled with skyrocketing land values and associated property taxes, had resulted in land being transformed from agricultural and timber uses into residential subdivisions. As agricultural production is located further and further from urban areas, final product prices inevitably increase due to more costly transportation.

The recommendation of the Committee had to strike a delicate balance between accomplishing the established goals and still maintaining the revenue source for local government without, in effect, writing a bill that was a give-away to the large land owners. 

Although not receiving full Council approval (because of a timing problem), the Committee's recommendation was introduced as 

House Bill No. 26 (1970 special session) and with minor amendments enacted into law.

Local government financing. During the first year of the interim, the Committee established as its number one priority the task of deriving a desirable supplement to existing revenue sources for cities and counties. The financial plight of municipalities was recognized by the 1967 and 1969 sessions of the Legislature, which had made direct appropriations to the cities. The Revenue and 

Regulatory Agencies Committee realized that local governments no longer desired to be at the mercy of the Legislature for short-run doles. 

Testimony at numerous hearings indicated that local government desired a taxing source with a built-in growth factor. After examining the alternatives, the Committee recommended that the cities and counties be authorized to levy a one-half of one percent sales tax with a distribution formula in the event that the cities of a - 48 -

county levying the tax also desired to do so. That formula allows for fifteen percent of the sales tax proceeds (from the one-half percent) within the incorporated area to be allocated to the county. 

Although the recommendation was not adopted by the full Legislative Council because of timing problems, it was enacted into law during the 1970 special session of the Legislature. 

Retirement home taxation. 

Under existing law, retirement homes which qualify pursuant to RCW 84.36.128 are entirely exempt from any property taxation. This exemption is granted regardless of the financial organization (e.g. founder's fee versus low income rental subsidy) of the institution or the wealth of the occupants. 

At the same time, low income senior citizens residing in conventional residences are only exempt from paying the first fifty dollars of their property taxes.

The Committee modified Recommendation No. 6 from the 1969 session to partially compromise some of the objections to that proposal. As finalized, this new proposal provided that retirement homes would be taxed proportionately to the number of occupied units occupied by individuals or couples with total incomes in excess of $4,000. In addition, the proposed statute would raise the maximum income qualification for the fifty dollar senior citizen's exemption to $4,000.

Technical amendments to the state income tax. The 1969 session of the Legislature approved a constitutional amendment and enabling legislation providing for a state income tax. Subsequent analysis of the statute indicated certain areas requiring clarification. The Committee was directed to review the technical aspects of the statute and propose those changes necessary for the orderly implementation of the state income tax. Prior to finalization of the Committee's recommendation, the voters of the state voted down HJR 42, thus making unnecessary the technical amendments to the statute.

This proposal requires that land surveyors file records of survey within ninety days after any survey which discloses certain variances from the description of record, or which discloses replaced or restored corners. The proposal specifies the form and content for records of survey and requires that a surveyor's certificate be filed with the record of survey. 

The proposal would make it unlawful for any person not duly qualified as a land surveyor to sign, stamp, or seal any document required to be filed under the proposed act.

There is a provision that a record of survey is not required in conjunction with certain surveys by public officers, preliminary surveys and mapping surveys of local subdivision or platting laws. This law has no direct effect on the state's 1969 platting and subdivision legislation.

The proposal provides that the charge for filing any record of survey shall be fixed by the county commissioners. 

53

The proposal establishes standards for the filing of survey records, for the marking of monuments and for the relocation of monuments, when construction or other activities affect their perpetuation.

The proposal provides that non-compliance with the act would constitute grounds for revocation of a land surveyor's authority to practice.

Purpose: The purpose of this legislation is to provide a method for preserving the evidence of land surveys. The proposal would reduce survey duplication, thereby saving costs completely in some cases, and substantially reducing it in others. There would probably be a reduction in the number of boundary limits, since records of survey would be more accurate and more easily found. proposal would provide a central pooling of survey data not previously available to attorneys, public officials, title companies, and the general public.

The Rec. No. 10 State volunteer workers medical coverage. 

This proposal would provide that medical insurance coverage be extended to state government volunteer workers under the state's 

Workmen's Compensation Act. 

Purpose: To cover state government volunteers with the same medical aid coverage as other state workers. The act provides that the state shall pay a premium for medical aid and the funds shall be placed in a separate medical aid fund for volunteers. The act has no effect on present mileage compensation given to volunteers and has no effect on the volunteer workers for public bodies other than state agencies. Most of the volunteers who would be covered by this provision are working in the Department of Institutions. 

The total biennial cost for such coverage would be approximately $3,000.

Rec. No. 11 

Uniform per diem. 

Under this proposal some boards would be increased from $5 or $10 per diem to a $25 figure, and other boards would be reduced from $50 or $75 to $25. Only mileage or transportation costs would be allowed in addition to per diem. All other expenses would have to come out of the $25 figure, except conference fees and necessary expenses which would be defined by the Office of Program Planning and Fiscal Management rule. 

Purpose: The purpose of this recommendation is to establish three uniform per diem sections in the revised code. All agencies, boards and commissions would be required to comply with these three sections. In effect, the per diem and travel expenses of any boards and commissions would be changed by this proposal. The proposal would set a $25 per day per diem for in-state travel and a $35 figure for out-of-state travel. The director of the Office of Program 

Planning and Fiscal Management would prescribe the requirements for implementing the proposal. - 54 ·

Rec. No. 12 County-wide port districts. This recommenda tion deletes reference in the RCW to port districts of less than county size. No port district could be created less than county size under this statute.

Purpose: The purpose of this proposal is to halt the creation of small port districts less than county size. Ports would still be able to consolidate under the present consolidation procedures and areas could still annex to existing ports under the existing statutes.

Rec. No. 1 Definition of poverty. 

Vancouver 

That RCW 72.23.230 be amended to change the definition of the poverty level from a fixed amount of $300 to a flexible figure in order for the Department of Institutions to become eligible for federal matching funds. 

Purpose: The purpose of the recommendation is to conform the definition of "poverty", correcting the discrepancy between degree of poverty level under which the Department of Institutions is operating.

The Legislative Council adopted the Committee recommendation at their meeting of September 6, 1969, and the recommendation was introduced into the 42nd session of the Legislature as Senate 

Bill No. 28. 

Changing the definition of the poverty level from a fixed amount of $300 by authorizing a lesser amount qualifies a person under the jurisdiction of the Division of Institutions for benefits under any kind of program, public or private, that he may be otherwise qualified for. Basically, it allows the Division of 

Institutions to reduce his level of poverty in order to qualify him for other programs in the Division's discretion. If the act were not changed, the $300 figure may be too high to get the person benefits in other programs and allows the Division to become eligible for federal matching funds. For instance, if "x" charity has benefits it wants to give poverty people, but in order to get these benefits a person has to have not more than $200, the present bill 

- 55-

would allow the Division to qualify this individual to this amount of $200 under the amendment. If the act were not changed, the person would not be able to qualify, because the act requires a poverty level of $300.

At the recommendation of the Department of Social and 

Health Services, the recommendation embodied in Senate Bill No. 28 

was incorporated into a larger scope departmental request bill.

Rec. No. 2 - Collecting reimbursement. Establishes a pro cedure for collecting reimbursement from mentally retarded and ill residents at state institutions, in lieu of guardianship, by having the superintendent pay from the personal accounts of the residents, the costs of care based on a finding of financial responsibility. 

This would be possible, however, only after the finding of financial responsibility had become final, after time for appeal had elapsed, and after a parent, spouse, or guardian had been served with notice, and the finding had become final by failure to take an appeal. 

Purpose: The purpose of the recommendation is to save the Division of Institutions the expense of $250,000 in having to obtain legal guardians, mostly attorneys, in every case involving a finding of financial responsibility of the resident where no appeal has been made.

The bill is largely remedial and attempts to fill the void which the supreme court created when it knocked out the authority for the attorney general to act as the legal guardian for all mentally retarded children.

A mentally retarded child qualifies for social security payments for his care. The payments go to the child but are given to the parent to disburse according to the requirements of the social security department. The problem is that benefits for care and maintenance have been accruing in their accounts, but the Division of Institutions has been unable to use them for the care of the child placed in the institution. The state has no means now of tapping these funds, except by a very lengthy and costly means of going out and paying for guardians to represent the retarded child so that the Division can then serve a notice of financial responsibility on the guardian and then collect the social security benefits for the child's welfare. Most of these guardians are, in fact, attorneys in private practice and the fees paid to these private guardians are not inconsiderable. The bill would enable the Division to serve a notice of financial responsibility on a parent, spouse, or personal representative of the retarded child, thereby circumventing the complicated guardianship procedure. If, and only if, no appeal is made and the finding of financial responsibility has become final by failure to take an appeal as provided in the statute, then the superintendent of the institution would become a representative payee of the child, receiving the social security benefits and then paying them over to the Division for the care of the child. If the child does not have a parent, spouse, or personal representative, then and only then, the Division would obtain a guardian to represent the child. - 56 -

The Legislature in 1967 had passed a law allowing the Department of Institutions to collect reimbursement for the care and support of the retarded at residential schools. The attorney general by statute at this time was designated as the guardian. 

The supreme court later overturned the authority of the attorney general to act as guardian for mentally retarded children at a state institution, saying that this authority infringed upon the prerogative of the court. This recommendation would remedy the gap in the law created by the supreme court decision. But, under the proposal the superintendent could not pay from the fund belonging to the resident until the finding of financial responsibility made by the Division of Collections had become final, e.g., after the time for appeal had elapsed, and then only if there was a parent, spouse or guardian, or other person standing in a representative capacity who had been served with notice and finding of financial responsibility, and the finding had become final by failure to take an appeal as provided for in the statute.

There are many cases involving residents of the state residential schools who have funds to the excess of $1,000, which are subject to the provisions of RCW 72.33.180 in which there is a parent, guardian, or a person standing in a representative capacity upon whom notice and finding of financial responsibility could be served and who could take an appeal under the statute from the determination made of financial responsibility. The proposal would disqualify the superintendent from making payments in those cases in which there is no living parent, spouse or a court-appointed guardian or other person acting in a representative capacity from paying over to the Division until such a guardian is appointed. 

This amendment pre-supposes that the superintendent who is an employee of the Division of Institutions is not impartial and cannot perform in an adversary capacity effectively against the Division of Institutions and, therefore, should not be permitted to make payments unless there is a person or persons able to take an appeal under the act from the findings of responsibility. 

The Committee's recommendation was adopted by the Legislative Council December 13, 1969, and was embodied into House Bill No. 

118 and Senate Bill No. 89 and introduced into the first extraordinary session of the Legislature in 1970, where it was adopted and became 

Chapter 75 (2nd extraordinary session), Laws of 1970. 

Olympic Mental Health and Mental Retardation Center, Bremerton. 

The Committee inspected and toured this facility in Bremerton on 

September 26, 1969. Olympic Center serves a variety of needs and people, is designed to act as a consultation agency to other community resources in enlisting their support and participation in mental health and mental retardation activities. It also serves as an out-patient clinic, a general hospital, in the sense that it is affiliated with three participating hospitals in Bremerton where referrals can be made. It serves also as an adult residence and has instituted a career development (rehabilitation) program, referring retarded adult and youth persons to the community, sampling job prospects which are attainable within the area of their competence. The center also has on-call emergency services and operates as a children's hospital and a children's day care program. as - 57 -

The Epton program is separately associated in Olympic Center and is for the less severely retarded. It is a private operation operating on a state subsidy and local charitable donations. 

There is no overlap between his program and that of Epton, as the Olympic Center works only with the most severely retarded, and the Epton program is for the less severely retarded. The program at 

Olympic Center can basically be described as offering services to persons in the community on a community level as an alternative to institutionalization. 

Olympic Center operates as a pilot comprehensive community health and mental retardation center for the state and serves Kitsap, Clallum and Jefferson Counties. Its purpose is to explore, through active research, solutions to the problems of utilizing and coordinating existing resources and of providing services for both a concentrated population center and a sparcely populated area. The Olympic Center demonstrates approaches to comprehensive community mental health and mental retardation programming, and developing techniques and methods of providing services which can be adapted and utilized by others in developing their own community programs. The center provides necessary specialized services and works with public and private community resources to deal with the problems of all individuals in the areas of mental health, mental retardation, geriatrics, dependency and delinquency. It also explores ways in which state health, welfare, education and institutions organizations may work cooperatively with their local counterparts and the private sector in the search for solutions to a variety of human problems. The primary long range goal of the center is to determine the extent to which a full array of community services can meet the needs of all persons with mental health and mental retardation problems and provide alternatives to institutional care.

Vancouver School for the Deaf. As a result of somewhat misleading statements in the Planning Prospectus, published by the 

Institutions Division of the Department of Social and Health 

Services, many individuals were concerned about the planned closing of the School. It was intended that this would be turned into a facility to deal with the multiple-handicapped. The central issue was one of whether educational opportunities for deaf children should be provided in the local school district or at the Vancouver School, or both. Currently, more than two-thirds of those deaf children who receive special instruction do so in special education classes in school districts. These children are afforded the opportunity of remaining in a family environment as well as associating with nonhearing impaired children.

Testimony given at three hearings held on this subject indicate that the Vancouver School does provide a fine education for the students, but that certain aspects (e.g. vocational education) need to be upgraded. The culmination of these meetings was that Dr. Conte and the Institutions Division recanted from their original position and suggested that the Committee support a resolution calling for the Legislature to improve the total resources for the deaf, as well as provide a facility for dealing with multiple-handicapped children. 

Fire Protection at the Rainier School. The Committee held a hearing at the Rainier School in Buckley to analyze the outbreak - 58 -

of arson fires at this facility and determine whether the overall safety of the residents had been severely impaired. This satisfied the members of the Committee and further study was deemed unnecessary.
