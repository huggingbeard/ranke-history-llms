Summary 

TVA is a Federal corporation which Congress established for the unique purpose of accomplishing the unified development of the natural resources of the Tennessee Valley area, in cooperation with the people and their local agencies. Its part in this regional program includes, along with improvement of the river and its tributary valleys, the provision of abundant electric power which it sells not for profit but to achieve widespread use at the lowest cost. With the greatly increased use of electricity it has been possible to reduce unit operating costs and, at the same time, contribute substantially to the support of local, state and Federal government. 

During the fiscal year 1964 TVA paid $8.2 million from power revenues to states and counties in lieu of taxes. In addition, the municipal and rural cooperative electric systems that retail TVA power paid $14.6 million. 

The rate of growth in these combined payments since 1937 when the power systems in the area now supplied with TVA power were largely privately owned has been greater than the rate of increase in state and local tax payments by private power systems in the United States. 

As a public enterprise wholly owned by the United States, TVA pays no Federal income taxes. Its power does, however, provide multimillion-dollar benefits for Federal taxpayers. These benefits arise from the strengthened regional economy, from large direct savings to other 

Federal agencies which use TVA power, and from earnings for the Federal Government from TVA's power operations. 2

The Nature of TVA and its Power Program 

Any logical discussion of the revenues received by the various levels of government from the TVA power program must recognize the basic differences between this program of a Federal development agency and the operations of a private power system. Comparisons which ignore the differences present a distorted picture - a picture not of TVA but of what TVA might look like if it were something else.

The unusual aspects of power supply in the Tennessee Valley include the following: 

1. TVA is wholly owned by the Federal Government. 

2. TVA is primarily a resource development agency of which electric power is one part. Its program also encompasses flood control, navigation, fertilizer research and development, improved farm and forestry practices, control of soil erosion, and diversification of industry. All of these are aimed at strengthening the national defense and improving the economic well-being of the Tennessee 

Valley and of the Nation. 

3. Bringing money into the public treasuries is not a main purpose of TVA, but to encourage economic development, which, in turn, does promote growth 3

in taxable property and taxable income of various kinds. The area served with TVA power now pays a considerably larger share of the total of the Nation's tax bills of all kinds than it did when TVA was created; and the share is continuing to grow. Abundant low-cost power as one of the tools of economic development is helping to bring this about. 

4. Unlike most private utilities, TVA does not sell power directly to retail customers except for a few large power-using industries and Federal agencies. TVA sells power at wholesale to independent locally owned and operated distribution systems. These local distributors retail the power to 14 million customers in an area with a population of six million. The distributors' operations, as well as TVA's, should be considered in tax comparisons between the Valley area and other areas. 

5. In developing the region's power resources, TVA's assignment by the Congress is to make the power available for widespread use at the lowest possible rates that will cover costs. The local distributors work toward the same objective. Under these circumstances the use of electricity grows rapidly. 

An incidental result is that tax payments by the power systems and their consumers grow large

even though the taxes per unit of power consumption may be comparatively small. 

6. In the TVA area, power is marketed primarily by publicly owned agencies-municipal and cooperative distribution systems-and tax policies applied to such agencies markedly affect comparisons among regions. There are more than 

2,100 municipal and 900 cooperative electric distribution systems in the U. S. and they serve one out of five customers. The number and percentage of customers served through such systems are unusually high in the Tennessee Valley area. 

Throughout the Nation such systems are exempt from Federal income taxes. 

7. About a third of the electricity TVA sells is used by the Atomic Energy Commission and other defense agencies of the Federal Government. TVA 

does not pay taxes to Federal, state, or local governments on these sales; this represents a large saving to the Federal taxpayers. 

All these major differences (and some less striking ones, such as the unusually high proportion of rural consumers and rural power facilities in the area served with TVA power) affect tax comparisons and their significance. 5 сл

State and Local Taxes PAYMENTS BY TVA 

TVA, as an agency of the Federal Government, is not subject to taxation by states or their subdivisions. However, the TVA Act provides for payments in lieu of taxes by TVA in the states in which it sells power and owns power facilities.

The present formula was established by Congress after extensive consultation with state and local officials in the area and independent experts in public fiscal policy. It results in TVA paying to states and counties in lieu of taxes 5 percent of its revenues from power sales except sales to the Federal Government. In fiscal year 1964 these payments amounted to $8,213,000. 

PAYMENTS BY DISTRIBUTORS 

The 106 municipal and county owned electric systems that distribute TVA power all make payments in lieu of taxes. Most of the 50 cooperatives pay state and local property taxes.

The power distributors' tax and tax-equivalent payments to state and local governments were $14,604,000 in fiscal year 1964.

COMPARISONS WITH TAXES OF POWER SYSTEMS IN OTHER AREAS 

The combined payments to state and local governments by TVA and the distributors ($22,817,000 in fiscal

year 1964) amount to about 7.5 percent of the power bills paid by the homes and businesses that use TVA power. In the case of the private power companies serving surrounding areas, the payments of state and local taxes range from 4.5 percent to 10.6 percent of the power bills of the customers they serve.

Thus the percentage of the customers' power bills that is paid to the states and local governments for tax purposes is neither disproportionately higher nor lower in the area served by TVA power than in surrounding areas. 

The change in this region from private to public power supply has affected favorably the tax receipts collected from the power operations by the taxing jurisdictions in the region. In 1937 when most of the power facilities in the area now supplied with TVA power were privately owned, the state and local taxes paid on all these electric operations were about $3.1 million. The combined payments by TVA and the distributors in fiscal year 1964 were more than 7 times what they had been 26½ years earlier. By comparison, the annual payments of state and local taxes by the power companies in the Nation during calendar year 1963 were less than 6 times those of 26 years ago. The chart on the opposite page portrays the more rapid increase in state and local tax payments by the electric systems in this area than for all privately owned utilities in the U. S.

In addition to the tax and tax-equivalent payments they receive, the local governments benefit financially in many ways from the improving economy. They also benefit from lower electric bills for public services.

Federal Taxes 

The principal Federal tax affecting private power systems is the income tax, or tax on profits. It is the policy of the Federal Government not to apply the income tax to an enterprise of its own, like TVA's power operations, or to municipal and cooperative enterprises like most of the distributors of TVA power. Even if the income tax were applicable, the payments would be low, because income taxes are based on profits, and TVA power is supplied essentially on a non-profit basis. 

Although TVA pays no Federal income taxes, the Federal Government receives very large financial benefits from its power operations in the TVA area. These benefits include the earnings from TVA's operations, savings to Federal installations supplied with TVA power, and tax and other financial benefits arising from the economic development of the area. 

TVA's investment in power facilities has been financed with funds from three sources: Federal appropriations; reinvested earnings of the power system; and proceeds from the sale of revenue bonds and notes, primarily to

private investors. TVA pays interest on the bonds and notes, makes two kinds of annual payments to the U. S. 

Treasury - one as a dividend on outstanding appropriations and the other as a repayment of appropriations. 

The dividend is equal to the current cost to the Government of borrowed money. In 1964, it was $40,206,000. 

The repayment of appropriations is on a schedule that will repay $1 billion of the appropriation investment in not more than 54 years. Last year it amounted to $10 million. 

Approximately a third of all the electrical energy sold by TVA is sold to other Federal agencies, primarily the atomic energy plants at Oak Ridge and Paducah. 

The fact that the region does not collect state and local taxes on these Federal power sales means large savings in the power bills of those Federal operations. Most of the 

Federal operations affected, unlike TVA's power operations, are tax supported; savings in their operating costs reduce 

Federal taxes.

TVA's low rates save the Atomic Energy Commission and other Federal agencies many millions of dollars every year. Moreover, the example of TVA's low rates to the 10

Atomic Energy Commission, by far the largest consumer of TVA power, has led to savings in AEC's cost of power from other suppliers. To achieve rates closely comparable to TVA's, the private companies that were established to furnish large blocks of power to AEC entered into arrangements that result in additional large savings every year in 

AEC's power bills.

Even more important to the Government and the taxpayers than the earnings from the TVA power system and the savings on the power bills of Federal agencies is the impact of TVA, through its power program and other resource development activities, on the economy of the region and of the Nation. Effective resource development brings about tax and other financial benefits to the 

Federal Treasury.

Because of economic progress the Tennessee Valley area now contributes annually hundreds of millions of dollars more to total Federal tax revenues than if its share of the total were still as small as it was before Congress created TVA. The abundant availability of low-cost TVA power is one of the important factors in improvement in the economy of the region and of the Nation. 11

Why Rates for TVA Power are Low 

The low rates at which TVA power is sold have brought benefits-not costs-to all levels of government, and hence to the general taxpayers. TVA power is not sold to earn profits but to encourage abundant use of this vital economic resource by selling it at the lowest possible cost to the consumers.

Part of the difference in TVA rates and those of privately owned systems is a difference in level of earnings; 

TVA contents itself with a smaller profit margin. Low rates have led to high customer use and mass production, both of which make lower unit costs possible. The region has also been able to take advantage of its economical hydroelectric and coal resources of which little use was formerly being made. The combined effect of all these factors is that the cost of a kilowatt-hour to TVA and the distributors of TVA power for production, transmission, distribution, accounting, and administration all average less than half the corresponding costs of the United States power industry as a whole.

In short, TVA treats electric power as a tool for economic development, encouraging its widest possible use at the lowest possible rates. High use of electricity among homes, farms, stores, and factories helps to develop the economy of the region; a stronger economy for this region contributes to a stronger Nation; it also contributes greater financial support to the national, state, and local governments. 12
