Pendarvis, the only grandson of Jonathan Bryan, born Sept. 12, 1708, died March 

12, 1788, and the great grandson of Joseph Bryan, of Colleton Parish, South Carolina.

Bryan."

stored with various but desultory reading-for he read solely for his own amusement. His integrity was unimpeached and unimpeachable-his honor unsullied. 

Quick in his resentments, but easily appeased when injured, and equally ready to acknowledge an error when wrong, provided the appeal was made to his sense of justice for he knew no fear. He was brave even to rashness, and his generosity bordered on profusion. 66 'Strange, wonderful man! Some fatality must have taken him from the sphere for which Nature designed him, and he has left his friends to regret that his talents, integrity, honour, unbounded and unexplained courage should be so early lost to them'-Such are the words of a friend, speaking of Mr. Bryan. The writer of this paragraph knows them to be true.” 

In the note accompanying this article Mr. Randolph said: "Dear Sir: Agreeable to your request, I send you a copy of the notice of Mr. 

Bryan's death 'in my own handwriting.' I am but a bad copyist, especially of my own works. The article in question was thrown together in great haste for The 

Press, which refused to publish it. It is nevertheless I trust a faithful, certainly not a flattering, likeness of a friend, towards whom my heart yearned from the first moment I set eyes on him, with whom I maintained an uninterrupted friendship to the day of his death (during sixteen years), and whom I loved better than any other man on earth. I am, Sir, Yours, (Signed) JOHN Randolph, of Roanoke. 

Richmond, Feb. 24, 1814.” 

When seventy-nine years of age, John Randolph Bryan wrote the following sketch of himself: "My father having died on Wilmington Island in September, 1812, and my mother left a young widow on a place ten miles from a post office, church or physician, it became necessary that some provision should be made for the education of her children. "Soon after this my mother's father, General Thomas Marsh Forman, of Cecil county, Maryland, came to visit her. He remained in Georgia sometime and, after making proper preparation, returned with his daughter to Maryland. "The war going on at that time, it was necessary to travel by land, and my mother determined to go in her own carriage, taking with her her son Thomas and daughter Virginia. Joseph, who was then an infant, was taken charge of by my aunt, Sarah Screven, and Uncle John Screven. My sister Georgia and myself were boarded at the house of Madame Cottineau, an excellent lady, who was a French refugee from St. Domingo. There we had the benefit of the instruction of the Abbe Carle in French as well as the family who spoke little of any other language. "The stay of my sister and self in this family lasted for more than two years, during which time I went to the school of a Mr. Johnson, who taught in Savannah. "My mother returned home to the Island the winter of 1813-1814. I followed Mr. Johnson to his school near Coosawhatchie, South Carolina, in 1815. 

My father's friend, John Randolph of Roanoke, had visited him on the Island before his marriage, and knowing the poor advantages existing there for educating boys, proposed to my mother to send my brother Thomas and self to Virginia for our education. This was done in February, 1816, when we went in a sailing vessel to Baltimore.

“There I first saw Mr. Randolph at the house of Judge Joseph H. Nicolson, where my brother and self were treated with the greatest tenderness. Mr. Randolph then as ever afterwards spoke to us as if we were his children, called me his godson, and was particularly tender to Tom, who was then only seven years old, and I ten. "From Baltimore we went to Rose Hill, the home of my grandfather, where we found a second mother in Mrs. Forman, who had been the widow Calender, a beautiful and childless widow. She made us call her aunt, and did all she could to supply the place of a real grandmother. "From Rose Hill our route to Virginia lay by sailing vessel to Baltimore, and then to Norfolk, where we stayed some days with Mr. Littleton Waller Tazewell, and from there by schooner to Hampton, and then by stage to Richmond. "Mr. Randolph met us in Richmond, and took my brother and self to Prince 

Edward county to the school of Mr. William Lacy, where we remained for eighteen months. In John Randolph Clay we found a companion at the school who Mr. Randolph had undertaken to educate, and who lived at Roanoke when not at school, as we did. Clay was the son of Joseph Clay, of Philadelphia, a political friend of Mr. Randolph, who continued to live with Mr. Randolph for many years. "Sometime in 1817 my brother, Clay and myself were removed to Halifax county, to the school of Abner W. Killpatrick. There we remained until the winter of 1819-1820, when my dear mother requested the return home of my brother and self. Mr. Randolph took us then to Washington, and from there we went by way of Baltimore to Rose Hill to see our grandfather and excellent aunt. While staying at Rose Hill our good aunt took my brother and self to visit our relations at Chester Farm, where I found Aunt Chambers (General Forman's sister) and her son, General Ezekiel Chambers, and other relations, as the Weeks and Ecclestons. 

We also visited Hope, the home of Mr. Robert Tilghman, who had married Henrietta Forman (General Forman's niece). There also I saw Aunt Augusta Forman, who after my mother's marriage had accompanyed her to Wilmington Island, stayed with her until after my birth, and having nursed me in my infancy, ever after called me her son. We corresponded in after life, and I had no relation more beloved. "Hope was a beautiful place on Miles river in Talbot county, and has passed away from the family. "We met Mr. Randolph in Baltimore in April, and from there went by steamer to Norfolk and to City Point and Petersburg. He then provided a light carriage, and old Quasher, a wagoner, to drive us to Georgia. There I met my mother and sisters and little brother Joseph after a separation of more than four years. "My mother's home was on the Island during the year 1820, and we had there an Irishman for teacher, Mr. Hessian. "In 1821, we lived in Savannah, and the last of the year I was sent to school near Sparta, in Hancock county, which was kept by the Rev. N. S. Beman, an eminent Presbyterian preacher and teacher. There I remained until the summer of 

1822, when I returned home, and soon thereafter went to New Haven, Connecticut, studied for a time at Ripton, preparing to enter Yale College, which I did in the Fall, say, October. There I stayed until the Fall of 1823, when I determined to apply for a warrant in the Navy, which was readily obtained for me by Col. 

Ed. F. Tattnall, the older brother of Captain Josiah Tattnall, who afterwards commanded the MERRIMACK in the Confederate service. (The home of the 

Tattnalls was on Whitemarsh Island by Wilmington Island.

"Mr. Randolph continued to write to me occasionally after we separated in 

1820, but unfortunately the letters from him which I received for between two and three years have been lost. "In the Fall of 1823 I returned home to Georgia and soon received orders to report to Commander Hull on board the frigate UNITED STATES destined to a cruise to the Pacific ocean, and then lying at Norfolk. "When I got to Norfolk the frigate had sailed, and I reported to Commander Sinclair, who gave me orders to the PEACOCK, sloop of war, then fitting out to follow the UNITED STATES to the Pacific. 

"After some disasters under the management of Captain William Carter, the PEACOCK reached the Pacific, and I was transferred to the UNITED 

STATES. 

"While I was attached to the frigate the important Carthes at Callao were blockaded by a Chilean Squadron, they being in the possession of the Spanish Royalists. This blockade lasted for two years after the battle of Ayacucho, in which the Spanish viceroy was defeated and all hope of recovering the Province for Spain ended. "General Simon Bolivar was invited to breakfast on board by Commander Hull, and all our officers were introduced to him. Lima was in possession of the 

Patriots for two years before General Rodil, who commanded the Carthes, surrendered, and the last possession of Spain in South America was lost. "I was enabled, through Commander Hull's kindness, to travel in the interior and crossed the Andes at three different passes, visiting the town of Tarma, the great silver mines of Cerro de Pasco, and Bella Vista. I witnessed some naval engagements between Patriot and Spanish men of war, which were not very decisive or worth recounting. "Early in 1826 I received letters informing me of my dear mother's death (died Dec. 16, 1825) and, having leave of absence from Commander Hull, returned to the United States in a merchant brig after a very tedious passage. 

A kind Providence separated me at that juncture from most dissipated association. 

I was the only passenger, and the society of Captain Center, a worthy old sea captain, was a blessed change. “After a visit to my grandfather at Rose Hill, I went to Savannah to see my relations, and during the summer received orders to attend as witness the court martial then sitting in Philadelphia to try the case of Captain William Carter for misconduct on board the PEACOCK. Carter was broke. "I returned to Georgia by way of Roanoke, where I stayed sometime with 

Mr. Randolph, and from there visited Fredericksburg. "Received orders to the DELAWARE line-of-battle ship, 74 guns, bound on a cruise to the Mediterranean. The ship was being fitted out at Norfolk, Downs captain. In January, 1828, sailed for Gibraltar, and from there to Minorca, and received order to take our passenger, Prince Charles Lucien Bonaparte and wife, to Leghorn. "Passed some eighteen months crusing in the Mediterranean, and then returned by way of Gibraltar home to be examined for promotion." 

To this narrative, Mr. Bryan in February, 1882, added through Rev. C. 

Braxton Bryan a brief account of the next great event in his life, which was written, verbatim, as follows: "I went to Roanoke in 1827 on my way to Georgia. While there Mr. Randolph spoke to me of your mother.

"I went to Georgia; went to Grahamville and Savannah, where there was yellow fever, and to the Island (Wilmington). And while I was in Savannah I was ordered to the ONTARIO, and went on to New York in the fall of 1827, and it was very curious, she had sailed before I could get to her, I think the day before I reached New York. So I went to Washington, and with a letter from Judge 

Chambers to the Secretary of Navy, got my leave of absence extended. I then received a letter from Mr. Randolph, inclosing the note of introduction to Elizabeth, and went to see her at Ellwood, and was there only two or three days. "Soon after received orders to the DELAWARE, and went to Norfolk where she was lying; then got leave of absence again and went to Fredericksburg and boarded there awhile and saw Elizabeth, daughter of John Coalter and Frances 

Bland Tucker, of "Chatham". Her good sense led her to see that the affair was too hurried, and she discouraged me. So I went back to the DELAWARE and sailed for the Mediterranean, and stayed there from the first of 1828 to the middle of 1829, and then returned to the United States, and renewed my visits to Chatham; and well received, and after a time the engagement was made. "I then had to go to New York to study for my examinations as passed midshipman, and after passing the examinations I had leave of absence, during which, in January, 1830, we were married." 

To the grandchildren and great grandchildren of John R. Bryan this Diary is of especial value as portraying the vividness of the impressions he received, no less than his marked power of expression. 

For the reader with no interest in the personality of the author the account is none the less most interesting, for it describes with the utmost simplicity the ravages of smallpox, and the appearance of deadly hospital gangrene on shipboard. It is difficult enough to fight these enemies with the aid of antiseptics, light, vaccination and germicides, but in an ill-ventilated sailing ship, lit only with whale oil, with ignorance of the presence as well as of the control of germs, the fight was against the unseen powers of darkness. 

Then, too, young Bryan saw the fall of the last Spanish stronghold; he met 

Bolivar and Lord Cochrane, the great English Admiral. He crossed the Andes, and in so doing reached the conclusion that the difficulty he experienced in breathing at high altitudes was due to the presence of antimony in the mines. His trip to the Andes was a difficult and strenuous undertaking, and one by no means devoid of serious danger.

To read his comments on the poverty of Sicily and the presence of the plague in the Mediterranean ports is to recapture immediately the picture that was universal for centuries in that part of the world. 

In 1828 he visited Tripoli, where twenty-four years before Decatur had gained his captain's commission by leading an expedition into the harbor to burn the U. S. Frigate “Philadelphia.” It was against the corsairs from Tripoli, Tunis and Algiers that Decatur acted with great success for several years after 1815. 

Prince Charles Lucien Bonaparte and his wife were of the ship's company in 1828 on the Delaware from America to Italy and Waterloo, was ten years nearer to the contemporary world then than the Armistice is to us in 1941.

On returning from his wedding trip John Randolph Bryan and his bride settled down at "Eagle Point," in Gloucester county, Virginia. There he lived until forced by the War Between the States to abandon his Tidewater home and go to "Carysbrook" in Fluvanna county, which he purchased in 1845. He lived there until it was sold in 1879. For the rest of his life Mr. Bryan lived with his children.

John Randolph Bryan was a person of extraordinary energy and strength, and though he was subject to outbursts of temper, nothing could obscure his charm and courage. Mrs. Martha Page Vandergrift, of Gloucester county, said of him on her 101st birthday: "The younger generation has a wholly wrong idea of Mr. Bryan. He was brave, kindly, generous, a good neighbor, and by all odds the most interesting conversationalist I ever met.' دو The war lost him "Eagle Point" and his plantation in Louisiana, and farming for him became an impossible means of livelihood; but nothing dimmed his personality or weakened his courage, and he remained thunderous and unpredictable to the end.

John Randolph Bryan died September 13, 1887, and was buried by the side of his wife on Pine Island, the “Eagle Point" burying ground. On his tombstone is the following inscription, written by his sons, Joseph and Braxton:

HERE LIES JOHN RANDOLPH BRYAN BORN ON WILMINGTON ISLAND, GEORGIA 

MARCH 23, 1806

EDUCATED UNDER JOHN RANDOLPH OF ROANOKE MIDSHIPMAN IN U. S. NAVY 1823-1831 MARRIED ELIZABETH TUCKER COALTER JANUARY 30TH, 1830 RESIDED HERE 1831-1862 DIED AT THE UNIVERSITY OF VIRGINIA SEPTEMBER 13TH, 1887. 

STRONG IN BODY, MIND AND CONVICTIONS: 

INFLEXIBLE IN INTEGRITY: 

A PATRIOT IN PEACE AND WAR: 

A FRIEND OF THE POOR. 

HE EARLY JOINED THE CHURCH AT ABINGDON, AND WAS FOR FIFTY YEARS AN EARNEST FOLLOWER OF CHRIST AND FOR ALL HIS MERCIES BY SEA AND LAND HE BLESSED AND PRAISED GOD TO HIS LIFE'S END.

Transcript of the record of John Randolph Bryan, furnished by the Navy Department, U. S. A. 

15 November 1938. 

John Randolph Bryan was born on Wilmington Island, Georgia, on March 23, 1806. On October 4, 1823, he was appointed an Acting Midshipman in the 

U. S. Navy, the appointment being sent to Savannah, Georgia. On November 4, he accepted the appointment, writing from New York, but requesting that orders be sent him at Savannah. On the same day (November 4) he was ordered to proceed to Norfolk and report to Captain Isaac Hull for duty on the U. S. Frigate 

UNITED STATES. 

On December 19, Midshipman Bryan wrote the Department that the orders addressed to him in care of Colonel Tatnall had been forwarded to New York and never received by him and that duplicates of the orders had been received too late for him to join the UNITED STATES. The following day he wrote the 

Department that having been advised that the U. S. S. PEACOCK was not to sail in company with the UNITED STATES for the Pacific, he was hastening to Nor folk to join the PEACOCK. 

The rolls of the PEACOCK show that he came on board under orders of November 4th, and was carried on board that vessel as a supernumerary for wages, etc., until August 22, 1824, when detached to the Frigate UNITED 

STATES. The PEACOCK sailed for the Pacific early in 1824. On April 1, off the Bermudas the vessel was struck by lightning which killed four men and injured others. She stopped at Rio de Janeiro in May and arrived at Valparaiso on July 15, having had a tempestuous passage around the Horn and having lost eight men from smallpox.

The PEACOCK joined the UNITED STATES at Callao, Peru late in August, and Midshipman Bryan went on board the latter vessel on August 23, 1824. 

The revolutions taking place in South American countries at this time called for the presence of American men-of-war for the protection of American lives and property, and the UNITED STATES, Flagship of Commodore Isaac Hull in the Pacific, remained for the most part close to the coasts of Peru and Chile. 

On August 29, 1826, Midshipman Bryan was granted permission by Commodore Hull to return to the United States at his own expense, his mother having recently died and family affairs urgently demanding his attention. He apparently II

left Callao about September 10, 1826, but was carried on the rolls of the UNITED 

STATES until January 22, 1827.

Bryan reported his arrival in Washington City from the Pacific on January 

10, 1827, and requested six months' leave, which was granted him on January 12.

His address at this time was Savannah, Georgia. 

On July 24, 1827, Midshipman Bryan was ordered to proceed from Savannah, Ga., to New York City and report to Commodore Isaac Chauncey for duty on the U. S. S. ERIE, but for some reason did not comply with these orders. 

Instead, on September 18, he was addressed at Washington and ordered to proceed to Norfolk and report to Commodore James Barron for duty on the U. S. S. 

DELAWARE, 74. He is shown by the rolls of that vessel as having come on board 

October 1, 1827 at Norfolk. On October 24 he was ordered to Philadelphia as witness for a court martial, and on December 18 he was granted two weeks leave of absence. On December 28, at Fredericksburg, Va., he was given permission to join the DELAWARE on her arrival at Annapolis. 

The DELAWARE, Captain John Downes, sailed from Norfolk on February 20, 1828, for the Mediterranean, where she served as flagship of Commodore 

William M. Crane.

Midshipman Bryan was detached from the DELAWARE at Mahon on February 17, 1829 to return to the United States. On May 29, he was addressed at Cecilton, Md., and granted leave until further orders. On September 7, at 

Washington, he was ordered to go to New York and report to Commodore, Chauncey for duty at the Naval School there. The following November 4, he was notified of the examination for Midshipmen to take place in January, 1830. 

During 1830 he was apparently waiting orders, residing during that time in 

Fredericksburg, Va.

He was ordered to Washington to report to Commodore Hull for duty as Acting Sailing Master on board the Frigate POTOMAC on May 26, 1831, but on June 2 wrote from Gloucester Court House, Va., stating that it was impossible for him to comply with the orders to the POTOMAC and requesting a year's furlough. On June 11 the Department revoked previous orders and granted him three months' leave.

On July 19, 1831 a warrant as Passed Midshipman was forwarded to John 

Randolph Bryan at Fredericksburg, Va., the warrant to take rank from March 

23, 1829. 

Passed Midshipman Bryan wrote from Fredericksburg on September 1, 1831, stating that inasmuch as his furlough was about to expire and as his personal affairs would not permit his going to sea at that time, he was forced to tender his resignation from the naval service. The resignation was accepted by the Secretary of the Navy on September 5, 1831. 12

Norfolk, Sunday, My dear Madam: 

March 26, 1824

In passing Old Point Comfort yesterday I boarded the PEACOCK, but your son had been sent to the fort in the boat, and while I was waiting for his return Captain Carter briefly informed me of (what I had never heard a syllable of before) his late rencontre with midshipman Doyle.

Randolph acted rashly but honorably, and Capt. C. has had them separated, D. being sent to the W. India station. R. was quarantined for five weeks, and on this enquiring of Capt. C., "Why he was not permitted to go ashore," he was answered: "That he had disobeyed the regulations of the Ship in going ashore without leave or order."

Captain C. mentioned nothing of the duel to R. who had shot off the butt of his adversary's pistol, and himself escaped unhurt. Mr. Doyle's right hand was severely wounded.

Capt. C. is a particular acquaintance of mine; he spoke in the handsomest terms of yr. son, who he said required only severe discipline to become a valuable officer.

Before my furlough had expired Randolph came on board and I had the gratification to shake him by the hand. He was much affected by the interview. 

But a few words could pass between us. The Frigate was under way, and I returned to the Steam boat while she bore down for the Capes and went to sea. 

I've hardly time to write this note. 

Sincerely yours, J. R. of R. 

Mrs. Bryan.

He is much grown and improved in person since 1822. 

You perceive that R. makes no mention of his trip to North Carolina with

Doyle, in the letter which I sent you. I am on my way to Richmond to see after 

47 hogsheads of my Tob. that have been lost, but shall return in the next boat to 

Washington. The PEACOCK will touch at Rio.

Commencement of a Series of Private Remarks on Board the U.S. Sloop of War, “Peacock," William Carter, Esq., Commander, by J. R. Bryan, March 26, 1824

On the 4th of November, 1823, I received order from the Navy department of the United States to report myself to Commander Hull on board the U. S. 

Frigate lying at Norfolk. I proceded on imediately from Savannah where I had received my orders to Norfolk, and on coming discovered that she had sailed three hours previous to my arrival.

On application I received orders from Capt. Sinclair to join the Peacock, then fitting out at that place, we did not receive sailing order's until the 20th of March, when we dropt down to Crany Island intending with the first fair wind to proceed to the Pacific ocean, the destination of the United States. From Crany

May 9th

Early this morning we had a complete view of the coast of Brazil, breaking out upon us with the morning's light; shewing a bold rugged coast interspersed with peaks and elevated hills. At 12 M. we had a full view of the entrance of the harbour of Rio, and the most romantic scenery I ever beheld burst upon our view. 

The country seems everywhere to be interspersed with peaks of rocks assuming different shapes; on your left as you enter there is a collection of rocks called Lord 

Hood's Nose, bearing the exact bust of a man reclining on his back. Half way between the entrance of the harbour and the city of Rio is the Sugar Loaf, a high peak the hight of which I could never exactly obtain. There are several marvellous stories told of this peak, but none sufficiently credible that I should insert them here. Nearly opposite the Sugar Loaf, over on your right, is a well fortified fort which, after the custom of Portuguese and Spanish towns, you are not allowed to pass before sunrise or after sunset. It being late we had not an opportunity of having a view of the town coming up the harbour.

Callao, August, 1824

On the 22nd, having previously reported myself to Commodore Hull, I came aboard the United States. She has been laying here 4 months and will most probably be detained some time longer, the unsettled state of Peru at present, absolutely demand's the presence of one of our men of war here constantly. On the slightest plea American property is condemned, and nothing but the most rigid enforcement of our priviledges will secure our merchants. Admiral 

Guy's* blockading squadron had left this place before our arrival, but is expected daily. Bolivar is on the road to Lima and we constantly expect to hear of the arrival of his army in that place. He is a very determined man and a good general and although he will find little difficulty in taking Lima, still his prospect of Callao is small, it is one of the best fortified cities in South America, and Rodil the commander is determined to stand out to the last extremity. 

September 1st

Admiral Guye's appeared of the harbour on the 28th and has again commenced blockading the Port. Yesterday we heard that Bolivar had obtained a signal victory over the Spanish General. September 25th

Nothing worthy of relating has occurred for the last 8 or 10 day's. Guye's, who formerly kept the harbour alive, has left here, and we have the prospect of lying here for 6 month's. October 8th 

At 7 a.m. the Spanish fleet made their appearance under sail from under cover of the guns of the Castle's, (the Patriot fleet having previously got under way and stood out to sea) and followed them in a close body. We also weighed anchor and followed by the Cambridge, Tartar and Mersey, the former an English ship of the line, the two latter frigates, stood out to sea, following two or three miles astern of the Spanish fleet. 

Admiral Guye's squadron consisted of his frigate the Macedonian and Rapido, he had received a reinforcement of three sail from the Columbian squadron, consisting of a sloop of war and two gun brig's. 

On the side of the Spaniard's were the Asia, a ship of the line, the Achilles, a 20-gun brig, with the Constante de Persuella brig and the Esther, a 28-gun ship.

The decided superiority of force on the side of the Spaniard's would have made it madness in Guyes to have engaged them particularly in the close manner in which they kept together, he therefore relied on scattering them, determined if he succeeded to fight the Asia with his frigate, but to perform that he was unable, for they did not separate the whole time they were out. 

There being a very heavy fog all the morning we did not see the two fleet's for sometime after we were out, but hearing a heavy cannonading concluded they were engaged, it seems as we afterwards heard that Guyes frigate got entangled in the fog with the Spanish fleet, and exchanged a broadside with the Asia. When the fog cleared up, Guyes with his squadron was a long shot of the Spaniards to windward, in which situation they continued occasionally firing on each other, keeping up a kind of running fight, thus they continued until 3 p.m., when the Spanish Admiral tacked and stood back into the harbour.

Diary page 34 Huacho

At 3 a.m. on the 21st we came to anchor in Huacha harbour, an agreable little place where fresh provisions, vegetables, etc. may easily be procured. 

During our stay we were treated with the greatest hospitality by the inhabitant's. Understanding that there were many curiosities to be discovered in some old Indian burying places in the vicinity of the town, a party of us having procured spades from the ship and an Indian guide, proceded to the place, where we saw many remains evincing that the place had frequently been dug about in different places. On diging to the depth of two or three feet, we discovered the bodies, very frequently, all lying with their head's to the East; they were completely enveloped with cloth which being taken off they had cotton or wool next to their skin; there bodies were in a complete state of preservation, with no offensive smell whatever, although the flesh was completely decayed and the skin like dried parchment on their bones. The cloth was in an astonishing state of preservation, and some fishing lines that were found in the graves in some parts quite strong. It seemed as if all their household utensils were bured with their bodies. We found various kinds of earthen pot's, called by the natives wakeros, which it seem's they used for purposes of devotion. In these Wakeros we found several ears of corn in as good preservation as if they were of the last year's crop.

On the 24th we left Huacho for the purpose of returning to Callao, and on the 27th came to anchor in the Bay of Callao. The Peacock had arrived five days previous to us from Chilea, she sails again in a day or two to Pisco. 

The Spanish squadron are a short distance to windward. Guye's did not venture a regular engagement but it seem's a few long shots were exchanged.

Lima commences to recover the interesting character, which this town has always borne and although the ravages of war are visible in every street, still there are a thousand novelties in it which must always interest a stranger. The inquisition, the Palace of the Viceroy, in which they show the room in which Pizarro was assassinated, the celebrated bridge over the Rimae and many other remarkable building's and places which most always interest the curious traveller. Even the Pantheon, a large building two miles from Lima where all who die in Lima are interred in a large wall in narrow receptacl's in which the bodies are placed and bricked up until a certain period when they are taken out and thrown in a large vault. All are entered in the same manner, although the holes for the Rich are superior to those for the poor, and their bodies are suffered to remain longer for their money, in this narrow receptacle.

August 26th, 1825

In crossing the Andes we experienced an extraordinary difficulty of breathing, as if we felt an unnatural pressure of the air, this is attributed in a great measure to the antimony in the silver mines which are very common in all parts of the Andes. Our beasts were so much affected as not to be able to go but a short distance without stopping to recover their breath, and it is common for them to fall dead in some of the passes of the mountains. In the course of the day's journey we passed a number of old deserted mines, many of which I understood to have been formerly worked to great advantage.

Catania, July 4th, 1828

We next visited the Benedictine convent, considered one of the wealthiest and most extensive of any on the island. 

We were received with great attention by some of the monks, and conducted up a magnificent marble stairway, through a long gallery containing many painting's on scriptural subjects into a range of room's richly furnished, in one of which we found assembled all the superiors of the convent, by whom we were entertained with great civility, and conducted through some very long gallery's adorned with paintings to their Chapel, where we were entertained by music from a very fine organ, which the priests asserted to be the third in excellence of tones in Europe.

The interior of the Chapel combine's as much elegance and simplicity in its ornaments as it is possible, and posses none of the gaudy gildings and finery of common Catholic churches. We visited a very good museum atached to the convent, but soon were compelled to take our leave of these good Fathers, who appeared to take the greatest pleasure in showing us everything curious, among which was the circumstance of the lava, in the great eruption of Etna, which destroyed nearly the whole town, having stopped at the foot of the Convent, and respected its sacred walls, while it pursued its devastating career all around it. 

We next visited the Museum of the Prince of Biscaria, which is said to contain one of the finest collection of antique's in Europe. We had only reason to regret the short space of time we could afford to the examination of thing's, to which we should like to have devoted day's, remains of sculpture, with some status in fine preservation, antique vases, armour, and arms, of every kind, and from every nation, affording specimens of the earliest attempts at gun making and the full equipments of ancient knights, etc., etc.

Remarks on board the U. S. Ship Delaware 

Port Mahon, January, 1829

On the 25th received directions from Capt. Downes to prepare to return to the United States, he having received directions from the Secretary of the Navy to allow all midshipmen of the date of 1823 to return to the U. S. to undergo their examinations. 

Owing to the ill feeling which exists between sailors of different nations, and that having been fostered to a great degree in this place, numerous affrays, have taken place between the Dutch, French and American sailors whom their respective commanders have permitted to go ashore on liberty, and whenever they have met each other have invariably commenced fights which have generally ended in bloodshed. Last winter a party of men from the Java, and a 

Dutch frigate meeting on shore, three Dutch sailors and one American were killed, and several other afray's have taken place which have ended equally fatally. These circumstances have prevented Com. Crane from ever allowing any men from the Delaware to go ashore on liberty. Notwithstanding, the Java's crew have several times since their arrival been permitted to go on shore, and several French men of War being at anchor in the harbour, their sailors meeting the Americans on shore has generally resulted in a fight, which on the night of the 14th came to a most serious issue. It appears that a party of the Javas crew met a number of French men from a brig of war lying at Georgetown, and according to custom commenced fighting, (and although most of the Frenchmen were armed with cutlasses, which their officers had permitted them to bring on shore,) the Americans with their slingshot were getting the better of them, when the first Lieut. of the brig and a party of men came up the former with a drawn small sword, and cried out to the men to kill the Americans, and took an active part in the fight. The result of the affair was that the officer was killed with slingshot by our men, an American sailor was run through the body with a small sword (who died the next day). The French men were so much incensed at the death of their officer that armed with pistols they proceded into the town and swore to kill the first American officer they met. A party of officers were that evening at a public ball in the city, and among them a French midshipman who was shot dead near the door of the ballroom (in the street) by mistake for an American officer. The death of this officer was highly mortifying to the 

French and he was buried in secret. On the evening of the 15th the French Lieut. was followed to his grave by all the officers of the American men of war. 

Two sailors pointed out by some police officers of the place as the men who killed the officer were delivered up by Capt. Downes to the authorities of the place, and before any farther notice was taken of the affair we sailed in the Warren.
