Author's Note 

Tax practice is concerned primarily with the conduct of controverted matters. The number of such controversies will be realized when we consider that although most tax cases are settled short of trial, the reports of litigated tax cases fill nearly 100 large volumes. While enslaved peoples may pay their taxes on demand, the day when the taxes to be paid by a free people will be determined without dispute will probably never come. 

In matters of procedure in tax matters there is a large measure of unanimity of views among practitioners. On the other hand, the background, temperament and experience of individual attorneys may cause a difference of views as to the tactics to be employed in a particular situation. The reader is therefore warned that none of those who have read this article in manuscript have agreed with all of the views expressed by the author. The reader is therefore free to disagree with the author whenever and wherever he pleases. In the last analysis, he must use his own judgment and depend upon his own ability, tact, and courage in each tax case, just as attorneys do in controverted matters in other fields of practice. 

The preparation of this article has been possible only because of the collaboration of Margaret F. Luers of the District of Columbia 

Bar. But for her modesty she would have been designated as coauthor.

Introduction 

No matter who you are, you are in business with Uncle Sam. 

He owns part of your pay check. He is your partner, your creditor, and your principal heir. In addition, he has the first call on corporate incomes, and in many instances, takes as much as 80%, thereof, collecting in cash, and leaving the corporation with the inventories and accounts.

Everyone has to make an accounting to Uncle Sam at least once a year. And, in the end, our executors have to divide our savings with him, if they amount to anything substantial. 

Uncle Sam is a relentless collector, and he will not be shortchanged. Dealing with Uncle Sam is an intricate and technical matter, calling for judgment and skill. 

The practice of dealing with Uncle Sam, heretofore, has been shared by the accountants and lawyers. For some time the 615

accountants dominated this field. Most lawyers did not seem to realize how important tax work really was. Many lawyers seemed unwilling to engage in the study necessary to qualify themselves to do intelligent tax work. The decided cases bristle with costly errors. Occasionally they shine with the brilliance of inspirations which saved large amounts for clients. A plodding, painstaking tax practitioner has proven himself to be a man of genius in more than one great tax case; while, on the other hand, mighty lawyers have met overwhelming defeat by taking the wrong turn at an obscure procedural crossroad.

Federal tax practice may be said to be the art of dealing with the federal taxing authorities. It may be divided into two great fields keeping out of trouble, and getting out of trouble. 

The former deals with current and future tax returns, the latter with past ones. As to the former, great knowledge, a foresight bordering on clairvoyance, and infinite skill are needed. Considering the matter of getting out of trouble, we shall follow an hypothetical tax case down through the years.¹

Tax Practice to Be Studied Through a Typical Case 

We shall consider the more or less current returns of a typical taxpayer, which we shall call the Ekselsior Hair Straightener Company. A series of returns for successive tax years will be discussed in connection with our hypothetical case, since, in actual practice, a tax return for the current year must reconcile with returns previously filed; and the handling of the current case must be conducted in the light of the manner in which the past settlements were made.

We shall consider: 

1. The Ekselsior Hair Straightener Company's returns for several past years, with a dispute arising as to one of them and continuing until settlement, and 

2. The Ekselsior Hair Straightener Company's returns for current years.

Make full disclosure. The best and safest way to handle any tax case is to make full disclosure of all the pertinent facts at the earliest opportunity. Such disclosure may help your client. 

Concealment will certainly hurt him. The safest view of any tax case is that, if you cannot win on all the facts, you had better settle, whatever the odds. The above rule would not apply in a fraud case where your client wishes to rely on his constitutional rights; but experience has shown that even in such a case withholding information is generally futile. 

Never waive a right. Never is a strong word. It always has to be qualified. If any move in a tax case requires that you waive a right in order to obtain an advantage, it might be wise to bargain away this right in consideration of new rights. But, an unreasoned waiver of a right is a very unwise act in a tax case. Since this proposition will develop in the practice case, no more need be said about it at this point. 

Consider qualifying all papers. The signing of broadly drawn papers to achieve a limited objective is sometimes fatal in tax cases. If you want an extension of a week's time, sign for a week's time-do not sign an unlimited waiver, or even a waiver for one year. If you are willing to sign an agreement waiving the right to go to the Tax Court of the United States, do not sign a waiver of the statute of limitations, or a waiver of the right to file a claim and recover the money, should it develop that it was not owed. Be careful not to sign inadvertently a waiver of one right thinking you are waiving another. The cases hereafter considered discuss this point in detail.³

Consider straddling all questions. You usually can go all the way through a tax case without taking a definite position to the exclusion of all others. For example, obsolescence, loss of useful value, and depreciation are almost, but not entirely, synonymous; so claim under all three. It

In the Land Title case, there was a loss, and a big one. was claimed as "obsolescence", and disallowed. Had it been claimed as "abandonment", or as a "loss sustained during the year", it might have been allowed. And yet, these terms are nearly synonymous.

As another example, assume it is discovered that a discharged bookkeeper "borrowed" $2,000 early this year. You try to get it back from him, but he has lost it on the races, and is down and out. 

When you claim the loss, straddle the question and claim it as a bad debt, as a larceny, as an embezzlement, and as a loss incurred I in business and not compensated for by insurance or otherwise. 

Prove the facts, and assert each of these grounds for deducting the loss, for one of these is tenable. After all, you did sustain a loss; and it ought to be allowed you. Your right to take the loss should not be dependent upon your ability to guess under which shiny shell the little pea reposes-but it does. 

Keep your client away from the conference. 

Your client may be able and likeable; but as a tax man he is no match for a government agent. If you want your client's case to have an even chance, deal with the government man yourself, or through tax counsel.

Know all the facts. It is not enough in a tax deficiency involving, say, the depreciation allowance, to build up an impregnable case on that point. You must know all the facts in all the transactions of the entire tax year. The tax case you are handling covers all the endeavors of an entire year or more. 

Although only one or two items are now in dispute, a careful investigation may disclose that a refund is due your client, even though he loses on the questions which were the basis of the deficiency. This refund may be the result of the correction of some error, or the claiming of some advantage in no way connected with the items under dispute.

On the other hand, facts may be lurking in the case which will create a huge deficiency in taxes on items not yet dug into by the government.

In either event, it is your duty to know all the facts of the case. If you fail to discover and claim a favorable adjustment, or if you needlessly run the risk of an unfavorable one, you have not served your client well. 

This should be self-evident. 

Know all the law of your case.

Yet, many cases have failed because the parties did not know the law-did not even recognize the legal problem involved. Several have gone to the Supreme Court. Later, when better presented cases came along, and the Court was asked to follow the prior decision, it dismissed the matter by pointing out that the real legal problem was not presented in the prior case. 

Awkward items always come to light. Awkward facts somehow never fail to come out-and at the most embarrassing moment. In many instances, when a file is handed to an attorney, and he studies it, he finds that there are some more or less embarrassing items in the accounts, such as, for example, the payment of a high salary to the proprietor's wife. The wisest practice is to disclose all the pertinent facts early in the negotiations. 

Some General Observations 

Let us next consider some of the truths which time and practice have brought out.

A great many tax cases can be decided either way, with small chance of reversal. Many cases turn on the paper work, and not on the actualities; and many turn on new matter which was not mentioned before the controversy arose. 

We will not discuss these three propositions at length, because you will soon see, in practice, how true they are.

Consider first the idea that many tax cases can be decided either way without risk of reversal. Assume that the questions to be decided are bad debts, depreciation of 5% claimed, and 4% allowed, and inventory value. No matter what the original court allows on the trial, the Circuit Courts rarely, if ever, will alter it. More complex cases are even harder to reverse.

But the most intriguing of these truisms is the one that many cases turn on new matter introduced by the tax counsel, or the government after the controversy arose. The inventory case was lost, the invested capital case had to be compromised at about half; but the patent exhaustion allowance turned a $750,000 deficiency into a two million dollar refund. 

Soon we will be considering an income tax case. It might be well to consider briefly some aspects of the income tax before we set to work.

A Suggested Analysis of The Income Tax 

The income tax deals with income; and income, in the final analysis, is gain. Although the income tax law defines income, taxable income under that definition may be equal to, greater, or less than the actual gain. And the Courts have sustained the theory that the right to levy the income tax extends to the gross income, which is certainly far more than the gain; and they have ruled that deductions for the costs of doing business are gifts or bounties from Congress. Notwithstanding the power in Congress to tax gross income, the taxing statute generally taxes the true gain. 

However, various matters of policy have arisen to cause Congress to excuse from the tax, for the present, certain gains, and to suspend the tax thereon until a more propitious future dayusually the day when gain has been translated into money. An example of this is found in the treatment of the gain on exchanges of similar kinds of investment property.8 Another is the so-called tax-free reorganization."

In the approach to an income tax case the following technique is suggested. It may seem childish, but it works. Every tax case involves the result of the endeavors of a period of time, usually a year. These results generally are made up of hundreds of items of intake, less hundreds of items of outgo. Most tax cases consist of disputes about the tax status of some of these items. Ask yourself a series of simple questions about each item of intake or receipt. Is it income? Whose income is it? 

When was it income? Apply the same examination to deductions. 

Is this a deduction? Who is entitled to this deduction? When was it a deduction? 

You will be surprised at the avenues these tests open up to you. Perhaps it is as well to glance down one or two of these long corridors in order to get an idea of the situation. A lawyer did a fine job for his aged client. The client complained about the bill for services, paid him only a niggardly fee, and exacted a release. The same lawyer earned another fee; and there was delay in collecting. He assigned his claim of $3,000 to a creditor in release of a debt of $800. The client paid the next day. Is the $800 income? Whose? When? Is the $3,000 income? Whose?

If the assignment had not been for value, the $3,000 would have been income to the lawyer, regardless of the fact that the assignee received most of the money. 

Again, this lawyer took an option on a piece of property, thinking to turn it over quickly at a profit. Later, he assigned the option to his son as a gift. Then the son sold the option at a fine profit. Is the profit income? Whose? When?

My investigation of the Eureka Hair Straightener Company convinced me that these people are very little, if at all, short of being swindlers. It seems that they then bought a few shares of stock, about $50,000 worth, I understand, at $110. The next day they offered it at $80; and by continuing this process, they contrived to get 50% control of the company for about $50,000, so that, in addition to selling out to this company for $2,000,000, they made about another million dollars buying it back.

Eureka's stenographer for making her bald. I saw her myself and she is not bald. I think this is just a way Eureka had of handing this girl $30,000, and making the government pay for it by means of a tax deduction.

Memorandum re Discovery and Reporting of Errors in Previous Returns 

(Dated June 12, 1941, in Handwriting of Homer Patrick)

Studying accumulated file; much disturbed by method of handling federal tax payments. Ekselsior Company made money in 

1938 and 1939. Must have owed federal income taxes for those years. 1938 tax would be payable in 1939, 1939 in 1940. The 1939 accounts do not show 1938 federal tax; the reconciliation of 1940 accounts does not show 1939 tax. Either the company made no returns for 1938 or 1939, or didn't pay any federal tax for 1938 or 1939; or the federal taxes for 1938 and 1939 were charged to expense. Charlie Penn said 100 to 1 federal taxes were charged to expense. 

Later, I found that Charlie Penn was right. Ekselsior's cash book shows $14,959, 1938 federal tax paid in 1939, and charged to expense in 1939. Cash book shows $5,900, 1939 federal tax paid in 

1940, and charged to expense.

On advice of Charlie Penn, took the figures I have and got an old 1938 tax return blank and figured the 1938 tax. Statements showed capital stock tax of $3,000 paid. This indicates $300,000 declared value. Using $70,000 income and $300,000 declared value, the 1938 tax would be $14,950. This checks with figures shown by cash book. 

Got a 1939 blank, and took 1939 income of $35,000 shown by

647 statement, and corrected to include $14,959 federal 1938 tax charged to expense in 1939 through error. This shows a new 1939 income of $49,959. Checking capital stock tax paid, find declared value was $500,000. Computing 1939 tax on these figures, find same should have been $8,742.21. Cash books show $5,900 was paid. 

Tried to reproduce $5,900 figure, and arrived at it easily. $5,900 would be federal tax on $35,000 income shown on 1939 statement. 

I therefore concluded Ekselsior Company owes further 1939 federal tax of $2,842.21. Will report error to government, and advise 

Ekselsior Company to pay additional tax. Charlie Penn says don't be too quick about this; there may be some offsetting items. He suggests a complete re-audit of 1939. But, he says to be sure to disclose the error before a revenue agent makes an examination. 

It might be too late then.

It appears that 1940 income reported to me on work sheets is understated by $5,900 because of 1939 tax charged to expense in 1940. If work sheet as given to me is otherwise correct, this would increase 1940 income from $90,000 to $95,900. Have decided to make 1940 return using corrected 1938 and 1939 figures.

This letter is attached to the income and excess profits tax returns of the  of Teaneck, New Jersey, for the calendar year 1940.

An earnest effort has been made to make the returns proper in every respect; but it is requested that you send your agents at the earliest opportunity to check over the books and accounts of this taxpayer, and verify this return. The company will cooperate with you to this end.

This corporation had one or two transactions which the officers feel should be called to your attention, not because they think the company has erred, but because they think it is their duty to give the government notice of all transactions: (1) This company wished to obtain government work in connection with the defense program, and having been unsuccessful in so doing, it employed a business man of , to look after its interests. As a result, this company was awarded certain government contracts, and received a bill from this man for $100,000, and paid him. This was a fee for services actually rendered. This man did not engage in lobbying for this company; nor did he influence legislation in any manner. (2) This company increased its sales from $400,000 in 1939 and $380,000 in 1938 to $700,000 in 1940. Officers' compensation was $5,000 in 1938, $30,000 in 1939, and $100,000 in 1940. These salaries happen to be in direct proportion to stock ownership. But the attention of the government is respectfully invited to the fact that the increases in salaries are pursuant to promises, and in recognition of services rendered, and to the further fact that, while the salaries are in exact proportion to stock ownership, such fact is purely coincidental. The company believes that the salaries paid the officers for 1940 fairly measured the value of the services rendered. (3) In 1940 this company inaugurated an employees' pension plan under a trust agreement. A copy of this trust has been filed with the ; and the Commissioner has been given full information concerning the same. Suffice it to say here that each employee of the company, from the highest to the lowest, is treated exactly alike. The cost of the plan is borne by the company; and each employee, upon retirement (should this plan survive) will receive a pension equal to 20% of his present

salary. Full notice of this pension plan was given to the employees during 1940 when the plan was inaugurated. 

Very respectfully submitted,  

Treasurer

Protest is directed to a thirty-day letter and agent's report showing an alleged deficiency in Income, Declared Value Excess Profits, Defense and Excess Profits Taxes for the year 1940 of $92,224.75.

A hearing on this protest is requested. Income

The agent erred in adding $150,000 to income as reported. 

Of this $150,000, $100,000 was paid to a contact man in Washington for procuring government contracts, and $50,000 was compensation paid to officers.

Commission to Washington Contact Man 

Commissions to salesmen for procuring contracts and selling goods are recognized ordinary and necessary business expenses. 

This situation is in no way changed because the customer is the United States Army. The commission in question was incurred and accrued in the year 1940. It was paid to a reputable business man for services rendered. It was fair and reasonable; and the deduction should be allowed.

Officers' Compensation 

The officers of this company were paid $100,000 for services rendered in 1940, and they earned it. In that year they elevated

TAXPAYER'S PROTEST 

677 this company from the ranks of patent medicine vendors to manufacturers of necessary defense materials which only they could manufacture.

It is true that, in prior years, when this business was struggling along, and indeed incurring losses, these officers worked for merely nominal salaries. But it had been agreed to reward them for their efforts, if ever those efforts bore fruit. And aside from that, prior salaries, unreasonably low, are not to be urged as a reason for disallowing present reasonable salaries. The only question here is that of the reasonableness of the present salaries. 

It must not be overlooked that the smoke screen value of the article manufactured by this corporation-the thing that made the 1940 income possible was discovered early in 1940 as a result of experimentation and development which these three men conducted at their own expense over a period of three years. 

Base Period Income 

The agent erred in reducing the base period income for the year 1938 by $60,000. The goods sent to Havana, to which he refers, were absorbed in the 1938 accounts under cost of goods sold, and thus eliminated. To demonstrate that the agent cannot be correct in what he says, attention is invited to the balance sheet at December 31, 1938. There is no account in which $100,000 of sales, not yet paid for, could possibly be concealed. The agent has his facts confused.

Invested Capital 

The agent erred in claiming that this was a new company organized for $100,000 capital in 1937.

This was a company organized for $2,000,000 cash in 1929. It is true that our minute book is badly and erroneously kept. But invested capital is not increased nor reduced by entries in a minute book. Neither does the investment of the ever changing parade of corporate stockholders determine the capital. 

Capital is defined in the law as (among other things) the money or other property paid into the taxpayer corporation. It cannot be successfully contended that the money paid into this corporation was anything less than $2,000,000.

While it is not a matter of great importance, attention is called to the fact that the Revenue Agent failed to take borrowed capital into account in computing the capital for his computation.

In filing this protest this taxpayer waives no rights, and expressly reserves the right to urge new and different matters of defense.

Please do not rely upon any statement contained herein, but check the facts and figures through your agents. An earnest effort has been made to state fairly the factual matter involved; but no representation of any fact outside the knowledge of the government officials is made.

Memorandum re Conference with Revenue Agent in Charge 

(Dated October 20, 1941, in Handwriting of Homer Patrick) 

Attended conference with conferees of Internal Revenue Agent in Charge. They talked about a settlement, which I couldn't possibly accept. I concluded that no acceptable settlement was possible with these conferees. We are too far apart. 

Charlie Penn tells me that I am now at the crossroads, and can do one of two things. I can either back out of these negotiations as gracefully as possible, and wait for a 90-day letter; or I can ask permission to go directly to the Technical Staff.

Charlie says the Technical Staff is a special committee of super tax men, who have broad powers regarding settlements, but whose endeavors are usually confined to attempts to make better settlements than the government will probably get at the trial table. He says that dealing with the Technical Staff is just exactly like trying to settle with opposing counsel in an ordinary controversy, except that the Technical Staff conferee, while an expert in the tax law, may not be a lawyer.

The conferee of the Internal Revenue Agent-in-Charge had told me that I could go to the Technical Staff; but I left the conference without reaching a decision on that, having determined I would talk to Charlie Penn before doing anything.

Have been discussing matter of appealing Ekselsior case to Board of Tax Appeals. Company can pay the deficiency, file a claim, and sue to recover; or it can appeal.

Charlie says this is momentous decision; and suggests that the following questions be considered. (1) Can client afford to pay a tax it does not owe? If client can't afford to pay out so much money, that ends it. Client must appeal. (2) Fairly computed how much, if any, does it really owe? If all other things are equal, and the present bill is close to right, it would probably be better to pay and file a claim. Stops accumulation of interest. (3) Is there any chance of an increased deficiency? If there is some hazard in the case, as yet uncovered, filing an appeal is foolhardy indeed, for it opens the door to greater deficiencies. (4) Are Board decisions in similar cases favorable or unfavor-

CONSIDERATIONS IN APPEALING TO TAX COURT 

693 able? If Board decisions are favorable, appeal is advisable. (5) Could this case be won before a jury? If this case is not a good jury case, appeal is indicated; but paying, filing a claim, and suing in the U. S. Court of Claims is an alternative. (6) Is it harder to defeat a deficiency or to obtain a refund? 

All other things being equal, it is easier to defeat a deficiency than obtain a refund.

Have weighed up all these factors, and have decided to file an appeal.

Charlie says make appeal short, but state a complete cause of action. He says some believe in filing a full petition so that if everything stated is proved, case would be won; but he advises against this. Says things change, evidence sometimes fools you and, in addition, too full a petition discloses your weaknesses, and betrays your strength.

The Court erred in allowing as invested capital of the petitioner, the $2,000,000 invested capital of the Eureka Company. The intention of Congress, in allowing a credit based upon invested capital,

MOTION TO VACATE 

711 was to allow the stockholders of a corporation to earn a fair return upon their investment, and this intention is manifest by the Reports of the Senate Finance Committee and the House of Representatives Committee on Ways and Means. 

Obviously, Congress did not intend to allow stockholders of a new and different company a credit based upon the original investment in a defunct company. The provisions of the Excess Profits 

Tax Law relative to reorganizations do not cover the transaction which took place in the case of this corporation. This petitioner is a new and different corporation than the Eureka Company. It has a different name, different stockholders, and a different product. 

The fact that the charter of the Eureka Company was amended to create this corporation may mean that this is the same corporation as the Eureka Company in so far as the state of incorporation is concerned; but it is a new and different corporation so far as the acts relating to the Federal Revenue are concerned. 

The Supreme Court of the United States has held many times, and as recently as Morgan v. Commissioner, 60 S. Ct. 424, that where there is a conflict between the federal revenue acts and the state law, the federal law must prevail.

I. Jurisdiction 

The appellant is a corporation duly organized and existing under the laws of the State of New Jersey, and has its principal place of business at Teaneck, New Jersey.

The appellant filed its income and excess profits tax returns for the year in question, namely, the taxable year ending December 31, 1940, with the Collector of Internal Revenue at Newark, New Jersey, which office is located within the jurisdiction of the 

United States Circuit Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, where this review is sought.

This petition for review is brought pursuant to the provisions of sections 1141 and 1142 of the Internal Revenue Code. A stipulation as to venue has been entered into between appellant and appellee.

II. Prior Proceedings and Facts 

On December 1, 1941, the appellee (respondent below) sent to the appellant a notice of deficiency, as prescribed by the provisions of section 272(a) of the Internal Revenue Code. Said notice showed a deficiency in income, and declared value excess profits taxes for the taxable year ending December 31, 1940, in the amount of $31,248 and $19,800, respectively, and showed a deficiency in excess profits taxes of $41,176.75 for the year in question. 

Said deficiency grew out of numerous adjustments made by the appellee to the appellant's tax returns for the year in question, among them the disallowance of $50,000 of the $100,000 officers' salaries paid.

On January 15, 1942, the appellant filed a petition to the Tax Court of the United States seeking a redetermination of its tax liability for the year in question, and alleging that the appellant erred in making each and every one of the adjustments. 

On February 25, 1942, the appellee filed his answer to the petition of the appellant, and prayed that the Tax Court of the United States find a further deficiency arising out of the disallowance of a further sum of $20,000 of the $100,000 officers' salaries paid. 

The combined effect of the disallowance of $50,000 by the deficiency letter, and of $20,000 by the appellee's answer, was to allow a deduction of only $30,000 of the $100,000 officers' salaries actually paid.

On March 2, 1942, the appellant filed a reply. Thereafter, on 

June 8, 1942, the cause came on for hearing before the Tax Court of the United States. On December 5, 1942, the Tax Court of the 

United States, formerly the United States Board of Tax Appeals, promulgated its Findings of Fact and Opinion. 

In said Findings of Fact and Opinion, the Tax Court of the United States ruled favorably to the appellant on all the issues involved, with the exception of the issue of the allowance for

officers' salaries paid, allowing a deduction of $70,000 for officers' salaries, in lieu of the $100,000 actually paid. 

On January 25, 1943, the Tax Court of the United States entered its decision in the above entitled cause as follows: "Ordered and decided: That there is a deficiency in income and income defense taxes in the amount of $6,249.60, and in declared value excess profits and defense taxes in the amount of $3,960; and that there is no deficiency in excess profits tax."

On March 2, 1943, the appellee filed a motion to vacate and for reconsideration, together with a motion for leave to file a motion out of time.

Subsequently, on March 16, 1943, the motion to file a motion out of time was granted. Appellee's motion to vacate and for reconsideration came on for hearing on April 5, 1943; and, on 

April 12, 1943, said motion was denied.

III. Nature of the Controversy 

This controversy involves the following questions:

1. 

The reasonableness of officers' salaries paid in the amount of $100,000. 

2. Did the Tax Court of the United States have sufficient evidence before it to support its finding that an allowance for officers' salaries of $70,000 was the reasonable and proper amount? 

3. Did the appellee bear the burden of proof as to the unreasonableness of officers' salaries paid in the amount of $100,000? 

IV. Assignments of Errors 

The appellant assigns as errors the following acts and omis sions of the Tax Court of the United States: 

1. The finding of the Tax Court of the United States that the amount of $100,000 officers' salaries paid was excessive, and that $30,000 of such amount was a dividend or a distribution. 

2. The conclusion of the Tax Court of the United States that $100,000 officers' salaries paid was excessive. 

3. The conclusion of the Tax Court of the United States that $70,000 was the reasonable and proper allowance for officers, as being contrary to the evidence. 

4. The failure of the Tax Court of the United States to recognize that the burden is upon the  to prove the unreasonabless of the salaries as deducted upon the appellant's returns. 

5. The making and entry by the Tax Court of the United 

States of its decision of January 25, 1943.

SOME COMMENTS ON RETURNS 

727 

Some Comments on Returns 

The making of a tax return requires skill. 

Did you ever see Sam Snead play golf? It is a very simple game. He just hits the ball straight ahead down on the fairway, and then he walks up to it, and hits it again straight ahead onto the green; and then he walks up to it again and, taking aim pushes it into a little hole. It is just as easy as can be. It's true that there is a lot of rough ground to the right and left, but Sam Snead stays off that. And there are a lot of pits and hillocks called traps and bunkers; but he keeps away from them too. There is really nothing to it, except, perhaps, if you are not as good as Mr. Snead.

This homely example will tell you more about how to make a tax return than twenty pages of text. If, without disturbance or annoyance, you follow the form, filling in all the blanks, the finished return will emerge as if by slow magic.

In all tax returns there are at least two points of great importance. The first is that unless you know all the facts you ought not sign the affidavit as printed, but should alter it as noted on the specimen returns.25 This is because the innocent affidavit which says that you have disclosed all of the facts that you know about may become an ominous instrument if someone suggests that you knew something which you did not disclose.

Suppose it should develop that this return is really fraudulent; and suppose the fraud consisted of the concealment of facts, which were concealed from you as well as from the government. When the matter comes up in the criminal courts, you may be named as one of the defendants. The guilty party may swear that he told you these facts, and that it was you, and not he, who concealed them, and that he relied on what you told him, which was to the effect that the omitted transactions were not taxable. 

The other important point is the taking of a receipt. There is a 5% penalty,26 i.e., 5% of the total tax shown by the return, for filing even one day late; and if you file late the statute of limitations does not expire as early as it otherwise would. It is important therefore to be able to prove beyond doubt exactly when the return was filed.

This is particularly important because the official

And yet, because millions of returns are filed in March, and there is a last-minute rush to file, many returns filed on March 15th bear a receipt stamp showing receipt as late as March 20th.27

The Government's Audit of Tax Returns 

When a return is filed with the Collector of Internal Revenue, it undergoes what might be described as a "mechanical" audit. 

Addition and subtraction are verified; and the tax is calculated on the income as shown, and checked against the calculation on the return. If the return contains a "mechanical" error, the Collector will write the taxpayer, and send him a bill for any deficiency arising out of correcting such error, or will give him credit for any apparent overpayment.

There is no right to appeal from an assessment that results from correcting a mechanical error. The justice of that situation is clear. But there are situations where the alleged mechanical error represents an intricate proposition of law. It would seem that in such a case the taxpayer ought to have the right to appeal. 

Some of the small individual returns are audited completely "in the field"; and the "final deficiency" is determined in the Collector's office. In such a case the Collector assumes the role of the 

Commissioner, and makes "final determinations", and sends ninetyday letters.

Excluding the small returns audited by the Collector, all other returns are sent to Washington where they are sorted, and in some cases, audited. The usual procedure is to sort them, give them a casual review, and return them to the taxpayer's district for field investigation.

They do not come back to the Collector. Instead, they are sent to the government's examining forces which are under the direction of an official known as the Revenue Agent-in-Charge. The 

Revenue Agent-in-Charge assigns the returns he has received from Washington to his field examiners. The field examiner appears at the taxpayer's office within eight months to two and a half years after the return was filed, and starts his investigation.

TAX PRACTICE 

729 

A Resume of Tax Practice Preparation of Taxpayers' Returns 

As a summary of what has so far been said about tax practice the following resume may be helpful.

In order to prepare the taxpayers' returns you need the following data:

CORPORATION RETURNS 

(1) Balance sheet at the beginning of the year, balance sheet at the end of the year, manufacturing or profit and loss statement for the year, and reconciliation between the manufacturing or profit and loss statement and the opening and closing balance sheets. (2) Statements similar to the above for all years from 1936 to the beginning of the taxable year for which you are making the return. (3) Copies of all of the corporation's tax returns from 1936 to date. (4) All government reports of audits of returns from 1936 to date, showing the conclusions reached.

INDIVIDUAL RETURNS 

(1) It is wise to get copies of the taxpayer's prior returns, but this is not absolutely necessary. (2) Since individuals usually have no balance sheets or profit and loss statements, you need a signed statement covering all the endeavors of the year involving income, losses, and capital transactions. It is well to have the client sign the statement at the bottom, certifying that it covers all his transactions for the year in question. (3) If the taxpayer's accounts have ever been examined by the government, get a copy of the government's audit report. 

Armed with the above, there should be no great difficulty in making the return, if all the spaces on the return blank are carefully filled in. Filling-in all spaces in the return is the very best check against errors.

You are advised against signing the form of affidavit which is printed on the return unless you really know all the facts of the case, and you rarely do. It is sufficient to swear or affirm that you have made the return from facts and figures supplied by the client, if that be the case.

Letter of Transmittal 

Every return you file should be accompanied by a letter explaining fully all things which should be explained, particularly the ones which are embarrassing.28 There always are such items. The only defense to the inevitable controversy is immediate disclosure. 

Things which your client hopes will not be discovered are usually discovered at the most embarrassing moment.

Request A Receipt 

When you file an income tax return, no matter how big or how little it may be, or how many you file, request a receipt. The receipt shows that you filed the return, prevents any controversy about delinquency, and may establish the time the statute of limitations begins to run.29

The Revenue Agent Appears 

In due time a revenue agent appears for the purpose of going over the books of the taxpayer in order to check the return. You should furnish him with everything he wants, including a quiet place to work; and you should cooperate with him. Nothing is gained by trying to hold back any papers, or by being hostile. 

In due time he will tell you he has reached certain conclusions, and that he would like to discuss them with you. He, of course, will seek a signed agreement. His first suggestion of deficiency and of settlement will be before he has written his report. If you are willing to settle on the basis which he suggests, tell him so, adding that you are willing to settle on that basis if it puts the matter behind you. He will probably tell you that the government reserves the right to come back for more at any time. This is a situation calling for the exercise of a fine degree of judgment.

THE REVENUE AGENT APPEARS 

781 You may have to decide to hold out for a settlement which is final, or to make a settlement which is not final, hoping that it will "stay put", or to let this chance to settle go by.

The agent may threaten a higher deficiency, if you do not make a settlement. If he does, he violates his instructions; and the only answer to him is that he ought not to threaten you. If you agree to settle, he will give you a Form 870 to sign. If you find that the instrument as presented does not give effect to the settlement you are actually making; and the agent tells you he cannot accept an altered Form 870, a way out may be to sign the Form 870 in blank, and to attach a letter qualifying it. This is discussed more fully at pages 740 et seq. below.

If you do not settle, the revenue agent will make out his report; and you will receive a 30-day letter.

Thirty Day Letter 

You are now at the first cross-road. A 30-day letter is an informal notice from the taxing authorities of a proposed deficiency, giving the taxpayer thirty days in which to protest. If you have filed a power of attorney with the Treasury Department you will receive a copy of this 30-day letter. While 30 days is the usual protest time-hence the term "30-day letter" such letters often give only 15 days time, and sometimes only 10 days.

A 30-day letter really amounts to a copy of the Agent's Report together with a receipt form, and a waiver form, called Form 870. 

The receipt is a rather crude document containing certain blocks in which you are invited to mark an "X". It seems to the author that it will create a better record if a courteous letter is written to the Revenue Agent-in-Charge acknowledging receipt of the 30-day letter.

At this point you may decide (a) to pay the tax and let the matter go, or (b) to pay the tax and file a claim for refund, and sue to recover the money. You may otherwise (c) protest, or (d) write a letter saying that you do not wish to protest, but disagree with the findings, or (e) do nothing. 

This, also, is a matter for the exercise of judgment because, if you decide to pay and seek a refund, you have the option of doing nothing, or of signing the waiver (Form 870). But before signing the waiver, if you decide on that course, consider carefully how to

preserve your right to a refund. If you decide not to pay, but to negotiate with the Bureau of Internal Revenue,-(c) (d) or (e) above do not sign the waiver (Form 870), and either request a hearing with the office of the Revenue Agent-in-Charge, or ignore the Revenue Agent's report, and await further action by the Bureau. 

Let us assume that you elect to negotiate with the Bureau of Internal Revenue, and that you file a protest, and ask for a hearing with the office of the Revenue Agent-in-Charge. Bear in mind that a protest may, and often does, result in a review of the facts, which, in turn, may result in added deficiencies. On the other hand, a protest may, and often does, result in a settlement. If it results in added deficiencies, your client will probably be dissatisfied. 

You file a more or less casual protest. A hearing is granted. 

At the hearing you set forth your reasons why the agent's changes should not be allowed to stand, and urge any new matter favorable to the taxpayer. The conferee, after consideration, sometimes offers to concede some of your points, or eliminate some of the items raised in the 30-day letter, and frequently offers to settle the case on a new and more favorable basis. To this end, he tenders you a waiver (Form 870) showing the revised tax, and asks you to sign it. This settlement is no more binding on the government than a settlement with the Revenue Agent; and what has been said above concerning that, applies with equal force here. 

If you decide against settling at the figure named (or unless it be final), and elect to negotiate further, you may request a hearing with the Technical Staff.

The Technical Staff has no power to change the findings of the agent, or to reduce the bill which the agent has found against the taxpayer, except for the purpose of reaching a settlement. Unless a settlement is reached, negotiations with the Technical Staff accomplish nothing; and the information which you disclose to the 

Technical Staff may be used against you later, or as a basis for further investigation. For the purpose of reaching a settlement, the Technical Staff may reduce the amount shown by the agent's report, and although the action of the Technical Staff is subject to review, its members are allowed a fairly liberal exercise of discretion; and their recommendations are seldom upset. 

If you do not reach a settlement with the Technical Staff, it would have been better had you avoided all these conferences. More-

733 over, the Technical Staff member may have discovered some hidden weakness in your case, and may direct that an added deficiency be determined.

 then send you a 90-day lettera deficiency notice.

Ninety Day Letter 

You are at the second cross-road. This is the most important one. Here you may either pay the amount shown by the 90-day letter and later file a claim for refund if you feel you do not owe the bill, or appeal to the Tax Court of the United States before paying. 

If you decide to pay the amount shown by the 90-day letter, and to file a claim for a refund, you may sign the waiver (Form 870), which accompanies the 90-day letter, taking care to reserve your right to seek a refund. Or, you may do nothing, merely allowing the 

90-days to elapse. To the author the latter course seems to be safer, although many lawyers deny the need for such precautions. 

Of course, letting the 90 days go by incurs interest.

If you decide to appeal to the Tax Court of the United States, you must file your petition with the Tax Court within ninety days after the mailing of the 90-day letter (deficiency notice).

It is unwise to wait until the last day. 

If you elect to appeal to the Tax Court, that becomes your sole remedy; and, having subjected yourself to the Court's jurisdiction, you must present your whole case to the Court. If you are entitled to a refund for the year or years involved, you must ask the Tax Court to so find. If you have any defenses to the points raised by the government in the deficiency notice (90-day letter) you must raise them here. Once the Tax Court has taken jurisdiction of your case, you can never sue or take any other action as to the year involved in any other forum, except to enforce the Tax Court's decision. The only relief from an adverse decision of the Tax Court is by appeal. 

After receiving the 90-day letter, but before filing your Petition to the Tax Court, you can, if you request it, obtain a hearing before the Technical Staff. If you have already had a conference with the Technical Staff prior to the issuance of the 90-day letter, and accomplished nothing, it is probably useless to have another, unless you have some new argument to offer.

Appeal to the Tax Court 

If you elect to appeal to the Tax Court, you must file your petition 30 (five copies clearly typed on letter size paper, fastened on the left-hand side, with a copy of the deficiency notice attached to each) within 90 days of the date of the deficiency notice with the clerk of the Tax Court. There is a $10 filing fee. 

Thereafter you are offered a conference with the Technical Staff. Whether or not you accept such a conference depends upon whether you have had a previous conference with the Technical Staff, and other considerations, discussed more fully above at pages 678, 679, 690, 691, 696 and 697.

The Commissioner answers your petition to the Tax Court within sixty days after the filing of your petition. If his answer raises the question of fraud, or any affirmative matter whatsoever, you must file a reply within forty-five days after the filing of the Commissioner's answer. The burden of proof of all such new matter is on the Commissioner.

Accompanying the Commissioner's answer is a request as to the place of hearing. If you have some preference as to the place where the hearing should be held, you may file a request with your petition, or at any time thereafter. If the Commissioner and the taxpayer request different places of hearing, it may be settled by argument before the Tax Court.

Motions such as are allowed to be filed in Federal District Courts may be filed in the Tax Court. But you cannot get a bill of particulars on the 90-day letter.

Next you receive notice that the case has been set for trial on a certain calendar, and that the calendar will be called at a stated time and place.

About this time you may be invited to (or you may seek) a conference with the member of the Chief Counsel's staff who will try the case. Whether or not you accept the invitation usually depends upon the advisability of settling, considering the dangers lurking in the case, and the sort of settlement possible. About two-thirds of the cases which get this far are settled at this point. 

If at the conference with the government attorney, you do not settle the case, you may stipulate the facts. It is wise to stipulate all undisputed facts.

APPEAL TO THE TAX COURT 

735 You appear before the Tax Court on the day that your notice indicates the calendar will be called. Calendars are called at 9:30 a.m. in Washington and 10 a.m. in the field. 

It is sometimes wise, when the Tax Court is meeting in a distant city, to arrange through the clerk or local counsel for an answer to the call and for a day certain. This may save considerable time and expense. But it may offend the judge, so be careful. You run some risk of default if your case is called for trial on the same day the calendar is called, and you are not there. 

When the Tax Court reaches your case, if you and the government attorney have reached a settlement, you may jointly offer a stipulation to that effect when your case is called. The Tax Court will accept the stipulation and thereafter enter decision in accordance with it. This will be a final settlement. If you have not reached a settlement with the government attorney, you answer "ready"; and the judge or clerk will then indicate the approximate day and time when your case will be reached, or instruct you to answer a further call later. 

When the trial day arrives and your case is called, if you and the government attorney have reached a stipulation of facts, you offer that jointly. If not, you make your opening statement and proceed with your evidence.

After the hearing is concluded you have forty-five days in which to file a brief. You should use at least five of those days in writing your brief; but don't make these the last five days. If you use an extra few days, the brief will show it, and the opinion may, too. The brief should be the very best of which you are capable. The brief wins many a borderline case. Few cases can survive a bad brief. A tax brief is not a job for a junior clerk. 

It is advisable to print the brief, although typewritten briefs are permitted. Make it as short as the subject matter will allow; and have it read smoothly. Adhere to the point, and avoid digressing to complain of the arrogance of the taxing authorities, or the tactlessness of the trial counsel. Briefs should be titled as to subject matter, and well indexed, both as to outline and as to authorities cited.

As a rule the government does not file a reply brief. But you should. Confine your reply brief to its purpose of reply. Make it short and clear; and print it, too. 

In time the court hands down its opinion. You may then do

one of two things. If you are aggrieved by the opinion, you may either file a motion for reconsideration within thirty days of the time the opinion comes down, or let the matter pass. If, on the other hand, you are pleased with the opinion, you may file a computation of the result, and a motion for a decision in accordance with the result; or you may wait for the government to do so, but don't wait more than thirty days.

After action on such motion, the Tax Court enters its decision, which is a determination in dollars and cents of the deficiency. 

This starts the running of the three months for appeal. 

If you win the case completely; and there is no deficiency according to the opinion, you should, nevertheless apply for entry of a decision. If no decision is entered, the three months for appeal does not start to run; and the government may, months or years later, have a decision entered, and then appeal within three months from the date of entry. 

Within thirty days after decision is entered, you may file a motion to vacate the decision.

Within three months after entry of the decision (or in the case of a timely motion to vacate decision, within three months after denial of that motion), you may appeal to the Circuit Court of Appeals, going to your own circuit as a matter of right, or any circuit upon a stipulation of venue. All the circuits have special rules concerning Tax Court appeals. It is advisable to obtain these rules and follow them.

Refunds of Overpayments 

The foregoing had to do with the procedure in determining the merits of a dispute before paying the tax. At several points it was noted that the tax might be paid and recovery had in another proceeding. This section deals with that procedure. 

A sovereign state does not have to allow appeals before taxes are paid; nor does it have to allow suits to recover taxes after they are paid. But a great sovereign can afford to be generous. The 

United States, recognizing the rights of its citizens, and understanding their tax problems, allows both remedies. Perhaps it would be more exact to say that it allows a choice of one or the other.

If you appeal to the Tax Court of the United States, you cannot later file a claim or pursue any remedy for refund of any taxes

REFUNDS OF OVERPAYMENTS

741 The revenue agent has a form 870 in his pocket when he talks to you about his findings; and you will meet it many times. If you settle a tax matter at any point short of a decision by the Tax Court, the settlement will be evidenced by this form. 

Except in a few situations the  must go through the process of making a final determination of a tax deficiency; must give 90 days notice, and, if the taxpayer appeals to the Tax Court, must wait until the matter is finally disposed of before he can make any assessment; unless he has a waiver signed by the taxpayer. Form 870 is the appropriate waiver. 

Form 870 purports to be a consent to an immediate assessment of a tax, waiving, for that purpose, the right to receive a 90-day letter or to appeal to the Tax Court. 

Taxpayers, when they waive the right to receive a 90-day letter, and to go to the Tax Court do not expressly waive the right to recover the deficiencies which, by signing the form 870, they agreed may be assessed immediately without further formalities. It may be that they are signing form 870 and agreeing to pay the amount stated, in the belief that that settles the case, and that it is closed. 

If they are laboring under that belief the probability is that they are mistaken.

It may be that they are signing the form 870, waiving the right to go to the Tax Court with the intention of paying the money, filing a claim for refund, and seeking remedies in the District Court. Upon the face of it they have a right to pursue this course; but they run the risk of having it said that the signing of the form 870 was such a consent as to set up an estoppel or some other defense, which prevents them from recovering the money.

It will be seen that in the two instances given above, the taxpayers' intent in signing the paper would be entirely different. 

Yet, the paper is the same.

Numerous cases have arisen involving executed forms 870. 

The author's analysis of them has lead him to the conclusion that this all-purpose paper must be treated with respect, and as a dangerous instrument.

He believes, therefore, that the taxpayer, in signing a form 870 ought to consider the advisability of either writing on it some clarifying statement as to what he thinks he is doing, or accompanying it with a letter which clearly sets forth his intention and pur-

pose in signing it. For instance, if the taxpayer is signing the paper in the belief that, when the assessment is made under it, his tax case for the year involved will be closed thereby, he should make that intention very clear, because the rule of law is that a settlement under form 870 is not final. The Commissioner, after collecting the money under the consent contained in the form 870 can turn right around and send a new deficiency notice for any further amount he chooses. The taxpayer, in such a case, could not raise a defense based upon the form 870.

It is thought by many that if the validity of the consent contained in the form 870 is conditioned upon acceptance by the Commissioner, and the assessment thereunder is conditioned upon the settlement being final, the taxpayer can, at least, have a day in court as to whether the Commissioner may reopen the case and charge a still greater deficiency. 

If on the other hand, the taxpayer, in signing the form 870, does not mean to admit a debt, and means to file a claim for refund as soon as he has paid the deficiency, and to seek the recovery of the money by recourse to the federal courts, it is the view of the author that he should say so, either by something written on the form 870 or in a letter accompanying it. For, if he signs the form 

870 in blank, and does not qualify it, the argument might be made, when he attempts to recover the money, that he has waived his right to recover, and that the form 870 was an admission that he owed the money.

Again form 870 does not anywhere say upon its face that it is a waiver of the statute of limitations. But such forms have been held to be such waivers; and if the statute of limitations becomes involved in a case after the signing of the form 870, it might be contended that the form 870 was a waiver of the statute of limitations.

If the taxpayer knows of this situation, and does not mean to extend the statute of limitations by the signing of the form 870, it seems to the author to be adviseable to write something on the form, or in a letter accompanying it, stating that the form is not to be interpreted as a waiver of the statute of limitations, and that the assessments involved must be made within the time provided in the statute.

It is true that, in practice, the revenue authorities frequently

To get around this situation, the practice has grown of delivering the form 870 with a letter attached to it limiting the scope of the form. From a practical point of view, it may be difficult at the trial to prove that there was such an accompanying letter, and that it actually accompanied the form 870. This difficulty is more real than theoretical.

Some lawyers who are anxious to avoid offending the government authorities, would much rather advise their clients to sign the form 870 in blank than run the possible risk of incurring the displeasure of the government servants. Conclusion: Estoppel Against the Taxpayer 

As the law now stands, all the rules of equitable estoppel apply with full and unabated force against all taxpayers. The taxpayer is bound by any and all representations he may have made which the taxing authorities relied upon to their injury. The taxpayer is bound by any conduct of his which may be said to have prejudiced the Commissioner, whether it has or not. On the other hand, no estoppel arises as a result of facts of which the Commissioner had notice and knowledge.

The Plea of Estoppel 

The Commissioner is not blind; nor are all taxpayers deceitful. 

But the plea of estoppel now finds its way into almost every government brief, as though both those conditions existed. It is assumed to fit everything. It is, of course, often proper, and frequently sustained. More often than not, a plea of estoppel in a government brief is an indication that the government has no real defense. 

Taxpayers have been said to be estopped to claim patent depreciation because they did not claim it on their original return. But patent depreciation is an allowable deduction to all who own patents and use them in their business, whether they be alert or slow.56

Taxpayers have even been said to be estopped to take any position contrary to that taken on their original returns. If that were so, no taxpayer would ever be allowed to recover an excessive tax voluntarily or mistakenly paid.

Taxpayers have been said to be estopped to recover because, when Congress provided a period for filing claims they filed their claims towards the end of such prescribed period. Congress enacted the period of limitations, and no fault can lie for using all of it, unless the late filing was in furtherance of an artful scheme of deception. Such a charge is easy to make but hard to prove, for it is usually unfounded. 

Countless other excuses for the application of the rule of estoppel have been urged. For example, read Helvering v. Brooklyn R.

Co., where the government contended that the statute of limitations ought not to afford a defense because the original return, on which the government claimed to have relied, had misled it. It is plain that such a position, if sustained, would supersede the statute of limitations on all deficiency matters. 

In the end the estoppel question comes back to the broad principle stated above, and found in the Stearns 58 case which amounts to this. When the taxpayer has misled the government he is estopped to take advantage of his conduct. It might be added that when the government claims the taxpayer misled it, the government • usually prevails, even though the claim is specious.

Estoppel Against the Government 

When we come to the other side of this question, and claim that the rule of estoppel should be invoked against the government, we find the  strongly entrenched behind our sovereignty-and thousands of subordinates. 

Whenever the conduct of the government is questioned the defense is raised that the rights of the government may not be prejudiced by the unauthorized acts of some public servant.59 

The government's position that no estoppel arises against it, withstood almost all attacks until the Bull case and the H. & B. 

American Machine Co. case came along. These cases indicate that some responsibility for the acts of government officials may sometimes be charged up to the government. 

In the Bull case the government sought to tax the same sum on two inconsistent theories, and without right. The Supreme Court did not allow it, even though the taxpayer had failed to file a proper claim.

In the H. & B. American Machine Co. case there was a letter by the government to the taxpayer which, at best, was not entirely frank. The Court did not fail to take note of the letter; and while not specifically turning the case upon the letter, the court was probably influenced by it.

Before leaving the subject of estoppel it is perhaps appropriate to remind the reader that this is a difficult phase of tax law. The foregoing discussion is necessarily brief and incomplete. If it warns the reader of some of the dangers, it will have served a useful purpose.

Power of Attorney 

The Treasury Department is to be complimented on its stand in regard to taxpayer representation and power of attorney matters.

On the surface all this formality might appear to be needless red tape. But the fact is that underneath there is an effort to curb a great evil in tax practice.

The Treasury Department, by its rules,60 provides that the original attorney shall have reasonable protection. The power of attorney under which he operates is not accepted until he has satisfied the Treasury Department that his contract is fair and not contingent upon success, or, if contingent upon success, that it meets the Treasury's requirements concerning contingent contracts.

Once he has qualified, someone else may not be substituted in his place without notice to him. If he has notice he can complain of any irregularity; and if it turns out that the new representative was guilty of improper conduct in obtaining the work, the Treasury Department will not allow the new representative to appear. If the old representative has had notice, and has been fully paid, or arrangements have been made to pay him and the new representative did not solicit the retainer, and has an acceptable contract, the 

Department will allow the substitution.

The Treasury Department, of course, will allow any taxpayer to discharge unsatisfactory counsel and take the case himself, or turn it over to new counsel whom he selects. The Treasury Department's rules, however, prevent the unauthorized settlement of one practitioner's case by another without notice. 

The Department of Justice and the Treasury Department, however, permit the taxpayer, without the knowledge of his counsel, to 60 Circular 230

Attorneys in the Treasury Department and the Department of Justice sometimes do not feel bound to refrain from dealing with the taxpayer without notice to his attorney.

The Tax Court does not interrogate new counsel when an appearance is entered. The answer you will probably get from the Tax Court if you complain that some attorney has entered an appearance in your case without your knowledge, is that the Tax Court does not concern itself with quarrels among attorneys.

A STATEMENT BY THE CHAIRMAN OF THE SECTION OF TAXATION 

Each year since 1934 the Section of Taxation, or its predecessor, the Committee on Taxation, has held one or more "clinics" for members of the tax bar in various parts of the country. These clinics, usually lasting one day, were well attended by attorneys interested in the legal and administrative phases of taxation. Similar clinics were sponsored by the Section in a number of cities during the years 1939 to 1943 in cooperation with state and local bar associations, law schools and other organizations.

When requests came to the Committee on Public Relations, the Committee on Unauthorizd Practice of the Law, and to the 

Section of Taxation of the American Bar Association for aid to practitioners in familiarizing themselves with the tax laws, it was natural that the Section's experience with its clinics should be combined with the experience of the Practising Law Institute, which, for a number of years, has conducted lecture courses in New York and other cities on the fundamentals of federal taxation.

In sponsoring these lectures it was not the expectation of the Section that a large body of "tax experts", if such there be, could be produced over night. Rather it was the hope that lawyers wishing to keep apace with the rapid changes in the profession would find in these lectures and pamphlets an exposition of the basic principles of taxation and some of the materials necessary to enable them to begin preparation for the practice of tax law a phase of the law with which almost every practitioner today must be more or less familiar in order to give the maximum service to his clients. If some progress toward this objective has been made, the courses will have served their purpose. It is to be hoped also that the experience gained in these courses will be of some help in the formulation of plans for additional postadmission education projects for the benefit of the bar generally. 

It seems eminently clear from the interest shown in these courses that the lawyers of the country are eager for practical, worthwhile instruction designed to enable them better to handle everyday legal problems. The generous contributions of time and effort 757

by the many persons who have helped make these courses possible, clearly demonstrate that the lawyers of the nation are anxious to assist in projects of this kind and are willing to pay something to make such projects self-sustaining. 

It would be impossible, in the limited space here, to acknowledge fully the services of all who have contributed to the success of the lecture courses. Much of the credit must go to the Practising Law Institute, its director, Harold P. Seligson, and the members of his staff who have spent many hours over the arrangements and administrative details in addition to supervising the publication of the pamphlets. Professor Erwin N. Griswold, of 

Harvard University, who not only served as editor-in-chief of the pamphlets, but who lectured in many of the cities as well, has earned the gratitude of the members of the bar for his contribution. The authors of the pamphlets who unselfishly contributed many hours of their valuable time during a period when many of them could ill afford to give the time because of their busy practice are entitled to recognition for their services, as are the numerous attorneys who gave editorial assistance in the preparation of the pamphlets, the officials of local bar associations and law schools and members of the bench and bar in many localities who gave much time and effort, and the many others without whose help the courses could not have been conducted.
