RHYTHMIC BEHAVIOR 

AN INTRODUCTION LIFEALY By Edward R. Dewey Director, Foundation for the Study of Cycles 

Rhythmic behavior is present in many phenomena that are of interest and importance to mankind. The alternation of day and night, the succession of the seasons, the beating of our own hearts, and the ebb and flow of the tides, are familiar to all of us. Science tells us that, in addition, many other familiar things behave rhythmically. With some of these, such as sound waves and light waves, we have all been long familiar, but in other instances the rhythmic nature of the fluctuations has not been realized until more recently. 

For instance, many years ago, the Hudson's Bay Company was troubled by the fact that there were tremendous fluctuations in the number of pelts offered from year to year. These violent fluctuations caused the company a great deal of diffi-

Moreover, if the same price is paid year after year for pelts, in times of scarcity the Indians and trappers will get barely enough on which to live; whereas in years of abundance the Indian is likely to drink too much "firewater" and otherwise to indulge in 1929 standards of living that cannot be maintained. How could they know ahead of time what these fluctuations were going to be?

After many years of experience in trading with the Indians it became apparent that the fluctuations in abundance of animals were fairly regular. Sometime or other someone connected with that organization got the notion of plotting on a chart the offerings of different sorts of pelts. Fig. 1 shows such a plotting, giving the offerings of lynx skins from 1844 to 1904. As the trappers work just as hard one year as another, and offer all the skins they catch, the number of skins offered is generally considered a fair measure of the number of animals at large. As you can see, the number of offerings fluctuates from about 4000 in a poor year to over 70,000 în a really good one.

When such a plotting was made it was immediately apparent that these fluctuations occurred with sufficient regularity so that even of themselves they provided some rough knowledge of when future peaks and valleys might be expected. 

If one takes all of the peaks and averages the time span between them and does the same for the bottoms, one finda that about 9-3/4 years fits all of the various tops and bottoms more closely than any other true rhythm that can be determined. For purposes of comparison, a perfectly regular 9-3/4 year rhythm has been superimposed, by means of a broken line, on the solid line representing the abundance of lynx. 

This expedient enables you to see the extent to which the actual abundance did or did not conform to perfect regularity. In this connection you will note that every second cycle (18 or 19 years) the peaks are higher. 

When this chart was drawn the figures were available only up to 1904, but the broken line was projected into the future to indicate the pattern that might be followed in the years to come. It is interesting to note that since 1904, even in spite of a general downward tendency in the trend, the peaks and valleys for the ontire thirty-six years from then to date have conformed quite closely to the projection. 2

The numbers of practically all wild animals of which we have any knowledge show a similar tendency to fluctuate, although the time intervals between crests and between bottoms vary as between animals. We don't yet know enough about this subject to offer any generalizations but it seems as if there might be some rough relationship between the size or life-span of the animal and the rhythms to which it adapts itself. For example, elephants have been reported to have periods of abundance at 62 year intervals, mice plagues come at four year intervals, and swarms of certain insects recur at intervals of about two weeks coinciding, amusingly, for the period for which I have figures, to one of the minor rhythmic ups and downs in the stock market. On the other hand, you can easily upset my generalization by pointing out the 17 year "locusts," a very small animal that has adjusted itself to one of the longer rhythms important in cotton prices.

How do these population cycles come about? We may get some light from the studies of Professor Hamilton at Cornell who has made exhaustive studies of mice in Central New York State. As far back as the records go there have been plagues of mice regularly at four year intervals; every 16 years they are worse. Moreover, by taking censuses of mice at frequent intervals throughout the cycle, and by trapping mice at the times of count, Professor Hamilton has been able to discover that there

are basic changes in mouse life that recur rhythmically. For example, as the cycle approaches its crest, that part of a year devoted to mating increases until it is almost continuous, as with us; moreover female mice can be induced to say "yes" sooner after giving birth to a family. Not only this, but the number of mice born in each litter increases and there are numerous other changes until, all of a sudden, a disease strikes from nowhere and the population is largely wiped out. This catastrophe occurs, incidentally, in years of Presidential elections a fact introduced to show that correspondence in timing and time span does not necessarily have any significance. This tendency for the recurrence of ups and downs at intervals of 40 or 41 months seems to be basic in our economy. Out of more than 500 different kinds of economic series that have been analyzed by the Foundation for the Study of Cycles and others who have worked in this field, I would judge that more than half show a tendency toward rhythmic fluctuations of something like this interval. For example, a basic wave of this length is present for common stock prices as a whole (Fig. 7) and for dozens of individual securities. It is present in figures of iron and steel production and in most of the business indices with which you are acquainted. It is true for the prices of many commodities and for the production and sales figures of many individual corporations. Also, interestingly enough, Dr. Stern of the Harvard 

College Observatory has determined that the variation in the heat received on earth from the sun, as determined by the Smithsonian Institution, fluctuates in the form of a compound rhythm, one of the component parts of which is exactly 40.8 months!

The 40.month wave in solar radiation is so clear and strong and regular that the odds are computed by Dr. Stern as only one in 250,000 of its being the result of chance.

There are also short term rhythms measurable in weeks and days present in the prices of most securities and most commodities, as well as in orders. For example, Fig. 15 shows sugar prices day by day for 1934 and 1935 compared with three weeks previously. Analysis of these fluctuations shows a definite tendency of ups and downs to recur at intervals of approximately 34 market days from crest to crest. 

This tendency does not always prevail - sometimes other and stronger forces upset it. But in the main this 34 day tendency does dominate as you can see by following the curve through in detail. This tendency holds good even in those times when, apparently, there was some attempt to control the prices, and we find that when the price structure breaks the time of breaking usually coincides with the normal timing determined when prices are free.

Still keeping to natural science, Dr. Omori of Japan has announced that a study of the eruptions of volcanos around the Pacific indicates increased activity in cycles of 65 years, every second one of which is bigger. Other writers have reported extensively on the periodicities of earthquakes, Fig. 19 shows a section cut through a tree that indicates the varying thickness of the annual tree rings. These get thicker and thinner largely as the result of changes in rainfall and climate. When rain is plentiful the rings are wide; when it is scarce they are narrow. Examination of thousands of sections of trees by Professor Huntington of Yale, Professor Douglass of the Carnegie Institution of Washington and others shows that these variations are rhythmic with intervals between times of broadest and narrowest bands of a little over eleven years. By studying the rings of the giant sequoias of the Western Coast and other long-lived trees, these men have been able to carry the records back for over three thousand years. 

Variations in climate can also be measured by the thickness of the annual sedimentary deposit in lake bottoms. In years of ample rainfall a good deal of sed- 18

iment is washed down and the layers are thick; whereas in dry years the sediment is muchless plentiful and the layer is thin. By measuring the thickness of hundreds upon hundreds of annual deposits it is possible to estimate the rainfall for hundreds and even thousands of years back. Those who have made such studies of rock strata have found evidence of the tree ring cycle continuously for several thousand years; isolated instances exist of a rhythm of this same length in rock samples estimated to be over 500,000,000 years old, so this is no recent phenomena that we are discussing. Let us now return to Fig. 20. Although the fluctuations around the trend line appeared, at first glance, to come at irregular time intervals, we have found from Fig. 21 that they can be largely explained in terms of two perfectly regular and perfectly symmetrical rhythms of lengths (7-1/2 years and 18-1/3 years) that are common not only to this and other economic series but to natural series as well fluctuations that come so regularly that they cannot reasonably be accounted for by chance. As these fluctuations exist for as far back as this series goes (57 years), we can say that it is unwise to ignore the possibility that they may continue into the future and that the fluctuations in the sales of this company may resemble the dotted line projected forward from 1939. As one can easily see, the crest of this dotted line comes in 1946, after which, if the past is to be any guide for the future, this company may suffer a decline of important consequence. As a matter of fact, if the underlying growth trends continue to flatten at the rate at which they have been flattening for the last twenty years, it will be more than 18 years before 22

the levels of 1946 are reached again. 

It is not hard to imagine how valuable this sort of information is to men The chart showed that 1938 and 1939 were faced with the problems of management. years when plants could be expanded boldly and at relatively low costs. From now until 1946 are years during which the prices of the common stock can be maintained by the complete distribution of earnings, or during which earnings can be held back in order to maintain stock prices later when earnings probably will not be so good, according to whichever may seem for the best interests of the company. Between 1939 and 1946 are the years in which re-financing should be possible on the most favorable basis (because of satisfactory earning statements), in contrast to the following ten or twelve years when the earning records may be on the down grade. 1944 and 

1945 are the years in which no new vice presidencies should be created! 1945 and 

1946 are the years in which employees should be taken on temporarily instead of permanently. 1946 and 1947 are the years in which employees should be weeded out rapidly in anticipation of the forthcoming decline and should not be kept on in anticipation of further increase in the business two or three years later. No plant at all should be scrapped between now and 1946 but after 1946 has come and gone and the downturn has definitely established itself, the company should consider scrapping surplus plant and equipment that will not justify carrying charges for the 18 years or so that will probably ensue before it will again be needed. The effect of the last war on normal behavior can be determined, and the knowledge thus obtained applied to the present situation.

This sort of information can be put to use, not only in connection with plant expansion, financing, dividend policy, and employment, but in every department of the business for example, in connection with wage increases and decreases, pricing, the timing of advertising and sales promotion, and as an aid to purchasing and proper inventory control. Without this sort of information it is impossible to determine present trends, or to make the best possible forecasts of the future. 

Sometimes I think the greatest use within a company is personal and intangible rather than financial. Profits are not everything; the peace of mind that comes from having a better idea of what is around the corner is important too. You know that at Yale they have induced a nervous breakdown in a pig by continually confronting him with the unexpected they call him the "Broker." 23

And values within a company are not everything either there are important social values to consider. This sort of information can be of real practical usefulness in minimizing waste of capital, in minimizing depressions and in stabilizing employment. Broader yet are the values to society through greater predictability in connection with the control of disease, pest control, game management and conservation, weather forecasting, and other phenomena that have not even been mentioned in this brief introduction.

By conducting research in this field, by helping to coordinate the efforts of the many scientists now at work on these problems, and by serving as a source of information for men in business interested in knowing more about the rhythms present in their own figures, the Foundation for the Study of Cycles hopes to have a part in bringing some of these values about.
