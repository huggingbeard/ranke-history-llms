4Technological Changes (1927-1940) 

In the thirteen years since the first anti-block booking bill was introduced in Congress, technological advances within the motion picture industry have virtually made it over.

Talking pictures brought about a veritable revolution. The first public exhibition of a feature motion picture with sound, occurred August 6, 1926 when DON JUAN was screened. In 1927, THE JAZZ SINGER made the future of the "talkies" apparently secure. By 1929, thirteen hundred of the larger theatres had been equipped for sound. Today, practically all motion picture theaters in the United States (17,003) and 48,003 in the rest of the world are so equipped. 

Sound film made possible also the recording of complete, specially prepared musical scores for feature pictures and also popularized a new type of film-the musical short subjects which are a part of most theater programs. Symphony concerts, classical arias and entire operas have also been filmed. 

Films in color constituted another important technological advance during this period, immeasurably enhancing the dramatic effects and increasing the artistic possibilities of this new art form. 

Short subjects, in the form of animated cartoons-a development sui generis, have become enormously popular with adults and children alike. Within the last three years feature-length cartoons have made their appearance, attracting unusual attention. "Glass" shots and rear projection now make possible complete authenticity of background, thus providing infinite variety of locales with consequent gain in educational and entertainment values. 

Sound recording and sound reproduction have steadily improved, as a result of the work of engineers specializing in this highly technical field. Latest development is "directional" sound on the screen.

Similarly, improvements in film manufacture, through development of fine grain negative and of panchromatic emulsions, have made possible superior photographic effects, adding to audience sensory appeal.

It should be noted that these technological advances have all been in the direction of greater authenticity in the reproduction of actual life situations and are mileposts in the evolution of a new art, which draws upon numerous older art forms such as the stage, painting, music, sculpture, etc. 

5 Artistic Advances (1927-1940) 

In the field of art we have seen the beginning and the maturity of a distinctly new art form in which America has excelled.

Between 1915, when Griffith's BIRTH OF A NATION took the motion picture out of the novelty class and definitely established it, to 1927 and the advent of sound there was a steadily growing interest in the screen as a new method of telling a story. 

Certain pictures have served as landmarks to indicate artistic progress: Examples-1932, GRAND HOTEL-the first picture with an all star cast and the first screen story confined to a single setting; 1934, ONE NIGHT OF LOVEopened a cycle of opera music appreciation; 1934, LA CUCARACHA-the first picture filmed in color; 1935, BECKY SHARP-the first feature length picture filmed in color; 1936, THE TRAIL OF THE LONESOME PINE-the first picture filmed out of doors in color.

Experiments in the use of Montage effects began in 1934. 

In 1935 the Museum of Modern Art recognized the screen as a new art form

Codes governing production and advertisings were formulated in and established it as a division of its work. 

There have been increasing interest in and study of the various arts represented in the motion picture-screen drama, original screen stories, original music compositions and adaptations, dance sequences including the ballet. 

The National Federation of Music Clubs has recently formed a Committee to study and comment on music in the motion picture. 

In 1927, the stage play was transferred in its original form to the screen. 

Approximately 5,000 schools in the United States have within the past ten years established classes in motion picture appreciation. Similarly, recent years have seen the introduction of the "History and Art of Motion Pictures" as undergraduate courses in leading universities. 

The motion picture has been recognized within the past ten years as a mass art of the twentieth century and has become increasingly instrumental in revealing the mental life of society. This parallels in modern times the classic period in Athens when the drama was a vital expression of the essence of national life. 

Since the addition of sound a vast field of history, literary classics and biography has opened for screen dramatization, increasing the knowledge and enriching the cultural life of the country. As an American art its natural source material is drawn from the great wealth offered by American life. This material has grown steadily more vital and its presentation more sympathetic and vigorous. This is a requisite of all great art. Examples-FURY, DR. EHRLICH'S MAGIC BULLET, GRAPES OF WRATH, OF MICE AND MEN and PRIMROSE PATH. 

Any great art must make its appeal, not to small groups but to the masses, and the past ten years have established this definite proof of motion picture artistry. 

6Commercial Developments in Motion Picture Industry (1927-1940) 

Between 1906 and 1927 the motion picture industry became firmly established as a successful commercial enterprise which provided entertainment for the millions at thousands of theaters within reach of most of the nation's population. 

Advent of sound in 1927 drastically changed the course of the industry. Within two years silent pictures became unacceptable to a large proportion of the movie audience. Millions of dollars worth of studio and theatre equipment suddenly became obsolete. Producers spent large amounts constructing and equipping sound stages, and completely revised the technique of making pictures. Entirely different dramatic material and talent had to be assembled. 

Theater operators installed millions of dollars worth of new equipment. Drastic changes in theatre design and acoustical construction followed. Both in production and in exhibition virtually a new business supplanted an old one, just at the beginning of a severe depression at home and abroad. 

Estimated weekly theater attendance in the United States has followed approximately the following pattern: 1922-40 million; 1927-57 million; 1930-110 million; 1933-60 million; 1938-85 million. Estimated average admission price in 

1927 was 28 cents; in 1939 the average, 23 cents. On the other hand, production costs have greatly increased during this period. 

American motion picture exports were seriously affected by the advent of talking pictures. No longer could the titles of the silent films be quickly and conveniently translated into foreign languages. English dialogue, understood by only a portion of the foreign audience, had to be summarized in sub-titles added to foreign prints. Now a new generation of foreign movie-goers have learned or are studying 

English so that an important by-product of talking pictures is marked acceleration in the use of English as a world tongue.

The quota systems abroad have further complicated the problem. Great Britain adopted a quota act in 1927. Numerous other restrictive measures governing imports, imposing tariffs and more recently governing exchange transfers have been enacted by various foreign governments. The current war in Western Europe has increased the gravity of the foreign exchange problem for American film exporters.

The purpose of the Neely Bill is to control the industry at the point of production by directing the quality of pictures through curbs upon the selling in blocks and in advance of actual production. 

Hearings attending these various attempts to legislate against block booking and blind selling indicate the existence of grievances against the industry.

In 1932 the Federal Trade Commission sought to abolish this method of distribution which it denounced as an unfair method of competition. The Commission's arguments against block booking were: (1) the practice limited the exhibitors' choice of pictures; (2) block booking was the direct cause of much overbuying on the part of exhibitors; (3) it unduly preëmpted the playing time of exhibitors, thus limiting the market for competitors' pictures and tending to eliminate competition; (4) block booking, by combining in the same group pictures of poor quality with those of better quality, forced inferior pictures upon the market; (5) the protected market offered by block booking resulted in over-production. 

In support of these contentions sixteen exhibitors testified to the restraints imposed upon the exhibitor by distributors' salesmen.10

2. Objectives of Neely Bill THE bill declares the practice of block booking and blind selling to be against our public policy because: (1) it interferes with the free and informed selection of films by exhibitors and local communities, and, (2) it tends to create a monopoly and burden commerce. 

Block booking may be defined as the licensing of motion pictures in groups, while blind selling is the selling of pictures in advance of production.

Both these practices are condemned by the proposed measure which seeks to make it unlawful to lease pictures in a group of two or more, requiring the exhibitor to take the whole group or none; or to offer films individually at such prices, in ratio to the price for petitor. Likewise, it is true that exhibitors prefer to buy blocks of pictures backed by an established trade name and manufactured by a company with a reputation for producing a consistently good product. It is also true that, because respondent can offer pictures in blocks, it has a price advantage over its smaller and weaker competitors. Likewise it is true that the smaller competitors of respondent, who cannot produce and offer for sale blocks of pictures, are at a disadvantage in competing with a company of the size of respondent, able to offer to exhibitors, season after season, a considerable number of pictures at one time. But these advantages are merely the legitimate advantages of business efficiency. "Every time respondent sells a block of pictures to one theater it has the necessary result of making it improbable that a competing distributor will sell its pictures for exhibition during the time consumed by the exhibition of respondent's pictures. 

Likewise such a sale prevents any other theater from exhibiting the pictures which have been sold to such exhibitor. Commission's counsel forget entirely that, by virtue of having sold all its pictures in a block to one theater, respondent has lost as possible customers all other competing theaters of the same class, which automatically become exclusively available to respondent's competitors. "But, let us again repeat, block booking as practiced by Famous Players-Lasky Corporation involves no covenant or understanding at all on the part of exhibitors not to buy pictures produced by competitors of respondent. "Indeed it does not even have that much of an 'exclusive' effect. There is not a shred of evidence in the testimony to show that Famous Players-Lasky Corporation has ever sold to the operator of any theater enough pictures to consume his entire available exhibition time for any substantial number of weeks. In 1920 

Famous Players-Lasky Corporation manufactured 93 pictures, but since 1921 it has never manufactured more than 75 pictures in one season. The number of motion picture theaters in the United States which do not require for the needs of any one season many more pictures than 75 is few. The vast majority of theaters in operation in the country today require in excess of 156 pictures a year."

3. Purported Benefits of Neely Bill

THE proponents do not appear to have agreed upon their objectives and purposes. Basically, the Bill is advocated because it is felt "it will remove an unfair trade practice, and by doing so, will insure to the public in any community greater opportunity to control the character of pictures shown to them in their local theaters." "Local communities," it is claimed, "ought to be freed from the situation in which they find themselves when the manager of the picture house offers what seems to be an unanswerable argument, that he is compelled to show a vulgar or licentious picture because he is the victim of a monopolistic system of buying blind or in blocks, and that he cannot run his theater unless he shows all the pictures in the block."

The benefits of this community selection which are assumed to follow from passage of the bill include not only the availability of "better" pictures from a qualitative standpoint, but the elimination of improper emphasis on love, sex and crime.

Also, it is felt, an incentive must be afforded the producer to compel the production of better pictures instead of "putrid" ones and this can be achieved if he must sell his pictures on their merits, not receive an advance security of block sales, irrespective of the type of picture produced.

Finally, the bill, its proponents contend, will insure additional playing time which will more than compensate for the loss of the playing time which the poor pictures now occupy and restore to the exhibitor control over his playing time and freedom to run his own business.

The proposed legislation is supported by foremost civic organizations and public-minded leaders in the earnest belief that it will bring about the production of better pictures, the elimination of offensive films and the raising of moral standards. The crux of the problem, it is made to appear, is the ethical rather than the economic issue-good morals rather than box office receipts. 17 How sound are these contentions and how firmly do exhibitors stand by the moral issue? Under the benign supervision of the

Neely Bill, proponents and advocates claim, inferior pictures cannot be passed off in blocks; crime and sex pictures would not be so prevalent; the exhibitor could consult his local clientele as to their choice, and order his films accordingly; civic organizations could bring pressure to bear upon the local exhibitor and only "desirable" pictures would be displayed.

What are the indications of better pictures? When is a film desirable? Who will constitute the local "judges"? To these and kindred pertinent questions the bill intimates no solution. An understanding of the true operation of public pressure and how extensive its effect will be in curbing the film industry must be had before so drastic a remedial law-which will undoubtedly have vast repercussions on the industry economically-is enacted. 4. Community Freedom of Selection 

AN avowed objective of the Neely Bill is the insurance of community freedom of selection.

At the present time public opinion does, to a very great extent, determine the types of pictures which are being produced. It would be absurd to claim that the practice of block booking completely insulates the producer and distributor from the pressure of the public. This pressure exerts itself through the box-office of the theaters since usually the return to the distributor is determined to some extent by the business done. Certainly in the long run, the box office receipts must affect the producer. The activity of the civic-betterment organizations in attempting to bring reform to the industry has had a tremendous effect upon the attitude of the producers. The Legion of Decency undoubtedly achieved a great deal in improving the picture industry. The producers realized that these organizations can affect box-office receipts by direct boycott, and also through their ability to bring forth state censorship. 

To avoid the uncertainties of that censorship, the producers imposed upon themselves a stringent self-regulation under the Motion Picture Production Code, and its administrative board justly boasts many a progressive achievement.18

It is further clear that the exhibitor is also amenable to community pressure. He can respond to this pressure through the power of cancellation and by bargaining with the competing producers.19 The belief of the public in the extent to which exhibitors are helpless under block booking is, no doubt, contributed to by the exhibitors themselves. 6. Price Determination 

THE methods whereby the statute seeks to achieve these objectives are price regulation and synopsis requirement. The elimination of block booking and blind selling, it is insisted by the proponents of this legislation, will eventuate in the desired control. 

It appears that the draftsmen of this bill sought to prevent distributors from offering any of the usual discounts in prices to induce the prospective buyer to purchase films in quantity or groups. 

Criminal penalties are imposed by the act to restrain or prevent the distributor from securing a license for a larger group of pictures from the exhibitor by offering such discounts in price. 

There is no question of discrimination in price quotations as between competing retail dealers such as is contemplated by the 

Clayton Act and the Robinson-Patman amendment thereto. The

issue relates solely to price differentials in quotations to the individual exhibitor (retail dealer) and then only as between the different articles purchased.

There is no such thing as standard price in the competitive buying and selling of motion pictures. Price is the result of trading and bargaining between a distributor and exhibitor.27 It is predicated on numerous factors such as seating capacity, number of performances, price of admission, nature of picture, and similar elements. Where two or more theaters are in direct competition for the same patronage in a neighborhood or community the number of pictures licensed is influenced by the competitive buying of rival exhibitors. Since each exhibitor seeks to license the most desirable line of pictures possible for his theater, there is keen competitive bidding. Resourceful exhibitors exercise all their ingenuity to maneuver better pictures away from their rivals. 

Under the proposed statute the price the small exhibitors would be required to pay for all the films used would have to be increased to a substantial degree. Many of these small exhibitors who in the aggregate carry a significant share of the production cost would be wiped out by such necessary price increases. Assuming that some could continue to meet such prices the continuous flow of pictures guaranteed under the current practice would be seriously curtailed since the distributor is compelled by the law to fix a price for each film offered on the basis that the retail exhibitor is going to purchase but one film and refuse any other irrespective of terms. 

Under the present system of wholesale distribution a very large number of theaters now operating secure the identical photoplay for $10 or $15, for which other theaters pay as much as $15,000 or $20,000. This is in part due to the fact that the small theaters in country towns and the small theaters in the neighbor- 27"Prices paid by exhibitors vary widely and in practically every case they are the result of a horse-trade type of bargaining between the exhibitor and the salesman. No fixed price exists and the actual contract price is always the outcome of negotiations. What the exhibitor has paid in the past for similar pictures, what the salesman thinks he should or can be induced to pay, the type of picture under consideration, the number of the particular distributor's pictures the exhibitor is willing to take, the type of theater in which the picture is to be shown, the amount of competition met with in that area, are only some of the factors influencing the final determination of terms." (H. T. Lewis "Distributing Motion Pictures" 1929, Harvard Business Review, Volume 7, pp. 267.)

hoods of the large cities have always bought their pictures in quantities of a number of films in each purchase. Realizing this universal practice and policy, the distributors are accustomed to quoting prices to these theaters on the basis of quantity sales. These wholesale prices or discounts enable these theaters to operate successfully, to stay in continuous operation, and to provide low-priced but first-class motion picture entertainment for the people of their community.

The bill would, in effect, insure retail selection at wholesale prices. It gives no definite indication as to what differential in price between a quantity purchase and the purchase of any individual item will be considered criminal and punished as such. No standard is indicated to guide the wholesale dealer as to how much difference in price he may charge for the same picture if it is sold alone or if it is sold with other pictures. Nor is there any possibility of his knowing in advance, or until after he has leased or licensed his film, whether he is violating the law in this respect. If he leases or offers to lease more than one motion picture film at the same time, he acts at his peril.

A further difficulty, it would appear, in designating a "lumpsum price for the entire group", is the practice of distributors to solicit offers, and the practice of exhibitors to make offers and conclude contracts for a stated license fee for each motion picture covered by the contract. In rare cases the individual license fee for each picture is the same for each of the pictures covered by the contract. In the great majority of instances they are not. The most typical solicitation by a distributor relating to a group of motion pictures and the most typical contract mutually agreed upon between a distributor and an exhibitor will have several price classifications for different numbers of motion pictures and normally, at least, one of the price classifications is one in which a certain number of pictures in the group are licensed on a percentage basis. Whether a motion picture meets its production costs depends upon the total revenues paid for it by all the exhibitors who contract to exhibit it. 

The bill provides in Clause 1 that salesmen of a distributor, if they offer more than one film, shall not require the exhibitor party to the negotiation "to lease all such films or permit him to lease none."

Under the proposed legislation the ordinary privileges of bargaining are denied the distributor's salesmen for they are prohibited from concluding a deal for more pictures than an exhibitor wished to contract for at the outset of the negotiations. 

Under the second clause a distributor is barred from insisting on an increase in prices or from achieving an increase in prices if individual motion pictures are accepted by the exhibitor. Since price determination in the motion picture industry is subject to so many varied factors which differ in each negotiation it would appear that the legislation should, at least, provide some definite standard by which those who are governed by the law may determine whether their conduct is legal or not (i.e. whether the negotiation restrained "the freedom of an exhibitor to select and lease for use and exhibition only such film or films of such block or group as he may desire and prefer to procure for exhibition, or as tends to require an exhibitor to lease such entire block or group or forego the lease of any number or numbers thereof, or that the effect of the lease or offer to lease of such films may be substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly in the production, distribution, and exhibition of films; or to lease or offer to lease for public exhibition films on any other manner or by any other means the effect of which would be to defeat the purpose of this Act."). 

Furthermore, in determining whether such compulsion was brought to bear upon the exhibitor an essential consideration would be the infinite variety of reactions experienced by different exhibitors in determining whether in their business judgment they can afford to pay more for certain motion pictures separately than when purchased in combination with other motion pictures, and that how much more they could afford to pay would vary with each exhibitor and with each picture. Some exhibitors might believe they could afford to pay 150 percent in excess of the wholesale price for selection of a choice picture. Others might believe that they could not afford to pay more than 25 percent or even 10 percent. 

It is obvious, too, that the increase which a distributor may in the first instance ask for, and more or less insist upon as to separate pictures taken out of a group, would depend upon the ability of the distributor's salesmen readily to sell the pictures not taken by

other theaters in the same area and would depend also upon the varying numbers which the exhibitor offers to take or accepts. A quotation on separate individual pictures is indicative that the salesman is attempting to conclude a transaction. But even if the distributor's salesman should feel finally that he has offered a price which gives the exhibitor absolute freedom to take those which he desires and prefers, may he ever be certain that the price he has quoted did not tend substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly in production, distribution and exhibition of motion picture films?

The real economic problem in relation to block booking is that of the unaffiliated exhibitor, and it is chiefly certain exhibitors who complain about the alleged evils of this practice. The small exhibitors not only find themselves at a distinct disadvantage in bargaining for pictures with the distributors, but they are placed at a competitive disadvantage against the exhibitors affiliated with or controlled by the distributors. To a large extent the troubles of the independents are not due to the burdensome trade practices of block booking, but to the economic fact that the large producerdistributor-exhibitor organizations are more strategically situated economically to earn money. They are more efficient, and they have a better competitive position.28 Stated differently the troubles of the exhibitors are not due to monopoly but to competition. This situation is characteristic of industry as a whole.20 A striking analogy is found in the chain store situation. The chain stores have a decided economic advantage over the independent retailers. To prevent the extinction of the small man, Congress enacted a price discrimination law. Subsequent developments, however, bear out the fact that the evil legislated against was not price discrimination but economic practices not altogether unjustifiable. The act was adopted not to cure the evil of price discrimination but merely as a device to attempt to shift the balance of economic advantage, through removing one type of competitive advantage of the large 28 The sound business reasons which prompt one distributor to prefer the circuit theaters operates similarly on all distributors. 7. Synopsis Requirement 

BLIND selling is inextricably interwoven with block booking If the latter is to be eliminated, some provision must be afforded to permit selection by the exhibitor of the pictures he wishes. It is clear that under the present system this is impossible. The exhibitors must accept blocks of pictures and must contract for them in advance of production. The information given out as to future production, though limited, is not uninformative.33 Exhibitors have not infrequently shown themselves to be indifferent to possibilities of learning more about the pictures they are to receive in advance. 

This, however, may in turn be partly explained by the fact that the exhibitors have not had sufficient power of selection to warrant their studying the contents of the pictures given to them. 

Exhibitors need never be compelled to contract in advance of production. They do so of their own volition and their choice is seldom if ever based on any synposis, but rather on factors entirely beyond the purview of the proposed statutory formula: competition, the desire to get favorable runs, to insure prompt delivery and to take advantage of the publicity attendant upon the release of a

The enforcement of this provision like that against block booking may well support an exhibitor's arbitrary cancellation even as in the case of block booking imaginary compulsion may lay the foundation for criminal prosecution of a distributor whose only offense was his bona fide desire to procure the best possible bargain. 

Superficial familiarity with the motion picture industry would reveal the implausibility of such a requirement. The producing companies and the studios without large financial resources would be unable to continue in business unless they could raise sufficient additional capital to enable them to carry a substantial inventory of unreleased but fully completed motion picture productions. 

8. Results of Proposed Legislation 

GRANTED, however, that neither community freedom nor competition are presently permitted by the current industrial set-up; that block booking and blind selling are undesirable practices, will the Neely Bill cure these "defects"?

Section 3 of the Neely Bill, after making it unlawful to require an exhibitor to lease a group of pictures, provides that the distributor cannot offer films individually, at such prices in relation to the lump-sum price for the group "as to operate as an unreasonable restraint upon the freedom of the exhibitor to select and lease" only such films as he may desire, or as tends to require him to take the group instead of individual pictures.

Block booking is, in reality, wholesaling. That there is an economy in mass selling is a marketing principle recognized in every line of business. Even the sponsors of the bill concede that some price differential between group and single leasing is justifiable. The bill raises the question: Just when does that price difference become such as to restrain the exhibitor in his freedom of selection? What test is used?

The standard for an unreasonable restraint on the freedom of the exhibitor to select is not, as would ordinarily be expected, whether the differential actually reflects the economies of mass selling, differences in cost and the necessities of competition rather than an attempt to compel the acceptance of the block or nothing.34 

Production cost and the negative from which the print is made is

What is the probable effect of the provision of the bill banning blind selling? The bill (section 4) provides that a distributor may not offer to contract or contract to furnish for public exhibition any motion picture film without at the same time furnishing the exhibitor party to the negotiations or contract, a complete and true synopsis of the contents of the film and that "such synopsis should be made a part of the lease," or licensing agreement for the picture. If detailed advance synopses were obligatory, as is intimated by the bill, such revision would be well nigh impossible. A "complete and true synopsis" of the contents of such films having been supplied to exhibitors, on the basis of the first conception of the picture, no material deviation could therefore be undertaken with impunity for the bill implies that such deviation may invoke criminal sanctions. The net effect would seem to be two-fold: Producers could no longer feel at liberty to improve or develop the original concept during the course of production, nor could the Production Code Administration guide production into socially desirable channels. 

The necessary result of this change in production methods will be a decrease in the quality of pictures produced. An even more serious dislocation of the present industrial practices may result from the synopsis requirement. If it was intended to proscribe sale in advance of completion the statute would merely have required the showing of the picture to exhibitors before leasing. The present measure for this reason may have disastrous economic effects on the proponents of the bill-the small exhibitors-which effects they have attempted in the bill itself to prevent. Abolition of the system of advance booking will certainly necessitate a complete reorganization of distribution methods. Before such a step is taken, careful analysis of this particular problem should be required. 

There are also certain indirect results which may follow from the Neely Bill, which the proponents thereof appear not to have contemplated; or having contemplated, have deemed unessential. 

The large producer-distributors will not permit the statute to destroy their business advantages over the independents' "lying down" if there are other means of maintaining their position. They may increase their control and ownership of exhibitors directly so as to minimize their dependence on the independent exhibitor. This would leave the small men in a much worse position than they were before the statute. The greater the difficulty which the producers confront in complying with the statute, the more likely is this expedient to be adopted. The producers may be forced to raise the price of both block and individual pictures because of the change in technique, which will further operate to the disadvantage of the small exhibitor against the distributor-controlled exhibitors. The very major companies sought to be curbed by this proposed legislation may alone be capable of surviving. 

The production of pictures under this bill would be sharply curtailed. The ability of independents to reject poorer production will necessitate a change to the production of less but better pictures. The cut in production schedule may lead to serious economic dislocation in the industry and a drastic reduction in employment. 

Unless the improvement of pictures cannot possibly be obtained in other ways, the end here sought certainly does not seem to warrant the means employed. It has already been indicated that the quality of film may be impaired by the statute, if passed, through destruc-

More generally, it may be stated that a measure which may so drastically affect a major industry to cure minor evils is hardly practicable at the present time. The value of piece-meal (i.e. industry by industry) regulation of industry has not yet been demonstrated satisfactorily enough to warrant its extension. 

II CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE NEELY BILL 1. Legal Aspects of Block Booking 

THERE is no question as to the ability of Congress under the commerce clause to regulate the leasing of films which admittedly constitute transactions in interstate commerce.37 The only other basis for objection is the Fifth Amendment. General objections on the basis of interference with freedom of contract appear insubstantial. The power of Congress to restrict the normal freedom of contract in the interest of the public good is too well established to require citation. The legislative determination of public necessity will be respected by the court unless arbitrary, unreasonable or capricious.

The underlying objective of the present proposal is apparently the elimination of unfair trade practices which tend to create monopoly. The case of Federal Trade Commission v. Paramount 

Famous-Lasky Corp.,38 does not establish the unreasonableness of this legislative purpose as a constitutional question. The holding there merely established that under the existing law block booking did not constitute an unfair trade practice; where the defendant's products were not essential in the trade and there was no conspiracy with other producers. But the practice of block booking now universally obtains and it is proper for the legislature to determine that even without collusion the practice is unfairly competitive and tends to create a monopoly, and should be legislated against.

The test in that field is fair return on investment, A Kentucky anti-trust act made guilt depend upon the question of whether a price established by a combination was the "real value" of the article,-"real value" to be ascertained from a finding of the price obtainable under normal circumstances with fair competition.48 This criterion was useless, since it referred to an imaginary state of normalcy which had not existed.. A Colorado antitrust act was also invalidated because legality of a combination depended upon its necessity to enable the participants to obtain a "reasonable" profit.49 No standard was supplied nor did any exist in business practice for the estimating of a reasonable profit. The penalty for charging "unjust" or "unreasonable" rates for necessities has also been held to supply no real standard.50 Any act deemed detrimental to the public interest might be rendered invalid thereby.

The important thing to note is that the requirement of definiteness is a plausible standard, and not real certainty, in the sense that a person of ordinary intelligence will not make an error in judgment, because the judgment of others may differ. It should also be noted that the definiteness of a particular statute depends on its own language in context and the surrounding circumstances.51 The practicability of the selection of a more definite standard is a factor which may not be ignored in this respect. 

Bearing these principles in mind, let us turn to the Neely Bill.

New penal offenses are created and predicated upon such vague and indefinite criteria as to suggest the precarious speculativeness of such regulation. Criminal statutes must be so framed "that those to whom they are addressed may know what standard of conduct is intended to be required." The law can hardly indulge such metaphysical considerations as the proposed measure seeks to impose.

2. Legal Aspects of Synopsis Requirement SECTION 4 of the bill presents a different constitutional ques tion. It makes unlawful contracting for the leasing of a picture until the distributor furnishes the exhibitor with a complete and true synopsis of the picture. This synopsis must include an outline of the story and the incidents and scenes depicted, and a statement describing the manner of treatment of dialogues concerning any scenes depicting vice, crime or suggestions of sexual passion. Variance of the picture produced from the synopsis is made criminal. 

What is the standard for the accuracy of a synopsis in relation to a picture? There is no aid found from any other fields of law or industry because no similar problem was ever treated. Nor is there anything in the motion picture industry to clarify the situation. 

Aside from this bill it was never necessary accurately to translate material from the film medium, which depends to a great extent upon visual patterns, into the written words. Verbal expression is ambiguous enough in any case, and the burden now placed upon the industry to describe such ideas as the suggestions of sexual passion found in a motion picture so that a wide variety of persons would understand the degree of such suggestions present, would seem severely to tax this mode of expression. The difficulty is critical in a field which depends on moral judgments which vary so greatly among the different areas of population. A further complication appears in relation to this provision, which was intended to enable the selling of futures in the industry without the evil of "blind selling." But if the synopsis requirement is a strict one, calling for much detail, it will eliminate the sale of futures. Assuming that production techniques may be changed so as to preclude the constant change in a picture by progressive revision which now characterizes the industry, the further difficulty appears of preparing a synopsis in advance which will accurately convey the same impression as scenes which have not yet been filmed but are merely scripts. The difficulty already indicated of expressing verbally a "pictorial action" is complicated by requiring a visualization from the script of what the "pictorial action" will be.

This problem involves also the factor of determining the technique of the director since direction often alters the entire impression conveyed by a scene. If Section 4 eliminates the possibility of dealing in futures its vagueness is less excusable than otherwise. In such event, there are far less complicated methods of preventing "blind selling", such as by showing to the exhibitor the completed picture, which would eliminate all questions of analysis or of deceit in the rendition of such analysis. Under these circumstances a court fully cognizant of the simplicity of such an alternative may not be as reluctant to declare a synopsis requirement unconstitutional because of indefiniteness.

III CONCLUSION 

THE evils of the motion picture industry sought to be corrected 

56286 U. S. 210.

have been the subject of many an eloquent plea. Pictures should not be foisted on communities against their better judgment. Their quality should be improved and the deleterious emphasis upon the excessive and improper treatment of love, sex and crime should be minimized-laudable objectives to the achievement of which the 

Production Code (The Will Hays Organization),57 publicly minded individuals58 and pressure groups59 have aspired. It is urged, however, that the progress in this direction is not commensurate with technological developments. 

To effect such commensurate progress the Neely Bill is proposed. Will it achieve any of its purported objectives? 

The general tone of the motion picture business has undergone many radical alterations which, to those unfamiliar with the industry and its problems, are almost unknown. Withal, none of these changes is accorded recognition in the proposed legislation. No cognizance is taken of the fact that many of the evils which have existed during the industry's infancy have been mitigated if not abrogated, and modifications effected which rank this industry among the first as a medium of education and instruction, as well as of entertainment.60

The Neely Bill does not offer any selective contract nor any option to cancel pictures after they are licensed for exhibition. In fact it would appear that the enactment of the proposed measure would bring about a discontinuance of selective contracts and option to cancel. Furthermore, it destroys the advantages of wholesale buying so essential to every retailer who deals in goods for mass consumption in order to provide retail selection in an essentially wholesale transaction.

A careful reading of the bill fails to reveal any insurance of higher moral standards. No inhibition is even implicit in the bill against pictures of questionable morality. In fact, such productions, which are in the main made independently61 and sold one at a

As the bill is drafted it is a standing invitation to disgruntled exhibitors to resort to litigation as a means of avoiding legitimate contractual obligations. They may also hold the threat of criminal sanctions over a distributor for any alleged or inconsequential deviation from the proferred synopsis. As a bid for private censorship through organized pressure groups, the measure is suggestive of numerous complications.
