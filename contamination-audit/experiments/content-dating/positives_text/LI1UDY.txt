Foreword 1. It is an historical fact that farm prices fluctuate sharply and in the extreme. It is also the case that individual commodity prices move up and down together in a somewhat uniform pattern. This uniform movement on the part of individual commodity prices occurs, first, because producers are continuously shifting out of the production of commodities relatively low in price and into commodities relatively high in price, and second, because consumers are continuously revising their shopping lists to eliminate relatively high-priced food items and to include relatively low-priced items. This process of substitution at both the production and the consumption levels acts "to hold commodity prices in line." Hence, in this study we treat food as one big commodity, and analyze the behavior of the food price level (an index of food prices). We can do this because the price level is generally representative of individual commodity prices. 

2. The basic cause of the extreme fluctuations in the food price level is to be found in the nature of the demand for and the supply of total food. In the short run, the average consumer in the United States does not vary his consumption of food to any important degree in response to food price level changes; we say the demand for food is highly inelastic. 

And producers vary their output of food in response to price level changes even less; we say the supply of food is almost perfectly inelastic. Consumers don't change because of the physiological need for food; producers don't alter production because total resources employed in agriculture change only slowly through time. Consequently any small shift in the demand for food, or the supply of food, sends the food price level spinning. The moderating influence of a reduction in the total amount taken and an expansion in the total amount offered in response to a price rise does not come about. Similarly, the moderating influence of quantity adjustments is lacking in a price level decline. 

3. Fluctuations in the level of food prices are most often initiated by shifts in demand. Consumer incomes increase, or decrease, for whatever reason; out of their increased incomes consumers decide to increase their consumption of food, spend more dollars for food to enrich their diets with more meat and other costly foods. Such changes in the total demand for food usually are not large. They don't have to be large. A small percentage change in demand, say an increase, is sufficient, where the moderating influences of quantity adjustments are at a minimum, to force a dramatic rise in the food price level. This we sometimes refer to as the 

10 per cent theory. The relationship of demand to supply, where total food is concerned, is such that a small percentage change in demand, the usual stimulus, forces a tremendous response in the prices received by farmers. Thus, we see that wide and sharp price swings are the norm for agriculture.

4. But the total output of food has increased over the years; we are all familiar with the record of agriculture on this score. It seems, however, that the expansion of total food output is somewhat more jerky than is generally recognized. Increases in aggregate output appear to be closely associated with expansions in demand. When the demand for food is expanding, farm prices are rising and income expectations are bright, farmers invest in new technological methods which increase worker productivity. Output in agriculture has increased primarily through technological advances rather than through the employment of more resources in agriculture. And periods of farm technological advance are closely associated with "good times," when investment funds are available and farmers are willing to sink these funds in investments of long duration. 

This process of expanding output through the farm adoption of new technologies is not, however, reversible; when demand ceases to expand the process of output expansion comes to an end, but output does not contract when demand contracts. In such cases, farm prices fall precipitously. 

5. This study is concerned with a logical and statistical verification of the foregoing summary statements. This verification involves the use of technical concepts, methods and language; and very often the methodology becomes involved and complex. There seems no help for this. We can assert in the simple everyday language of the foregoing statements; but we cannot provide evidence, which is in the nature of a verification, in the same easy way.

The severe ups and downs of the farm price level have long been recognized. This peak-trough pattern has been variously attributed to sun spots, Wall Street, ineffective total demand, wars and their aftermath. 

But during the interwar period the pieces of a more serious explanation began to emerge. Numerous studies were published indicating or illustrating the relative inelasticity of demand for various farm products.¹ 

More recently, the even greater inelasticity of demand for all foodsfood in the aggregate-has been pointed out. In other words, regardless of what happens to the food price level, Americans go on eating about the same total amount of food, and within major food groupings about the same amounts of the same kinds of food. 

Arguments on the supply side, however, have not been so clearly formulated. The total output of agriculture has with persistence, if not steady cadence, marched to ever higher levels over the past 80 years.2 

On the other hand, total farm output has shown a peculiar unresponsiveness to price level changes. Further, no one seemed to know what the supply function for an individual farm commodity, say wheat, looked like, or if there were one. The haze of unknown has lain too deeply over the conventional demand and supply tools of analysis to permit an explanation of the wide swings of the price level or of the price changes of the principal commodities comprising it. Still the various indices of farm prices and farm production have historically acted in a way such as to provide prima facie evidence that farm price behavior could be explained if we assumed that both demand and supply were highly inelastic. And this is the assumption we most often make in the classroom, when for purposes of illustration, we draw in demand and supply curves for agricultural commodities.

If we can forget for the moment the problems of supply in agriculture and simply assume that the output of agriculture is a fixed quantity, then we have made a start toward providing a rationale to the fluctuating farm price level. The Complete Theoretical Structure 

The elegance of this argument is considerably enhanced if we free ourselves from the static Marshallian cross and pursue a "path" analysis.

The aggregate output curve for agriculture (in the statistical analysis this concept is limited to total food) is, we assert, exceedingly inelastic for any given period and given productive organization. The peculiar unity of occupational functions (labor, technological and business management) and the importance of overhead costs as compared with operating costs on family farms, as well as the constant supply of both land and labor in agriculture, argue for the plausibility of an inelastic aggregate output curve.

In this connection the term output curve is used advisedly, for the concept involved is not the instantaneous supply schedule commonly attributed to the grain trade or the record of successive price equilibria (trend line) of Norman Silberling.5 We have in mind an obvious concept -the size of the total product which farmers in the aggregate will produce at varying levels of price in any given year. Thus the aggregate output curve under consideration is, in an orthodox fashion, a function of price. But since agricultural output can emerge only through time, farmers cannot stand ready to produce instantaneously varying quantities of product at different levels of price. A farm output schedule conceptually comparable to the supply schedule of grain dealers, who may at any instant of time, stand ready to sell varying quantities of product at different prices, is inappropriate here. But a schedule of intentions to produce the product that farmers in the aggregate plan to produce at varying levels of price-is conceptually possible. Therefore we define the output function in terms of this schedule of intentions to produce (this postulated inelastic schedule is shown graphically as curve II in figure 2, chart A).

This schedule of intentions to produce is an adaptation of Marshall's short run normal supply curve. It ignores the particular circumstances of the market, indicating what farmers plan to produce in the short runusually a season where the basic farm plant may not be altered. The degree of resource utilization (i.e., the intensity of use of different resources) may vary, but the plant is fixed.® 

The aggregate demand function, DD in figure 2, chart A, is conventional in most respects. But the introduction of a schedule of intentions to produce raises the problem, among others, of a time sequence in the demand, price and output relationship. The demand function cannot be related to a schedule of intentions to produce-such a relationship has no meaning; buyers are seeking goods not intentions. Thus, the demand function assumes a meaningful role in this context only when related to realized output. The schedule of intentions to produce is a function of price, but a definite price solution results only after the production process is complete and the bargaining over a real basket of goods yields a unique price (price level in this case). In a period of stationary or contracting demand, "know how” and enhanced physical productive capacity accumulate, so to speak, in an unused pool. Now given an expansion in demand, output increases as known technologies are put into practice, with the result that the aggregate output curve jumps to the right or drifts to the right through a succession of temporary positions, taking up a new fixed position defined by a new productive organization centered around the technologies recently placed in operation. But once the pool of unused technologies is incorporated into the production organization, limited always by the labor force on family farms, further increases in demand fail to increase productivity—to shift the output curve to the right.¹º Further increases in demand simply develop a stimulus-response sequence centered around the inelastic output curve, II, in figure 2, chart B, yielding substantially higher prices and inconsequential quantity increases. In general terms then, an increase in demand may, in one phase, increase price and not output, and in another phase, may increase output and not price. 

The skipping action described above, however, is not reversible.¹¹ If, for example, the aggregate demand curve DD moves sufficiently to the right in the necessary technological context to cause the output curve to also shift to the right, the output curve does not shift back to the left with a contraction in demand. The demand curve will rather initiate a stimulus-response sequence centered around the output curve I₁I₁ wherein prices fall precipitously and quantity changes almost not at all. For the behavior of aggregate output in the field of agriculture following a general price decline is not one of contraction. On the contrary, it is one of sustained output. Once the output function, schedule of intentions to produce, has shifted to the right, it remains fixed in that inelastic position until some new demand stimulus causes it once again to shift to the right. 

But to return to the central argument, given a severely inelastic demand curve for the total product of agriculture and an output curve such as (II) diagramed in figure 2; it necessarily follows, except for the special case where the output curve shifts to the right, that a movement in the aggregate demand curve (DD) must be accompanied by a substantial price change and by an inconsequential quantity change. The geometry of the demand-output cross is simply such, granting the above exception, that any shift in demand (assumed to be the more flexible edge in the scissors relationship) must result in a relatively small response along the quantity axis and a relatively large response along the price axis.

II: Price Behavior in the Interwar Period 

We shift away from the familiar single commodity type of analysis in this study, to an aggregative approach. An explanation of farm price level behavior will be developed through the use of aggregate demand and supply functions. We follow this approach for one important analytical reason: the various food commodity prices tend to travel together over the same tortuous road.¹ The reason for this uniform movement is to be found in the omnipresent process of substitution. There is some degree of substitution between individual farm enterprises within most agricultural areas and a significant degree of substitution at the extensive margin of all areas in response to commodity price changes,2 but not between farm and nonfarm enterprises. For example, farmers shift readily between potatoes and sugar beets or between the feeding of corn to hogs and the cash sale of corn, but not between farming and banking or farming and manufacturing. 

On the demand side too, particularly in the case of foods, consumers are continuously substituting less expensive items for more expensive items. Through the ever-operating process of substitution, prices of individual agricultural commodities hold their relative positions with some precision even though the level of all agricultural prices may be shifting radically.

The agricultural commodity price level does not move in close harmony with the nonagricultural price level for the reason that the process of substitution, both on the production side and on the consumption side, breaks down or is severely restricted between the agricultural and nonagricultural segments of the economy. To an important degree agriculture represents a watertight compartment within which there is considerable fluidity, but the connective valve between the agricultural compartment and the rest of the economy works poorly and sometimes almost not at all.

Now should we attempt to explain the behavior of all farm prices, or more correctly the level of all food prices, by a single commodity type of analysis, we obtain results which are not useful here, even though they may correctly describe and explain the behavior of that one commodity. 

The forces at work in the determination of price are the same in both the single commodity and aggregative approaches, but the changed emphasis as regards substitution leads to quite different results. The substitution between one food commodity and all others in response to a price

And in point of fact the price of pork is stable in relation to all other foods. But from this single commodity analysis we have received little help in explaining how it is that the food price level swings up and down in a violent fashion relative to the nonfood price level. To find an explanation for the harmonious and extreme behavior of all food prices we must treat all foods as a unit. If within this unit or group there is a high degree of substitution and around it there exists a wall over which substitution may not take place, then the price level results should be sharp and meaningful. Fortunately these conditions are well satisfied for all foods.

As we said previously there will be some, perhaps a lot, perhaps a little, substitution between enterprises on a given farm or within a given farming area, but not between hog farming and banking. So we study all foods together as if they were one inclusive commodity, which they come close to being. And since we are dealing with one big commodity for which there are no close substitutes either at the consumption or production level, we may expect the demand for food as well as its supply to be extremely inelastic. Either you demand food or you don't: there are no close substitutes which come into play when its price changes relative to nonfood items. It is from this background of ideas that our theory of farm price behavior springs. We turn now to the interwar experience to test the theory.

Past theory would lead us to believe that output should influence price and price should in turn influence output. Here the responsive relationship seems all but broken down.

Beginnings of an Operational System 

Having observed the behavior of the more important variables affecting the food price level in the interwar period, we now want to combine certain of them into an operational system to explain specific price movements. In other words, we seek to develop a systematic method, using time series data, which uniquely determines the price-quantity position of the aggregative commodity, food, when measures of the influencing factors are known. As a first step in the building of such an operational system, we introduce the concept of aggregate demand. The aggregate demand for food may be defined as a functional relationship between (1) the price (index) of all food and (2) the quantity (index) of all food, which states the amount of food that will be demanded at each level of food prices. 

The pure concept of demand, which defines the amount of a given commodity that buyers stand ready to take at varying prices, assuming all outside influences constant, is not too difficult. But statistical measures, that must always be after the fact, of those amounts are most difficult to derive. And in this case the problem is given a new twist, since we are dealing with aggregates. But from experience we know that food consumption, the amount taken, is affected by prices, incomes, and changing tastes through time. The Problem Defined III: The Supply Problem 

More important than the failure of the demand supply functions to effect perfectly accurate price solutions for the interwar years is the problem of the time period in which the operational system may be employed. In fact, we will argue that the divergence between the realized price level and the price solution indicated by the intersection of demand and supply in the operational system is not in the nature of an error or discrepancy, but rather reflects the difference between Marshall's market price and the short run normal price.¹ In this case, the actual price as reported in the prices received index is akin to the market price and the price intersection in the operational system to the short run normal price. 

But why so selective as to the time period in which the operational system may be used? If the technique is useful why can't it be used in any period? Why is its use limited to the 14 year period 1923-36 in the interwar period? The reason, and in the larger sense the problem, hinges on the conditions of supply. Just as Marshall had to develop time periods to bring meaning into the complexity of supply, so do we; and our position is particularly delicate in that we are dealing with dated time and statistical functions which are considerably less malleable than unquantified concepts.

Without embarking on a thorough going analysis of the supply problem at this point, it is perhaps possible to make clear the reasons for the limited application of the operational system as it now stands, and point out the direction which the analysis must take to make possible a more general formulation. The consumer supply function is developed on a per capita basis and takes into account the effects of foreign trade; hence, it may be related to the aggregate demand function where a total output function may not. We may then expect the consumer supply function to take statistical form when the aggregate demand is shifting and the conditions of supply remain constant, so that the resulting price-quantity points trace out the unknown supply function. This does not mean that the supply function may not have slope, but rather that nothing may occur in the period under question which would cause the slope or position of the supply function to change. For if the determinants of

Rather they become the intersectional points on different and unknown supply functions.

Now what are the principal determinants of the consumer supply function? By all odds the most important one must be the aggregate output of food itself—the output of food from farms in the United States. If the aggregate output of food is changing, for reasons other than uncertain weather, then we know the consumer supply function must be shifting; hence, the intersectional points resulting from a shifting demand cannot trace out a supply function. Other determinants may enter, such as foreign trade, but obviously the first determinant to check is the aggregate output function for food. 

From prior knowledge the author felt confident that the conditions of supply held constant over the period 1923-36. We have seen in figure 4 how total output held constant over the period 1923-36, which would seem to indicate that the conditions of supply had not changed. Actually such evidence is not conclusive and neither do the data need to be so circumscribed. The significant point is that the aggregate output function for food must not have moved, regardless of its slope or elasticity, during this period. Now knowing or feeling certain that the principal determinant of the consumer supply function was positionally fixed over the period 1923-36, it seemed proper to try to derive a consumer type supply function from historical data for that period. It was derived and it worked out rather well in historical testing. 

At the present stage of the analysis, we are forced to operate within periods where the conditions of supply are known to be constant. For only in such a period may a consumer supply function be derived to relate to the aggregate demand function and thus complete the operational system. The supply problem is thus posed: (1) how may the aggregate output function be derived statistically? and (2) how does this function shift through time? For if the operational system is to be freed from specific and limiting time periods, we must learn how to move from one period to another. Stated differently, until a shifting supply function has been incorporated into the operational system, it remains incomplete. 

The Aggregate Output Function 

An aggregative output function for agriculture may be constructed with some meaning and facility. But an aggregate demand function for the product of agriculture is not so easily constructed, and once constructed along compromised lines, it has little economic meaning. Thus, we have derived an aggregate demand for food alone, and we now seek an aggregate output function for food alone. This procedure weakens the analysis in one way: Some substitution takes place in the production process between tobacco and cotton on one hand and food commodities on the other; hence, the output curve for total food may be somewhat more elastic than the output curve for the total product of agriculture. But a 26

meaningful demand function related to the output function is needed to complete the analysis; thus, there seems to be little choice but to accept an output function which represents something less than the aggregate product of agriculture.

Since the quantity data we shall be working with in the empirical analysis are realized data (the plotted observations in figure 9 represent the amount of product that farmers are induced to produce by the prices received during the planning phase, modified by plus and minus influencing factors at work during the production period), in order to test the aggregative hypothesis it is necessary that one critically important analytical requirement be satisfied. That requirement is that the demand curve moves along, and the stimulus-response sequences thus initiated occur along a positionally fixed schedule of intentions to produce. It should be clear why this analytical requirement must be satisfied if we are to obtain a true output curve. For should the schedule of intentions to produce, the output curve, move and the demand curve remain fixed, the resulting points would trace out a demand curve. And if, as it may happen, both unknown curves are shifting, we obtain nothing more than a line connecting an historical series of price-quantity points. So if we are to test the hypothesis that the aggregate output curve is severely inelastic using realized data, we must find a period or periods in which (1) aggregate demand is shifting, and (2) changes in productivity, changes in output per unit of input, are at a minimum.

Shifting the Aggregate Output Function 

Since we know the slope of the aggregate food output function to be similar to the curves AA, BB, CC, and EE and we also know by reason of the position of the realized output points that the output function is drifting to the right, it follows that a curve perfectly inelastic or nearly so could logically be drawn through each of the annual observations 1937, 1938, 1939, 1940, 1941, 1942, and 1943. We must now face up to the problem: How it is that the output function remains stable or fixed in a given quantity position over a number of years, then shifts to the right to a new and more productive position, sometimes remaining in this new position for another series of years, or in another context continues to shift to the right taking up successively more productive positions each year. To explain adequately pricing behavior in agriculture we must provide an explanation for this shifting action. 

It will be remembered that an hypothesis was set forth in Section I which said that the movement of the output function is related to the movement of the demand function, and it is the movement of demand which is causal. Given a situation wherein a pool of unused technologies and "know how" exists, or the introduction of an innovation sufficiently important to force a reorganization in the productive organization occurs; add to this a strengthening in demand conditions, a shift in the demand function to the right, and we may expect the output function itself to shift to the right. The strengthening in demand induces operators to 33

invest in new technologies, and the new technologies in turn increase the output per unit of input. On the other hand, a contraction in demand, a shift of the demand function to the left, does not result in a reversing action by the output function. 

Observing the shifting action of the aggregate output curve in figure 9 and reviewing the economic history of the period between 1912 and 1950, it is evident that each movement by the output curve to the right occurred: (1) as the demand for food in the aggregate was increasing, and (2) in conjunction with a significant technological development at the production level. There are periods within the historical range 191250, however, when demand conditions were strengthening or at least maintaining a strong position, when the output function does not shift to the right as in 1918-1920, 1925-1929, and 1944-1947. Demand was increasing in each of those periods but not output per worker, consequently the output function does not shift to the right under the stimulus of increasing demand. It is also abundantly clear from figure 9 that the output curve does not shift back to the left with a contraction in demand. 

Neither does it assume a more elastic position, slope downward and to the left. With a decline in demand the resulting quantity points trace out a curve as nearly perfectly inelastic as it is possible to draw a curve. Falling prices simply fail to induce a smaller output. Thus, the cushioning or braking action of a contracting supply present, in theory at least, in the usual demand-supply relationship does not come into play to help stabilize the farm price level.

The basic technological development occurring on farms in the 191250 period was the development of the gasoline engine with all the accessories surrounding it. The strong trend toward mechanical power on farms diverted thousands of acres of land and other resources away from the production of feed for horses and mules to the production of food for human consumption. It is estimated, for example, that 50 million acres were released for food production between 1919 and 1944.9 

The number of tractors on farms increased rapidly between 1912 and 1916, and reached such total numbers by 1917 as to affect production techniques, especially on the Great Plains. Also, the number of acres harvested increased between 6 and 7 per cent in the 2-year period 1916-18, a factor which was probably more significant than the gasoline engine in effecting a jump in worker productivity in these years. Thus an explanation is offered for the shift in the output curve from the position, AA, to the position, BB, between 1917 and 1918. And if the period 

1917-19 had not been a war period, during which machinery and equipment supplies for domestic use became extremely scarce, it is possible that the output curve would have continued to shift to the right. For, other things being equal, farm operators probably would have purchased and placed in operation a greater number of tractors and other machinery than they actually did. But aggregate output did not respond to the

Consequently the outlet for the greatly expanded demand took the form of higher prices. 

When the demand for food in the aggregate drops precipitously in 1921 and 1922, the output curve does not shift back to the left and take up the original AA position. Rather, the resulting quantity points trace out a curve perfectly inelastic as the unseen demand curve in figure 9 works down a perfectly inelastic schedule of intentions to produce. Following the business recession in 1921 and 1922 the demand for all foods expands once again, but contrary to the war experience we do not obtain an extreme price response. With the increase in aggregate demand the output curve shifts to the right taking up a new and more productive position, CC.

The aggregate food output curve does not retrace the 1918-22 phase for one important reason, the gasoline engine. The gasoline engine became an important source of power on farms during the 1920's as tractors on farms increased from approximately 250 thousand in 1920 to nearly one million in 1930. Consequently considerable productive capacity was released from the production of feed grains and hay for work stock to the production of food products for human consumption. And these increases in output per unit of input made possible by the gasoline engine and made practical by a strong demand literally jumped the output curve to the right into the more productive position, CC. 

The demand for food, however, continued strong throughout the 1920's, and tractors and power machinery were added to the national farm plant at a rapid rate over the entire 10-year period. Why is it then, that the output curve does not continue to shift to the right between 1925 and 1928 when the conditions of a strong demand and the proper technological base would seem to be satisfied? Frankly, we are not sure. 

But two considerations may be involved. First, although the general purpose tractor was introduced in 1924, it was not widely known or used until the middle and late 1930's. And it may well be that simply adding old type tractors with wide treads was not enough. Once the older type of tractor had forced a reorganization in productive organization in the Great Plains, it was spent as an efficiency producing force for its design severely limited its use in the Corn Belt. Second, although prices were high absolutely as compared with the "Golden Age of Agriculture," 1910-14, they were not high relative to prices and price expectations immediately following World War I when heavy indebtednesses were incurred by farmers in the purchase of high priced land. The outlook of farm people was dampened in the late 1920's so that they did not purchase and put into operation the amount of new machinery and equipment that the abstract conditions of figure 9 would seem to indicate. 

The aggregate demand for food did not continue to expand and force the level of food prices to the dizzy heights obtained in 1920. The demand 35

for food began to fall in 1930 and it fell violently in the early 1930's. 

But here we observe that the output curve does not skip back to the 1918-22 phase, BB position, in a reversing action with a contraction in demand. With the decline in demand that occurred in the early 1930's the empirically drawn aggregate output curve traces out a perfectly inelastic curve. The output function in this phase is a classic example of sustained output. Being blessed or cursed with relatively high overhead costs and low operating costs, farmers maintained production at or near the capacity levels achieved in the 1920's. If the costs of a business enterprise are largely sunk costs as they are in farming-land, family labor supply, heavy machinery and breeding stock-there is little incentive to restrict the total output of the enterprise. In fact, there may be an incentive to increase output, or try to increase output, in an effort to maintain income as prices are falling. 

During the early 1930's technologies continued to pour forth, but at the experimental level rather than the production level. Again those developments were organized around the gasoline engine. Important innovations also occurred in the field of biology. But in the main they were not put into practice. The demand and price situation did not warrant their adoption. Hence, a pool of technologies and "know how” accumulated during the early 1930's, the various items of which began to find their way into individual farm operations between 1937 and 1939. But when farm prices and incomes advanced with the war boom, 1940-42, the latent technologies were jammed into practice. 

The period, 1937-43, is the perfect example of the output skipping action postulated in Section I. The aggregate demand for food became increasingly stronger over the period creating the favorable price and income expectations necessary to heavy investments in machinery, equipment and new methods. As a consequence farm productivity rose over the 

7-year period. By 1944, then, we obtain a situation wherein aggregate output at any given price is substantially greater than was the case in 1936. This situation is described in more vivid terms by Barton and

The great price advances, which did not materialize between 1937 and 1943 because each time the demand function shifted to the right the output function shifted with it, did come into being between 1944 and 1948. The output function stopped shifting to the right after 1944, but aggregate demand continued to expand; hence, the farm price level mounts with each succeeding year between 1944 and 1948. It might appear that the war effort blocked a further expansion of the aggregate output function in that extensive investments in machinery and new technologies were shut off. But we must recognize that the aggregate output function did not resume its expanding action in the postwar period of high level demand, 1946-48. In retrospect it would appear that the burst of technological development organized primarily around the tractor and hybrid seed corn, was spent by 1944; the national farm plant was organized into the best adapted production organization by 1944 so that further investments in machines, equipment and new methods did not increase productivity. Upon the achievement of, or the realization of, the best adapted production organization, further increases in demand simply traced out a new aggregate output function, EE.

As we have already observed from table 1, worker productivity in agriculture began to increase once again in 1949. But this time the aggregate output function for food does not shift to the right. It contracts modestly to the left. But we would not expect the aggregate ouput function to long remain in the suggested position, FF, given the war climate existing during the winter of 1950-51. We would expect worker productivity to increase greatly and the aggregate output function for food to once again begin drifting to the right. This expectation rests on three foundation stones: (1) the great variety of recent and prospective technological developments,¹¹ (2) the war demand for food inducing a widespread adoption of these technologies on farms, but (3) the war effort favoring extensive investments in these new technologies. In short, all the elements necessary to the expansion of food output-a shift in the aggregate output function to the right-appear to be present in 1951. 

Relation to the Operational System 

Now that we have some knowledge of the aggregate output function for food, perhaps we should look more closely at the relationship between it and the consumer supply function. It has already been pointed out why we must have this go-between concept of the consumer supply function. But a more definite statement concerning that relationship should be helpful, and it will provide a necessary link to the complete operational system of the next section.

Particular circumstances of the market at the retail level may enter the picture and distort the relationship between the aggregate output and consumer supply functions in any given period. This is the continu-

ing problem of normal versus market phenomena, and in all cases our functional relationships represent normal relationships. Recognizing the possibility of discrepancies on this score and then promptly ignoring them, the consumer supply function differs from the aggregate output function in that the former: (1) is constructed on a per capita basis and takes into consideration changes in the civilian population, and (2) is based on the net food supply after the effects of imports and exports have been taken into account. Except for war years, these differentiating forces have been of minor significance. Basically, the controlling force is the aggregate output of food. 

Mechanically there are some difficulties in transferring a measure of the force of aggregate output to that of consumer supply. The consumer supply function relates to the price ratio, food prices to nonfood prices, and the aggregate output function to "responsible prices," an adaptation of prices received by farmers for food. Most simply stated, a shift in the aggregate output function cannot be transferred directly to the consumer supply function since they do not have common axes. A more sophisticated measure is required to relate the controlling force of aggregate output to the consumer supply function. This we achieve through a straight line of regression relating the variables (1) the index of per capita food consumption and (2) the index of food production for sale and home use.12 Now an expansion or contraction in aggregate output can be made to affect the consumer supply function which has as its X axis the per capita consumption of food. To repeat again, the consumer supply function has no significance in itself. It was developed solely as a mechanical link in the operational system, and perhaps greater insight into these problems will provide a technique for eliminating it. 

But the importance of the relationship between the consumer supply function and the aggregate output function should be dawning. If a clear relationship exists between these two functions, and we have a measure of the controlling one, then we can formulate and shift the dependent function in accordance with the dominating one. Such a development frees the operational system from the limitation of specific time periods. 

Its application is now much wider; our systematic method may be used to explain price level movements both within periods where the conditions of supply hold constant and between periods where the conditions of supply are changing.

IV: An Operational Theory of Farm Price Behavior The Parts in Relation to the Whole 

To this point we have been engaged in constructing an operational system for explaining price level movements in agriculture, in developing theory, in devising relationships, and in preliminary testing. Our plan now calls for the development of a complete system which can be used to explain past price level movements, for analyzing current price move- 12 The equation for this line of regression is given by Y 61.34 + .398X when Y = the index of per capita food consumption and X = the index of production for sale and farm home consumption. 38

ments, and for considering, but not predicting prospective price movements. But it seems advisable at this point to review the steps in the analysis to see the whole system in perspective. We will not be concerned here with the technique of construction, but rather with the relationship of each part to the whole. 

First the aggregate demand function for food: it sets forth a functional relationship between the ratio of retail food prices and nonfood prices on one hand and the per capita consumption of food on the other. From this relationship we can indicate in precise terms the quantity changes per capita food consumption associated with changes in the price ratio. If, for example, food prices fall and nonfood prices hold constant, a common historical occurrence, the price ratio will decline and the increase in per capita food consumption associated with that change is uniquely determined.

But this does not mean that the aggregate demand for food is stable, that the function does not move. The price-quantity relation described above holds for any given position of the demand function. Our knowledge of the shifters of demand, however, make it clear that the demand function is forever changing its position. It is the moving, causative force in the entire analysis.¹ As the disposable incomes of consumers increase and decrease, the aggregate demand function for food expands and contracts. And because of the extreme inelasticity of the function, assuming no change in quantity, a small increase in demand forces a large rise in price and vice versa. Also, as we observed in the last section, an expanding aggregate demand for food encourages investments in agriculture and hence induces increases in output, both in the aggregate and on a per capita basis. Thus, a restless, shifting demand for food provides the initiating force, the consequences of which we attempt to measure in terms of aggregate output, per capita consumption, and the various price indices.

For purposes of completing the operational system the numerous supply concepts presented in the previous chapter may be organized in the following manner: For any given period, the aggregate output of food is constant, fixed, and sustained. In economic terms, the aggregate output function is perfectly inelastic, the total output of food changes not one bit as the index of responsible prices increases or decreases. This condition in any given period does not, however, extend over the four decades of the analysis. The aggregate output function takes up a new position each time worker productivity changes significantly. The question then arises: What causes this perfectly inelastic output function to shift to the right or to expand? It expands whenever investments in new and more efficient technologies cause worker productivity to increase, the laboring force being unchanged. This investment usually takes place when the demand for food is expanding.

But the aggregate output function cannot be used directly in the operational system. That system is constructed throughout on a per capita basis; thus, the aggregate output function must be converted into a per capita concept, which we do, and re-name the consumer supply function. 

Within the operational system then, the consumer supply function will be shifted when and to the extent that the aggregate output function is found to have shifted.

Analytically, the system is complete. An aggregate demand function and an aggregate supply function have been defined conceptually, measured statistically, and a technique has been developed for shifting them. 

But operationally it is not complete. We must consider the final link, the relationship between the price ratio and the index of prices received by farmers for food. The statistical analysis has been developed in terms of a ratio of retail food prices to nonfood prices, which theoretically is a valid concept, but in terms of policy or action it is rather roundabout. 

We want price solutions in terms of the conventional prices received index. This we achieve through the formulation of a relationship between the price ratio and prices received, a relation which has some economic meaning but which here takes the form of an operational gadget. 

Where this latter relationship is good the operational system provides useful or representative solutions in terms of the index of prices received. 

But, unfortunately, this relation is not always too close, and in those cases the final solutions tend to stray from the historical record. 

The Complete System 

The graphic presentation of the complete operational system, figure 10, takes as its point of departure the estimated price-quantity points of 1936. In other words, the demand function, D36D36, and the consumer supply function, CS of figure 8, are transferred inviolate to figure 10. 

Thus a price ratio of 100.0 (or prices received of 104.3) and a per capita consumption of 99.6 provide a jumping off place for the analysis which follows.

This immanent method is used for definite reasons. Summary 

Extreme price level movements in agriculture are normal and are to be expected. Basically they grow out of the inability of consumers to adjust quantities taken or of producers to adjust quantities provided in response to price level movements where total food is concerned. In this context, where both curves in the demand and supply cross are highly inelastic, any small shift in one or the other sends the farm price level scooting.

We know that the aggregate demand for food most often fluctuates around a positionally fixed supply curve. In other words the index of per capita food consumption does vary at any given price level as per capita disposable income varies; this in no way conflicts with the prior statement that the quantity of food consumed varies only slightly with price level changes. Now these shifts in demand, usually small in amount, give rise to extreme price movements. This is our principal point; a shift in the aggregate demand for food does not have to be large to force an important price change. 44

For example, the per capita consumption of food declined modestly through the early years of the Great Depression. This decrease, albeit a modest one, in the amount consumed cannot be related to a change in food prices, for we know that the food price level fell during the period 1929-32, and even where demand is inelastic falling prices would lead to some expansion in the amount taken. Since that important determinant of demand, personal disposable incomes, was contracting over the period 1929-32, we conclude that the decline in per capita food consumption represented a contraction in the aggregate demand for food. 

For any given price, the absolute number of dollars allocated to the purchase of food declined over the period 1929-32, which had the effect of reducing slightly the number of pounds consumed and causing consumers, insofar as they were able, to substitute low resource-using foods for high resource-using foods, such as fresh vegetables for dairy products. 

Now this contraction in the aggregate demand for food between 1929 and 1932 was not large, about 12 per cent. But this 12 per cent contraction, gave rise to a fall in prices of 52 per cent. A small shift in aggregate demand can take a nation from a condition of shortages and high prices to a condition of surpluses and low prices. And we saw just the reverse occur during the winter of 1950-51. 

We have seen that the aggregate output function sometimes drifts to the right, expands, in response to a strengthening of aggregate demand. 

This occurs when important new technologies are being invested in and adopted on farms generally. The period 1937-43 beautifully illustrates this type of development. And where the output function is shifting to the right, it exerts a moderating influence on prices. 

Supply responses in the aggregate, and we assume for the farm firm, do not typically occur along a fixed supply curve with a positive slope. 

Supply responses typically occur through technological advances, where a new combination of resources makes possible a greater output from a fixed amount of resources. Thus, the aggregate supply function shifts to the right when expectations are bright and investments in new methods are widespread.

But once a new position is achieved, the aggregate supply curve does not contract to the left in the face of a contracting demand. It does not because farmers do not stop using new methods in which their capital is sunk or about which a habit is formed. Consequently when a burst of technological development is spent and the output function ceases to expand, the aggregate demand function then fluctuates around a positionally fixed and a highly inelastic supply curve. For this fundamental reason, we argue, that price instability in agriculture is the norm. The peculiar inelasticity of both aggregate demand and aggregate supply, give agriculture a built-in price instability. Any small movement in the usual causative element, aggregate demand, gives rise to a substantial price level change.

Employing a modified version of the analytical method developed in Section II, we can measure the price level consequences of expanding aggregate food expenditures by some amount, say $1 billion. But before we trace the consequences of such an expansion we must know or assume something about the economic situation in which that action is to take place. The possible situation we have in mind is a severely depressed year to which we give the name 195d. The economic dimensions of this depressed year run as follows: Gross National Product, $170 billion; personal disposable income, $130 billion; and prices received by farmers for food, 123 on the index. Further, this is conceived to be a stable situation, one in which the general pattern is carried throughout the year, although that stability derives from continuous income injections on the part of the Federal Government. 

We are now in a position to estimate the extent to which the index of prices received by farmers for food may be increased by expanding the total expenditure for food by $1 billion. The action involved is that of raising the expenditures of low income consumer units to a level where a defined list of foods may be purchased, the Food Allotment Program. 

With few exceptions the analytical method is similar to that developed and employed in Sections II and IV. The first exception arises out of the fact that there are two separate and distinct reactions on the part of the demand function to a program which increases the food expenditures of low income consumers by $1 billion.
