CHAPTER II SUMMARY

It is the purpose of this report to discuss the recommendations of the Commission as they have been ascertained to date from the point of view of problems and opportunities of implementation, and the proposed strategies of change for bringing these recommendations into effect.

Assumptions of theory. It will be important to identify, at the beginning, certain key assumptions which we find in the writings of the Commission staff which concern both the nature of crime and criminal justice, on the one hand, and the process by which the formal criminal system may be changed, on the other hand. Identifying these assumptions is a way both of pointing to some of the problems of implementation (which will be discussed in Chapter 3), to which some of these assumptions seem to lead, and, additionally, to make clear our own starting points where they differ from those of the Commission.

We find these views concerning the nature of crime and the system of formal criminal justice: 

1. It is possible to treat the formal criminal system in isolation from the rest of society. Such an assumption seems to underlie all of those recommendations which would look towards improved operations of the formal criminal system, without taking into account the effects of those changes on other systems or on our society as a whole. We find this to be true particularly in regard to those recommendations which seek to draw into the criminal system other groups--such as mental health, welfare, and social work organiza-

tions--who are not currently central to the system. 

It is also implicit in the phrase "war on crime", which seems to direct a good deal of the Commission's work, it is at least suggested by this phrase that crime can be reduced or eliminated without significant change in the fundamental institutions of our society. 

We will begin from the assumption that we are as unlikely to be able to wage effective war on crime without working toward fundamental institutional change in our society as we are to be able to wage an effective war on the parking problem without fundamental change in transportation systems or an effective war on divorce without change in the institution of marriage. 

2. In various places the writings of the Commission suggest different values for the system of criminal justice, and these values occasionally conflict in ways that stand in the way of implementing recommendations. 

Perhaps the most important of these conflicts is that between the value of "efficiency" in the operations of the system and of "justice" or equity afforded by the system. For example, the effort to reduce delay in court operations runs counter to the feeling of some judges and lawyers that inefficiencies in the administration of justice have a positive value, since they provide opportunity for more complete defense. The introduction of more lawyers into the system in order to provide counsel where none is being provided now would aim at increasing equity in the distribution of justice, but

would threaten to introduce further delays into the system.

Conflict between these values is reflected in conflict among parts of the formal criminal system itself. 

3. Several different models, both of crime and of criminal justice, seem to underlie the work of the Commission and to reflect themselves now in one set of recommendations, now in another.

The first of these may be called the regulatory model, according to which crime is a breach of the law; the process of criminal justice consists of detection, apprehension, trial, and punishment; the purpose of punishment is deterence; and the system of criminal justice is seen as a mechanism of formal control, according to law. 

In this sense, an ideal prototype of criminal justice would be the system of traffic control. This model would appear to underlie those of the Commission's recommendations that move toward increased efficiency of operations, and particularly those which would intensify repressive and deterrent techniques available to the police.

According to a second model, crime is a form of deviance or disease; the system of criminal justice consists of detection, apprehension, trial and treatment; and treatment consists in appropriate therapy conducted in institutions for that purpose.

According to a third model, crime is a form of social alienation and is to be taken as a symptom of something

wrong with the community as a whole. The system of criminal justice, then, is a form of community rehabilitation which brings the offender into the community and works toward improvement of the community as a whole. 

Those of the Crime Commission's recommendations which focus on the provision of "alternate tracks" and the mobilization of resources not now involved in criminal justice would appear to share one or the other of these models. 

Where problems of implementation suggest themselves, in particular, is where the logical consequences of these models, while they are apparent enough, have not been followed through in the recommendations themselves. Thus, for example, the provision of alternative tracks to prosecutors, based upon social service work-ups of defendants, would appear in the long run to lead to a system of criminal justice as therapy, in which the state functions as therapist. implications of this position for manpower skill and capacity in the fields of mental health and welfare have not been, in our view, adequately faced.

The 

Out of this discussion, our own presuppositions become equally clear: We begin by seeing crime and the formal system of criminal justice intimately linked with the society as a whole and with its other institutions. Prevention of crime, or its control, therefore We goes hand in hand with modification of other institutions. find multiple values for the system of criminal justice and several models under which to view it, and find that these, when pushed to their logical conclusions, are not necessarily consistent.

In addition to these assumptions about crime and criminal justice, the Commission seems to be making an assumption about the process of change, as well: That it is possible to adopt toward the system of criminal justice itself a regulatory model rather like the one adopted by that system toward the criminal. 

That is to say, that the system of criminal justice may be changed by the formulation of new law or policy and the imposition of that law or policy through sanctions and rewards, through "carrots and sticks". Along with the legislative model goes a certain mechanical view of change which would use formal criteria and explicit procedure as a means of assuring conformity to policy and would pursue each piece of change desired on its own merits and in relative isolation from other changes also desired. 

One of our principal findings, however, is that, for the most part, the implementation of the Commission's recommendations cannot be handled effectively on a piecemeal basis. The formal system of criminal justice is, in many respects, a tightly interconnected system. In order for most proposed changes to be carried out, others must be carried out as well. It is often fruitless to change one part of the system without taking into account effects of that change on other parts of the system. Therefore, although some useful piecemeal change may be effective, the Commission's fundamental strategy of change, we believe, should incorporate a plan capable of affecting the system as a whole. 

Findings and recommended approach. 

It should be clear in this report that our optimism about the feasibility of affecting

the system as a whole is a modified optimism. If we put aside, as the Commission has put aside, issues closely tied to the power structure of our society and of the cities in particular--namely those issues concerned with organized, with corporate, and with national crime--, then we believe it is possible to invent and carry out long-term strategies of developmental change which are designed to promote learning in and about the system. The effectiveness of this strategy of change will be limited by its confrontation with sources of power in our society not taken into account in this report.

From the point of view of feasible change, we have found it useful to organize the recommendations of the Commission into three basic categories; those aimed at 

1. improved operations in the formal criminal system, 2. mobilization of resources of the non-criminal system for prevention and control of crime, 3. increased equity in the administration of justice. 

The first of these areas of recommendation requires a different kind of social change than the second and third. It suggests different analogies for the process of change, raises different problems of implementation, and suggests different strategies of change and projects to test out those strategies. On the other hand, all three areas share problems of implementation relating to - leaders and champions of change, to --resources of manpower and dollars for change, --conflicts among parts of the formal criminal system and between the formal criminal system and other institutions,

--public and political climate for change.

1. 

Recommendations to improve operations of the formal criminal system.

These include more efficient procedures to promote faster flow of people through the system, methods of upgrading personnel, reorganization, and new information systems and management methods. 

Although there are a number of obstacles in the path of these recommendations, taken separately, our principal finding is that they would be unlikely to achieve their intended objectives in police departments, courts, and correctional institutions, unless they are treated as parts of an approach toward organizational development and renewal. New procedures and tools require organizational change and change in individual attitudes. 

Reorganization itself, without organizational development, tends simply to shift the locus of the problem. 

Upgraded personnel may simply cancel out one another or encounter increased frustration. If we look at the police, courts and correctional systems as organizations--with a hierarchy of management, functional divisions, resources, traditions and constraints-much can be learned from the experience of organizational development and renewal in industry. 

Many of the Commission's recommendations call for new forms of collaboration among elements of the criminal system

which are used to functioning in isolation from or in conflict with one another. Again, much can be learned from the experience of management and consultants in creating conditions for collaboration among conflicting persons and groups.

We suggest an approach to organizational development, and to related training, for police, courts and correctional institutions in pilot urban areas; and we outline the ways in which the Commission's recommendations for improved operations could be integrated into such an approach. 2. 

Recommendations to mobilize resources of the noncriminal system for prevention and control of crime. 

3. Recommendations to increase equity in the administration of justice. 

Both of these sets of recommendations call for new involvement of non-criminal system resources in problems of crime prevention and control. These resources include schools, mental hospitals, lawyers, social workers, welfare agencies, psychiatrists, business and civic groups, labor unions, and the like. But resources like these are already inadequate to the tasks they are being asked to perform. Moreover, they tend to reject involvement in the criminal system because of its dangers, its proneness to failure, and its status as a second-rate Recommendations which would funnel offenders activity.

into these resources must confront feed-back effects stemming

from the inability or unwillingness of these resources to participate in the criminal process.

In order to carry out these recommendations, organizations and individuals in fields like mental health, social work, and psychiatry must redefine both "crime" and their own functions. 

In order to attract competence of these kinds, the formal criminal. system must be given a visibility and centrality it is now, for the most part, lacking.

As a way of moving toward these ends, we suggest a total community model, building the correctional process and treatment of offenders into the organizations and activities of the city and coupling them to the activities of professional schools, who could use them to train new types of professional concerned with the criminal process. We suggest approaches to the establishment of such a community model, and indicate how the Commission's recommendations could be pursued in connection with it. 

These two sorts of change projects we call first-order strategies for implementing the Commission's recommendations.

But such efforts at systematic change cannot be undertaken everywhere at once; constraints in budget, competence, leadership, local acceptance, and legislative framework would prevent it. 

Moreover, the learning derived from such projects cannot be disseminated in a uniform or mechanical way.

It is necessary, therefore, to develop a second-order strategy of change, addressed to the following sorts of questions:

--What is the source of power and authority for change? --What are the institutional resources required for change? --Who are to be the agents and managers of change? --What are the resources required for change? --How is a national and local climate for change to be establis To this end we propose a developmental strategy of change, whic begins with those systematic pilot projects that can now be initiate and builds outward, step by step, as competence, interest, and acceptance can be established.

We present the outline of such a strategy, over a five year period, along with developing institutional and budgetary requirements.

A. Grouping Recommendations 

As we have suggested in Chapter II, the recommendations of the Commission staff fall into three basic categories: 

those directed at improving the operations of the formal criminal system. 

For example: recommendations to reduce delays in the operations of the courts, to introduce "command and control" technology into the police system, or to simplify and improve the organization of the courts. those directed at mobilizing resources of the non-criminal-system for prevention and control of crime.

For example: inducing educational, welfare and mental health extablishments to address themselves to problems of crime; encouraging prosecutors to employ "alternative tracks" to criminal proceedings, and providing them with resources to do so; or integration of the correctional process into the community. those directed at increasing equity in the administration of justice. 

For example: bail reform, and provision of counsel for all defendants. ...

A number of other recommendations, such as the regularization of "plea bargaining" or the three-part police agent - officer - community service officer distinction, cut across these three categories; and recommendations which fall well within one of the categories (such as integration of correctional processes into the community) may have indirect consequences affecting the others. Nevertheless, it is useful to group these recommendations together in terms of their principal impact. For each of these three categories of recommendation requires a different kind of social change, raises different problems of implementation, and suggests different strategies of change and projects to test out those strategies.

17. 

In all cases, a number of themes or factors emerge which affect the process of social change involved in implementing recommendations. These include: a) leaders and champions of change 

the availability of men of talent, energy and commitment to serve as entrepreneurs or champions for change in the system of criminal justice. the motivation of leaders of institutions, within and outside the system of formal criminal justice, to respond to initiative for change. b) resources for change 

the availability of resources of trained personnel, inside and outside the formal criminal system, able and willing to carry out the functions that recommended changes would require; the availability of trainers and educators for such persons; the availability of facilities for them and for their training. the availability of funds to initiate and provide continued support for change; short and long term, narrow and broad implications for cost,are critical c) relationships among parts of the formal criminal system, and between the formal criminal system and other institutions. 

the existence of implicit conflicts among institutions, at local, State and national levels, which would be affected by the proposed change.

- feelings of ownership and territoriality among vested interests 

inside and outside the formal criminal system: conflicts of authority associated with these feelings. attitudes toward crime and criminal justice shared by groups inside and outside the system; conflicting models or theories of crime and criminal justice. A particularly important conflict of this kind concerns justice or equity, and efficiency of operations. - legal, administrative and regulatory provisions which restrict change, present boundaries for change, or conflict with one another. d) public and political climate for change. 

the link between the system of formal criminal justice and the political process. Recommendations for change are apt to get caught up in the political process, sometimes in unexpected ways; in some cases, political support is essential to change. -- the salience of issues for particular localities (States, 

communities, or regions) which affect attitudes toward change in those localities. the climate of opinion, shared or promulgated by the press and by organized opinion leaders, toward proposed change.

These factors underlie the questions we ask, in the following pages, about proposed recommendations. They cluster in different ways around the three basic groups of recommendations. We will discuss them, as they have emerged from our interviews and discussions, but without attempting to relate all factors to each recommendation. 

One of our principal findings is that sensible strategies of change cannot be devised for the recommendations taken piecemeal. Again and again, it has become clear that, in order for one set of changes to be carried out, others must also be carried out; that it is fruitless to change one part of the criminal system without taking into account effects of that change on other parts of the system; and that strategies of change should be adopted which envisage the system as a whole and incorporate a plan by which multiple change efforts can reinforce one another over time and from one region to another, so that the system as a whole can be affected. 

One way of looking at the problem of implementing Commission recommendations, from this point of view, is in terms of a ladder or hierarchy of changes. With respect to any given recommendation or set of recommendations, it is possible to effect: a. 

change in formal procedures b.-- change in behaviour c. -- change in attitude d. change in operations of any one system (e.g. the courts) e.- 

change in operations of a larger system (e.g. the system of formal criminal justice) f.-- change in the social consequences of the system's functioning. 

Change in a. does not necessarily carry with it change in b. and so on down the line.

For example, institution of formal procedures to regularize plea bargaining does not mean the prosecutors will actually follow those procedures. To the extent that they follow those procedures, their attitude toward plea bargaining (e.g. their tendency to abuse it) may remain unchanged. If their attitude and behavior change, the operation of the courts may still remain unaffected (e.g. because of other offsetting factors). 

Change in operations of the courts, through regularized plea bargaining, may have adverse, unanticipated effects on the system of criminal justice as a whole, because resources are not available to follow up on offenders whose cases are disposed of in this way. Unanticipated social consequences for example, loss of respect for the courts - 

may result from effective implementation of all of these changes. 

In so far as possible, in considering a given recommendation, we have tried to look as far down the ladder as possible. This has led to the development of two principal strategies of change, designed to affect broad areas of the formal criminal system, and its relationship to outside resources. We believe the Commission's recommendations can best be pursued through these strategies, and in Chapters IV and V we indicate how we think this may be done.

In discussion of recommendations in this chapter, we have concentrated on problems of implementation, rather than on the positive aspects of proposed changes, in order to lay a groundwork for strategies of change. In our view, many of the Commission's recommendations which are vulnerable when treated separately, have a good chance of implementation when made part of a systematic strategy of change.

(2) Minimum training standards for police

This recommendation would require a minimum number of training hours; for example, a minimum of 400 hours of training for police recruits prior to assuming their duties. There is emphasis, as far as training content is concerned, in going beyond training in police mechanics to programs which help the police understand their role more effectively, particularly in areas such as community relations. 

Police give training low priority when they are unable to 7use current resources adequately (see -1- above). 

Training for what? Where are the preformance criteria for the functions of the police, particularly for the operations of the police in urban neighborhoods? How can we design training programs when there is broad uncertainty about police role? 

This is like the formulation of specifications for products, without performance criteria, e. g. specifying that building foundations must be 8" by 24", and made of concrete of a certain consistency, rather than indicating what loads the foundation must bear, what soil movements it must resist, what corrosion-resistance it must have. The development of performance criteria for tasks, as a way of designing sensible training programs, has been undertaken in the military and on the industrial production line. (see, for example, the training of Navy submarine crews). It has become a subject of concern in the field of health services. Better definition of performance required of health service workers, as against professional specifications of training, permits more effective use of manpower, more economic

training programs. (see the work of Thompson, at Yale medical school: experiments in Peace Corps training of health workers in Malawi).

In World War II, the military learned the value of clear definition of tasks, particularly for close knit teams such as submarine crews or command-and-control centers, and the development of measures of accomplishment associated with those tasks. Training programs were developed, then, to meet the performance requirements established by these definitions and measures. Term-training was introduced, and the use of simulations of various kinds as team-training devices. A similar training concept has gone into the development of training programs for SAGE system crews, and for NASA teams.

In the field of health services, particularly in remote areas such as Indian reservations in the United States and underdeveloped nations like Malawi inAfrica, efforts have been made to define the tasks to be performed by health workers and the measures of accomplishment for those tasks, rather than rely on traditional professional definitions of training and education requirements for health workers. The effect has been to permit development of more effective training programs and to open up the possibility of using sub-professionals for sets of tasks (e.g. testing for tuberculosis) previously handled only by professionals. Peace Corps training models are interesting, in general, with respect to the police; they are discussed at greater length in Chapter IV.

-~ One useful approach to the development of performance criteria would be through the formulation of user requirements for police training where the users are taken to be police managers, their subordinates, and members of the community they serve. This approach to user requirements would require participation by police management and subordinates, and the community served itself. It would require analysis of what police actually do in the neighborhoods they serve, how they, their superiors, and the community see their role; and, then, joint formulation of appropriate tasks and measures of accomplishment. It would constitute one starting point for police-community co-operation in crime prevention. Our experience in interviewing police in Massachusetts suggests that their suspicion of one another and of outsiders would make necessary considerable preliminary work in order to establish the degree of trust and openness which would permit free discussion of what the police actually do and are called upon to do. 

(See the description of the California School Systems Development 

Program, appended to this report, for a treatment of the relationship between user requirements, peformance criteria and specifications.) 

Will the training be congruent with (a) what the policeman sees going on around him in the system in which he operates, (b) with the idea of the police role shared by other officers and their supervisors, (c) with rewards and punishments used in the system; for example, promotion policy, and (d) with measures of performance levied on police peformance as a whole?

If the policeman sees operating around him a system that is corrupt, brutal or negligent (for example, with respect to the making of arrests; see comments under -1-, above), then training which runs counter to what he sees will strike him as irrelevant, or lead him to behavior for which he may be punished in the force.

The training of industrial foremen, in a number of well-known studies proved irrelevant or harmful when the foremen re-entered a job in which the attitudes of others (bosses and subordinates) were unchanged. These foremen found themselves caught in a squeeze between workers and managers, because neither group was prepared for the efforts at openness and co-operation which the foremen brought back with them.

Training may further confuse the police officer when he is already confused about the role he should be playing in the community. "There is a tendency for confusing roles. The policeman often is confused as to whether he should be a law enforcement officer or a therapist in the community." (Lecturer, Police Academy)

Training in community relations, particularly, will confuse as long as this role confusion remains. "This is too much for me!" (policeman; reaction to community relations lecture) 

What is the motivation of the policeman to accept training? Is he motivated to "get ahead"? To be sure he is motivated to memorize enough material to pass the civil service examination for promotion. But training in how to memorize material for tests seems irrelevant to anything else he has to do. 

The credibility of training will be tied to his view of what he is really called upon to do, how he is rewarded or punished, and what others expect of him. 

This goes both to the need for police management to accept and act on the values built into training; and to the need for them to be able to implement their convictions in this....

respect. Civil Service regulations and administrative procedure must permit reward and punishment on the basis of merit, as concepts of merit are built into training. "The civil service thing is a problem. Civil service law makes it impossible to penalize and remove without endlessly complicated proceedings. The police should be a quasi-military organization, with an enormous amount of special training. We must have educated policemen. But we have to be able to reward and punish them under civil service." (candidate for Attorney General, Massachusetts)

If training does not fit into the actual system of rewards and punishments, and make connection with the demands of work as they are seen, it will become a ritual activity, undertaken listlessly (if it is required) or eventually ignored. 

Something very much like this has happened to professional and sub-professional training programs in both government and industry. In 1965, the University of 

Michigan surveyed professional training at the National Bureau of Standards, where they found that many of the programs of technical and professional training which had been developed over time at the Bureau, and which had been consistently used by workers there, appeared to the staff to be formal or ritual means of promotion. 

They did not, in many instances, mesh with the actual work done, and they did not meet the staff's sense of need for professional development and organizational vitality. 

Analysis of those staff members and groups judged to be exceptionally effective did not show that training correlated with effectiveness. In general, those judged to be more effective were younger staff members who had taken less training and had come more recently to the Bureau.

Similarly, training cannot successfully counteract deeply held values and attitudes of the police (such as, "It's us against them".), if everything else about the system reinforces them.

Training cannot effectively compete with measures of police performance if it advocates behavior counter to those measures. If training advocates more diligent

police work, which leads to high arrest, and therefore crime rates, while police departments are rewarded for low crime-rates, it will tend to be ignored. 

All of this simply indicates that training programs proceed effectively as part of a systems change in organization, which includes work at changing measures of performance, rewards and punishments, promotion policy, inhibiting regulations, attitudes of management and the like. Our understanding of the proposed distinction is as follows: the police agent is a high-powered fighter of serious crime, who replaces the detective, but with broader scope; the police officer performs more nearly menial, routine, trooperlike functions, under the police agent; the community service officer, who is neither uniformed nor armed, is a sort of in-house social worker who specializes in community relations and enforces minor breaches of codes. 

We understand the distinction to be aimed at (a) more efficiency in operations through specialization of function, (b) a more effective career ladder, in which a kind of elite level is made available in the form of the police agent, (c) assurance that police will perform the community relations function. 

The distinction may help to clarify confusion of role, for the police, and provide a starting point for the development of performance criteria for police operations which could be the basis of training design. 

Nevertheless, out of the interviews, and our reflections on them, a series of questions arise which suggest approaches to testing out the recommendation.

(6) Simplifying Court Organization 

The Commission staff recommends strong central administrative supervision of the state systems of the courts and the introduction of modern management techniques, both of which are designed to rationalize the court system and to reduce delays, to provide more equitable justice, and to provide for more efficient use of manpower. There is no direct statement concerning the method by which more effective organization is to be adopted but reference to the role of local communities suggests that the Commission intends to enunciate policies along this line and leave to local communities the problem of adapting them to their needs. 

One of the problems about the measure to improve efficiency in the courts is the fact that the courts have intimate dealings with the political process. 

Lower administrative personnel, such as Clerks of Court, are frequently political appointees. Powerful administrative persons, such as the Clerk of the Supreme 

Court of Massachusetts, can be prime actors in the political process in the State, using their position in the courts as a sinecure and a base from which to conduct political operations. What this means is that the courts have functions in the political system in addition to their open function in the judicial system. It raises questions such as these: 11 Who has incentives, within the courts, to adapt measures to promote efficiency? Why should a Clerk of Court, for example, whose disposition will be to milk the tasks given to him and to prolong the job, adopt measures designed to speed the judicial process?

If the political pressures within the courts are strong, and the political

interests of administrative persons within them are high, then when efforts to improve judicial administration are "left to the communities" the likelihood is that these measures will be either pro forma or ignored. -- What will be the systems effects of changes designed to make the judicial process more efficient? If the court becomes more adept at processing cases and turning out processed offenders, what will be the effect on correctional institutions' capabilities to handle these? What about the capabilities of the existing resources of probation officers, social workers and the like? What estimates can be made of the numbers of persons to be processed in these ways as a result of increase in court efficiency? 

As members of the Commission have pointed out, there are values in the judicial process other than efficiency. The use of delays by defense counsel to permit opportunity for the raising of further questions, or to gain time for the preparation of better defense or to provide every opportunity for the client, must be balanced against the image of a rationalized and well-run judicial system, just a rationalized efficiency of the system must be balanced against inefficient but informally effective operations elsewhere in the formal criminal system. 

In general, organization experience in industry and government agencies and.in every large-scale formal organization suggests the fruitlessness of the attempt to introduce administrative changes or management techniques with efforts at overall organizational development and change (see Chapter IV). The same arguments apply to the need for training and retraining of personnel within the system even at the level of judges, lawyers, and administrative workers. The fact that these are college gradiates and white collar workers does not mean that they are necessarily more quipped to handle the special problems of the judicial

system than are the police to handle the special problems of law enforcement. D. Recommendations aimed at mobilizing resources outside of the formal criminal system for prevention and control of crime. 

These include, (1) Encouragement of education and welfare establishments to think about and respond to criminal problems. (2) Encouragement of prosecutors and judges to use tracks other than those of the formal criminal system in order to funnel cases into the community; and provision of resources for them to do so. (3) Establishment of a youth service agency to stand between courts and police. (4) Establishment of new correctional facilities, closer to the community, with greater flexibility, so as to integrate the correctional process into the community.

These recommendations are aimed principally at attracting new forms of competence to the handling of criminal problems. By and large, problems of implementation associated with them have to do with, 

the shortage of competence in the institutions and fields we wish to attract. the fragmentation of existing competence within institutional, educational, professional, and (in some cases) legal boundaries. conflict of these resources with one another and with elements. of the formal criminal system. the aura of isolation, second-rateness, and proneness to failure which makes the criminal system a magnet for incompetence, and wards off first-rate resources from other fields.

Social inventions aimed at overcoming these problems are required for implementation of this category of recommendations. A proposed approach is contained in Chapter V. 

(1) Encourage prosecutors and judges to funnel cases out of the criminal process and into the community, and give them the resources to do so. 

This recommendation concerns decisions to arrest, charge, and sentence. 

It recommends the availability of the option for alternate tracks to criminal prosecution and for disposition without trial. It would give the prosecutor and judge new information on which to make such judgments, and would co-ordinate noncriminal resources, such as mental health workers, social workers, psychiatrists, with the administration of justice. It proposes formal procedures to effect this co-ordination. 

A substantive objection, which turned up in the interviews, particularly among lawyers, has to do with the value of discovery of new facts about the defendant, for both sides, and the possible narrowing of trial issues. 

It may well be that it is not in the best interests of the defendant to have this lengthy investigation reveal facts which may not be in the interest of the successful defense of the case. If all the facts are made available in a pre-trial discovery process which relies heavily on a social service kind of investigation, many facts related to the crime, whether or not the defendant actually committed the crime, could be uncovered and used against him. The Commission has been confronted by a number of dilemmas. crime can be considered as something apart from the other institutions of our society, and attacked by them as an enemy; or it is part and parcel of our institutions, so that any attack is reflexive: we declare war on ourselves. Either we attend to crime prevention and become the optimistic agents of cultural reform and development, or we attend to crime control and become the pessimistic agents of apprehension, punishment and repair.

It has been an explicit premise of American culture that such contradictions be institutionalized in a system of "checks and balances". 

One undesired consequence of that system is that it leads to a one-sided reliance on bargaining and compromise as our main means of resolving conflicts and dilemmas, and provides no impetus for a search for integrative resolutions.

Fragmentation of institutional efforts is one result;

cancelling-out of the efforts of one institution by those of another is a second result. Thus placing responsibility for crime rovention in one jurisdiction, and responsibility for the criminal process in another, reduces the internal dilemmas that each jurisdiction must shru with. The emphasis throughout this report on strategies of organizational, social, and cultural development and change is a direct response to the multiple dilemmas which are inherent in the criminal system. What have been singled out as "problems of the criminal system" are inevitable consequences of the values, institutional mechanisms, and mentality of our society. The basic question of implementation confronting the Commission is: what can be done to change American culture in the direction of greater human dignity? And this is the problem confronting all the Great Society programs.

The strategies, theoretical basis, and objectives of modern approaches to cultural change towards greater human dignity can be summarized as follows. Our present culture is relatively effective in shaping its members so that they will perform adequately as "cors" in highly structured organizational machines". W iddle class" relues, and mechanisms of child rearing and education, serve to put the mass o our children through a process out of which they emerge with a conscienco which is quite demanding and authoritarian. It operates so as to imose obedience to authority on the "ego", who feels guilty if he has an impulse to rebel against a superior. 

Compensation for this impositio. is provided in having subordinates upon whom the individual can in turn impose. The middle class family and our educational system stress respect for the authority of parents, teachers, and all who are in superior roles.

The fundamental key to personal and organizational development is iv in the concept that what is involved is personal growth. Repair (i.e. therapy) is not the major issue for organization members, though it is certainly a necessary first step for criminals or mental patients. either is it a matter of changing the person in some arbitrary way. Personal education and organizational change must go together, since the reward and punishment system of the organization determines what the individual will do on a day-to-day basis. An educational experience fro which he learns a different way of behaving will fade out quickly if the organization punishes this way of behaving. In that sense, the organizat

Hence organization change is essential if individual behavioral change is desired.

Much has been written in recent years about the shortcomings of traditional approaches to education and organization change. The traditional approach to education emphasizes the teacher as expert and authority, the learner as passive and subordinate, the transaction as a purely intellectual one, and the test of learning by means of written examinations. The results: the learner learns to be subordinate and passive behaviorally, learns only the words involved in the content being taught rather than the behavioral skills, learns to be interested in passing and getting a certificate rather than in continued learning, learns to use his creative potential in finding ways of beating the system (e.g. cheating on the examination).

The emphasis on rote-learning in much police training, to the end that the police officer can pass a civil service examination for promotion, is in complete accord with the traditional approach to education generally.

In contrast, newer approaches to education stress involvement of the whole person, providing experiences which enable words and concepts to be translated into problem-solving behavior and the development of action skills, the learner as active experimenter and the teacher as consultant, not as disciplinarian, expert and judge. The successful experiments in organization development referred to earlier have contributed theory, methodology and technique to roving profession of organization development. Its approaches to change arc often called "organic", in contrast to mechanical approaches.

Organic approaches to change and education depend for their efficacy on different variables from those engaged in mechanical approaches. Here the term "re-education" can be applied to the individual, and the organization; both are conceived of as being capable of unlearning outworn practices, and learning new ones. A fundamental principle is the involvement of the person, group or organization (the "client system") in the identification of its own needs for change and development, and of its problems and sources of frustration. Further, the client system is involved in the development of means for meeting its needs or solving its problems, and in evaluation of the success of its efforts. Hence, positive motivation, rather than subordination, is the critical factor in learning or change. 

Since an organic approach depends on the client system's positive motivation to learn to reduce obstacles to optimum performance, sources of frustration, etc., this motivation must be sparked by some experience that provides a basis for optimism - that something can be done to improve the situation. Providing such an initial motivating experience is called "seeding". Like actual seeding, the results are statistical. Some will not even expose themselves to the motivating experience; some who do will choose not to get involved, or lack the energy to follow through, or for other reasons go no further. This process, then, is a way of identifying the persons or groups who are most ready to go to work. Particularly during the early phases of development programs, a further selective process is desirable, to identify from among those who are ready to work, the persons or organizations with greatest potential to be "change agents" or catalysts for further change.

These persons or groups those with a natural talent for leadership, those with the most vitality and interest in experimentation, etc. then become centers for development work, producing models or demonstrations of what can be done, and involving other parts of the organization in the design, conduct and evaluation of experiments. Except, of course, insofar as his behavior begins to merge and closely parallel the acceptable behavior for people engaged in work in the culturally approved institutions of the country. 

However, many of the people who are processed through the criminal system could be sent along alternative tracks. What separates them is not their pathology, but their symptoms. The sympton in this case being an act which has put the offender within the intricate network of relationships known as the criminal law system, rather than on the belt of alternative, less totally sanctioned paths. The non-criminal system has its methods for dealing with the alcoholics, defectives, people with sexual problems and psychotics. 

However, for a veriety of reasons, such as apathy, indifference, fear, unwillingness to commit resources, etc. the non-criminal system does not participate in any meaningful way in either medically therapeutic or psychologically rehabilitative aspects of the work of the

criminal system. The result is that the offender, who may be equally deserving of assistance, is almost automatically deposited at the most incompetent level of the "professional" and "sub-professional" helping system. The lawyers practicing criminal law are often among the least competent of the profession; the medical facilities, including both physical and mental health services, are frequently far below minimum standards available at large urban hospitals; the correctional staff at an institution or the probation of parole officers supervisiing the rehabilitation of the offender lack the intellectual and moral sophistication to help on any but a haphazard and chance basis; the vocational training personnel have neither the talent nor the equipment to train people for work that could make it possible for them to effectively compete in the job market. Thus, performance of an act which brings someone within the cloak of the criminal law serves to reinforce the offender's position within the sphere where his incompetence and that of the community meet and merge. 

A long-term organic approach to the problems of crime prevention and control would require a stretching of the web so as to bring the offender within the circumference of the established, even traditional, ways of dealing with the legitimately maimed. What is required is more comprehensive and differentiated response to an enlarged theory of disease. What this means in practical terms is a new definition of the role of such institutions as hospitals, universities, mental hospitals in the community. 

A change in the function of these institutions implies an attitudal change on the part of those in the community who are responsible for their operation. This has its parallel in the organizational and behavioral change required among these people and institutions who formally operate the crime system. Thus the strategies to be devised relate to ways of involving presently non-related people and institutions to the work of the criminal system.

There should be as much concern with these issues as there is with change in what is now formally identified as part of the system. A community based model means not only physical closeness to the offenders community of origin but also to the community of limited resources able to effectively contribute to his rehabilitation. (See Organizational Behavior section) 

The implications of this for the Federal role which are discussed below. 

The kinds of changes that are involved would include: -- A restructuring of the mental and physical health facilities of the community so as to better equalize the use of the limited resources of skilled people and well equipped institutions. For example, community based correctional institutions might not only be located in areas close to, or made part of, the so-called first class related institutions such as physical and mental hospitals and universities. Illustrations of what might be done include: a) The assembling of all chronic cases of similar medical problems in one place, irrespective of the act that led them to be placed in the definitional labyrinth of crime, mental illness, or organic defect. Illustrations: 1: the chronic, untreatable cases at a hospital for the criminally insane might be brought together with similar cases from mental hospitals in one institutional center. A staff most concerned with the problems of these custodial cases could then be centralized, rather than dispersed throughout several institutions in the region. In addition, the institution would become the training center for medecine, law, and social work schools, each of whom could use the institution for training and research purposes.

to hospitals that have the staff to offer at least minimally competent treatment measures. A hospital like the Massachusetts Mental Health Center, considered to be one of the best psychiatric training centers in the country, views court cases as burdensome, and almost always ships them off as rapidly as statutorilly possible. The ultimate destination of these offenders is to be sent to the medical minor leagues, where practically speaking, nothing happens. If a hospital such as Massachusetts Mental Health Center kept court cases and made them a regular part of their treatment and training work, there would be a greater equalization and spreading of treatment skills throughout all strata of the community.

Also if the offender was physically located near vocational opportunities, it might be possible to establish a training system whereby he was released a certain number of days a

CHAPTER VI A STRATEGY OF DEVELOPMENTAL CHANGE

A. 

A Generic Description 

There is a fundamental dilemma which confronts any federal agency that sets out to be an agent of change with respect to major social problems at the national level. This dilemma comes out of the contrast between social problems which are massive, complex, diverse, spread out in nature, poorly understood, interconnected, and constantly changing; and resources for attacking them which are meager, centrally located, and constricted by territorial boundaries, by legislative and regulatory constraints at the state and federal levels, and by the unwillingness of sources of power and influence to accept their intervention. 

The 

Every federal agency which has responsibility for an area of public systems need faces a version of this problem. fundamental problem poses some related dilemmas: 

The effort made to cope with the problem may be massive or small. If it is massive, it risks outrunning its resources and outrunning the level of understanding it can bring to bear on problems, therefore making mistakes; and if its operations are in the limelight, as they are likely to be, these mistakes can prejudice its future operations. If it launches a program which is consistent with its ability to act on understanding of the problems it confronts that effort may appear trivial in relation to the problem.

If the agency acts without adequately anticipating the consequences of its action, it risks producing effects which run counter to its objectives (for example, U.S. agricultural aid to Yemen led the Yemenites to use their fields for the production of marijuana; new public housing developments in the Bronx are leading to the extension of the Harlem ghetto in ways that were neither anticipated nor wanted). On the other hand, simply studying the problem without action is likely to produce an effect of "doing nothing", and is not likely to produce much insight into the nature of the system worked with, since action in the system is the most effective way to discover how it functions. 

The agency is likely to face strong political pressures to be equitable in its distribution of funds to all States and regions. Those who are most effective in seeking funds and pressing for them may not need them most or be most able to use them effectively. 

On the other hand, narrow selection of the use of funds may result in political sanctions.

Models for Change. Those faced with problems like the ones described above tent to share models for the process of change in which they are engaged, although these models are seldom made explicit. 

There is a widespread allegiance to what may be called the "legislative" model. According to it, a law, regulation, or policy is promulgated. 

There may then be coupled with this the use of sanctions against non-compliance or rewards for compliance. 

The use of federal grant money as a "carrot on a stick" to enforce

state or local compliance with program criteria has been common in the Poverty Program and the health and housing programs of the current administration, for example. On the other hand, the policy or regulation may be held up as a "banner" and used in an exploitative way without sanction. 

In either case, a mechanical approach tends to accompany the legislative model. Given the overwhelming problem of establishing direct contact from a central source with all of those groups and institutions to be influenced throughout the country, it is tempting to operate by fanning out dollars from a central source to decentralized institutions, and to attempt to control the work done with those dollars by means of criteria and procedures formally established, thus assuring automatic or mechanical adherence to program intent. 

The problems of this sort of operation are represented in the recent experiences of the State Technical Services Act of 1965,¹ which is, in this respect, representative of many federal efforts to produce social change in the states and regions and localities of the country. The most current example of a similar set of problems is, perhaps, the Demonstration City Program.

The problems encountered include these: 

There is a lack of institutions to work with. 

Existing institutions, perhaps at the State level, have boundaries different from those of the more natural boundaries of the problem. For example, water pollution

and water resource control problems tend to be regional in nature, even though State institutions exist for coping with these problems and clamor for special access to funds. 

In general, there is a lack of competence at the State and local level to carry out programs of change and to engage in the planning process with respect to change.

There tends to be a lack of understanding, first of all at the central federal level, concerning the nature of local interests and problems, and then at the local level concerning the intent underlying federal programs.

As dollars are directed at State and local institutions, they tend to be deflected from the agency's intent and brought into the orbit of the interests of the local institutions. It is as though these institutions, responding to the "carrot-stick" strategy of the federal agencies, were presenting proposals in order to get money which reflected the interests of the agency, and then taking as much freedom as they feel they legally can, use the money to meet the objectives which they see as important.

Measures introduced by the federal government with one objective in mind tend to get caught up in the political process at the local and State levels, sometimes in unexpected ways. to 

Examples are the reaction of mayors poverty program legislation, the recent reaction of

the Massachusetts State legislature to community mental health centers, and some of the problems encountered by juvenile delinquency programs. In 2 all instances, political concerns, particularly with reference to the generation of new sources of political power or use of political rewards, overbalance the original intent of the program. 

The requirements for an effective federal strategy, then, would seem to include the following:

It should start small, but without the penalties of smallness, i.e. start small but be perceived as part of a large and significant program. 

The first steps should have the effect of building competence which does not currently exist to carry on the program.

Mutual understanding between federal agencies and local and State institutions should be gained through personal contact.

The agency programs should find a way to link up with champions for change and current front-runners in the field at local and State levels. 

Instances should be chosen at the outset where it is possible to work "downhill", i.e. where some initial successes can be achieved.

In so far as possible, programs should take advantage of local initiative.

The number and kind of programs undertaken at local levels should be such that central consulting, guidance, and help can be provided for them. 

The whole process of program development should make it possible to establish a cumulative data base and to provide continuing learning from the first instances. 

Means of learning from demonstrations and pilots are badly needed, and the mere naming of a project as a "pilot" or "demonstration" by no means guarantees transfer of learning from it to other instances.

The projects first undertaken should be part of the design of a long-term process of change whose basic lines are spelled out, still maintaining a flexible enough form to permit it to take advantage of what initiative emerges at the local level.

These criteria point to a developmental strategy for change which has the following properties: 1. 

The basic line of development is as follows: 

Time The first instances are undertaken under the umbrella of the projected total program. They start with small inputs of money and build to larger inputs as competence is created and acceptance gained. 2. 

The first pilot instances are as nearly systematic as possible. That is to say, they incorporate, in so far as possible, the full range of change strategies intended.

3. 

The first instances are chosen on the basis of the readiness of individuals associated with them to go to work, local initiative and competence, existing champions for change, and a favorable local climate. There is need for a "seeding" process to identify such pilot instances; in so far as possible, means should be found to make the candidates for such programs self-selecting. 

4. For these first pilot programs there should be made available tools, information, and consulting and training competences. 

5. A nurturing process should take place: it will be necessary to maintain personal contact and protection of the first instances in order to assure some success at the outset. 

6. The early instances are used, then, for learning about the change process intended; as a showplace of demonstrated accomplishment, in order to generate enthusiasm in others whose support or involvement is desired; as a training ground for additional competences (including consultants and trainers and champions of other programs); and as a way of building a case for legislative acceptance. They serve, as well, to provide concrete instances of the kind of change involved, without which it may be difficult for people to conceptualize; they provide people with the ability to say "another", as it is now possible to talk about "another" Synanon or "another"

Provo experiment.

7. There is much to be gained from avoiding political confrontations, in so far as possible, until the first instances have been established as solid ones. 

It should be noted, however, that there are issues of power and resistance to change based on power which this strategy does not confront. The strategy is aimed at the spreading of learning about change and the creation of a constituency for change, but that constituency may or may not have ways of producing change. 

In the case of organized crime, for example, and the linking of vested interests to organized crime, resistances to change may be so strong that leverage and equivalent power, rather than learning, are the principal issues. Aside from the use of the "federal banner" to provide support, and the prestige of federal officials to provide encouragement, the strategy discussed here does not confront such questions of power directly.

The seeding process. 

One of the first tasks of the central team will be the identification of individuals and institutions capable of carrying out the first pilot projects in organizational development and community systems. These kinds of persons would include: -- individuals at State and local levels in police departments, courts, and correctional institutions; --leaders of non-criminal system institutions such as mental hospitals, schools of social work, law schools, medical schools, engineering schools, welfare departments, and the like, whose involvement in the process of criminal justice is desired; -- individuals capable of serving as trainers and consultants to the formal criminal system; --educational institutions capable of providing broad programs of education and training for members of the formal criminal system; --other agencies of government whose support for the community programs will eventually be wanted.

The maintenance of other agency relationships is a difficult and time-consuming task. It should be, from the beginning, a fulltime job assigned to an individual of high skill and considerable governmental experience.

The research and developmental process. In a sense, the whole program of implementation proposed here comes under the heading of research and development in the process of crime prevention and control, and is, we would argue, the most significant form of research to be undertaken. There is, however, a set of additional opportunities for research and development. Many of these have been identified by the Commission Task Force on Science and Technology, e.g. police communication systems, "command and control" information systems. In order for these areas of research to be carried through effectively, we would propose that they be related on the one hand to a process for involving industrial research and development capability, and on the other hand to the generation of user requirements for new devices, systems, and technologies on the part of members of the formal criminal justice

system.

Such a process would include the following elements: a) generation of user requirements through the participation of members of police, courts, and correctional institutions.

The pilot projects themselves provide a vehicle for the generation of user requirements. series of studies to be undertaken with local police, court, and correctional hierarchies, aimed at user requirement formulation, would also set as part of the "seeding" strategies to be undertaken by the central staff as a way of identifying promising sites for pilot projects. This part of the research and development process should, in our view, begin immediately upon establishment of the central team. b) the development of performance criteria for subsystems and devices, based on user requirements and undertaken in the light of industrial capability. 

A further series of meetings with firms who possess capability in the communications and information systems fields could serve to continue their interest in the criminal system as a market and suggest modifications of performance criteria as these are developed. c) As pilot projects grow and reproduce and user requirement studies progress, the opportunity for citywide, regional, and national purchasing of equipment and systems emerges. Again, the assembling of intercity police groups, correctional, and court groups to review and establish consensus on performance criteria for equipment and systems (still leaving flexibility within

performance criteria for specifications related to local needs) would permit large manufacturers to see the possibility of significant markets. d) Grant funds would be made available to develop prototypes of equipment meeting these performance criteria, in order to demonstrate the possibility of meeting them. e) introduction of equipment and testing out its effectiveness would proceed best as part of the one or more pilot programs of organizational development, since in these programs it should be possible both to work on acceptance of the new technology and to determine that part of its effectiveness or ineffectiveness which is inherent and that part which is due to organizational use of it. What is suggested, therefore, is the beginning of a research and development process, rather than the establishment of a research and development institute, from the very beginning of the operations of the central team. This process should be integral with the organizational development projects, with local police, court, and correctional organizations, and should draw on these projects for user requirements. The establishment of a formal research and development budget and department should wait until the early phase of pilot project development has been completed. This would put it roughly between the second and third year of operation.

The use of public relations and the press. In the field of crime prevention and control, the press itself is an important agent for change. The Commission faces the possibility of strong negative press reaction for reasons suggested in Chapter 3. 

Reporters, principally, are unlikely to find much that is newsworthy in the Commission's report, except those negative features which they can magnify. On the other hand, the editors of newspapers, who produce editorials, could become an important source of support, a group of active supporters of local efforts at change through pilot programs, and even a means of seeding and identifying promising sites for pilots.

The Commission might experiment, therefore, with an attempt to involve newspaper editors more deeply in their findings and in their strategies for change than it is normal with presidential commissions to do (a similar effort in the Department of Commerce to involve building industry editors in understanding of and support for the Department's approaches to new building technology resulted in a very positive response: it turned that it was a lack of feeling of understanding and involvement, rather than hostility, which had been responsible for earlier bad publicity). 

The effort to hold a series of group sessions with editors would proceed best, in all likelihood, through the State committees, or perhaps on a regional basis. The process is one which should begin some months prior to the formal issuing of the Commission report.

Local pilot projects of organizational development and community change could again provide a first rate opportunity for involvement

of the press in process, rather than after the fact. Many editors are sufficiently disillusioned with large crash programs conducted in the name of the Great Society, that they may respond with considerable support to a proposal for a long-term project of change which is realistic with respect to the problems and practical in its gradual approach to work on them.

PILOT PROJECT SUPPLEMENT TO OSTI'S REPORT TO THE PRESIDENT'S COMMISSION ON LAW ENFORCEMENT AND ADMINISTRATION OF JUSTICE

MEMORANDUM FROM: Dr. Donald A. Schon TO: Mr. James Varenberg Executive Director President's Crime Commission SUBJECT: 

Outline of Pilot Projects Supplementing OSTI's Report to the President's Crime Commission

This outline continues the line of thought developed in OSTI's report to the President's Crime Commission. There we indicated two major kinds of social change, related to crime prevention and control, which could be embodied in pilot projects. 

In this paper we will outline two approaches to pilot projects. The first is an approach to crime prevention through community development, undertaken so as to involve outside resources such as the university community. The second is an approach to improved organization and operation of one part of the formal criminal system, the administration of justice. We have illustrated the first with references to starting points in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The second is described in the context of the District of Columbia. Both examples are intended to illustrate projects which could be carried out in many communities. 

I. Community Development and the Mobilization of Outside Resources 

Our starting points are these: 

1. There is a critical need to mobilize outside resources for work on problems heretofore considered problems of the criminal system. These outside resources center on the university community, but include as well the medical, mental health and industrial communities. The problem is to attract members of these communities to work which is generally considered second class, or failure-prone, and, in the process, to re-define their functions in relation to the criminal system so as to develop new classes of competent, trained persons for work in that system. 2. 3. 

Effective operation of parts of the criminal system--police, courts, correctional institutions--depends on organizational and management development of these units and their interconnection, following the models of industrial organization and training. These models suggest a need to begin with problems of management development and training of existing staff, to link up fragmented or competing units, to give central status to activities previously hidden, to pool resources and ideas, and to start with what exists rather than working back from an image of ideal organization. 

The prevention of crime (other than organized, business or white collar crime) is essentially equivalent to the total development of community systems--that is, health, housing, jobs, training, education, mental health, waste disposal, transportation, the use of leisure, and all the other public features of community life, as well as the formal criminal system itself. The problem of crime prevention (for example, in the area

of juvenile delinquency) quickly leads to work will all aspects of the community. The problem then becomes one of discovering boundaries within which it is possible to work more or less simultaneously on all aspects of community problems, to discover and develop local initiative for change, and to devise strategies for moving from such initiative to broader and continuing change.

Boundaries of the Problem 

For these reasons, the pilot projects outlined here will focus on a particular community--roughly the size of Cambridge, Massachusetts or Stockton, California--and will move outward from that community to larger geographical and political areas, as the project permits or requires. Objectives a. To undertake an integrated program of crime prevention and control in a small community of about 50,000 to 100,000 persons. b.

To link this program to resources normally considered outside the criminal system--universities (especially graduate schools of business, law, medicine, psychiatry and social work), mental health institutions, industry and business--so as to draw these resources into the work of crime prevention and control and to train new workers in the field.

Approach a. b. C.

Define a series of tasks essential to crime prevention and control; assemble the best of the many existing models of approaches to parts of the problem of crime preventions.

Build on and stimulate local community initiative in undertaking these tasks.

Establish a program of outside support for these tasks--among the universities, mental health institutions, business and industry-so that a series of university-community collaborative projects gets underway. d. Design the university-community effort as training programs. e.

Link these programs together so that they can learn from and reinforce one another. f. Establish the integrated program as a demonstration and training activity for other communities.

Some Methodological Starting Points a. b. c. d. e.

Start with the improvement of existing institutions, the training of existing staff.

Within the broad framework of the program's objectives, start with the places in the system where individuals with some power and influence are ready to go to work on change in the system. 

Start with initiatives already taken, and devote initial effort to providing support for these initiatives and linking them to one another.

Choose as first targets those which have a high likelihood of success. Work downhill, and use early projects to build teams, support, and momentum for later change.

Among outside resources, work to locate individuals who can serve as champions and entrepreneurs for change; make them part of the team. f. Insofar as possible, bring users of and participants in the system into the design of the projects and the formulation of requirements for change. g. 

Treat institutions within the formal system as organizations analogous to business firms and government agencies; use approaches to management and organizational development which have been effective in these other kinds of organizations. h. Emphasize and work on problems of fragmentation and conflict among parts of the formal criminal system. i. 

Start with the community itself and its crime-related institutions and let these beginnings lead to wider areas of problem and need, e.g., legislative change at the state level; coordination among agencies at metropolitan, state and Federal levels. j. Link with State and National bodies to secure support for change at these levels.

Tasks to Be Undertaken as Part of the Project These are set forth as a partial list of what is to be done--not yet a statement of how it is to be done or a strategy for getting it done over time.

These tasks fall into two basic categories: organizational development and reform in the formal criminal system, and crime prevention through community development.

a. 

Organizational Development and Reform in the Formal Criminal System 

The formal criminal system is meant here to include police, courts and correctional institutions.

In all three, we find a negative cycle (or closed system) of poor management, poor public image, poor staff, poor training, and inadequate or corrupt practice. In all three instances, there are exceptional instances which demonstrate the possibility of significant improvement. In all three there is a need to: --discover whom to start working with. --induce those persons to begin a process of change. --confront the system as a whole and undertake concurrently programs of organizational development, training, and improvement in operations (the need for this sort of approach is spelled out in our earlier report).

In the community-university project under discussion here, we propose that work begin with the community police department and with its district courts (with links to higher courts as required by the project). 

Correctional institutions, generally, exist at the State and county level. Rather than work with existing institutions here, we propose establishing new in-city correctional processes in order (a) to treat the more promising class of offenders among those drawn from the community; and (b) to treat those funneled out of the formal criminal process from the police and courts.

Establishment of a New, In-City Correctional Center 

Purpose --to make the correctional process visible and central to the community. --to link it to outside resources. --to provide separate and more effective treatment for those judged more susceptible to rehabilitation.

The client community for this process would include, principally, residents of the pilot community itself. It would consist of those who have been convicted of crimes and judged most susceptible to rehabilitation. The judging process would require collaboration of courts, police correctional institutions, and institutions of mental health.

The client community will include, as well, those who enter the criminal system at earlier stages (in connection with the police or courts) and who are judged to be in need of help, treatment, or surveillance.

Tasks to be undertaken in connection with an in-city correctional center:

Success of such an in-city correctional process will depend not only on the ability of inmates to enter fully into the life of the community, but also on the center's ability to attract outside competences, such as those described above, to the correctional process as one of a sufficient prestige, possibility of success, and interest to justify work and career commitment.

The Establishment of a "Change Clinic" Among the Various Parts of the Criminal System 

Once projects of change have begun among parts of the formal criminal system such as police or courts, it will be useful to establish change-oriented work groups which cut across these departmental boundaries. Initially such groups may be most effective if they are homogeneous with respect to rank so that judges and police chiefs, for example, work together, as do clerks of courts and police patrolmen. Later, groups of mixed ranks may be established. 

The purpose of these working groups will be, a) mutual learning and support with respect to the change process, b) better understanding of problems of interaction between parts of the system, and c) development of specific projects of collaboration.

Study of Law Enforcement in the Ghetto 

In some instances, university-based studies of social problems can be combined with efforts at community development and organizational change. An example of this may be the problem of the use of law and law enforcement in the ghettoes of the center cities and their meaning to ghetto residents. An understanding of these features may provide a sounder basis for law enforcement practices in the community and

help to form a bridge between the policeman, the social worker, and residents of the community. 

One of the outstanding features, at least in Negro ghettoes, is the presence of families of related women, often of several generations in depth, and corresponding associations of men, rarely exceeding a generation in depth but persisting long after the formal structure of gangs has withered. 

Lack of self control, and impotence to coerce others without recourse to violence are underlying thematic qualities. It is not surprising, and to those who know ghetto life it is apparent, that the meaning attached to officials such as the policeman and the judge, and to the law they represent, will be shaped in the minds of the poor by these underlying themes. The law will mean one thing to the people in the ghetto and another to people in middle class neighborhoods around it.

It would be interesting to see what the institutions of the law and its officials mean to people in ghettos by observing how the institutions are used. It is a mistake to believe that only common hostility characterizes the relationships between the police and the poor. Hostility against the forces of oppression and rejection, of which the police are seen as instruments, will persist. Anger, despair and impotence will take the form of categorical accusations of brutality. Meanwhile, too often, the law will be used to settle family disputes.

A study of the use of the law and the police can take several shapes. Preliminary observations suggest that the law is ofted used by women to coerce men, and more often as an instrument of vengeance. It is used to threaten children. By contrast, it is rarely used by related women against each other, and is rarely used within the associations of men. Perhaps use of the law follows principles that divide people into socially and emotionally significant groups such as sex, generation, limited incorporations within a generation, and maternal families that include people in different generations.

It might proveinteresting to explore the social structure of the ghetto as revealed by the use of the law. Another approach to understanding the significance of the law and its institutions would be to try to assess what people feel when they use the law or when the law is used against them. Is the feeling of impotence implicit in calling the police into a family quarrel transformed into a feeling that the law itself is impotent? It would seem that when men use the law to revenge themselves against each other, the restoration of their self-esteem is only partly achieved and their respect for the law also suffers. It is better, in the sporting life, to revenge yourself if you have been attacked, and better still to

g. that prowess makes you immune from attack. Since it is practically impossible for many in ghetto life to acquire self-esteem through status by belonging to significant and socially valued organizations, conflict develops between the value of individual reputation, the need for security, and the search for vengeance when vengeance cannot be obtained privately. 

Some answers to these questions would seem useful if increasing the power and domain of law enforcement is contemplated. This study assumes that the practices of law enforcement vary in different kinds of communities and that the differences may reflect not only the economic level of a community, but also its social configuration. 

In so far as practices of law enforcement are able to adapt to the community, this study could suggest a basis for guiding the adaption of law enforcement in ghettoes.

I. Organizational Change and Development in the District of Columbia Courts 

The needs for reorganization in the D.C, courts appear to have two dimensions. The first involves administrative integration of the several courts; the second, improved management techniques within each court. Administrative integration and internal reorganization are not independent; they are closely related to one another and changes in one direction will affect changes or the ability to change in the other.

Both types of reorganization make assumptions concerning the relationship of the existing court structure to the desired ones. 

A concept of court organization appears to be at the heart of the issue. Its dimensions are poorly understood, yet it is the current level of understanding about this concept which provides the base line in evaluating the performance of the existing court structure and for describing the directions of desired structural change. 

But determining the nature and dimensions of court organization and the relationship of the desired structure to that which exists in the D.C. courts, is not enough. The forces to carry out these plans must be identified, nurtured, and set in motion at the proper time.

In other words, the forces of change must be built into the program design. 

So far we have considered only the mechanistic side of court reorganization. In many senses, the court operates like a firm. 

It faces management problems similar to those of a firm; and in turn, it faces opportunities to incorporate modern management techniques for expanding the capacity of the court to

analyze and solve its problems. 

The design of a program for generating the dimensions (physical, legal, administrative, social, and economic) of the court organization, for assessing the existing D.C. court organization as it relates to these, for determining strategies of change which would be involved, for expanding the opportunities of the court system to assess its problems and its effectiveness, and for effecting desired changes is described in the following 

Starting Points. 

Recently there appears to be growing support for the need to reorganize the D.C. courts. The need for change is posited by 

Justice Department attorneys, judges, lawyers, local newspapers, city officials, and others engaged in the administration of justice, e.g. policemen, probation officers, and so forth. Their arguments are based, in large part, upon observed undesirable conditions, such as lengthy delays from arrest to trial, and large backlogs of cases awaiting trial.

At least two types of responses to these needs can be seen-the use of stopgap measures within the existing court structure, and attempts to determine what kinds of reorganization are needed. 

The use of stopgap measures is not to deny the need for reorganization; only to point to the pressures of daily problems for immediate solutions. These responses have been somewhat effective, but have also produced some undesirable practices in the courts. 

For example, plea bargain days in response to overcrowded court calendars; the tendency for shifts of civil cases from the U.S. 

District Court to the Court of General Sessions by reducing the

ollars at stake; pressures for U.S. Attorneys to try cases as hisdemeanors, rather than clutter the U.S. District Court with Felonies.

It appears that this resistance came from opposition to the level of approach for identifying solutions (i.e. that a broader approach than a purely mechanistic application of management information systems, for example), rather than opposition to the need for reorganization. 

From the several observations, it becomes clear that: --Reorganization efforts have been at various levels of complexity.

Consequently, proposed reorganizational changes, while viewed separately, may seem to

be a step forward, when taken in combinations, may overlap, conflict, and even offset one another. --The dimensions of the court organization as a legal, economic, social, and technical process are not clearly understood at this time. --The scope of change and development within the courts is, therefore, not clearly perceived or internally consistent yet; but we can see that it involves such 

a) mechanical changes, e.g. reallocation of caseloads of judges as workloads shift, increasing the number of judges, prosecuting attorneys, etc.; b) statutory changes, e.g. changes of civil and criminal procedures; c) developmental changes, e.g. changes internally generated through programs, developing interpersonal and interorganizational networks; d) administrative changes, e.g. better information gathering and transmission, queuing dockets, etc.; e) physical changes, e.g. conversion of file rooms into hearing rooms and waiting rooms, restructuring courtrooms to fit the functional needs of trial process; f) structural changes, e.g. jurisdictional changes among the several courts of the District of Columbia.

--Current efforts to look at the problems are fragmented, uncoordinated in terms of objectives and level of analysis, inadequately funded, part time, and internal (i.e. lawyers studying processes of law). --While there are such efforts to look at reorganizational needs, there are no efforts TO EFFECT CHANGES in the directions which these studies indicate.
