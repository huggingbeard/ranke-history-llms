Is This Your War? 

"These are the times that try men's souls. . . . Tyranny, like hell, is not easily conquered; yet we have this consolation with us, that the harder the conflict, the more glorious the triumph." Thus spoke Tom Paine in 1776. Aren't his words just as true for us 

Americans today? We, too, are passing through a period that will test our physical and our spiritual resources to the very limit. 

And we, too, are at war with tyranny, a modern, scientific, streamlined "blitzkrieg" kind of tyranny, which makes the despotism our forefathers faced seem pale indeed by contrast. 

What if We Were to Lose?

We are engaged in a struggle for survival-physical survival, as well as the continuation of our way of living. None of us likes to consider the tragic consequences of our defeat and fascist domination here. We all dread the thought of religious persecution and personal enslavement. Just because we do, it is perhaps well that we should think of the possibilities of defeat. The boys and girls of France, Poland, Denmark, Greece, Norway, and other occupied countries, have learned to their sorrow what Hitlerism means. They know the fear, the hunger, the misery, and the torture that go with Hitler's concentration camps, censorship, and Gestapo. Their fate should spur us all, Americans old and young, to do our utmost for victory.

The time has come. The possibility is here. Your nation is in peril. It is calling you to do your duty. And you—each and every one of you—must be ready to do your part. You must do it willingly, gladly, enthusiastically. For we fight to make real the great 

American dream of democracy with equal rights for all. In this fight you must serve in whatever capacity you can, coolly, courageously, and understandingly. 

How Will You Be Affected?

While we fight this war against modern barbarism, you will of necessity be called upon to make tremendous sacrifices. Your educational course may be changed or cut short; your family will be denied its usual quantity of goods; your use of coal, oil, electricity, and other essentials may be curtailed; you may be deprived 3

of many luxuries, like automobiles and cosmetics, to which you have become accustomed; the business in which your father is engaged may be interfered with; your family's financial resources may be tapped to the straining point by heavy taxes and forced loans; bombings may destroy your home; and young men, near and dear to you, may pay their "last full measure of devotion." 

These are, indeed, sacrifices you cannot face lightly. And yet they must be accepted willingly and cheerfully. The only alternative would be to yield to the ruthless fanatics whose aim it is to destroy us. We Americans realize this, realize what it means for each of us. We do not need the lash of a dictator to drive us to do our duty. The glory of democracy is the willingness of its citizens to make voluntarily whatever sacrifice is necessary for the common good, or for the preservation of their freedom. You, too, will surely brace yourself to your task, humble though it may be, and by doing your share help create a world where free men may work and live in peace and plenty. 

This pamphlet will try to help you understand your part in this struggle of men for freedom. Let us consider together what meaning this struggle should have for you; how you can help to win it; and how you can help to win the peace that will follow. It is your duty, as a loyal American, to think these problems through.

What Does Fighting for Democracy Mean to You? 

We are all soldiers of democracy. We are all engaged in this war. What are we fighting for? What does democracy fight for?

It may be that the future of our country rests on how well you understand the meaning of these questions. 

What Are We Fighting For? 

First of all, we soldiers of democracy fight for survival. We fight to protect our soil, our freedom, our way of life. We fight for military victory-decisive, complete. But we fight for more than these things. We fight for the right to complete a job we have started for the right of free people to work out their own destiny, and to solve their own problems. For we have always had problems, and we have problems now-problems that will not be solved by the war. 

There are some who say, "What have we to fight for? What have we to defend? Shall we fight to defend slums, unemployment, poverty, discrimination? Are these things worth defending?" 

These people are blind. They are blind for two reasons. First, because in spite of the obvious imperfections of our democracy, we have much that is worth fighting for, even dying for. Second, because they do not realize that democracy keeps on growing and developing, even in war time. The war, like the depression, is bound to be a great educational experience for the American people.

What Democracy Means 

For as we fight for democracy, we ask ourselves what democracy really is. We realize that democracy means more than our traditional civil liberties, precious though they be. We realize even more clearly than we do in peacetime that democracy means freedom and security for the common man-freedom of speech, freedom of religion, freedom from want, and freedom from fear. 

It means the right to a job at decent pay; the right to a minimum standard of living that will permit health, decency and family life; the right to be free from racial, social, or religious persecution; the right to equal opportunity for advancement. And so we democrats fight, not only to safeguard these precious rights we already have, but also for the opportunity to fulfill the promise of

democracy to our generation. We fight for the right to build a better America and a better world, for ourselves and our children. 

This is what fighting for democracy means. 

Because democracy fights for these things, it cannot fight as our enemies fight-with fear, terror, oppression, suspicion, censorship and hate. Democracy does not fear and mistrust its people.

Democracy does not impose undue burdens on the poor who cannot afford them. Democracy does not permit a few to grow rich while others sacrifice. Democracy does not permit a few to use the pretext of war to destroy hard-won social gains. 

Democracy does ask of each of us that we act unselfishly in the common interest, and that we all share in the sacrifices that must be made, up to the limit of our ability. But this war must be paid for out of a lower standard of living. The great masses of our people cannot sacrifice much without serious loss to their health, productive ability, and morale. Such sacrifices would damage our war effort. The greater sacrifices must therefore be made by those whose incomes are well above the minimum necessary to health and decency. This all genuine democrats will be happy to do. As we learn to appreciate these things more deeply, our democracy will improve and strengthen, even as we fight the war. We must strive to perfect our democracy now. The better we make it, the harder we will fight to preserve it, and the more precious will be the victory we shall win.

Why Must You Make Sacrifices? 

The United States will produce, this year, the greatest volume of goods and services in all our history-greater by far than we produced in the "prosperous" year of 1929. And yet for each of us there will be less, considerably less, to consume. "Why is that?" you may ask. For the simple reason that a very large share of what we produce will be going to our armed forces and those of our allies to help in the prosecution of the war. 

How Much Must We Sacrifice? 

Just consider the facts. In 1929, we produced goods and services in the amount of 81 billions of dollars. Practically all of these goods and services were for civilian use. In 1941 our total production (even when figured at lower prices), amounted to 94% billions of dollars. And production for 1942 is expected to total 

117 billions of dollars. But of this huge amount of goods and services, 48 billions are expected to be war goods, leaving only 69 billions of dollars of civilian production. The longer the war lasts, the greater will be the proportion of our production devoted to carrying on the war. The most recent estimates expect war production to reach the rate of 73 billions of dollars yearlyleaving only 45 or so billions for civilian production. These figures should indicate exactly how much sacrifice we people of this nation, taken as a whole, must now make in our standard of living.

The Choice Before Us 

Where do you want our aluminum to go into pots and pans, or bombing planes? Where would you like to see our steel-in typewriters, bicycles, and automobiles, or in tanks, ships, and guns? Where do you want our sugar-in coffee, jam, and pie, or in munitions? Where do you want our rayon and nylon-in clothing or in parachutes?

Because of our vast natural resources, our machine equipment, our skilled labor, our technological ability, we must serve as the arsenal of democracy in this world-wide conflict. But in spite of our huge productive power, we cannot produce enough for "lifeas-usual" and for victory too. Many of our giant industries, such as the automobile, radio, washing machine, and typewriter indus-

tries have been, or are being, converted to war production. Many scarce raw materials, such as rubber, wool, sugar, and practically all the metals, are being denied to civilian industry. Manpower is flowing to where it can best serve the war effort. This is one way, then, in which we must all make sacrifices, by denying ourselves cheerfully things we would otherwise wear and use. Fortunately, we need not anticipate serious food shortages. With few exceptions, we shall have all the food we need. 

Giving Your Time 

In addition, there are other sacrifices you will have to make. In war time, there is much work to be done. Sewing, knitting, messenger service, first aid work, summer farm work, army and navy auxiliary services, salvage and reclamation of paper, metals, and waste of all kinds, the planting of Victory Gardens-these are some of the jobs that need to be done. And here the job is so vast in scope that everyone must pitch in and do his share. Our time and our energy-these we are each privileged to contribute to the common cause. 

Giving Your Money

And how are we to pay for this war? Our government must tax us heavily if it is to pay for the guns, ships, planes and tanks we need. Beyond that, it asks us to lend it our savings, and for the same reason. Here again we must sacrifice. Our government is now spending at the rate of $100,000,000 every day. By the end of the year this rate will have reached $200,000,000 every day. 

This means that 73 billion dollars will be spent by our government, every year, out of a total income of perhaps 120 billion dollars. 

In other words, more than half the average family's income will have to go to the government in one way or another. This will give you an idea of just how much we will have to give to the government, either in taxes or in war bond and stamp purchases. 

Unless we pay and pay, and save and save, until it actually hurts, we cannot say that we are doing our share. 

Democracy needs our time, our energy, our money, our devotion. These require sacrifices. But these sacrifices we should be proud to make. Our willingness to make them is proof of the respect we have for ourselves as human beings.

How Can You Help to Prevent Inflation? 

Almost as serious as our military war against our enemies, is our domestic war against inflation. Your family's war ration book, the heavier taxes you are paying, the bonds and stamps you are buying, our price-fixing regulations, the President's special message to Congress-all these are evidence of the seriousness of the fight.

Why Do We Fear Inflation? 

Inflation is to be feared for several reasons. For one thing, when prices rise rapidly, wages and other forms of income seldom keep pace with them. This means less goods and services can be bought with the family income. It means a lower standard of living.

Secondly, inflation (or rapidly rising prices) increases the cost of the war to our government. Already, price increases have added $35 billion to the cost of this war. We are today devoting more and more of our total production to war purposes. Our government is the largest single buyer in the world. If a given number of ships, tanks, planes, and guns can today be bought for 

100 billion dollars, then a price increase of 50 per cent would increase the cost of that same equipment to 150 billion dollars; a price increase of 100 per cent would increase the cost to 200 billion dollars. And since we shall have to pay for all this later in the form of higher taxes, it is plain that it is to our interest to keep prices, and the cost of the war, down as low as possible. 

There is an additional reason why inflation is to be feared. As 

President Roosevelt has pointed out, "whatever goes up must come down." A rapid rise in prices during war time is always followed by a disastrous fall in prices after the war. Now rapidly falling prices always create trouble. Business men are unable to sell their goods at a profit; many factories close; discharged workers lose their purchasing power, which causes other factories to close; farmers, business men, and consumers who have borrowed money when prices were high find it difficult to repay those debts, as money becomes scarce and "dear"; and unemployment and depression generally follow. If we are to avoid depression after the war, then, it is urgent that we prevent inflation now. 

There is still another reason for avoiding inflation. In war time 10

we expect everyone to share the burden. "Equality of sacrifice" is the rule. And yet, rapidly rising prices affect certain classes of people much more than they do others. Unorganized, unskilled workers suffer much more than do organized, skilled workers who are better able to demand more pay. People who live on fixed incomes cannot buy as much as they did before. This means that government employees, school teachers, widows, disabled veterans, and old people living on pensions and insurance payments, suffer greatly. And well-to-do people, whose incomes are large, suffer least of all.

Why is such a condition undesirable? In the first place, it is obviously unfair. It means that scarce goods go in large measure to those who have the most money, while others less fortunate are made to bear the entire brunt of the war. But it means more than this. It hurts our ability to carry on the war successfully. People in low income groups, who are unable to buy the necessities of life, suffer in health and morale. They can neither work nor fight well. And it would not be surprising if they came to feel that this was not "their" war.

For these reasons, it is imperative that we prevent further increases in the cost of living.

How Can We Stop Inflation? 

In order to stop inflation, we must understand what brings it about. Inflation may come about in different ways, and for different reasons; but it is very clear why prices have been rising in the United States. In the first place, our government has been spending tremendous sums on the war program; and this money has been paid out to the people of America. We have, as a result, more money to spend than we had before. At the same time, because of shortages of manpower, raw materials, and factory equipment, we have cut down or eliminated much of our former production of civilian consumers' goods. Leon Henderson, our 

Price Administrator, has estimated that in 1942 consumers will have 86 billion dollars to spend, while only 69 billion dollars worth of goods and services will be available for purchasers. As President Roosevelt pointed out, "You don't have to be a professor of mathematics or of economics to see that prices will have to rise." 

If the chief causes for rising prices are more money in circula- 11

tion and less goods to be bought, it is clear that any successful program of inflation control would have to deal with these causes. 

It would have to increase the amount of consumers' goods available for purchase, or remove the excess purchasing power in the hands of consumers, or both.

What Have We Done to Prevent Inflation? 

The chief step taken so far to prevent price inflation was the passage of the Emergency Price Control Act, which gave to a 

Price Administrator the power to "freeze" or control prices.

Reliance was placed on the establishment of price ceilings on selected or "key" commodities. This measure was weakened by the farm bloc in Congress, which first held up passage of the bill, and then succeeded in practically exempting farm prices from the intent of the act. Obviously, if farm prices could rise, workers' food costs would go up, and workers would demand higher wages. Labor, too, won its fight against wage controls while farm prices and industrial profits were rising. Labor took the position that wage controls would prevent millions of workers from improving intolerable wage conditions. 

A second measure taken to control inflation was the enactment of the excess profits tax. If a corporation could not earn more profits as a result of war orders, they would have no incentive to raise prices, nor would they be able to pay out huge dividends to stockholders which would add to excess purchasing power. But the excess profits tax, as it was written, contained several "loopholes." 

American corporations earned two and three times their pre-war earnings, after payment of the new war taxes.

The third major step in our attempt to prevent inflation has been the sale of war bonds and stamps. Americans have responded patriotically to their government's appeals. Sales rose to a peak of $1 billion last January, immediately after Pearl Harbor, but have since dropped off considerably. At the present rate of sale, the amount of purchasing power being taken off is altogether inadequate to the task of taking excess purchasing power out of the hands of consumers.

The test of how effective our attempts at inflation control have been may be found in two facts: first, that prices have risen steadily throughout 1941; and second, that at the present rate of taxation and bond sales, purchasing power will exceed consumer 127

production in 1942 by $17 billion. This has been called "the inflationary gap."

What Must We Do To Prevent Inflation? 

Alarmed by the rising cost of living, and by the 17 billion dollar “inflationary gap", President Roosevelt delivered a special message to Congress on April 27, 1942. In this message, he asked for a vigorous fight against inflation. Heavier taxes, including a limit on individual incomes of $25,000; higher corporation and excess profits taxes to limit profits to "a reasonably low level”; rationing of all scarce commodities; "stabilization" of wages and farm prices; the extension of price ceilings to all items affecting the cost of living; the lowering of ceilings on farm prices; further restrictions on credit and installment buying; and vast increases in the sale of war bonds on a voluntary basis-these were the main features in his program. 

Opposition to this program has already developed. The farm bloc opposes curbs on farm prices; business opposes more severe profits taxes; labor opposes wage limitations; wealthy individuals oppose the $25,000 limit on their incomes, and the public may not respond sufficiently in its purchase of war bonds and stamps. 

Even if the program were to be completely adopted, we may doubt whether it would prevent inflation. Would it take out of circulation the 17 billion dollars of excess purchasing power now in circulation; and the even greater amount of excess purchasing power predicted for 1943? That is the crucial question. If it will not, then no price control act in the world can prevent inflation. 

The experience of Germany, Italy, Russia, France, and England proves that, where excess purchasing power exists, a "black market" is bound to develop. People with money will illegally buy scarce goods from traders or individuals who cannot resist the temptation of profitable prices. Such a "bootleg" market would break down inflation control.

How can we prevent the breakdown of our efforts? First, workers, farmers, business men and others must be really willing to make sacrifices, instead of trying to pass the burden off to the other fellow. Taxes must be increased to the point where they really hurt-provided that those who can best afford the biggest sacrifices are asked to make them. Second, we shall, in all proba- 13

bility, have to abandon the scheme of voluntary purchase of war bonds and adopt compulsory savings. And third, rationing will probably have to be extended to cover a great majority of the goods which most people buy. 

These methods will effectively reduce the demand for scarce goods. We should also take steps to make goods less scarce. In this connection we might consider the release of vast stores of government-held wheat, cotton, and other commodities (so far prevented by the farm bloc); the widespread adoption of labor-management councils to increase productive efficiency; the employment of the three or more millions of people still unemployed; the more widespread use of scientific management; the standardization of styles, and the elimination of frills; and the development of substitutes for scarce materials. 

What can we learn from the failure of our attempts thus far to control inflation? Does it not point out the need for everyone to sacrifice? For obviously, unless there is "equality of sacrifice," no one group wishes to be made the goat.

We need more production to fight inflation. How can you help?

You can plant a Victory Garden, to increase our food supply. 

You can enlist for farm work during the summer. You can try making and repairing goods at home. You can get a useful summer job, or take a training course which will equip you to work later on.

We must reduce the demand for goods to control inflation. How can you help? You can abstain from buying goods unless they are necessary. You can save, and lend to your government, every penny you possibly can. You can use sparingly those things you already have, and make them last longer. When you do buy necessary goods, you should check carefully to make sure that the seller is not charging more than he is permitted to do under the price-fixing order of Leon Henderson. Our government lacks the manpower to check observance of these legally-established prices; it is counting on us to do so. Lastly, you can try to persuade your friends, family, and neighbors also, to help in all these ways.

This last, which is primarily an educational job, is very im- 14

portant. What you, as a single individual may do, is not too important a contribution to our fight against inflation; but if you, and every American, not only understood the need for inflation control, but took the necessary steps to prevent it, our fight against inflation would definitely be won.

How Can You Contribute to the War Effort? 

We have, even with our current war expansion, no more than 12 million factory workers in the United States, about 40 million workers in all non-agricultural employments, approximately 55 million workers in all, including farmers and self-employed. From these workers' efforts is pouring out an unending stream of food, clothing, furniture, houses, war materials, and services of all kinds. To equip our armed forces properly, to help our allies, and to provide for our civilian population will require a much larger labor force. Imagine how much could be added to that effort by the small individual contribution of America's twenty-odd millions of school children, young and not so young! Even a small effort on your part, multiplied millions of times, could increase our total production by a vast amount. How much will be asked of you before the war is over, no one knows. But as a loyal American, you will want to know what you can do to help now. 

First, you can help by doing your daily job better, and more efficiently. Your job today is to complete your education, as thoroughly, as rapidly, and as well as you can. Languages, mathematics, social studies, sciences, health education-these and other studies have a real role to play in the fight for democracy. You can't handle other jobs well unless you prepare for them by doing your present job well. The habits you form now will help or hinder you later on. Lack of preparation today, unnecessary absence or lateness now, or failure in school subjects are wasteful, unfair, and even unpatriotic. 

Secondly, you can start your own private "war" against waste.

As a consumer you can buy wisely and use carefully. Every penny you spend requires the use of men, materials, machines, transportation, power-the productive resources we need to carry on the war. Before you buy an article you want, ask yourself, "Do I need this? Will it unnecessarily divert our national energies from more important tasks?" Once you make a purchase, make good use of it, take good care of it. That too will mean money saved for you; and more labor, raw materials and machinery available for war production. Avoid waste in your use of electric power at home, in unnecessary use of the family automobile, and so on, all along the line. 

Thirdly, buy more bonds and stamps. Your government needs 16

the money; they are a good investment for you to make; and as we have just seen, you will be helping to prevent price inflation. 

Fourthly, be loyal in thought and faith, as well as in deed. Have faith in your government. It's easy to grumble and criticize; but such actions don't help much. Your government believes in freedom of speech, and welcomes your constructive criticism. But it hasn't the time or the energy to cope with malicious "sniping"; nor can it allow itself to be goaded into revealing valuable information that may be of use to the enemy. Criticize, yes; but make sure your criticism is of the helpful, not of the destructive, variety. 

And finally, be sure to take care of your health, both physical and mental. If you eat properly of nourishing and body-building foods, if you get enough rest, recreation, and exercise, you will be making yourself fit and strong for whatever demands your country may make of you, whether as worker, farmer, housewife, professional, or soldier. "What can I do to help?" That is the question our boys and girls ask today. "Here's what I am doing!" That is the answer you should be able to give tomorrow.

How Can You Best Withstand Propaganda?

In any nation at war rumors are plentiful. "The English aren't fighting. They want us to do their fighting for them.” “We can't trust the Russians. After we beat Germany, we shall have to take on and lick the Russians." These are some of the stories people are repeating. Perhaps you have heard them. Perhaps you have believed them. Perhaps you have repeated them. But are they true? Will they stand up under examination? Who is responsible for them? Who will benefit by our believing them-we or our enemies?

In our country, in any country, there are some people who are natural pessimists. Their attitude is: "What's the use? We can't win." Others are natural optimists. Their attitude is: "What's the hurry? They can't hurt us. We've got lots of time. Take it easy.”

Both are wrong. And both are dangerous, because if we were to listen to them, we would not exert our full energy in the prosecution of the war.

Hitler has boasted that he will not need an army to conquer the United States. He is confident that he can get us to start fighting among ourselves. That is why skillful agents of the Axis, financed by millions and millions of dollars, are trying to create confusion, doubt, misunderstanding, jealousy, and hatred in the minds of Americans. They are trying to poison our minds. And a few traitorous Americans, with the help of a great many foolish Americans who do not realize the part they are playing, are acting as Hitler's agents here.

What are some of the rumors and lies they are spreading? They are trying to convince us that every step we take for the defense of democracy is a step toward fascism; that our allies are not to be trusted; that we are doomed to defeat; that democracy is weak and inefficient; that our president wants to be a dictator; that we were dragged unnecessarily into this war; and a good many other lies of a similar nature. 

Do you fall for this Axis propaganda? If you do, you are justifying Hitler's claim that he can capture us from within. 

How can we guard ourselves against overconfidence, defeatism, suspicion of our allies, internal dissension, lack of faith in democracy? One way is to ask, when we hear statements of this kind: "What is the source of this statement? Who is responsible for it? 18

Whom will it help us or the enemy?" Another way to guard against such propaganda is to read carefully the facts that come to us from official or reliable sources, so that we can check up on these stories. A third way is to be always cool, calm and unbiased, for most of these stories flourish best in an atmosphere of hysteria, panic and prejudice. A fourth way is to read and study, as much as you can for malicious propaganda cannot live where truth and knowledge prevail. 

Does all this mean that you shouldn't criticize? Should you be silent if you see evidence of discrimination, graft, inefficiency, or injustice? Not at all! Truth can never hurt the cause of freedom; it can only advance that cause. Democracy needs self-criticism. 

Constant discussion and education are the guides to intelligent action. It is your duty to inform yourself of the real facts of every problem that confronts us. Knowledge and truth are powerful weapons in the arsenal of democracy. They are also our best defense against propaganda.

Are You Doing Your Share? 

One evening during the past vacation, a group of high school boys and girls in a large American city met in the home of one of the boys. They were all friends and lived in the same neighborhood. 

They had been considering whether or not to form a social club. 

That evening, however, the discussion seemed to be lagging. Fred, a high school senior, sensed this. "What's the trouble, gang?" he asked, "Last month, when we met, you were all enthusiastic about forming the club. Now everyone seems to be in the dumps." 

There was an embarrassed silence, while everyone cleared his throat and looked at the floor. Finally one of the group, Edith, a bright, attractive young girl, spoke up. "I was thinking about our proposed club this week. Then, my brother Bill got his 1A classification from the draft board. Frankly, I didn't feel much like joining a social club after that." "That's right," said Jim, one of the acknowledged leaders of the group. "We're in a war now. We can't go on as we did before. 

Of course we're too young to join the army; and we've got to live, and go to school, and play. But it seems to me our attitude has got to be different. Why don't we form a Victory club? We can help sell war savings bonds and stamps, and organize salvage campaigns for waste paper and do lots of other useful things. 

And above all, we can discuss some of the problems connected with winning the war and the peace to follow. We can learn, have swell meetings, and help democracy, all at the same time.” "That's the idea," chorused the others, "that's the kind of club we want."

This group of young Americans is doing its share. Are you doing yours? Have you thought about what this war means to you, what democracy is; whether or not you are preparing for the kind of job that can best help your country? Have you discussed the many problems connected with an all-out war effort? 

A democracy depends on the understanding of all its citizens, not only those old enough to vote, but of its youth as well. Do you understand what is happening? Can you honestly say that you are a worthy citizen of democracy?

The boys and girls referred to above, Fred, Edith, Jim and the rest, are average American boys and girls like you. They not only discussed these problems among themselves. They raised them in 20

their classes, particularly in their Economics, History, Civics, and English classes. They felt they were really getting something vital out of school. They were learning to think about America's problems, about their problems, about your problems. 

One day, after they had been studying and discussing every day for several months such problems as "Why aren't we producing enough planes, tanks, and ships?", "What are we doing to speed up war production?", "What sacrifices must we make?", "How shall we pay for the war?", and they felt that they were qualified to come to certain conclusions, Fred summed up their findings in the following report.

Youth Learns from the Past 

"Until the attack on Pearl Harbor, most people in this country didn't take the war very seriously. They felt it was no concern of ours. Some people still feel that way. As a result we made an awful lot of mistakes in the period from 1939-1941. We still haven't corrected all of these mistakes. "What were these mistakes? Why were they made? The whole thing may be summed up by saying we were living in a "businessas-usual" period. That is, although we had promised to become the arsenal of democracy, we were unwilling to do anything that would interfere with the normal course of events. We students carried on much as we had before. Business men were concerned with their profits. Why should they change over to the production of war materials if they could make more profits from their peace time activities? Why should they expand their plants if these plants wouldn't be of any use after the war, or if expansion might lead to overproduction later on? Labor also had a me-first attitude in many cases. 'If industry is making a lot of profit,' many workers undoubtedly said to themselves, 'why shouldn't we get our cut too in the form of higher wages?' Farmers, seeing an opportunity to make up for the long years of the depression, demanded higher prices for farm products. Many housewives, remembering what happened in the last war, when sugar and other things became scarce and went sky-high in price, rushed to the stores and stocked up on everything on which they could lay their hands. This, of course, only forced prices up still further, for hoarding always brings panic and inflation. Speculators, quick to take advantage of other people's misfortunes and the chance to 221

make an easy dollar, began to withhold vital supplies from the markets. This made the situation still worse. Many selfish Congressmen, worrying about reelection, held up the price control bill and finally passed a very weak law. The Administration, itself, did not assume leadership, but seemed to be waiting for the people to tell the government what to do and how to do it. No one had full responsibility for the war effort. Countless agencies were set up. 

Red tape impeded everything. Stupidity and selfishness were too frequently found. Washington, the capital of our government, was a scene of confusion.

Youth Looks Forward 

"Pearl Harbor has made us realize that this war is a battle of production. We cannot say we are doing our best-until every man, woman and child, every factory, mine and mill, every machine and every tool is properly organized to do its utmost to win the war. Germany is devoting some 70 per cent of her productive energies to the war. This means that 30 per cent is the bare minimum necessary to keep the population alive. In addition, Germany had been preparing for this war for years. They have a headstart of at least six years. We can be sure that Japan has been doing the same thing. Meanwhile, in 1940, less than 5 per cent of our energies were being devoted to war. In 1941, the percentage rose to a maximum of 15 per cent, a big increase; but we were still taking things in our stride. We lived better last year, on the average, than ever before in our history. Now, even though we are the richest country in the world, with the greatest degree of technical skill, with a mass production industry that is the envy of every country in the world, we cannot lick the forces of fascism with one hand tied behind our backs. Bombers, tanks, and ships on order cannot win the battle of India, or the battle of Russia, or the battle of the Atlantic, or any other battle except the battle of blueprints. It is only the things we have on hand that win the military battles. "What changes are we making? What further changes must we make? We must, it should now be obvious to everyone, make our effort an all-out effort. We must convert our tremendous resources and skill to the making of instruments of war. We must. do this job speedily, and yet we must do it democratically. Our slogan must be: Those who can must sacrifice the most; but 22

everyone must sacrifice. We must take the profit out of war. We must prevent inflation, for rapidly rising prices are disastrous and can ruin everything. We must plan to utilize all our manpower and resources intelligently and efficiently. Everyone must be given the job he is best fitted for, whether it be in the front line trenches, on the assembly line, or on the farm. "This obviously means a drastic change in our accepted way of living. For the country as a whole, our standard of living must decline by at least one third. For the rich, those who can afford it, it must decline even more. We must assure a minimum health level for all. Starved workers cannot work long hours or produce efficiently; nor do they make good soldiers when they are selected for service in our armed forces. We must end all forms of discrimination now, for a worker who is refused a job because of his race or religion hurts the cause of democracy just as much as does an enemy machine gun. In short, we must make our democracy work now as it really can work. Otherwise, it is open to question whether we can survive at all."

The boys and girls who made this analysis may not have all the answers to all the problems confronting us. But they made a real attempt to understand and to solve our problems. Such an attempt is part of the duty of every American today. Are you doing your share?

Need You Fear a Post-War Collapse? 

At this moment, when we are gripped in a life-or-death struggle with an enemy who is determined to destroy us, it may seem a waste of time and energy to plan for the period after the war. 

Looking and planning ahead are not separate, however, from the war effort; they are part of it. We cannot afford to ruin a military victory by an economic defeat. 

Why do so many Americans fear a post-war collapse? Every great war since the Industrial Revolution has been followed by a long and severe depression. Demobilized soldiers add to the army of the unemployed. These people, whose incomes have disappeared, cease to buy goods. Thus still other workers lose their jobs. The depression grows in severity like a snowball rolling downhill. Its effects are felt for years. Your parents probably remember the depression of 1921 that followed the last war. They probably realize also that the Great Depression that began in 1929 was caused, in large measure, by World War I. 

The Size of the Problem 

Looking forward, our problem at the end of this war may seem even more difficult to solve. Never before will so many millions of workers have been engaged in war industry. Never before will our country have had so many millions of men under arms. According to the National Resources Planning Board, we shall have about 23 million workers engaged in war work by the end of the war. Our general staff is planning an army of from 

7 to 10 million men. At first glance, the unemployment problem we shall face will be tremendous. And yet, there are many reasons for believing that the end of World War II will usher in, not a period of depression, but one of tremendous boom. Why are most economists today confident that this is so? 

Reasons for Confidence

In the first place, we have learned a great deal from the lessons of the last war. We are already curbing civilian demand in many ways. Many civilian industries have already been closed down. 

Heavy taxes are cutting our spending power. The government is making a determined effort to borrow from us as much money as 24

it can. This means that at the end of the war we shall all have accumulated postponed demands for a variety of goods-automobiles, refrigerators, radios, washing machines, houses, and many other durable goods. At the same time, we shall have accumulated savings in war bonds which the government shall then pay out to us. In addition to this, we shall be able to draw upon billions of dollars in Social Security funds. Thus purchasing power will be available to support civilian industries while we convert from war to peace.

Again, there is no doubt that after the war there will be four tremendous jobs for American industry to do. One of these jobs will be to convert to civilian production the huge war plants that we have built and are still building. The second will be to produce steel, machinery and other goods of all kinds for the war-torn countries of Europe, and for the backward countries of the world. 

A third job will be to replace the worn-out machinery which is now being worked without rest to produce for the war. A fourth job will be to produce millions of homes, of which a shortage already exists. These jobs alone will provide employment for millions of men and women. 

Furthermore, it is hardly likely that, at the end of the war, our government will repeat the mistake of last time and throw millions of soldiers and sailors on the labor market to look for jobs. It is far more likely that the government will keep these men in the service for a limited time, and train them for jobs in civilian industry as those jobs develop.

And then again, even today industry is conducting research as never before to find new products, new methods, new services, with which to take up the post-war slack. 

And lastly, we have learned that planned public spending on a large scale, as for public works, can aid tremendously in solving the unemployment problem. It is therefore likely that, as long as the need exists, our government will do whatever is necessary to prevent human misery, distress, and want. 

For these reasons it seems clear that a post-war slump is not inevitable. It can be avoided, if we pursue the right policies. What we really need to fear is not a post-war slump, but what one economist has called "the post post-war slump." What he means is that after the war we may enter upon another great period of 25

boom such as that of 1923-1929, ending in another, and perhaps fatal, depression. Such an unplanned, unhealthy boom is indeed to be feared. That is why we must consider now how we can create in America permanent peace and prosperity after the war.

How Can You Help Ensure a Life of Abundance 

After the War?

Hitler proclaims that he is fighting for a "New Order." The 

Japanese speak of a "Greater Co-Prosperity Sphere for East

Asia." The United States, in such historic documents as the 

Atlantic Charter, the United Nations Pact, and the Four Freedoms speech of President Roosevelt, has also declared to the world that we do not intend to go back to the kind of world that existed before World War II. What kind of world do we intend to create?

The answer to that question will be determined largely by the kind of America we build here at home. The social, political, and economic policies we pursue within our borders will be reflected in our foreign policy. Democracy, like charity, begins at home. But the policies we adopt will be policies of your making. That is why it is important that you begin to think of these problems now. 

Americans have always dreamed and worked for a life of peace, liberty, and plenty. Never before has the realization of these goals been so nearly within our power. 

How Much Can We Produce? 

The American people have never yet enjoyed a satisfactory standard of living. Even in 1929, our most prosperous year before this war, 6 million families received incomes of less than $1,000 a year ($20 a week). In that same year, another 10 million families received incomes between $1,000 and $2,000 a year ($20 to $40 a week). These 16 million families constituted well over half the total number of families in the United States. And yet, economists have estimated that in 1929 an income considerably larger than $2,000 a year was necessary to provide adequate living conditions for the average American family. 

Are we in the United States physically capable of providing a comfortable living level for every American family? Figures show that there are about 30 million families in the United States, plus approximately 10 million single individuals living outside of families. To provide $3,000 a year for each family, and $1,500 for each individual, would require a national income of at least $105 billion. We produced, during 1941, goods and services valued at $94½ billion; and we expect to produce, in 1942, and income of $117 billion. For 1943 the production total, even reckoned 27

at current price levels, is expected to go considerably higher. In other words, we can produce enough to create a life of comfort for all, and still reward unusual ability and effort. 

How Can We Do the Job? 

Our first problem, it will be evident, will be to continue to produce as fully in peace time as we are now doing in war time. 

Instead of producing huge quantities of ships, tanks, planes, and guns, we must use the same energies to produce more food, more clothing, more furniture, more housing, more of all the goods and services that will make life richer and worthwhile. 

Our second problem will be to see that America's millions of workers, farmers, professionals and business men receive enough purchasing power to buy the products of our fertile farmlands and our busy mills. To this end, obviously, a high wage policy is necessary. "Standard" wages must be raised. Sub-standard wages must be relegated to the past. High prices, and the monopolies that maintain them, must go. There is no place for them in an economy of abundance.

A third problem will be to see that all this vast economic activity is balanced and coordinated, so that it does not go awry. There must be balance between industry and agriculture, between production and income, between savings and investment. Wages cannot go up without an increase in productive efficiency. Production cannot increase without an increase in wages. Savings should not be permitted to accumulate, if they cannot be invested. 

Things such as these have caused us serious economic breakdowns in the past; they have caused us to produce far below our capacity to enrich life for the average man. 

In achieving full production and employment, and in maintaining them, lie our best hope for a happy and prosperous America. Unemployment, insecurity, and poverty create dissatisfaction, discord, and strife. Employment, security, and comfort promote satisfaction, cooperation, and happiness. If we work as hard to win the peace as we are working now to win the war, we can make our land a real and joyous home for the American people. We can make real the American dream of freedom, equal opportunity, and abundance for all. In this future you and every other American youth would have ample opportunity to lead a useful, happy, and well-balanced life. 28

To get such a future you must be worthy of it. You must be willing to think, to discuss, to read, to study, and to work. Our best leaders and thinkers are writing and lecturing, trying as best they can to reach you with their message. Your task is to be open to all these ideas and influences; to seek amongst them for the truth; and then, as a citizen in our great democracy, to make your voice heard in the great decisions that lie before us. The kind of America that you will live in the rest of your life is the America you will make. It will be of your making whether it results from your interest or apathy, your understanding or ignorance, your participation or failure to participate. If government is to be of the people and for the people, surely it must be by the people. You-your family, your friends, your neighborsyou are the people. The choice is up to you. You have shown yourself willing to work and plan to win the war. Are you willing to work and plan to win the peace?

How Can You Help Create a Lasting Peace? 

This war has brought home to us Americans a terrible lesson. 

It is possible to win a war and lose the peace. We helped win 

World War I in 1918; today we realize that what we won was not peace, but an armistice. That is why we are today determined to win, not only the war, but the peace as well. We are determined that victory this time shall bring with it a just, beneficial, and lasting peace.

Is this, however, the time to talk of the peace we shall make after we win the war? Must we rather not win the war first, and make sure that we shall be the ones to write the peace? It is tempting to think so; but there are at least two good reasons for believing this is not true.

In the first place, war creates pain, anger, and hate. If we wait till the war's end, we are much less likely to think out the problems of the peace coolly than we are now. Second, and much more important, planning for peace now will help us to win the war. 

If our war and peace aims are democratic and meaningful, if they hold out the prospect of employment, security, and better living conditions for our own people and all the people of the world, our cause must be thereby advanced. The boys in our armed forces will fight harder; the citizens behind the lines will make more willingly every sacrifice we ask of them; we will inspire hope in the hearts of untold millions of oppressed colonial peoples, so that they will go "all out" for a democratic victory; peoples in Axis-dominated territory, and even the people of our enemy nations, will become a democratic "fifth column" in the fascist camp. 

What kind of peace, then, shall the victorious United Nations make? What kind of "Brave New World" shall we create? Before we attempt to answer this question, let us look back to the end of World War I; for although we failed last time to win the peace, there is much that we can learn from that experience. 

Lessons of World War I 

Why did the Versailles Treaty fail? Some people claim we made a great mistake in treating Germany as leniently as we did. Others believe the Versailles Treaty was much too harsh; that the burdens it placed on Germany made it impossible for her 30

to prosper, and thus led to the rise of Hitler to power. Still others hold that the failure of the United States to enter the League of Nations condemned that attempt at world federation to certain weakness and ultimate collapse.

None of these reasons, however, tells the whole story. Essentially the peace failed because in making it we failed to solve the problems that had brought on the war. The hopes and dreams of 

President Wilson were frustrated and shattered by selfish and imperialistic power politicians. These men were not motivated by a desire to build a better world. They had no vision of a democratic world community in which cooperative peoples could work, trade, and live in peace and plenty. They wanted a peace which would preserve within their own nations an aristocracy of wealth and privilege; they wanted a peace which would retain for their own nations continued domination over colonial areas and peoples. 

In other words, they wished to preserve, domestically and on a world scale, the status quo. But such a status quo was unjust; and being unjust it was bound to fail. Mussolini in Italy, Hitler in Germany, are evidences of its failure within nations. World 

War II is evidence of its failure on a world scale. 

Learning from the Past 

What can we learn from the mistakes of Versailles? First, the peace we make at the end of this war must solve the problems that bring on war. It must create, within nations and among nations, those conditions that make life tolerable for the common man. 

Democracy is not only a political system; it is a way of life. It means cooperation, sharing, harmony. It means an absence of privilege, an equality of opportunity for everyone, everywhere; for all peoples, rather than for a few. Democracy, if it is to exist, cannot exist in the United States and a few advanced European nations alone. It must exist in India, in China, in 

Russia, in the Middle East, in the East Indies, in Latin-America. 

Within nations and among nations, democracy must be permitted to fulfill its promise to the peoples of the world. This is what 

President Roosevelt meant when he spoke of the Four Freedoms for everyone, everywhere.

Now obviously, it is easier to state our goals than it is to work out detailed methods of achieving them. Nor will it be possible to 31

bring into immediate existence such a world after the war. For one thing, it may not be possible to trust our defeated enemies with complete self-rule immediately after the war. Nor will we be able to raise, all at once, the standards of living of millions of economically backward peoples so that they, too, can be "free from want." The problems of transition will be many and difficult. 

But if we hold our goals clearly before our eyes, we shall solve these problems one by one. The important thing is to know what we are after. The rest will come.

After the war America will probably exercise the most effective voice in the making of the peace—not only because of our youth and strength, but because the peoples of the world trust in our generosity and our ideals. But you are America! There will be many voices after the war calling upon you to support and follow them. There will be angry voices, calling upon you for vengeance.

There will be selfish voices, calling upon you to reap selfish national advantage. There will be undemocratic voices, calling upon you to preserve class and partisan privileges. Your job in making the peace is to reject these voices. If you will hold before you constantly the high ideals of democracy and humanity; if you will cherish the vision of a better world for everyone, everywhere; and if you will do everything you can as a student, a citizen, a human being-then we Americans and the common peoples of the entire world will secure a peace that will permit all of us to live happy, secure, useful, and richer lives.

Is This Your War? 

"These are the times that try men's souls. . . . Tyranny, like hell, is not easily conquered; yet we have this consolation with us, that the harder the conflict, the more glorious the triumph." Thus spoke Tom Paine in 1776. Aren't his words just as true for us 

Americans today? We, too, are passing through a period that will test our physical and our spiritual resources to the very limit. 

And we, too, are at war with tyranny, a modern, scientific, streamlined "blitzkrieg" kind of tyranny, which makes the despotism our forefathers faced seem pale indeed by contrast. 

What if We Were to Lose?

We are engaged in a struggle for survival-physical survival, as well as the continuation of our way of living. None of us likes to consider the tragic consequences of our defeat and fascist domination here. We all dread the thought of religious persecution and personal enslavement. Just because we do, it is perhaps well that we should think of the possibilities of defeat. The boys and girls of France, Poland, Denmark, Greece, Norway, and other occupied countries, have learned to their sorrow what Hitlerism means. They know the fear, the hunger, the misery, and the torture that go with Hitler's concentration camps, censorship, and Gestapo. Their fate should spur us all, Americans old and young, to do our utmost for victory.

The time has come. The possibility is here. Your nation is in peril. It is calling you to do your duty. And you—each and every one of you must be ready to do your part. You must do it willingly, gladly, enthusiastically. For we fight to make real the great 

American dream of democracy with equal rights for all. In this fight you must serve in whatever capacity you can, coolly, courageously, and understandingly. 

How Will You Be Affected?

While we fight this war against modern barbarism, you will of necessity be called upon to make tremendous sacrifices. Your educational course may be changed or cut short; your family will be denied its usual quantity of goods; your use of coal, oil, electricity, and other essentials may be curtailed; you may be deprived 3

of many luxuries, like automobiles and cosmetics, to which you have become accustomed; the business in which your father is engaged may be interfered with; your family's financial resources may be tapped to the straining point by heavy taxes and forced loans; bombings may destroy your home; and young men, near and dear to you, may pay their "last full measure of devotion.” 

These are, indeed, sacrifices you cannot face lightly. And yet they must be accepted willingly and cheerfully. The only alternative would be to yield to the ruthless fanatics whose aim it is to destroy us. We Americans realize this, realize what it means for each of us. We do not need the lash of a dictator to drive us to do our duty. The glory of democracy is the willingness of its citizens to make voluntarily whatever sacrifice is necessary for the common good, or for the preservation of their freedom. You, too, will surely brace yourself to your task, humble though it may be, and by doing your share help create a world where free men may work and live in peace and plenty. 

This pamphlet will try to help you understand your part in this struggle of men for freedom. Let us consider together what meaning this struggle should have for you; how you can help to win it; and how you can help to win the peace that will follow. It is your duty, as a loyal American, to think these problems through.

What Does Fighting for Democracy Mean to You? 

We are all soldiers of democracy. We are all engaged in this war. What are we fighting for? What does democracy fight for?

It may be that the future of our country rests on how well you understand the meaning of these questions. 

What Are We Fighting For? 

First of all, we soldiers of democracy fight for survival. We fight to protect our soil, our freedom, our way of life. We fight for military victory-decisive, complete. But we fight for more than these things. We fight for the right to complete a job we have started for the right of free people to work out their own destiny, and to solve their own problems. For we have always had problems, and we have problems now-problems that will not be solved by the war. 

There are some who say, "What have we to fight for? What have we to defend? Shall we fight to defend slums, unemployment, poverty, discrimination? Are these things worth defending?" 

These people are blind. They are blind for two reasons. First, because in spite of the obvious imperfections of our democracy, we have much that is worth fighting for, even dying for. Second, because they do not realize that democracy keeps on growing and developing, even in war time. The war, like the depression, is bound to be a great educational experience for the American people.

What Democracy Means 

For as we fight for democracy, we ask ourselves what democracy really is. We realize that democracy means more than our traditional civil liberties, precious though they be. We realize even more clearly than we do in peacetime that democracy means freedom and security for the common man-freedom of speech, freedom of religion, freedom from want, and freedom from fear. 

It means the right to a job at decent pay; the right to a minimum standard of living that will permit health, decency and family life; the right to be free from racial, social, or religious persecution; the right to equal opportunity for advancement. And so we democrats fight, not only to safeguard these precious rights we already have, but also for the opportunity to fulfill the promise of

democracy to our generation. We fight for the right to build a better America and a better world, for ourselves and our children. 

This is what fighting for democracy means. 

Because democracy fights for these things, it cannot fight as our enemies fight-with fear, terror, oppression, suspicion, censorship and hate. Democracy does not fear and mistrust its people.

Democracy does not impose undue burdens on the poor who cannot afford them. Democracy does not permit a few to grow rich while others sacrifice. Democracy does not permit a few to use the pretext of war to destroy hard-won social gains. 

Democracy does ask of each of us that we act unselfishly in the common interest, and that we all share in the sacrifices that must be made, up to the limit of our ability. But this war must be paid for out of a lower standard of living. The great masses of our people cannot sacrifice much without serious loss to their health, productive ability, and morale. Such sacrifices would damage our war effort. The greater sacrifices must therefore be made by those whose incomes are well above the minimum necessary to health and decency. This all genuine democrats will be happy to do. As we learn to appreciate these things more deeply, our democracy will improve and strengthen, even as we fight the war. We must strive to perfect our democracy now. The better we make it, the harder we will fight to preserve it, and the more precious will be the victory we shall win.

Why Must You Make Sacrifices? 

The United States will produce, this year, the greatest volume of goods and services in all our history-greater by far than we produced in the "prosperous" year of 1929. And yet for each of us there will be less, considerably less, to consume. "Why is that?” you may ask. For the simple reason that a very large share of what we produce will be going to our armed forces and those of our allies to help in the prosecution of the war. 

How Much Must We Sacrifice?

Just consider the facts. In 1929, we produced goods and services in the amount of 81 billions of dollars. Practically all of these goods and services were for civilian use. In 1941 our total production (even when figured at lower prices), amounted to 94½ billions of dollars. And production for 1942 is expected to total 

117 billions of dollars. But of this huge amount of goods and services, 48 billions are expected to be war goods, leaving only 69 billions of dollars of civilian production. The longer the war lasts, the greater will be the proportion of our production devoted to carrying on the war. The most recent estimates expect war production to reach the rate of 73 billions of dollars yearlyleaving only 45 or so billions for civilian production. These figures should indicate exactly how much sacrifice we people of this nation, taken as a whole, must now make in our standard of living.

The Choice Before Us 

Where do you want our aluminum to go-into pots and pans, or bombing planes? Where would you like to see our steel-in typewriters, bicycles, and automobiles, or in tanks, ships, and guns? Where do you want our sugar-in coffee, jam, and pie, or in munitions? Where do you want our rayon and nylon-in clothing or in parachutes?

Because of our vast natural resources, our machine equipment, our skilled labor, our technological ability, we must serve as the arsenal of democracy in this world-wide conflict. But in spite of our huge productive power, we cannot produce enough for "lifeas-usual" and for victory too. Many of our giant industries, such as the automobile, radio, washing machine, and typewriter indus-

tries have been, or are being, converted to war production. Many scarce raw materials, such as rubber, wool, sugar, and practically all the metals, are being denied to civilian industry. Manpower is flowing to where it can best serve the war effort. This is one way, then, in which we must all make sacrifices, by denying ourselves cheerfully things we would otherwise wear and use. Fortunately, we need not anticipate serious food shortages. With few exceptions, we shall have all the food we need. 

Giving Your Time 

In addition, there are other sacrifices you will have to make. In war time, there is much work to be done. Sewing, knitting, messenger service, first aid work, summer farm work, army and navy auxiliary services, salvage and reclamation of paper, metals, and waste of all kinds, the planting of Victory Gardens-these are some of the jobs that need to be done. And here the job is so vast in scope that everyone must pitch in and do his share. Our time and our energy-these we are each privileged to contribute to the common cause. 

Giving Your Money

And how are we to pay for this war? Our government must tax us heavily if it is to pay for the guns, ships, planes and tanks we need. Beyond that, it asks us to lend it our savings, and for the same reason. Here again we must sacrifice. Our government is now spending at the rate of $100,000,000 every day. By the end of the year this rate will have reached $200,000,000 every day. 

This means that 73 billion dollars will be spent by our government, every year, out of a total income of perhaps 120 billion dollars. 

In other words, more than half the average family's income will have to go to the government in one way or another. This will give you an idea of just how much we will have to give to the government, either in taxes or in war bond and stamp purchases. 

Unless we pay and pay, and save and save, until it actually hurts, we cannot say that we are doing our share. 

Democracy needs our time, our energy, our money, our devotion. These require sacrifices. But these sacrifices we should be proud to make. Our willingness to make them is proof of the respect we have for ourselves as human beings.

How Can You Help to Prevent Inflation? 

Almost as serious as our military war against our enemies, is our domestic war against inflation. Your family's war ration book, the heavier taxes you are paying, the bonds and stamps you are buying, our price-fixing regulations, the President's special message to Congress-all these are evidence of the seriousness of the fight.

Why Do We Fear Inflation? 

Inflation is to be feared for several reasons. For one thing, when prices rise rapidly, wages and other forms of income seldom keep pace with them. This means less goods and services can be bought with the family income. It means a lower standard of living.

Secondly, inflation (or rapidly rising prices) increases the cost of the war to our government. Already, price increases have added $35 billion to the cost of this war. We are today devoting more and more of our total production to war purposes. Our government is the largest single buyer in the world. If a given number of ships, tanks, planes, and guns can today be bought for 

100 billion dollars, then a price increase of 50 per cent would increase the cost of that same equipment to 150 billion dollars; a price increase of 100 per cent would increase the cost to 200 billion dollars. And since we shall have to pay for all this later in the form of higher taxes, it is plain that it is to our interest to keep prices, and the cost of the war, down as low as possible. 

There is an additional reason why inflation is to be feared. As 

President Roosevelt has pointed out, "whatever goes up must come down." A rapid rise in prices during war time is always followed by a disastrous fall in prices after the war. Now rapidly falling prices always create trouble. Business men are unable to sell their goods at a profit; many factories close; discharged workers lose their purchasing power, which causes other factories to close; farmers, business men, and consumers who have borrowed money when prices were high find it difficult to repay those debts, as money becomes scarce and "dear"; and unemployment and depression generally follow. If we are to avoid depression after the war, then, it is urgent that we prevent inflation now. 

There is still another reason for avoiding inflation. In war time 10

we expect everyone to share the burden. "Equality of sacrifice" is the rule. And yet, rapidly rising prices affect certain classes of people much more than they do others. Unorganized, unskilled workers suffer much more than do organized, skilled workers who are better able to demand more pay. People who live on fixed incomes cannot buy as much as they did before. This means that government employees, school teachers, widows, disabled veterans, and old people living on pensions and insurance payments, suffer greatly. And well-to-do people, whose incomes are large, suffer least of all.

Why is such a condition undesirable? In the first place, it is obviously unfair. It means that scarce goods go in large measure to those who have the most money, while others less fortunate are made to bear the entire brunt of the war. But it means more than this. It hurts our ability to carry on the war successfully. People in low income groups, who are unable to buy the necessities of life, suffer in health and morale. They can neither work nor fight well. And it would not be surprising if they came to feel that this was not "their" war.

For these reasons, it is imperative that we prevent further increases in the cost of living.

How Can We Stop Inflation? 

In order to stop inflation, we must understand what brings it about. Inflation may come about in different ways, and for different reasons; but it is very clear why prices have been rising in the United States. In the first place, our government has been spending tremendous sums on the war program; and this money has been paid out to the people of America. We have, as a result, more money to spend than we had before. At the same time, because of shortages of manpower, raw materials, and factory equipment, we have cut down or eliminated much of our former production of civilian consumers' goods. Leon Henderson, our

Price Administrator, has estimated that in 1942 consumers will have 86 billion dollars to spend, while only 69 billion dollars worth of goods and services will be available for purchasers. As President Roosevelt pointed out, "You don't have to be a professor of mathematics or of economics to see that prices will have to rise." 

If the chief causes for rising prices are more money in circula- 11

tion and less goods to be bought, it is clear that any successful program of inflation control would have to deal with these causes. 

It would have to increase the amount of consumers' goods available for purchase, or remove the excess purchasing power in the hands of consumers, or both.

What Have We Done to Prevent Inflation? 

The chief step taken so far to prevent price inflation was the passage of the Emergency Price Control Act, which gave to a 

Price Administrator the power to "freeze" or control prices.

Reliance was placed on the establishment of price ceilings on selected or "key" commodities. This measure was weakened by the farm bloc in Congress, which first held up passage of the bill, and then succeeded in practically exempting farm prices from the intent of the act. Obviously, if farm prices could rise, workers' food costs would go up, and workers would demand higher wages. Labor, too, won its fight against wage controls while farm prices and industrial profits were rising. Labor took the position that wage controls would prevent millions of workers from improving intolerable wage conditions. 

A second measure taken to control inflation was the enactment of the excess profits tax. If a corporation could not earn more profits as a result of war orders, they would have no incentive to raise prices, nor would they be able to pay out huge dividends to stockholders which would add to excess purchasing power. But the excess profits tax, as it was written, contained several "loopholes." 

American corporations earned two and three times their pre-war earnings, after payment of the new war taxes.

The third major step in our attempt to prevent inflation has been the sale of war bonds and stamps. Americans have responded patriotically to their government's appeals. Sales rose to a peak of $1 billion last January, immediately after Pearl Harbor, but have since dropped off considerably. At the present rate of sale, the amount of purchasing power being taken off is altogether inadequate to the task of taking excess purchasing power out of the hands of consumers.

The test of how effective our attempts at inflation control have been may be found in two facts: first, that prices have risen steadily throughout 1941; and second, that at the present rate of taxation and bond sales, purchasing power will exceed consumer 12

production in 1942 by $17 billion. This has been called "the inflationary gap."

What Must We Do To Prevent Inflation? 

Alarmed by the rising cost of living, and by the 17 billion dollar "inflationary gap", President Roosevelt delivered a special message to Congress on April 27, 1942. In this message, he asked for a vigorous fight against inflation. Heavier taxes, including a limit on individual incomes of $25,000; higher corporation and excess profits taxes to limit profits to "a reasonably low level"; rationing of all scarce commodities; "stabilization” of wages and farm prices; the extension of price ceilings to all items affecting the cost of living; the lowering of ceilings on farm prices; further restrictions on credit and installment buying; and vast increases in the sale of war bonds on a voluntary basis—these were the main features in his program. 

Opposition to this program has already developed. The farm bloc opposes curbs on farm prices; business opposes more severe profits taxes; labor opposes wage limitations; wealthy individuals oppose the $25,000 limit on their incomes, and the public may not respond sufficiently in its purchase of war bonds and stamps. 

Even if the program were to be completely adopted, we may doubt whether it would prevent inflation. Would it take out of circulation the 17 billion dollars of excess purchasing power now in circulation; and the even greater amount of excess purchasing power predicted for 1943? That is the crucial question. If it will not, then no price control act in the world can prevent inflation. 

The experience of Germany, Italy, Russia, France, and England proves that, where excess purchasing power exists, a "black market" is bound to develop. People with money will illegally buy scarce goods from traders or individuals who cannot resist the temptation of profitable prices. Such a "bootleg" market would break down inflation control.

How can we prevent the breakdown of our efforts? First, workers, farmers, business men and others must be really willing to make sacrifices, instead of trying to pass the burden off to the other fellow. Taxes must be increased to the point where they really hurt-provided that those who can best afford the biggest sacrifices are asked to make them. Second, we shall, in all proba- 13

bility, have to abandon the scheme of voluntary purchase of war bonds and adopt compulsory savings. And third, rationing will probably have to be extended to cover a great majority of the goods which most people buy. 

These methods will effectively reduce the demand for scarce goods. We should also take steps to make goods less scarce. In this connection we might consider the release of vast stores of government-held wheat, cotton, and other commodities (so far prevented by the farm bloc); the widespread adoption of labor-management councils to increase productive efficiency; the employment of the three or more millions of people still unemployed; the more widespread use of scientific management; the standardization of styles, and the elimination of frills; and the development of substitutes for scarce materials. 

What can we learn from the failure of our attempts thus far to control inflation? Does it not point out the need for everyone to sacrifice? For obviously, unless there is "equality of sacrifice," no one group wishes to be made the goat.

We need more production to fight inflation. How can you help?

You can plant a Victory Garden, to increase our food supply. 

You can enlist for farm work during the summer. You can try making and repairing goods at home. You can get a useful summer job, or take a training course which will equip you to work later on.

We must reduce the demand for goods to control inflation. How can you help? You can abstain from buying goods unless they are necessary. You can save, and lend to your government, every penny you possibly can. You can use sparingly those things you already have, and make them last longer. When you do buy necessary goods, you should check carefully to make sure that the seller is not charging more than he is permitted to do under the price-fixing order of Leon Henderson. Our government lacks the manpower to check observance of these legally-established prices; it is counting on us to do so. Lastly, you can try to persuade your friends, family, and neighbors also, to help in all these ways.

This last, which is primarily an educational job, is very im- 14

portant. What you, as a single individual may do, is not too important a contribution to our fight against inflation; but if you, and every American, not only understood the need for inflation control, but took the necessary steps to prevent it, our fight against inflation would definitely be won.

How Can You Contribute to the War Effort? 

We have, even with our current war expansion, no more than 12 million factory workers in the United States, about 40 million workers in all non-agricultural employments, approximately 55 million workers in all, including farmers and self-employed. From these workers' efforts is pouring out an unending stream of food, clothing, furniture, houses, war materials, and services of all kinds. To equip our armed forces properly, to help our allies, and to provide for our civilian population will require a much larger labor force. Imagine how much could be added to that effort by the small individual contribution of America's twenty-odd millions of school children, young and not so young! Even a small effort on your part, multiplied millions of times, could increase our total production by a vast amount. How much will be asked of you before the war is over, no one knows. But as a loyal American, you will want to know what you can do to help now. 

First, you can help by doing your daily job better, and more efficiently. Your job today is to complete your education, as thoroughly, as rapidly, and as well as you can. Languages, mathematics, social studies, sciences, health education-these and other studies have a real role to play in the fight for democracy. You can't handle other jobs well unless you prepare for them by doing your present job well. The habits you form now will help or hinder you later on. Lack of preparation today, unnecessary absence or lateness now, or failure in school subjects are wasteful, unfair, and even unpatriotic. 

Secondly, you can start your own private "war" against waste. 

As a consumer you can buy wisely and use carefully. Every penny you spend requires the use of men, materials, machines, transportation, power-the productive resources we need to carry on the war. Before you buy an article you want, ask yourself, "Do I need this? Will it unnecessarily divert our national energies from more important tasks?" Once you make a purchase, make good use of it, take good care of it. That too will mean money saved for you; and more labor, raw materials and machinery available for war production. Avoid waste in your use of electric power at home, in unnecessary use of the family automobile, and so on, all along the line. 

Thirdly, buy more bonds and stamps. Your government needs 16

the money; they are a good investment for you to make; and as we have just seen, you will be helping to prevent price inflation. 

Fourthly, be loyal in thought and faith, as well as in deed. Have faith in your government. It's easy to grumble and criticize; but such actions don't help much. Your government believes in freedom of speech, and welcomes your constructive criticism. But it hasn't the time or the energy to cope with malicious "sniping"; nor can it allow itself to be goaded into revealing valuable information that may be of use to the enemy. Criticize, yes; but make sure your criticism is of the helpful, not of the destructive, variety. 

And finally, be sure to take care of your health, both physical and mental. If you eat properly of nourishing and body-building foods, if you get enough rest, recreation, and exercise, you will be making yourself fit and strong for whatever demands your country may make of you, whether as worker, farmer, housewife, professional, or soldier. "What can I do to help?" That is the question our boys and girls ask today. “Here's what I am doing!" That is the answer you should be able to give tomorrow.

How Can You Best Withstand Propaganda? 

In any nation at war rumors are plentiful. "The English aren't fighting. They want us to do their fighting for them." "We can't trust the Russians. After we beat Germany, we shall have to take on and lick the Russians." These are some of the stories people are repeating. Perhaps you have heard them. Perhaps you have believed them. Perhaps you have repeated them. But are they true? Will they stand up under examination? Who is responsible for them? Who will benefit by our believing them-we or our enemies?

In our country, in any country, there are some people who are natural pessimists. Their attitude is: "What's the use? We can't win." Others are natural optimists. Their attitude is: "What's the hurry? They can't hurt us. We've got lots of time. Take it easy."

Both are wrong. And both are dangerous, because if we were to listen to them, we would not exert our full energy in the prosecution of the war.

Hitler has boasted that he will not need an army to conquer the United States. He is confident that he can get us to start fighting among ourselves. That is why skillful agents of the Axis, financed by millions and millions of dollars, are trying to create confusion, doubt, misunderstanding, jealousy, and hatred in the minds of Americans. They are trying to poison our minds. And a few traitorous Americans, with the help of a great many foolish Americans who do not realize the part they are playing, are acting as Hitler's agents here. 

What are some of the rumors and lies they are spreading? They are trying to convince us that every step we take for the defense of democracy is a step toward fascism; that our allies are not to be trusted; that we are doomed to defeat; that democracy is weak and inefficient; that our president wants to be a dictator; that we were dragged unnecessarily into this war; and a good many other lies of a similar nature. 

Do you fall for this Axis propaganda? If you do, you are justifying Hitler's claim that he can capture us from within. 

How can we guard ourselves against overconfidence, defeatism, suspicion of our allies, internal dissension, lack of faith in democracy? One way is to ask, when we hear statements of this kind: "What is the source of this statement? Who is responsible for it? 18

Whom will it help us or the enemy?" Another way to guard against such propaganda is to read carefully the facts that come to us from official or reliable sources, so that we can check up on these stories. A third way is to be always cool, calm and unbiased, for most of these stories flourish best in an atmosphere of hysteria, panic and prejudice. A fourth way is to read and study, as much as you can—for malicious propaganda cannot live where truth and knowledge prevail. 

Does all this mean that you shouldn't criticize? Should you be silent if you see evidence of discrimination, graft, inefficiency, or injustice? Not at all! Truth can never hurt the cause of freedom; it can only advance that cause. Democracy needs self-criticism. 

Constant discussion and education are the guides to intelligent action. It is your duty to inform yourself of the real facts of every problem that confronts us. Knowledge and truth are powerful weapons in the arsenal of democracy. They are also our best defense against propaganda.

Are You Doing Your Share? 

One evening during the past vacation, a group of high school boys and girls in a large American city met in the home of one of the boys. They were all friends and lived in the same neighborhood. 

They had been considering whether or not to form a social club. 

That evening, however, the discussion seemed to be lagging. Fred, a high school senior, sensed this. "What's the trouble, gang?" he asked, "Last month, when we met, you were all enthusiastic about forming the club. Now everyone seems to be in the dumps." 

There was an embarrassed silence, while everyone cleared his throat and looked at the floor. Finally one of the group, Edith, a bright, attractive young girl, spoke up. “I was thinking about our proposed club this week. Then, my brother Bill got his 1A classification from the draft board. Frankly, I didn't feel much like joining a social club after that." "That's right," said Jim, one of the acknowledged leaders of the group. "We're in a war now. We can't go on as we did before. 

Of course we're too young to join the army; and we've got to live, and go to school, and play. But it seems to me our attitude has got to be different. Why don't we form a Victory club? We can help sell war savings bonds and stamps, and organize salvage campaigns for waste paper and do lots of other useful things. 

And above all, we can discuss some of the problems connected with winning the war and the peace to follow. We can learn, have swell meetings, and help democracy, all at the same time.” "That's the idea," chorused the others, "that's the kind of club we want."

This group of young Americans is doing its share. Are you doing yours? Have you thought about what this war means to you, what democracy is; whether or not you are preparing for the kind of job that can best help your country? Have you discussed the many problems connected with an all-out war effort? 

A democracy depends on the understanding of all its citizens, not only those old enough to vote, but of its youth as well. Do you understand what is happening? Can you honestly say that you are a worthy citizen of democracy?

The boys and girls referred to above, Fred, Edith, Jim and the rest, are average American boys and girls like you. They not only discussed these problems among themselves. They raised them in 20

their classes, particularly in their Economics, History, Civics, and English classes. They felt they were really getting something vital out of school. They were learning to think about America's problems, about their problems, about your problems. 

One day, after they had been studying and discussing every day for several months such problems as "Why aren't we producing enough planes, tanks, and ships?", "What are we doing to speed up war production?", "What sacrifices must we make?", "How shall we pay for the war?", and they felt that they were qualified to come to certain conclusions, Fred summed up their findings in the following report.

Youth Learns from the Past 

"Until the attack on Pearl Harbor, most people in this country didn't take the war very seriously. They felt it was no concern of ours. Some people still feel that way. As a result we made an awful lot of mistakes in the period from 1939-1941. We still haven't corrected all of these mistakes. "What were these mistakes? Why were they made? The whole thing may be summed up by saying we were living in a "businessas-usual" period. That is, although we had promised to become the arsenal of democracy, we were unwilling to do anything that would interfere with the normal course of events. We students carried on much as we had before. Business men were concerned with their profits. Why should they change over to the production of war materials if they could make more profits from their peace time activities? Why should they expand their plants if these plants wouldn't be of any use after the war, or if expansion might lead to overproduction later on? Labor also had a me-first attitude in many cases. 'If industry is making a lot of profit,' many workers undoubtedly said to themselves, 'why shouldn't we get our cut too in the form of higher wages?' Farmers, seeing an opportunity to make up for the long years of the depression, demanded higher prices for farm products. Many housewives, remembering what happened in the last war, when sugar and other things became scarce and went sky-high in price, rushed to the stores and stocked up on everything on which they could lay their hands. This, of course, only forced prices up still further, for hoarding always brings panic and inflation. Speculators, quick to take advantage of other people's misfortunes and the chance to 21

make an easy dollar, began to withhold vital supplies from the markets. This made the situation still worse. Many selfish Congressmen, worrying about reelection, held up the price control bill and finally passed a very weak law. The Administration, itself, did not assume leadership, but seemed to be waiting for the people to tell the government what to do and how to do it. No one had full responsibility for the war effort. Countless agencies were set up. 

Red tape impeded everything. Stupidity and selfishness were too frequently found. Washington, the capital of our government, was a scene of confusion.

Youth Looks Forward 

"Pearl Harbor has made us realize that this war is a battle of production. We cannot say we are doing our best—until every man, woman and child, every factory, mine and mill, every machine and every tool is properly organized to do its utmost to win the war. Germany is devoting some 70 per cent of her productive energies to the war. This means that 30 per cent is the bare minimum necessary to keep the population alive. In addition, Germany had been preparing for this war for years. They have a headstart of at least six years. We can be sure that Japan has been doing the same thing. Meanwhile, in 1940, less than 5 per cent of our energies were being devoted to war. In 1941, the percentage rose to a maximum of 15 per cent, a big increase; but we were still taking things in our stride. We lived better last year, on the average, than ever before in our history. Now, even though we are the richest country in the world, with the greatest degree of technical skill, with a mass production industry that is the envy of every country in the world, we cannot lick the forces of fascism with one hand tied behind our backs. Bombers, tanks, and ships on order cannot win the battle of India, or the battle of Russia, or the battle of the Atlantic, or any other battle except the battle of blueprints. It is only the things we have on hand that win the military battles. "What changes are we making? What further changes must we make? We must, it should now be obvious to everyone, make our effort an all-out effort. We must convert our tremendous resources and skill to the making of instruments of war. We must do this job speedily, and yet we must do it democratically. Our slogan must be: Those who can must sacrifice the most; but 22

everyone must sacrifice. We must take the profit out of war. We must prevent inflation, for rapidly rising prices are disastrous and can ruin everything. We must plan to utilize all our manpower and resources intelligently and efficiently. Everyone must be given the job he is best fitted for, whether it be in the front line trenches, on the assembly line, or on the farm. "This obviously means a drastic change in our accepted way of living. For the country as a whole, our standard of living must decline by at least one third. For the rich, those who can afford it, it must decline even more. We must assure a minimum health level for all. Starved workers cannot work long hours or produce efficiently; nor do they make good soldiers when they are selected for service in our armed forces. We must end all forms of discrimination now, for a worker who is refused a job because of his race or religion hurts the cause of democracy just as much as does an enemy machine gun. In short, we must make our democracy work now as it really can work. Otherwise, it is open to question whether we can survive at all."

The boys and girls who made this analysis may not have all the answers to all the problems confronting us. But they made a real attempt to understand and to solve our problems. Such an attempt is part of the duty of every American today. Are you doing your share?

Need You Fear a Post-War Collapse? 

At this moment, when we are gripped in a life-or-death struggle with an enemy who is determined to destroy us, it may seem a waste of time and energy to plan for the period after the war. 

Looking and planning ahead are not separate, however, from the war effort; they are part of it. We cannot afford to ruin a military victory by an economic defeat. 

Why do so many Americans fear a post-war collapse? Every great war since the Industrial Revolution has been followed by a long and severe depression. Demobilized soldiers add to the army of the unemployed. These people, whose incomes have disappeared, cease to buy goods. Thus still other workers lose their jobs. The depression grows in severity like a snowball rolling downhill. Its effects are felt for years. Your parents probably remember the depression of 1921 that followed the last war. They probably realize also that the Great Depression that began in 1929 was caused, in large measure, by World War I. 

The Size of the Problem 

Looking forward, our problem at the end of this war may seem even more difficult to solve. Never before will so many millions of workers have been engaged in war industry. Never before will our country have had so many millions of men under arms. According to the National Resources Planning Board, we shall have about 23 million workers engaged in war work by the end of the war. Our general staff is planning an army of from 

7 to 10 million men. At first glance, the unemployment problem we shall face will be tremendous. And yet, there are many reasons for believing that the end of World War II will usher in, not a period of depression, but one of tremendous boom. Why are most economists today confident that this is so? 

Reasons for Confidence

In the first place, we have learned a great deal from the lessons of the last war. We are already curbing civilian demand in many ways. Many civilian industries have already been closed down. 

Heavy taxes are cutting our spending power. The government is making a determined effort to borrow from us as much money as 24

it can. This means that at the end of the war we shall all have accumulated postponed demands for a variety of goods-automobiles, refrigerators, radios, washing machines, houses, and many other durable goods. At the same time, we shall have accumulated savings in war bonds which the government shall then pay out to us. In addition to this, we shall be able to draw upon billions of dollars in Social Security funds. Thus purchasing power will be available to support civilian industries while we convert from war to peace.

Again, there is no doubt that after the war there will be four tremendous jobs for American industry to do. One of these jobs will be to convert to civilian production the huge war plants that we have built and are still building. The second will be to produce steel, machinery and other goods of all kinds for the war-torn countries of Europe, and for the backward countries of the world. 

A third job will be to replace the worn-out machinery which is now being worked without rest to produce for the war. A fourth job will be to produce millions of homes, of which a shortage already exists. These jobs alone will provide employment for millions of men and women. 

Furthermore, it is hardly likely that, at the end of the war, our government will repeat the mistake of last time and throw millions of soldiers and sailors on the labor market to look for jobs. It is far more likely that the government will keep these men in the service for a limited time, and train them for jobs in civilian industry as those jobs develop. 

And then again, even today industry is conducting research as never before to find new products, new methods, new services, with which to take up the post-war slack. 

And lastly, we have learned that planned public spending on a large scale, as for public works, can aid tremendously in solving the unemployment problem. It is therefore likely that, as long as the need exists, our government will do whatever is necessary to prevent human misery, distress, and want. 

For these reasons it seems clear that a post-war slump is not inevitable. It can be avoided, if we pursue the right policies. What we really need to fear is not a post-war slump, but what one economist has called "the post post-war slump." What he means is that after the war we may enter upon another great period of 25

boom such as that of 1923-1929, ending in another, and perhaps fatal, depression. Such an unplanned, unhealthy boom is indeed to be feared. That is why we must consider now how we can create in America permanent peace and prosperity after the war.

How Can You Help Ensure a Life of Abundance 

After the War?

Hitler proclaims that he is fighting for a "New Order." The 

Japanese speak of a "Greater Co-Prosperity Sphere for East

Asia." The United States, in such historic documents as the 

Atlantic Charter, the United Nations Pact, and the Four Freedoms speech of President Roosevelt, has also declared to the world that we do not intend to go back to the kind of world that existed before World War II. What kind of world do we intend to create?

The answer to that question will be determined largely by the kind of America we build here at home. The social, political, and economic policies we pursue within our borders will be reflected in our foreign policy. Democracy, like charity, begins at home. But the policies we adopt will be policies of your making. That is why it is important that you begin to think of these problems now. 

Americans have always dreamed and worked for a life of peace, liberty, and plenty. Never before has the realization of these goals been so nearly within our power. 

How Much Can We Produce? 

The American people have never yet enjoyed a satisfactory standard of living. Even in 1929, our most prosperous year before this war, 6 million families received incomes of less than $1,000 a year ($20 a week). In that same year, another 10 million families received incomes between $1,000 and $2,000 a year ($20 to $40 a week). These 16 million families constituted well over half the total number of families in the United States. And yet, economists have estimated that in 1929 an income considerably larger than $2,000 a year was necessary to provide adequate living conditions for the average American family. 

Are we in the United States physically capable of providing a comfortable living level for every American family? Figures show that there are about 30 million families in the United States, plus approximately 10 million single individuals living outside of families. To provide $3,000 a year for each family, and $1,500 for each individual, would require a national income of at least $105 billion. We produced, during 1941, goods and services valued at $94½ billion; and we expect to produce, in 1942, and income of $117 billion. For 1943 the production total, even reckoned 27

at current price levels, is expected to go considerably higher. In other words, we can produce enough to create a life of comfort for all, and still reward unusual ability and effort. 

How Can We Do the Job? 

Our first problem, it will be evident, will be to continue to produce as fully in peace time as we are now doing in war time. 

Instead of producing huge quantities of ships, tanks, planes, and guns, we must use the same energies to produce more food, more clothing, more furniture, more housing, more of all the goods and services that will make life richer and worthwhile. 

Our second problem will be to see that America's millions of workers, farmers, professionals and business men receive enough purchasing power to buy the products of our fertile farmlands and our busy mills. To this end, obviously, a high wage policy is necessary. "Standard" wages must be raised. Sub-standard wages must be relegated to the past. High prices, and the monopolies that maintain them, must go. There is no place for them in an economy of abundance.

A third problem will be to see that all this vast economic activity is balanced and coordinated, so that it does not go awry. There must be balance between industry and agriculture, between production and income, between savings and investment. Wages cannot go up without an increase in productive efficiency. Production cannot increase without an increase in wages. Savings should not be permitted to accumulate, if they cannot be invested. 

Things such as these have caused us serious economic breakdowns in the past; they have caused us to produce far below our capacity to enrich life for the average man. 

In achieving full production and employment, and in maintaining them, lie our best hope for a happy and prosperous America. Unemployment, insecurity, and poverty create dissatisfaction, discord, and strife. Employment, security, and comfort promote satisfaction, cooperation, and happiness. If we work as hard to win the peace as we are working now to win the war, we can make our land a real and joyous home for the American people. We can make real the American dream of freedom, equal opportunity, and abundance for all. In this future you and every other American youth would have ample opportunity to lead a useful, happy, and well-balanced life. 28

To get such a future you must be worthy of it. You must be willing to think, to discuss, to read, to study, and to work. Our best leaders and thinkers are writing and lecturing, trying as best they can to reach you with their message. Your task is to be open to all these ideas and influences; to seek amongst them for the truth; and then, as a citizen in our great democracy, to make your voice heard in the great decisions that lie before us. The kind of America that you will live in the rest of your life is the America you will make. It will be of your making whether it results from your interest or apathy, your understanding or ignorance, your participation or failure to participate. If government is to be of the people and for the people, surely it must be by the people. You-your family, your friends, your neighborsyou are the people. The choice is up to you. You have shown yourself willing to work and plan to win the war. Are you willing to work and plan to win the peace?

How Can You Help Create a Lasting Peace? 

This war has brought home to us Americans a terrible lesson. 

It is possible to win a war and lose the peace. We helped win 

World War I in 1918; today we realize that what we won was not peace, but an armistice. That is why we are today determined to win, not only the war, but the peace as well. We are determined that victory this time shall bring with it a just, beneficial, and lasting peace.

Is this, however, the time to talk of the peace we shall make after we win the war? Must we rather not win the war first, and make sure that we shall be the ones to write the peace? It is tempting to think so; but there are at least two good reasons for believing this is not true. 

In the first place, war creates pain, anger, and hate. If we wait till the war's end, we are much less likely to think out the problems of the peace coolly than we are now. Second, and much more important, planning for peace now will help us to win the war. 

If our war and peace aims are democratic and meaningful, if they hold out the prospect of employment, security, and better living conditions for our own people and all the people of the world, our cause must be thereby advanced. The boys in our armed forces will fight harder; the citizens behind the lines will make more willingly every sacrifice we ask of them; we will inspire hope in the hearts of untold millions of oppressed colonial peoples, so that they will go "all out" for a democratic victory; peoples in Axis-dominated territory, and even the people of our enemy nations, will become a democratic "fifth column" in the fascist camp. 

What kind of peace, then, shall the victorious United Nations make? What kind of "Brave New World" shall we create? Before we attempt to answer this question, let us look back to the end of World War I; for although we failed last time to win the peace, there is much that we can learn from that experience. 

Lessons of World War I 

Why did the Versailles Treaty fail? Some people claim we made a great mistake in treating Germany as leniently as we did. Others believe the Versailles Treaty was much too harsh; that the burdens it placed on Germany made it impossible for her 30

to prosper, and thus led to the rise of Hitler to power. Still others hold that the failure of the United States to enter the League of Nations condemned that attempt at world federation to certain weakness and ultimate collapse.

None of these reasons, however, tells the whole story. Essentially the peace failed because in making it we failed to solve the problems that had brought on the war. The hopes and dreams of 

President Wilson were frustrated and shattered by selfish and imperialistic power politicians. These men were not motivated by a desire to build a better world. They had no vision of a democratic world community in which cooperative peoples could work, trade, and live in peace and plenty. They wanted a peace which would preserve within their own nations an aristocracy of wealth and privilege; they wanted a peace which would retain for their own nations continued domination over colonial areas and peoples. 

In other words, they wished to preserve, domestically and on a world scale, the status quo. But such a status quo was unjust; and being unjust it was bound to fail. Mussolini in Italy, Hitler in Germany, are evidences of its failure within nations. World 

War II is evidence of its failure on a world scale. 

Learning from the Past 

What can we learn from the mistakes of Versailles? First, the peace we make at the end of this war must solve the problems that bring on war. It must create, within nations and among nations, those conditions that make life tolerable for the common man. 

Democracy is not only a political system; it is a way of life. It means cooperation, sharing, harmony. It means an absence of privilege, an equality of opportunity for everyone, everywhere; for all peoples, rather than for a few. Democracy, if it is to exist, cannot exist in the United States and a few advanced European nations alone. It must exist in India, in China, in 

Russia, in the Middle East, in the East Indies, in Latin-America. 

Within nations and among nations, democracy must be permitted to fulfill its promise to the peoples of the world. This is what 

President Roosevelt meant when he spoke of the Four Freedoms for everyone, everywhere.

Now obviously, it is easier to state our goals than it is to work out detailed methods of achieving them. Nor will it be possible to 31

bring into immediate existence such a world after the war. For one thing, it may not be possible to trust our defeated enemies with complete self-rule immediately after the war. Nor will we be able to raise, all at once, the standards of living of millions of economically backward peoples so that they, too, can be “free from want." The problems of transition will be many and difficult. 

But if we hold our goals clearly before our eyes, we shall solve these problems one by one. The important thing is to know what we are after. The rest will come.

After the war America will probably exercise the most effective voice in the making of the peace-not only because of our youth and strength, but because the peoples of the world trust in our generosity and our ideals. But you are America! There will be many voices after the war calling upon you to support and follow them. There will be angry voices, calling upon you for vengeance.

There will be selfish voices, calling upon you to reap selfish national advantage. There will be undemocratic voices, calling upon you to preserve class and partisan privileges. Your job in making the peace is to reject these voices. If you will hold before you constantly the high ideals of democracy and humanity; if you will cherish the vision of a better world for everyone, everywhere; and if you will do everything you can as a student, a citizen, a human being-then we Americans and the common peoples of the entire world will secure a peace that will permit all of us to live happy, secure, useful, and richer lives.
