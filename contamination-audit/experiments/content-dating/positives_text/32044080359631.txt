GENERAL VIEW OF PANAMA CANAL.

PANAMA AND COLON*.'. 

ANAMA and Colón-gateway cities to the greatest of man-made waterways-were there long before the oceans were united. Both coasts of the Isthmus began to figure in exploration, romance, and commerce a few decades after the discovery of the New World. 

Deep-water passages on or near the tenth degree of north latitude are numerous around the world. There is Bab el 

Mandeb, leading into the Red Sea just a few degrees north of this imaginary line; not far south of the same latitude lies the busy Strait of Malacca, separating the Malay States from Sumatra; similarly located is the Strait of 

Mindanao, leading into the Sulu Sea; Palk Strait between

India and Ceylon is bisected by latitude 10; near the same latitude is the Strait of Maracaibo, opening to world shipping the petroleum deposits of Venezuela; and in the region of latitude 10 Trinidad divides ocean waters and inakes two straits leading to the asphalt lakes of the same country. Then comes man and cuts a hemisphere asunder at Panama and provides another of the world's most useful straits or canals. And this canal is only 1° south of latitude 10!

Let us look at the location of the Isthmus of Panama. 

Could a north-and-south line be drawn from Niagara Falls, it would cut through Panama. The narrow neck of land extending east and west and joining North and South America makes a considerable bend; south of this bend is the Gulf of Panama. About midway of the semicircular shore line of the gulf stands the present city of Panama. *Panama, population, 60,000; Colón, 31,500.

A GLANCE INTO HISTORY 

Columbus himself, it will be remembered, on his fourth and last voyage sailed his caravels along the coast of what is now Panama, endeavoring to discover the long-sought water route to Cathay. In his wake came Balbao, Pizarro, and hosts of others, who succeeded in establishing a settlement in what they called Darien, naming it Santa María de la Antigua. It was from that base that Balboa and his men went forth to penetrate the jungle that led to an unknown sea. After 26 days of suffering and hardship, reward came on the morning of September 26, 1513, when 

Balboa, "standing silent upon a peak in Darien," saw the vast expanse of what is probably now San Miguel Bay, a part of the great western ocean. A few days later Balboa actually reached the waters of the Pacific and claimed them for the King of Spain. 

It was not until 1519, however, that one Pedrarias (Pedro Arias) founded the base or settlement on the southern or Pacific side of the Isthmus, which became known as Panama,an aboriginal name applied to those who fished in river or ocean; but the earlier name of Darien, which 

Spaniards bestowed on the eastern part of the Isthmus, still clings to the region. Nearly two decades passed before 

Emperor Charles V by decree established the "Real Audiencia de Panamá" (1538), which was given jurisdiction far northward and hundred of miles to the south. As the settlements on either side of Darien began to grow, other 

Spaniards, led by Pizarro, Almagro, and Father Luque, were forming plans and preparing craft for sailing southward to lands reputed to hold gold and other treasure in more abundance.

From a jungle path there was gradually constructed a highway-a "camino real"-between the oceans, and over this route many a burden-bearing animal and human slave were pressed into service. The Atlantic terminus of this route, Porto Bello, became the destination of the galleons from Spain; and Panama, the Pacific terminal, the port of the coastwise ships that bore the minerals northward from Peru.

Time passed. Panama waxed prosperous. It became the envy of English and other European explorers. Sir Francis 

Drake, from a "goodly and great high tree" somewhere in the jungle of the Isthmus, is said to have been the first Englishman to see the Pacific Ocean and the little

Spanish settlement on its shore. In one of Drake's raids, that on Nombre de Dios, he and his men are said to have captured a stack of silver bars "70 feet long, 10 feet wide, and 12 feet high," which were en route from Peru to Spain. Drake died in 1596 and, according to one version of history, his body was buried in the bay of Porto Bello not far from the present site of Colón. 

The destruction of the town of Panama, however, was not accomplished until after the arrival of the notorious raider Henry Morgan, who, landing at Porto Bello one dark night in 1669, captured, looted, and destroyed that port. Returning the following year with a force of 1,400 men, he proceeded overland to the conquest of Panama, which had grown to be a place of 8,000 or more population. A thousand houses, more or less, among them a number of massive stone structures, met the gaze of Morgan and his buccaneers when at sunrise, January 28, 1671, the battle began. Before the dawn of another day Panama lay a heap of ashes, with here and there the remains of stone buildings. Today the vine-clad tower of the cathedral, a few massive walls, stone arches of at least two bridges, and some minor ruins are all that remain of what must have been an interesting and prosperous frontier of civilization.

The location chosen for the new settlement was about six miles west of the destroyed town, where a high and rocky peninsula seemed to offer natural defense advantages. In the rear rose a great hill, known today as Ancon; to seaward lay many shoals, coral reefs, and several islands, but with certain deep-water passages between them. 

It was on this peninsula that a new Panama began to rise. 

Bishop relates that as the King of Spain stood one day gazing to westward from a castle window, some one ventured to inquire the reason for such intent interest. “I am looking for the expensive walls at Panama-they cost enough to be seen from here," replied the ruler.

Many of the new buildings were of stone and masonry three to four feet thick, with iron-clad doors; windows appeared only on second floors, while church windows were from 15 to 20 feet above street level. Streets were laid out running north and south and east and west, almost exactly with the cardinal points. Approximately in the central part of the peninsula a new cathedral began to rise, and near by a large space was reserved for a plaza or "parque central;" the extreme point of land jutting into the bay became the Plaza de Armas, while the numerous attractive sites reserved for churches indicate the strong religious spirit of the Spaniards, which contrast strikingly with some of their actions. Panama streets in the early days were narrow, the plazas and the flat roofs of the houses being about the only places where the sea breezes could be really enjoyed. On the other hand, the dungeons, where unruly slaves and law breakers were confined, were as horrible as the worst prison elsewhere in the world, judging by the specimens still existing in Panama.

1847-48 Congress began to consider the matter of transportation, with the result that shortly before the California gold discoveries (1848-49) startled the world, the establishment of two mail lines of steamships was authorized. One of these lines was to operate from New 

York to the Chagres River, on the Isthmus of Panama, near where the city of Colón is now located; the other was to connect Panama and San Francisco. How these mails were to be conveyed across the 46 miles of tropical jungle to Panama was a problem left to be solved. A few men, however, were wise enough to see the necessity of some means of transit across the Isthmus, for the discovery of gold in California had started thousands of people from the Eastern States toward the land of supposed riches. 

To these adventurers three routes lay open: That of crossing 3,000 miles of new country; the 14,000-mile voyage by sailing ship around Cape Horn, or by voyage to Panama and across the jungle, connecting with ships bound for California.

In this emergency six American financiers, of whom the well-known New York merchant, William II. Aspinwall, was the leading spirit, supplied capital for building the much-needed railroad across Panama. John L. Stevens, Henry Chauncey, George Law, Alexander Center, and

David Hoadley were his associates and assistants. The chief engineer was George M. Totten, aided by numerous other distinguished engineers from the United States. The financiers provided a total of about $8,000,000. The survey was begun in 1850, and at midnight on January 27, 1855, during a tropical downpour, the last rail in the road was laid. Panama and Aspinwall (now Colón) were at last connected by a railroad, and the following day a locomotive passed from ocean to ocean.

THE PANAMA CANAL 

About this time canal building in the Old World was the subject of attention, and its influence was eventually felt in the New World. Despite the opposition of Great 

Britain, the French, under the leadership of Ferdinand de Lesseps, secured a concession for constructing a canal from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, a distance of about 100 miles. Such a stupendous undertaking had never until then been considered feasible, but de Lesseps was successful in raising capital, and actual work was begun in 1859. During the following decade the Suez Canal gradually assumed form, until in 1869 it united the Mediterranean with the Indian Ocean. Success at Suez raised the fame and prestitge of de Lesseps and his co-workers, who then turned to the greater task of cutting the Americas asunder at Panama. By 1879 concessions from Colombia were secured, a company was formed, and work commenced. The cost was to be $214,000,000, while the period of construction was set at eight years. Shortly after this period the United States found urgent need, in the Atlantic, of a great war vessel, the Oregon, then in the Pacific Ocean. The war with Spain had begun; the course before the Oregon was 14,000 miles, or two months' steaming time. The voyage was made in safety but this emergency was an object lesson to the American people. Shortly thereafter there arose a demand for a canal at Panama to the end that the United States Navy should never again be so greatly divided. This demand ripened into congressional action, and eventually the United States Government purchased the rights of the

The activities of the French had caused Panama and Colón to grow in population, but when, after negotiating a treaty with Panama, the United States undertook the task of canal construction, both places again assumed new life, while the hamlets along the canal route and on the railroad likewise became populous labor communities. 

Workmen were recruited from many lands, to such an extent that it was said about half a hundred nationalities and all races of mankind were represented in the working forces. Panama and Colón naturally became a rendezvous for motely throngs, and even if they were not actually the scenes of major operations there was a vast amount of work in progress at their doors. Another vital factor in the upbuilding of the two Isthmus gateways was their perfected sanitation. Not long after the Americans began planning a canal, a chief sanitary officer in the person of Col. William C. Gorgas (later 

General Gorgas) was appointed. With a staff of specialists Colonel Gorgas established a rigid quarantine against ships arriving at Colón and Panama from all infected ports. He proceeded to clean up both cities, as well as the entire length of the canal route that connects them. By virtue of the treaty with the Government of the Republic, and aided by the President, Doctor Guerrero, himself a specialist on tropical diseases, the authorities quickly brought about a changed condition. 

Another important step in connection with improved conditions in the city of Panama was the construction of the Tivoli Hotel. Intended primarily for the use of canal officials and higher-grade employees, but also permitted to operate as a general hotel, the Tivoli became popular with travelers. This modern hotel caused older hostelries of Panama to clean up, paint up, and improve. Another hotel, located in the business district, is the Central, where many foreign travelers find pleasant and comfortable accommodation.

A few years later the United States Government erected a new and permanent hotel at Colón-the Washingtonthe operation of which also had a tendency to improve the service at a number of small hotels in that city. Thus the United States has, among its many operations at the Isthmus, made it possible for the traveler to visit either Colón or Panama and be entertained at fine modern hotels. 

Likewise facilities have been provided for "seeing the canal," the start from either terminal being at the option of the visitor. The latter finds on newsstands or in shops guidebooks and histories of the canal and of the Republic through which it passes.

ONE OF THE CONTROL BOARDS OF THE PANAMA CANAL. 

The control boards are approximately operating miniatures of the locks themselves. 

They have indicating devices which show the exact position of valves, lock gates, chains, and water levels in the various lock chambers, whose movements are synchronous with those of the locks themselves and the operating machinery. 

The walls of the locks are represented by cast-iron plates and the water by marble slabs. In designing the indicators on the board efforts were made to represent the actual machines whose operations were to be indicated. The exact position of every appliance, the water level in the locks, status of the intake gates, location of the vessel--in fact, every detail of the transit of a vessel through the locks-is thus constantly before the eyes of the operators.

CLIMATE 

A visit to the Panama Canal means a sojourn in either Colón or Panama city, as no provision is made along the route of the canal or the railrooad for the entertainment of visitors. Naturally the prospective voyager inquires as to the climate of the two ports as well as that of the Isthmus in general. In the first place, it is well to realize that the whole region is warm or hot and that lightweight clothing is needed. Along the Canal Zone, as well as at the terminal cities, one sees the popularity of clothing of white duck and other lightweight materials, white duck shoes, the white helmet-all so typical of tropical life everywhere. 

From about the beginning of the year to the end of April Panama experiences the dry season. During these months the average temperature in the ports is about 80° F., but the thermometer often rises into the nineties. 

The winter, or rainy season, extends from May to December, the heaviest rains falling in the months of October and November. Rainfall on the Atlantic side of the

Isthmus, averaging 140 inches a year, is more than double that of the Pacific side, a fact attributed largely to the trade winds. The humidity, which often becomes oppressive, averages about 80 per cent, sometimes higher. 

By far the best or most agreeable part of the day on the Isthmus is between 5 and 7 o'clock in the morning, when the temperature ranges from 74° to 77° F. Mid-afternoon. is the hottest part of the day, the temperature usually being well up in the eighties; but as evening comes the cooling sea breezes offer delightful relief.

With the coming of the French canal builders the town, which had been given the name of Aspinwall, assumed new life. PASSING THROUGH THE CANAL 

It took Balboa and his men, as we have noted, 26 days to cross the Isthmus. 

Now, how different! At dawn, at midday, at dusk-indeed, all day long, the airplane is transporting people across the same Isthmus in an hour's time. Here, as elsewhere, days of land travel through the jungle have been reduced to minutes of air travel over the jungle.

And ships of all nations-every day has its quota-are passing from ocean to ocean. Big passenger vessels clear their upper deck and place easy chairs thereon. Then their passengers are invited to these outdoor vantage points. where they gaze upon the results of the "greatest liberty man ever took with nature."

THE SPILLWAY AT GATUN. 

Gatun Lake was formed by the damming of the Chagres River. The spillway, through which the overflow from the lake is allowed to escape, accomplishes the task of utilizing the tremendous power of this escaping flood so that its energy is transformed into electrical power sufficient to furnish the energy for the electric locomotives and for lighting the Canal Zone, as well as for the opening and closing of the tremendous gates of the locks and other appliances.

Midday comes around. Invitingly spread tables, served by stewards clad in immaculate white, are placed upon the upper deck, and luncheon is served in the open air as ship and passengers slowly move onward. The winding route. and the near and distant views, the locks and the lakes, the spectacle of nature harnessed by the skill of man and the beauty of tropical verdure gradually unfold. Could any voyage of 46 miles be more thrilling, more interesting, more inspiring?

Here and there along the canal we are meeting ships. 

Ahead is an oil tanker bearing petroleum from Peru to

England; a vessel from Puget Sound is loaded with building lumber for New York; then comes into view a famous 

British liner en route tot Australia, bearing emigrants on their way from England's crowded cities to the sparsely peopled lands of the Antipodes. In Gaillard Cut a big 

German steamer passes and salutes; a ship with sturdy sailors under the Norwegian flag moves slowly by, bound for the South Seas. Next we meet a minute craft hewn in as ancient a manner as those Balboa saw centuries ago. 

A man and a woman are its sole occupants; probably these two human being, born and bred in the Panama jungle, are the builders of this tiny but useful canoe. 

There is no end to this active life-these busy days along the Panama Canal. Every morning there are ships waiting at each entrance, and every night counts vessels which have passed from ocean to ocean and are again on their courses to ports all over the world. 

Panama with its 60,000 population and Colón with half that number, are strikingly cosmopolitan. One recalls that during canal-construction days at least half a hundred nations were represented in the laboring forces. Work completed, many of these laborers never returned to their nativ lands. Panama became their permanent home; and here their children have been born and reared.

GOING SHOPPING 

The business of going shopping at Panama or Colón presents an interest that seems to be eagerly awaited by every passenger arriving by steamship or by airship. Far and wide have been heralded the fame of the Isthmian shops. 

These are owned or operated by Panamanians, Hindoos, Greeks, Chinese, Japanese, Turks, Americans, Italians, or other people from far corners of the earth. The products they offer the visitor are even more varied than are the keepers of the shops in and about which the tourists gather. 

Every day there is a ship in port and every day the old "jingle bell" coaches and the motor cars are whisking the newcomer from ships and hotels to the so-called bargain counters. Bergains of course, there are to be found, but one must be his own judge. Prices drop astonishingly as experienced shoppers make offers for a given article. 

What do the shops display? The answer might beeverything. Perfumes and soaps from France, silks of many colors made into a hundred articles from Japan, China, India, teakwood figures from the Irrawaddy country, men's silk suits made in Ceylon, furs from Arctic lands, "Panama" hats (which come from Ecuador), feather pictures from Mexico and Colombia, Spanish wines and liquors, pearls found off Panamanian islands, tagua ornaments, jewelry of every description, leather bags, trunks, waterproof coats, steamer rugs. Indeed, these shops have made or imported almost everything that is needed to extract the dollars from those who possess them.

HE PAN AMERICAN UNION is the inter national organization maintained at Washington, D. C., by the 21 American Republics, as follows: 

Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Costa 

Rica, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Guatemala, Haiti, Honduras, Mexico, Nicaragua, Panama, Paraguay, Peru, Salvador, United States, Uruguay, and

Venezuela. It is devoted to the development of commerce, friendly intercourse, good understanding, and the preservation of peace among these countries. It is supported by quotas contributed by each country, based upon their populations. Its affairs are administered by a Director General and Assistant

Director, elected by and responsible to a Governing Board, which is composed of the Secretary of State of the United States and the diplomatic or special representatives in Washington of the other American Governments. These two executive officers are assisted by a staff of international specialists, statisticians, editors, translators, compilers, librarians, stenographers, and clerks. The Union publishes Monthly 

Bulletins in English, Spanish, and Portuguese, which are careful records of Pan American progress. It also publishes numerous special reports and booklets on countries, cities, commodities, etc., of Latin America.

Its library, the Columbus Memorial Library, contains 75,000 volumes, 230,000 index cards, and a large collection of maps.

There is also a collection of photographs, lantern slides, and negatives. The
