DAMNED This is the story of Dessa Dornay, dark, slender, beautiful, whose somber vivid personality attracts the attention of Karl Meister, one of the greatest surgeons of Europe, a man without nerves or compassion whose countless successful operations are due to the fact that he cares nothing about his patients. He smiles at their screams and sufferings. To him they are merely animals on which he can experiment for his own amusement. Whether they live or die is no concern of his. And because so great is his skill, usually they live and he is acclaimed throughout the continent. When the German sterilization laws. were passed they put in his hands a strong club which he was quick to turn to his own uses. He had always been a sadist and now in his love for Dessa Dornay all the conflicting forces of his nature fight for supremacy. Love and brutality, the kinship of passion and pain. At times the book rises to moments of breath-taking beauty.

Again it repels by its sheer horror. But from it all, the character of Dessa Dornay emerges, stronger than any of the strange forces about her, stronger than pain or despair, stronger than the stark passion of wind swept nights. Dessa

Dornay is a character not soon to be forgotten.

CHAPTER DESSA 

ESSA DORNAY was twelve years old when she entered into that period of her life that was to be a prelude to romance and adventure. At least that is how she viewed her leisurely pilgrimage from Switzerland, accompanied by Nok Dornay, her father and Tia, her mother. They had appeared over the crest of the hill, riding in an ancient wagon that might have been the cart of gypsies. It was however no indication of their financial position for since the War, Nok had become quite affluent. Many things had contributed to this pleasant condition chief among which was the fact that he had not been a citizen of any of the warring nations. 

He had been living on neutral territory in 1914 and even though he had been ever restless, he had never once set foot across the frontier during the four years of the War. It was only now when quietude was settling down once more over Germany that he was returning to the forest that he loved. At that moment, the sun which had lain dormant behind a cloud suddenly came to life and flung down a feast of gold upon them. 

Nok Dornay slapped his knee and laughed softly. "That's a good omen, little one," said he. "See, the

ΙΟ 

Black Forest greets you with gold. It is black no longer." 

Dessa threw out her small arms in an attitude of adoration as though she would gather all that beauty to her breast. She could not speak, so overcome she was by emotion. For this was the Schwarzwald where her father had dwelt as a boy. How she had listened, breathless, when he told her of the awesome stalactite cave, with the black underground river, a cave tenanted only by white flies and blind spiders. And of the dark mysterious lake, evidently an extinct volcano crater, of unknown depth, into which a church had once sunk. 

Occasionally when the water was utterly still one could get a glimpse of its spires glistening in the far shadows. 

The father of Nok Dornay had been a musician born in the Southern Tyrol of Austria but of Swiss descent. 

His mother had been a German peasant born in Strasburg. For years the family had lived on a farm near 

Karlsruhe and the strong sturdy mother had gone off into the fields, tilled the earth, tended the farm animals and walked with bare feet in the new-turned soil. Perhaps due to the fact that she so seldom wore shoes her feet were enormous, large enough for her to walk firmly upon the ground, in striking contrast to Joseph Dornay, her husband who was as undependable as a will-o'-thewisp. 

The Dornay homestead was typical of all the other peasant farms throughout the Black Forest. The ground floor was of stone. The upper floor was of wood. 

The roof was deeply thatched. An outside staircase led

II to the upper rooms. It was here that the family lived while the farm animals occupied the lower floor. 

In winter the Dornays always shared the family fire with the animals and many mornings Nok awakened to find a half-dozen chickens roosting on the foot of his bed. During those cold winter nights Nok always slept in a huge German plumeau, a mattress of feathers on top of another mattress. It was wondrously comfortable but when the long, lonesome winter was over and spring came, he longed to sleep outdoors. At heart,

Nok Dornay was a wanderer. Perhaps far back there was an intermingling of gypsy blood somewhere in the Dornay line. But if there was, it was totally unknown to Nok or to Joseph, his father. The floors of all the farm rooms were bare, no rugs nor carpets of any sort; bare and austere but not cold. Rough wooden walls and well-seasoned oak rafters. A tremendous open-fire with a seat always ready for the weary passerby. The house was lonesome but there was about it an air of good cheer. During the long winter evenings, Joan Dornay sat quietly sewing. She made the family clothes and was justly proud of her own handiwork, especially on festive days when they all set out for the village fair, dressed in the colorful costumes of the Schwarzwald peasants. Joseph Dornay's costume consisted of a short jacket and a red waistcoat with silver buttons. Nok was fitted out as a small replica of his father. While 

Joan was arrayed in hoops and petticoats and a long black dress that trailed in the dust as she walked. 

Joan Dornay was a huge woman, tall, strong, mus-

cular but without an ounce of fat upon her. Nothing but muscle and sinew. Her complexion by midsummer was usually so bronzed by the sun it resembled that of an American Indian. Her face was almost expressionless, devoid of any trace of beauty, the typical face of the Schwarzwald peasant. For few women of the Black Forest are good-looking. For the most part they are prematurely old. Their lives are colorless. 

They are little more than farm drudges, breeding machines, automatons without any personality whatsoever, under the complete dominance of their husbands. 

Yet they never suffer from nervousness nor hysteria. 

They are placid, calm. Their minds being sluggish do not hunger for commotion. Their husbands scarcely ever spend an evening with them but go off to a beer garden if there is one nearby. All through the night they drink huge mugs of beer, with lusty companions, and sing folksongs at tremendous pitch.

Joseph Dornay did not care much for this sort of carousing though he was at home even less than his neighbors. Each spring he went from town to town playing in an orchestra at various casinos. Usually 

Nok went with him. For Nok, too, was an accomplished musician and his father spent long hours teaching him all the songs he knew. Nor did he neglect classical music-Mozart, Beethoven, Wagner. With

Nok's natural talent it might have been easy for him to have attained an enviable position as a violinist.

But he cared nothing at all about playing before an audience. He liked best to play when he was alone in

the mountains or in the beer gardens where everyone was so jovial and happy. Nok was naturally lazy and it was an effort for him to study anything. But there was a haunting quality about his music, something not easily forgotten.

Joan Dornay made no protest at the many desertions of her husband and son. Stoically she remained working on the farm. Alone with the farm animals. 

She never complained. She never opposed her husband in anything. It was her creed that a wife should be submissive. A wife was one step below that of her husband. 

It was her duty to obey.

There was something fine about Joan Dornay, something majestic as she walked out barefoot into the lush warm fields, with her roots in the soil as firmly as those of a tree. Perhaps the earth gave her a certain food of comfort even as it nourishes pine trees and oaks. 

With his father, Nok roamed about the Black Forest until he was familiar with every mountain-top and stream. The Höllental which means "The Veil of Hell" and the Himmelreich hard by which is "The Kingdom of Heaven". Kandel Mountain whereon is a bottomless lake, said to be kept in place by a huge stone. Hirschsprung, renowned for the legend of the hart that eluded its pursuers by springing across the gap at this point. Gunsterstal and Schauinsland Mountain, Freiburg Cathedral and the charm of the old Schwabentor or "Peasant's Gate". Trossingen wherein is located the largest harmonica factory in the world. The blue hills

of the Vosges. The rocks of Alt Breisach with the waters of the Rhine tumbling merrily about them. 

And Nok listened to countless folk-tales and legends which seemed to put a glowing veneer on the forest. 

Some his father recounted, some he heard from peasants who stopped for a moment to rest beside the road. Legends of Wolfrianne, of Kuno of Stein. Of 

Griselda, married to the dwarf Poppolinus who after his death became a goblin that haunted the highways. 

Of Berthold the First, a charcoal burner who helped his Emperor to regain his throne by financing his enterprise with the roomful of silver treasure he had found, over a period of years, in the ashes of his charcoal fires. He was rewarded by being Duke of 

Zähringen. Thereafter he became a monster of cruelty, given to every excess. Once he ordered a child to be cooked for his dinner that he might taste of the tender flesh but later when the roast was set before him, he was revolted at the sight. He refused to eat and was repentant for the rest of his days. Of the Feast of

St. Hubert each year when the ghost hunt haunts the neighborhood of Waldkirch. If one listens intently one can hear the hunting-horn, the baying of wild hounds and horses galloping over roof-tops. Peasants always bar their doors and windows on St. Hubert's night to keep out evil spirits.

Endless were the legends Nok Dornay heard and he devoured them eagerly. They became infused into his blood. And he grew to love the Black Forest with something akin to worship. That is why years later he

sold his farm in Switzerland and journeyed leisurely with his family into the heart of the magic forest that was to be his new home. He was fulfilling a promise which he had made to Dessa when she was a baby not much higher than his knee.

DESSA and Nok were vagabonds in a way, for many nights they had slept in the open underneath the stars. And while the fat and complaining Tia had dozed beside the campfire, Nok had taken out his violin and played such beautiful haunting music, Dessa was thrilled to her very being. At such times it almost seemed as though the Creator washed the face of the earth and all things stood out bright and clear and glowing. Then beside the campfire Dessa would dance, a slim lovely figure, a wild girl to stir the hearts of poets. In every motion there was rhythm, harmony, perfume. The sweet haunting strains of Nok's violin mingled with the music of the trees, even with the music of the stars. Like startled eyes they gazed down on the glory of Dessa dancing. No figure of earth could be quite as lovely as this. But then, strictly speaking, Dessa was not a creature of earth. She was a dryad, she could see figures moving about in the air that were invisible to ordinary folks. She could hear voices. in the wind. Sometimes they made her smile. Occasionally such a surge of emotion overcame her that she burst into laughter. There was something super- 16

natural about Dessa, something that made one breathless. A fiery, graceful, fragile child. No wonder Nok worshipped her, Nok who was master of magic music but who stumbled over words as he read and could scarcely write at all. Perhaps that is why he gave so much of his time to dreaming. Perhaps to some extent the world suffers from decadence because men have forgotten how to dream. It is one of the fine arts, as important as painting or literature. 

Nok Dornay liked to sit basking in the sun, smoking an old briar pipe that was so seasoned and blackened by time and constant use, it was almost as strong and potent as smoking opium. That pipe was his most prized possession, a beloved companion. For it was in the smoke of it that all his most colorful dreams had been painted. To him, Dessa was like one of the dream ladies that he imagined in the smoke. There were times when he was breathless as he gazed upon the beauty of her, especially when she bathed nude in a brook in complete innocence. Her body was like porcelain, with warm flesh tints. Her breasts were just emerging into slight prominence. Her legs were slender, strong and beautifully formed. Her hair was black with blue tints as the light played upon it. She always wore it straight back, parted in the middle and flowing like a cloud of deepest night about her shoulders. 

Her jet black eyes glowed like stars. They danced with hidden flames. Her cheeks were blemishless and her lips were like crushed cherries. So beautiful she was even flowers trembled at her approach and swayed

toward her. They loved her for she was almost like one of themselves. Sometimes she stooped and drew a slim blossom to her lips. And they whispered their devotion in increased perfume, for perfume is the voice of flowers even as light is the voice of the stars. 

But Dessa never plucked a flower. She would not have given pain to a single bud for all the wealth of Midas.

Tia was not a dreamer though she slept much. She had little patience with Dessa's love of legends and fairy tales. All she cared about was eating and gossiping with other women who might stop for a moment to converse with her. Tia was out of tune with Dessa. 

They walked to different music. For there is a certain harmony in life and those of us who are sufficiently attuned to unscramble the discord, to distinguish the real music of it, are the only ones who ever gain some measure of happiness. Tia was not unkind to Dessa, perhaps because she had too little opportunity to be so. Dessa was an elusive figure and as soon as she had finished eating she was off once more into the hills and forests that she loved. Even when the cart had come over the hilltop on that memorable journey,

Dessa had spent most of the time rambling in and out of the woodland along the path. Once she stopped because a gorgeous butterfly had alighted upon her shoulder. How beautifully its black and yellow wings glowed in the sun. Somewhere she had read that flowers are only butterflies resting for a moment on a branch.

Thus this butterfly was merely a flower off in quest

of romance. Nor did she move until the butterfly had taken wing again and disappeared among the trees. 

Dessa loved to read, especially fairy tales and books of poems. Once Nok had made her a present of a ponderous book containing odd tales from all nations and for hours she read the stories over until she knew them almost by heart. All the characters in the stories were very real people to her. She would not have been surprised to have met any one of them in the fields or beside a stream. Nor did she consider the stories absurd or fantastic. They were no more extraordinary than things that happened frequently to her. Dessa had never gone to school yet she could read perfectly. 

And Nok listened to her and marveled.

Although Dessa did not know it, she was not Nok's own child. Nor was Tia her mother. One day when

Dessa was a tiny child, he had come upon her when a man was beating her unmercifully. That had been one of the few times in his life when Nok had become blind with anger. His usual lethargy slipped from him and he sprang at the man as though he had been a tiger. The child, released, lay whimpering beside the road. Nok hated work and seldom did he carefully plan any of the enterprises upon which he embarked. But this time he worked from good plans. Before he ceased his pummeling his opponent was almost a pulp. Then he commenced questioning the man. At first he was surly and refused to talk but a few jabs in the face carefully delivered made him positively eloquent. To

Nok's surprise, the man offered to sell him the child

for a gold piece. Fortunately Nok was not without funds and at once he purchased the little one. He was very happy as he picked her up and felt her tiny cheek against his as she nestled in his arms. Now Nok had always longed for a daughter of his own, and this was his daughter. They were so much alike in temperament. 

Dark, brooding, dreamers, lovers of poetry and music. 

Lovers of the dark, of the sky and the night wind.

From that day forth Dessa had been brought up as his own child. Never for a moment did she know that she was really his slave, that he had bought her for a bit of gold. And despite the fact that a bridge of years separated them, Dessa had crossed it successfully and danced her way into his heart. Although nobody was aware of it, Nok was in love with Dessa and in the smoke of his briar pipe he dreamed, dreams of such tender loveliness that they would have amazed her had she known. But Dessa must never know. He wanted her to be happy. He wanted her to always believe that he was her father. 

That is why when he gazed at her fragile body when she bathed in the rippling stream, he sighed in misery and longed for his youth again. His whole life had gone awry. Tia, his wife, was no wife for him. She was not in sympathy with him. He had married her when he was drunk but he had always been faithful to her in his fashion. Her fat ungainly body was repulsive to him. Fortunately Tia was inclined to be frigid and her calls upon her husband were very infrequent. A good meal was far more exciting to her.

I 12 Her greatest emotions were those connected with taste and the stomach. Nok bought her rich foods whenever he was able. He was a good husband.

It was during those moments when Nok played for Dessa to dance beside the campfire that he was happiest. Through his music he wooed her. There was no need to be careful that he did not betray himself through such a medium. No wonder Dessa danced with such wild abandon. She was like a being possessed. Even Tia stirred from her sleep long enough to watch. She marveled at the glory of the picture and vaguely it troubled her. For Tia was immensely superstitious. She believed in fortune-tellers, in omens, in talismans, in spiritualists, in witches and a host of other absurdities that have plagued the world for ages. She had a vague fear that Dessa might be a child possessed. Dessa talked with spirits. She hoped they were not evil ones. Did she not occasionally laugh when there was nothing apparent to laugh about? 

And now she was dancing. Never had Tia beheld a sight so beautiful and yet that made her afraid at the same time. Tia did not know that mankind the world over always fears what it cannot understand.

In Dessa, dancing, there was so much of beauty, so much that was divine, it was beyond the scope of her limited comprehension. Thus she marveled and was afraid. 

But not so Nok. Music came from his fiddle that would have made the stars envious. And the moon glowed in supreme brilliance as it lay in rapture on the

warm blue breast of the sky. It was a night of rapture, a night of ecstasy and love. Dessa dancing by the campfire and into the heart of poor Nok who loved her. Now she was softly weeping, she did not know why. There was so much of beauty, so much of heaven in the perfumed hush of the night. Her slender body swayed to every rhythm. In the woodland every sound was hushed. All nature had stopped to watch. It held its breath for Dessa was dancing. 

At last she stopped abruptly and flung herself into 

Nok's arms. He put aside his violin as she clung to him and his great hands caressed her fragile form as tenderly as though they had been the hands of a woman. Tia's drowsy eyes had closed and now once more she was sleeping. The campfire died down until only a heap of glowing ashes remained. A firefly flitted in and out among the trees. In a distant field a cow mooed contentedly. The wind sighed sleepily through the tree tops. The flowers swayed toward each other. 

Calm was the night, peaceful, glorious. The horrors of war seemed very far away. It was a night of tenderness. The wind blew softly over the country and stopped for a moment to kiss the soft cheek of the little dancer. Now she was no longer weeping. She had dried her eyes. She was very tired. And her father's arms were about her. Oh, how comforting they were. "Sleep, little one," he whispered. 

Dessa sighed but she did not reply. Her slender body trembled as sleep descended upon her.

FOR years the old dark house had stood untenanted far back from the roadside as though it felt despondent over the continued neglect and wished to get as far away from the stream of humanity as possible. For houses have life. They absorb the personalities of the people who dwell within them. Nobody would like to live in a dead house or a house that constantly wept. And in the past this old gray house that was now crumbling into ruins, this house that was badly in need of paint and with the chimney that was all bent over with age, had been a cheerful place. It had been the home of Fritz Berger, a brewmaster, who in the cities nearby had had several factories. He was fat and bland and smiling. He radiated good cheer and brewed good beer. Thus doubly did he bless his neighbors. Almost every week large parties were given at the Berger home. And even though there were no children dwelling in the house, the halls echoed with laughter, the laughter of visiting children who came to partake of the delicious spice cakes and sugar buns which the beloved Hedwig Berger prepared for them. Though the house had many servants, 23

Hedwig herself prepared all the cakes for the children. 

Once when Fritz twitted her about her idiosyncrasies, she only smiled. "One would imagine," said he, "that you had some special recipe for those cakes, some secret ingredient that you put into them.' 99 "I have," she said softly. "And what is that?” he wanted to know. "Love," she said wistfully.

He came over and placed his great arm about her shoulder. Although he was not a demonstrative man and hated the parading of sentiment, for once the bars were down. "And longing, Mama," he said. "I'd give all the money and estates I possess for the rich blessing of poverty and children."

Hedwig pushed him aside and pretended to be annoyed. "Drat take the man," she scolded. "I'll never be finished with my work." 

But she was not ill-pleased and she blew her nose loudly to cover up the fact that her eyes were full of tears. Hedwig had never known want or hunger, except the greatest want and hunger of all. The want of little baby hands to touch her cheeks, the hunger for a child of her own. "There be things beyond the ken of ordinary folk," that was her favorite saying. 

Why Nature had seen fit to bestow upon her all the charm and affection that a mother could have, and then deny her children was a problem unsolvable. "Never mind," Fritz continued after a moment. "Better times are coming."

But he was wrong. Better times were not coming. 

A month later Hedwig died suddenly in her sleep. 

Her heart for years had been weak. Everything was fatiguing. Then it grew so weary it could not go on. 

So great was the blow to Fritz, it staggered his world. 

It came tumbling down about his ears, a heap of ruins. 

He was dazed, crushed, but he did not cry. He closed the great house and went away. In travel he tried to forget. But his quest was fruitless. Every time he saw a laughing child, he thought of Hedwig who had always been childless.

Then the war had come. A means of escape. Although Fritz was growing old, he enlisted. Into the war he plunged in search of sanctuary. For in the war there would be no children to trouble his mind. 

Weary, dreary, blood-drenched years. Then the Armistice. But Fritz did not come out of the war. No one ever knew what happened to him. Perhaps in the shelltorn fields he found sanctuary. Perhaps in Flanders' fields he found sleep at last, sleep without dreams.

Sleep from which there was no bitter awakening. 

Meanwhile the house grew lonesome. Far back from the edge of the road by the fringe of the forest it waited. It longed for the laughter of children, the children who had come to the parties and crammed themselves full of spice cakes, the children who had slid down the banisters and whose laughter had echoed through the rooms, sweetest of all music. The old dark house was lonesome. It was grieving. And time wore on and the path to the door grew choked with

weeds. The paint dried and came off like scaly scabs. 

The chimney sagged perilously. The hinges on the gate grew rusty.

Then over the crest of the hill had come the dilapidated cart in which rode Nok and Tia and occasionally Dessa when she grew weary of walking along the hard dirt road. To the surprise of the farmers who dwelt in the neighborhood, the cart stopped before the door of the deserted Berger homestead. The chimney seemed to straighten up. The house shook itself as though roused from sleep and seemed to smile again.

Nok was the new owner of the house. He had had most of his money in gold and when the mark dropped in value and panic seized the nation, panic at least insofar as it affected material things, he had bought the old dark house with the broad fields at a ridiculous figure. For years the house had been for sale and no one had ever even looked at it. The bank who managed the estate were glad to be rid of such an eternal eye-sore. For the first time in his life, Nok had become a member of the landed gentry, for now he was master of a house vastly different from the various farm huts he had owned so briefly. One can forgive him somewhat for walking up and down and swaggering a little as he said, "We're home."

He might have added that he had bought the house especially for Dessa. No place in the world did he consider as beautiful as the Schwarzwald wherein he had played as a child. Dessa was a jewel, a white ivory

lady, with skin that glowed more glorious than gold.

He was glad that she never objected when he watched her bathe in the sunlight. As he viewed her nude body he felt as though he were very close to heaven. She was his little golden idol. He worshipped her. But never by thought or action did he make known to her his hopeless infatuation. Had he done so, he reasoned, he might have broken her heart. 

Now as Dessa gazed at the house, she cried, "It's wonderful. How I'd love to explore those rooms, to meet the people who live there." "Why nobody lives there," explained Nok. "The house is empty." "No house is ever empty," she said thoughtfully, "after it has once been lived in.” 

Dessa was old beyond her years in some respects, yet in others she was amazingly childlike. That year she was twelve years old, that amazing year when she emerged from obscurity. "We have never lived until now," she cried rapturously. 

But Tia sighed. She got ponderously down from the cart and showed no enthusiasm. "Who's going to clean all those windows?" she asked petulantly. "What matter?" said Dessa. "We don't have to look through windows to see the sun."

From the very start Dessa loved that house, perhaps because the house held out its arms to her in welcome. It drew her to its breast. Once more it had

come to life. Occasionally the halls echoed with Dessa's laughter. Her room was in a far wing of the house, a great room with four huge windows. No matter how cold it was, Dessa always slept with the windows open and in the thinnest of nightgowns. Sometimes she lay nude in the moonlight and the cold slim fingers of the moon caressed her body. And Nok would sit beside her playing on his violin. A sight to dream of, not to tell, the slender, fragrant body of the girl, glazed by the silver glory of the moon-mist. And as

Nok played her eyes were half-closed and the darkness of the room seemed to fade into blue-distances. 

Somehow she felt as though she were in a long blue room, lit by star-dust. Yet it was not a room either, it was the blue fields of the sky. She was walking among the flowers. And so sleep crept over her. Nor did she awaken when Nok put aside his violin. 

Tremulously he stood gazing down upon her. Then abruptly he stooped and kissed her shell-like breast.

THE old house still remained exactly as Fritz Berger had left it. The furniture, the books, the draperies were untouched. So, too, most of the charm remained. As they entered the front door a thick layer of dust had carpeted everything but even so on the air there floated a certain subtle fragrance, like old lavender. Only Dessa noticed it but then Dessa's faculties were far more acute than those of anyone about her. Sometimes when she stood on a hilltop she could see the little riders of the wind, dashing madly off on their elusive white chargers to whence no man may know. Occasionally she tried to point out the wraithlike figures to those about her, but only to be met by derisive laughter. People thought Dessa was odd, queer. It is always easy to dub a person a lunatic when one cannot understand him.

As Dessa went through the house, she walked on tip-toe as though she were in a sacred place. She could sense the great love that had dwelt there. It seemed to envelop everyone in a cloak of comfort. Even Tia complained less than Nok had expected. At night when she dozed in a great chair before the open fire in the 29

library, a certain peace seemed to creep over her. On the hook above the open fire, Nok always hung a great tea-kettle filled with water. "Listen, little one," he murmured. "Do you not hear? The kettle is singing. It is singing songs of contentment for a little house." "It is so beautiful," said she softly, “it makes me want to cry. Father, why does one cry when one is happy?" 

Nok gazed into the fire for several moments before he spoke, then he said, "I guess it is because laughter and tears are brothers. They are very close together. 

It is oftimes hard to distinguish one from the other, for they are twins."

Nok spoke well. He made few blunders in word usage. Just as he played by ear, he talked by ear. He had traveled much with his father and always he remembered the beautiful words and phrases he had heard. They were like music to him. His lack of education was not the fault of his parents. They were willing for him to go to school and his mother had pleaded with him to study. But Nok was indolent, shiftless. Yet he was a natural born farmer and when he did walk out into the fields with his mother she was very happy for he accomplished a maximum of result with a minimum of effort. Those days were rare moments of happiness for his mother. In Nok there were two warring elements. From his mother he inherited a love for the soil. He was in sympathy with it and it responded readily to his efforts. From his

father he inherited the love of the open road. Wanderlust. Therefore he could not content himself for any considerable length of time in the fields. 

After the death of his parents, Nok decided that he would give all his time to farming. He worked long hours and the results were splendid. But Nok was lonely on the farm. Especially did he miss his father who had been a beloved companion on so many journeys through the Schwarzwald, from Basle and Constance on the south and on up through the romantic region of the Höhgau to Donaueschingen to Baden- Baden on the far north fringe of the Forest where the pines recede and the road sweeps out into an open plain. And now once more the call of the wild was disturbing his blood. He could not sleep. At last in despair, he sold the farm and became a wanderer. 

But even then he was not contented. A man is not happy who is a homeless wayfarer. So once more he bought a run-down farm near Freiburg. He worked hard and thought of nothing but the earth until his first crops were harvested. He had painted his house, cleared up the litter about it, mended the thatched roof. Then when everything was in order and he had a moment to rest, he could find no rest. So once more he sold his farm and took to the road. Thereafter his life was repetition. He bought farms and sold them. 

He camped in the woods, played music to the night, but always a feeling of unrest remained. At last in despair he went down into Switzerland. He roamed the Swiss Alps from Zugspitze to Mont Blanc. Finally

he bought an Alpine farm. Thereafter for many years he remained in Switzerland. In one respect he had ever been fortunate. Whenever he purchased a farm, he made money. For he improved it greatly and it was always easy for him to dispose of it at a profit. And somewhere along the path of his restless journeys he had acquired a wife. Tia Dornay was the last woman on earth Nok ever should have married. She was a 

German peasant, stolid, plain and always half asleep.

Her people had been farmers who eked out their living by carving toys, wooden horses and dogs, squat men with little dumpling faces. Day after day Tia had sat painting the toys without ever smiling. Imagine sending a regiment of wooden soldiers off to war without even a cheery nod. There was glamour in her work. 

Her people were in a way partners of Santa Claus, manufacturing droll toys to make children happy. But these things had no effect on the soul of Tia. She did not like children. They were nuisances. Nor had she any use for imagery. Yet incongruously she had married a dreamer. Thus the life of Nok became in some measure a tragedy. His mind was tortured, in prison. 

He was tied down to a mountain of a woman, coarse and gross and totally lacking in appeal. Only through his violin could he find escape. And sometimes the music was almost a wail. It sounded like the cry of a wounded animal struggling toward freedom.

As Dessa moved about the house, she felt as though ghosts still dwelt there, not horrifying ghosts with

cold dank bony fingers such as haunt the mountains and the turrets of old castles. But ghosts that were sad and wistful, that made one think of romance and soft colors. Once in the night she imagined that she heard footsteps, the sound of someone walking slowly up the wooden-stairs of the attic. She knew that it was not Tia because her mother walked very heavily, slowly and ponderously, pausing every few steps for breath. Nor was it Nok because he walked quickly, with a certain rhythm. She would have known his footsteps anywhere. Except for her parents she was alone in the house.

Cautiously she rose from her bed and slipped on a kimono. She was not afraid. Dessa was not in fear of anything. But she was interested. Carefully, so that she would make no sound, she walked to the doorway. 

The door stood open and there before her was the stairs. Although there were no lights burning in the house, the moon was at the full. Never had it appeared more brilliant. It illuminated everything in a restful light. And Dessa beheld the figure of a woman just vanishing into the shadows of the attic. Without a moment's hesitation, Dessa mounted the stairs. But when she reached the attic there was no one there. Yet on the air there floated more distinctly than ever that sweet elusive fragrance of lavender. Dessa knew that it was not imagination. She believed in spirits thoroughly. Many times she had beheld them. They were constantly in the air about her. Sometimes she heard their voices. She had listened to their songs. They in-

terested her but she was not afraid. To her their existence was a natural thing. She had always heard voices in the wind. There was nothing startling about it. 

Although Dessa seldom went to church she believed in God and heaven. She believed God was in the forest, in the fields and in the mountains. The voice of God is silence and that is why peace enters a man's soul so frequently when he walks through the slumbering trails of a forest. Dessa believed that heaven, too, was right here on earth. When a man dies his spirit is set free to wander in vast distances of blue. No longer is he tied down to a frail body. If he wishes he can walk about the sky. But as a rule the spirits remain on earth, near the homes they dwelt in. To guard and watch over those they love. There is no death. There is only transition. A sort of lifting up from an earthly plane to a celestial or spiritual plane. Some day man will learn to understand that thing which men for want of a better word call death. They will grow to understand that it is not death but birth, birth into spirituality. Such was the religious beliefs of Dessa. 

She was secure in the belief that life is eternal. And all the sorrows of life are brief interludes. Clouds obscure the sun. But by tomorrow the darkest day will have dissolved into shadowy distances. 

Now Dessa knew that she had seen the figure of a woman mounting the stairs and even though she was unable to find her she commenced searching about for some evidence of her visit. The fact that she had come up the stairs but a few minutes after the woman

meant nothing. For in the world of spirits there is no such thing as time. Time is only a relative illusion. In the spiritual world it doesn't exist.

Now she stopped before an open trunk. It stood in the direct path of the moonlight. On the tray several dresses had been laid out, old-fashioned dresses but silk-soft in texture, all laces and ruffles. They were clean as though they had only recently been laundered. 

From them had evidently come the fragrance which 

Dessa had noticed. 

Uttering a little cry of ecstasy, Dessa threw off her kimono and tried one of the dresses on. It fitted her perfectly. And as she fastened the last hook, bits of lace and dress material fell to the floor as though the dress had been altered especially for her. That was why that lovely lady had gone up the stairs into the attic. She wished to be seen, to direct Dessa's attention to the trunk. 

Never had she been so happy. Never had she worn clothes as elegant as these. What matter that they were old-fashioned, that the sleeves puffed out and the waist was very high? It only made her appear more alluring. 

She walked over to the window. The night was so clear she almost felt as though she could pluck a star from the velvet blue sky. The fronds of the trees loomed up clear-cut as though stenciled against the brightness. Then Dessa leaned forward, scarcely breathing, for in front of the house far down at the road was a carriage attached to which were two black horses. It was an open carriage. In it a man sat wait-

ing. She tried to make out the features of his face but it was impossible for her to do so. The whole equipage was very shadowy and occasionally when the wind blew strong it seemed to dissolve into the air but always it reassembled again.

Presently the figure of a woman emerged from the house. It was the same woman whom Dessa had seen on the attic stairs. Rapidly she walked down the path toward the gate and as she did so, she looked back. 

Dessa thought that she had never seen a woman more beautiful. If only Tia looked like that. And then the woman noticed Dessa in the window. She smiled and waved her hand.

When she reached the carriage, the man helped her in. Once more she turned toward the window and threw a kiss. Then slowly the black horses set off down the road. Even as they did so the wind came again and carriage, horses, everything dissolved and floated away into the shadows of the Schwarzwald. 

Only the fragrance of lavender remained.

For a long time Dessa still stood by the window. 

She was stirred by exquisite emotions. Never before had she been so happy. For she knew that although the lovely lady was gone she would return again. And always while she lived in that beloved house there would be new scenes, new dreams, new songs.

If disease were banished from the earth, gone would be the greatest source of interest for many people.

Tia did not particularly care for Dessa but she never told her that she was not her real mother. Nok had warned her against that. He had instilled considerable fear in her for she knew that whenever Nok 37

Its dreadful futility. And there he played music so wild, so passionate, so desperate even the trees trembled. It was not music but anguish, the cry of a soul, comparable only to the sob of a wind gone mad. Nor did he return home until he was utterly exhausted. Then he would sit smoking in the chimney corner until naught but ashes remained in the grate, cold, dead ashes like unto the ashes in his heart. In that section of the Schwarzwald, the nights were inclined to be chilly. That is why the fire on the hearth was kindled so frequently. Besides there was a friendliness in the open fire. Though its charm was nothing as compared to that of a gypsy camp-fire, under the spell of the moon, with the odor of balsam and pine in the air and Dessa dancing. Like a reed in the wind she danced, like a reed that swings to every wind but breaks not in the tempest. Sometimes she danced almost nude, and the sway of her body was more seductive than the vapor of poppies.

It wove a spell about Nok. It mingled with the music of his violin. And the two fused and became one, the rhythm of her dancing and the harmony that flowed so smoothly from his violin. He wooed her with his music and she danced with greater feeling. She was conscious of some great force surging about her but she had no knowledge of what that elusive power really was. Yet always when he had finished playing she went and kissed him. To Nok the feel of her soft warm lips upon his was more intoxicating than old wine. 

These two understood each other. They were both in tune with the heavens. They could appreciate beauty. They gloried in grandeur.

But Tia was a being outside. Though they were willing to admit her to that divine circle it was useless. 

For usually when she sat with them by a camp-fire she dozed and her sleep was pleasurable for she dreamed of food.

She was never disobedient but then perhaps that was because Tia never asked her to do anything. Dessa helped occasionally with the housework but more often she was in the mountains, rambling about among the rocks and streams that she loved. Every bird and tiny animal of the woodland was her friend. They did not fly away at her approach.

They were not afraid. For Dessa, they knew, was a dryad. She belonged as much in the woodland as they

did. She was a child of the wild, a child of nature. 

She walked in beauty and every place was lovely when she had passed. 

Only once had Tia ever lifted her hand to strike her. Then almost immediately she was sorry. As soon as her hand had struck the child brutally across the mouth, tears came to her own eyes. "Don't cry, mother," she pleaded. "I hate to see you sad." And then she kissed Tia on the cheek, and because her lips were bleeding from the blow, she left blood marks on Tia's cheek. It made Tia shudder. For the first time in her life, she was moved by great emotion. She had struck Dessa in anger and the little one had kissed her in love. 

That night she made a special fruit cake with white icing for supper. It was her manner of attempting to atone for her momentary viciousness. She was aware that Dessa particularly liked that kind of cake. 

So the years rolled on and Dessa grew in grace and loveliness. Her form filled out somewhat. Her gentle breasts developed into small hills of ivory whiteness, just big enough to fill a cupped hand. They were soft pillows against which any man might joy to rest, and resting forget his allegiance to family, life or God.

Men did not appeal to her. No man except Nok because he played so splendidly. They talked a different language from ordinary people, perhaps because they were both slightly mad. 

She was still fascinated by the old dark house. Often at night she lay upon her bed listening. She believed that in spirit Hedwig and Fritz Berger had returned to dwell in the house wherein they had passed so many happy hours together. Love never dies, it only fades until one must have keen vision to comprehend that it still exists. And oftimes she heard footsteps on the stairs, hushed voices whispering. In the little village of Enz which was not quite a mile away, Dessa was a constant subject for gossip. 

Legends sprang up about her. It was said that she was fey, there was much that was unearthly about her. No

When she was in town she did not stop to talk much with anyone except old Tobias Conrad who kept the Gasthof. Although his hostelry was small, the food was excellent and his rates were reasonable. Thus he seldom wanted for customers. But Tobias was an idler who liked to sit under a tree outside his door and converse with his neighbors. It was his wife, Elsa, who was the real general of the Gasthof. It was she who ran the place, tended to the meals, saw that everything was spic and span, and occasionally caused poor Tobias to sigh.

She attempted to run Tobias with the same competency that she ran the hotel. But it was so much wasted energy. All that she succeeded in doing was to run him out of the house. Only under the tree was he safe from her nagging tongue. How he longed for a little less spic and a little less span in his life. He'd have welcomed a speck of dust on something. It would have made things more homelike. Tobias was far from happy. Yet ironically enough Elsa made the Gasthof homelike for all the guests. Only poor Tobias was the orphan of her storms and endless eruptions. 

Tobias loved to talk with Dessa. To him she was like a being from another world. Her voice was gentle.

She never raved or shouted. When she spoke it was like the tinkling of a mountain brook. Although Tobias was not musical, she brought music into his life.

Many people of Enz frowned on Dessa. For one thing she was certainly not German. She was dark. 

Her eyes were like the night, before the moon has risen. Although she spoke German fluently she did so with a slightly foreign accent. Languages came naturally to Dessa. She had a retentive memory. Almost everything she heard or read, she remembered. Once 

Elsa had been sick and Tobias had been dreadfully worried. Although he hated having her about, he could not have lived if she had left him. Tobias resembled a barnacle. He was helpless, lost unless he had something to cling to. So now he was troubled when Dessa came to him. When he acquainted her with the cause of his gloom, she said impulsively, "Take me to her."

He did so gladly. He always liked to have other people do his thinking for him. Within his Gasthof, in her own room which was cluttered up with enough furniture to fit out several hotels, Elsa lay moaning in a feather-bed so soft and huge that Dessa had to follow the sound of her voice to find out where she really was. Now she walked over and put her cool soft gentle hands on Elsa's burning forehead. She commenced to sing softly. It was a gay little song which the immortal Goethe had once written. "Know'st thou the land where the lemon trees bloom, Where the gold orange glows in the deep thicket's gloom, Where a wind ever soft from the blue heaven blows, And the groves are of laurel and myrtle and rose."

Elsa had been sleeping but at sound of the lovely voice she stirred in her sleep. Dessa bent over her, softly smoothing her forehead. And it seemed as though there was some mighty mystic power in the girl's fingers for gradually the fever abated and Elsa opened her eyes and smiled. It is a known fact that certain people of earth have a healing power in their finger-tips. For some time Dessa sat beside the bed and continued to smooth her brow and it was as though as she did so she waved all Elsa's cares away. The sickness vanished. Before night, the good housewife was up and around again. From that day forth she continually sang Dessa's praises. But she sang them in too loud a note. She declared that Dessa had performed a miracle. It wasn't a miracle but a natural phenomenon. But the townsfolk listened and believed and the story was repeated over and over again. Many added to it. Until at last so much of legend had grown up about her that many of the weaker-minded avoided meeting her. A woman with such tremendous power might just as easily cast an evil spell over one as a good.

Thus in Enz many people believed Dessa was bewitched. Others, among which were Tobias, considered her bewitchingly beautiful. But all the children of the town adored her. The apple tree in front of Tobias's door was a constant meeting place when Dessa was in town. There all the children gathered round her nor would they leave until she had told them a story. 

Sometimes she smiled to herself and reflected that

It was called, "The Little Cat's Feet", the story of a cat who walked so far that his feet were very tired. He was looking for a nice kind home because he was sort of lost, you know. Then he met a cobbler who made him an elegant pair of shoes.

But best of all, the children liked, "The Moon of the Servant's Cat" and very drolly did Dessa read it to them. It was translated from the Chinese but still much of the original simplicity and philosophy had been preserved. "There was once a mighty Mandarin, Shun Feng, who owned a cat that was worth more gold than an elephant could carry. Among its seventeen names was 

Wan Sway Yeh, which means, 'Master of Ten Thousand Years.' It was a sacred cat insofar as its ancestry could be traced back to a cat that had been owned by the mighty Confucius. Now in all this world of mystic trails and mountains there are few things harder to find than a pedigreed cat whose lineage is authentic. 

Therefore who shall marvel that before it men bowed down in wonder? In perfumes was it bathed and ten men constantly watched over it. It had its own private physician. Never was it permitted to go out of doors for fear it might stray away or catch cold. Its days were spent reclining on a silken cushion in a monstrous golden room. When it walked across the

stone floor ten men who were the watchers marched after it and the physician marched after the ten men. 

It was a very bothersome existence and the sacred cat was bored with life. Each day was a replica of every other day. Each meal was a replica of every other meal. It was truly miserable. The weight of that golden room seemed to bear down so heavily that the cat was almost crushed. "Now it happened that in the household of the Mandarin there was a servant who was famed for his cherry tarts. His name was Tze Tze which you do not pronounce but sneeze. Now Tze Tze also was possessed of a cat but no one could trace its lineage even back six months. It did not even have a name. 

So that no one in the palaces should be bothered by the cat it was kept in the garden. After the sacred cat had eaten its meals Tze Tze carried what was left out to the common cat that lived in the garden. The meals were thoroughly enjoyed. After he supped in splendor the servant's cat would stretch himself at ease beneath a willow tree and doze until the moon rose into the sky. Then he would wake up, climb onto the high wall that surrounded the garden and sing love songs to the moon. He was in love with the moon and the moon was in love with him. For the moon, you must know, is really a great white cat that clambers about the sky in quest of star-mice which frolic there in vast abundance. And the cat of the Mandarin with his seventeen names, his ten watchers and one physician, in his golden chamber was very unhappy. He had

ESSA DORNAY never had a real close friend. Nobody understood her. They ridiculed her when she told about the voices she heard in the wind or else they looked at her in awe. Only Nok did not smile when she told him that day she had heard a pine tree singing. 

Nok mused, "And why shouldn't a pine tree sing? 

Why shouldn't it be joyful, standing on a hillside with its feet buried in the lush warm soil and its branches flung out toward the sky?"

Sometimes Dessa walked off alone in the mountains, especially in the springtime. And there she would throw herself flat against the soil. With every nerve alert she listened. She could hear the vines and flowers murmuring in the soil. She could hear the anthem of spring. Long before the first buds had sprouted, spring was in her heart. It had come from the soil. Spring always moved her strangely for it is in spring that the world is born again. New hopes, new songs.

As the years rolled on and she grew to realize that people everywhere imagined that she was queer, she 48

drew into herself. Only to Nok did she speak without restraint for Nok believed in her implicitly. She was his religion. He worshipped her. His life became beautiful merely because he walked in her footsteps. To him she cast off a lovely light, almost intangible. No one else beheld it. Only he was conscious of the glow. 

Sometimes as he sat beside her on a hilltop, smoking his old briar pipe, he would grow philosophical. "Man is always afraid of the new," he reflected. 

Dessa sighed. "Yes," she said. "It is too bad but it is true. Why should anyone laugh because I said I went into the forest and found a flower that was in tears? Is anguish a cause for rejoicing?" "Too bad," said Nok, "that so few of us understand flowers. Blessed indeed is he who is able to go off into the hills and commune with nature."

Dessa did not care a great deal about clothes but she had the knack of appearing well in the simplest of costumes. Frequently she tried on the dresses which she had found in the trunk in the attic. But she never wore them out. She knew that Hedwig still dwelt in the house. It made her very happy. Even though it was only in spirit, nevertheless she was there. And 

Dessa lived on the borderline of fantasy. Occasionally she was able to peer into the spiritual world. It was not odd therefore for her to have spirit friends. 

Perhaps that is why she so loved the mountain trails. Flowers and trees are more nearly spiritual than any other living things. Animals and men are grosser by comparison. Even in their wooing flowers are more

ideal.

Perfume voices, music in the wind, magic of the stars. 

Dessa had discovered a spot in the forest which she looked upon as her very own. A tiny waterfall crashed over smooth stones. The clear cool stream came laughing down from the mountain. Completely naked Dessa loved to stand by the waterfall, the sharp splash of the water striking her flesh like needles. The sun broke through the trees and bathed her body. It made of her a slim golden statue with pink tipped breasts. 

When she had finished bathing, she stretched out upon a long flat rock and permitted the sun and the wind to dry her skin. The sun stroked her body with warm fingers. The wind kissed her lips. Once a rose fell upon her breast, even though there were no flowers growing nearby. The wind itself was in love with her and that was the wind's gift. 

One day as she stood nude by the spray of the waterfall she became aware of the fact that she was not alone. She felt certain that someone was gazing at her. She knew it wasn't Nok for she was used to his presence. Often he sat and played softly to her as she bathed. This was different. She felt the scrutiny of dangerous eyes.

Startled, she gazed furtively about and at last she beheld a man leaning against a tree and gazing at her. 

He was smiling in a manner that he tried to make pleasant but there was a certain savage cruelness about his lips.

"The loveliest sight I have ever beheld,” he said rapturously. 

Dessa had never minded being seen nude but this was different. This man looked at her in a manner that made her tremble. "Please go away," she whispered. "Impossible," said the man. The very sound of his voice was harsh and unpleasant. It spoilt the harmony of the forest. It disturbed the rhythm of the trees. "I couldn't, even if I wanted to. So marvelous you are, I cannot turn my eyes away. Coming from me that ought to be a compliment. For I am a doctor, Dr. 

Karl Meister. I am in charge of the hospital at Enz.

In fact I own the controlling interest in it. Come, be not afraid."

As he came toward her, she stepped from the water and seized her clothes. Naked she disappeared into the forest. She was fleet of foot but nevertheless he pursued her. But his success was negligible. It almost seemed as though the trees grew together to block his way. Continually he became entangled in unseen hanging branches. Once he fell headlong over a protruding root. He cracked his head so hard against a tree trunk he was almost stunned. After that he abandoned the chase. He would be patient. He could wait. Eventually she would be his. He would leave no stone unturned to possess her. She was the loveliest being he had ever beheld. Although he had had all sorts and conditions of women and had experimented with all the intricacies of love, both normal and abnormal, until his emotions

were drained, nevertheless desire flamed as intensely within him at the sight of Dessa's body as though he had been once more an adolescent boy.

As he turned back toward the main road, he said, "Damn the trees."

Meanwhile in the deep forest, Dessa dressed quickly. 

She wore few clothes. No girdle encircled her waist or imprisoned her small white breasts. As she dressed she felt very sad. She was not frightened but she felt as though sacrilege had been committed in that forest. 

That day she bade farewell to freedom. No longer could she bathe in peace and quietude. For always there might be unfriendly eyes staring at her. Those hidden baths, those hours of sun-bathing were spoiled.

When she returned home she said nothing about it to Nok. She knew that he would worry about her. 

He might caution her about roaming about in the mountains alone. She could not bear the thought of being chained to the house. Perhaps she would have been even more dismayed had she known that on that day Dr. Meister had come down from Enz to visit her mother. Tia had been reading her medical book and was revelling in an imagined attack of pleurisy. 

Quickly the symptoms developed until she was really sick. 

Although as a rule Dr. Meister did not leave his hospital to go out on visits, he was glad to personally attend to Tia. He had for months patiently waited for such an opportunity. For he had beheld Dessa one day telling fairy tales to the children before the

door of the Gasthof in Enz. Later he had talked to old Tobias Conrad about her. Tobias was only too glad to sing Dessa's praises. Perhaps in his enthusiasm he talked too much. In any event he succeeded unwittingly in arousing Karl Meister's appetite for this delectable girl.

Therefore when he visited Tia he told her much to her delight that she was a very sick woman. He would have to visit her again on the morrow. It was one of the happiest moments of Tia's life. In her appreciation she had entered into such a discourse on diseases it was very hard for Dr. Meister to get away. 

But finally he escaped. It was then that the whim seized him to search about in the mountains for Dessa. 

By sheerest accident he found her. He had no complaint. Never had he beheld a girl in a more exquisite setting. She had been lovelier than the dawn. More than ever he hungered for her body.

Ат AT this time Karl Meister was about fortyfive years old but he looked much older. He was tall, heavily built, muscular, inclined to stoop somewhat. 

Perhaps from constantly poring over books and experiments in his laboratory. His hair was long and he wore it parted on the side. It usually hung down over his forehead. He wore a small black moustache so tiny that it was ludicrous in contrast to his huge face. Children did not like him. Whenever he appeared upon the streets of Enz they slunk away. 

Karl Meister was very well educated. His family was rich and powerful, one of the few families who had been able to weather the storms of war and come through still wealthy. As a matter of fact the Meisters were more powerful in New Germany than they had been under the old regime. For they were clever schemers. They cared solely for themselves. They had neither love of country nor of God. But they stuck 54

together like leeches. Karl was the only member of the family who was a doctor. All the others were bankers and industrialists. Karl had studied to be a doctor because he gloried in the contemplation of pain. Like the Marquis De Sade of whom he was secretly a disciple, he believed there was a certain kinship between passion and pain. He had read "Justine" when he was a mere boy. Thereafter the book was his Bible. The sight of new blood caused a sort of hysteria to sweep over him which he had difficulty in hiding from the eyes of his family. Sometimes he would go off into the forest, set traps to catch rabbits and birds. Later he would subject the tiny victims to every conceivable torture.

He was brilliant, a fine student, a clear thinker. As a medical student his averages were high. But he was not popular. In experiments with living animals he was needlessly brutal. He was interested in torture as a fine art.

Every sort of abnormality interested him, fetiches, bestiality, masochism and demoniacal possession as exemplified in the beliefs of primitive people. Naturally he was in later years a profound psychologist. He liked to study the exposed surfaces of the brain during trepanning operations. Intellection is of course activity of the gray surface of the brain. He was trying to see thoughts functioning. 

Thus endlessly did Karl Meister study and muse.

Psychology, psycho-analysis, psycho-pathology-these

were the subjects to which he devoted all his spare time.

Because he was so wrapped up in his work, despite the fact that he gloried in taking living dogs apart, he became a splendid operating surgeon. Long hours he worked in the operating rooms of various hospitals, studying surgical methods, watching intricate operations. After all what matter that he derived extreme pleasure out of beholding patients writhing in pain? 

During the World War, Karl Meister finished his education. As soon as he had graduated he enlisted at once in the army medical department. And because, despite his comparative youth, he was an able surgeon, he was at once assigned to a base hospital. 

For his work he was decorated many times. Even foreign governments praised his accomplishments. All day long he worked at the operating table till his arms were a mass of blood and there were great crimson splotches down his clothes. When he stopped to eat he washed his hands perfunctorily and often as he ate his arms were still stained. The visitors to the hospital were loud in their praise of a man who could so submerge his personality in the cause of humanity.

Much of the renown which Karl Meister achieved was merited. He rebuilt faces, grafted on skin and performed delicate operations on the brain. He succeeded because he took all sorts of chances. He had human beings to experiment with. Human dogs to take apart. Occasionally some kink in his brain caused him to turn out a gargoyle.

Werner, a nurse so brave that she might fittingly be compared to Edith Cavell even though fate decried that they should be on opposite sides. Without thought of personal safety she marched out into the field in quest of wounded. Time after time she crept over No

NEVER had the Black Forest seemed so beautiful as when they were free once more, that is when they had the illusion of freedom. For actually one is never free. There are a thousand invisible bonds that bind us and hold us captive to this earth. There are countless laws both natural and unnatural that keep every man enslaved. And perhaps it is as well for few men are fit to rule themselves. But the tragedy is that most of the leaders are unfit also. Perhaps the sages of old China were supermen when they evolved the doctrine of tranquillity. To live gently, to sleep serene, to doze under the soft coverlet of night, meditating and dreaming of Manchu girls with golden skin and soft arms for caressing. CHAPTER 23 

CHIDE 

HIDE LEYDEN although only thirty-two had already made quite a name for himself in medical circles. His family had always been wealthy and of late years had retired from active business to enjoy life on their estate just outside of Düsseldorf. Chide had never married. Perhaps he was too busy to marry.

Always experimenting, trying to allay the fevers of this world, both mental and physical. To him marriage was a sacred thing. He had never met a girl who had sufficiently interested him for him to want her as a wife. Chide was utterly wrapped up in the great mystery of being. He believed that there was some great vital force that controlled the universe but he had no conception of what that thing was.

In appearance he was tall, well-built and extremely good-looking. His hair was dark. His features were clear-cut. Women were attracted to him but he had little use for the frivolities of life. He was capable of intense loves and intense hatreds. To him friendship was a sacred thing. If he was your friend, he'd stick to you though the whole world walked out. He was 172

a clear thinker, never influenced by the opinions of others. Cases must be judged on their merits.

That is why he was giving so much thought to the question of sterilization. Broadly speaking, he was against sterilization because in his opinion it had come years too late. Had this subject come up prior to the World War, he would have been more interested. As it was, the job had already been done. For 

War is the great sterilizer. But the dreadful thing about it is that War sterilizes the fit and permits the unfit to flourish and to multiply. It is always the custom of countries in wartime to select the strongest, cleverest and most physically perfect men for field service. During the World War more than eight million men were killed in action. These men represented the cream of the world's young manhood, coming as they did from thirteen different countries. What would have happened to all those brave men if they had lived? Most of them would have married and raised families. The majority of the geniuses preordained for the future would have come from their loins. Now all this precious life fluid had been lost to the world. 

The most devastating blow humanity had suffered since the crucifixion of Christ. Perhaps even a greater blow, for mayhap Christ himself walked on the battle fields and was crucified again. When we figure up the cost of the War we must figure untold billions in gold to make up for the terrific loss to humanity that was to come. The War successfully sterilized every nation, so that it was unable to give birth to certain

splendid sons. In the meantime, the morons, the cripples, the tubercular surfeited themselves in orgies of love and a constant stream of sickly, feeble children poured into all nations. No wonder hospitals for the feeble-minded are being constantly built and enlarged everywhere. The whole history of the world has been changed by the War. What would have happened to all those millions of men if they had lived? What would they have accomplished in the realms of medicine, of science, of art, of literature, of commerce? 

It is most important to educate men to a point where they can visualize the awful wastage and futility of 

War. Eliminate the medals, the stirring music, the gold braid, the pomp and exhibitionism of War. War should not be viewed in the warmth of passion but in the cold gray logic of dawn.

Because these were his views, Chide Leyden could not get up much enthusiasm for sterilization. The more he mused over the problem the more completely did he agree with the findings of a London committee of doctors who had been selected to study the question of sterilization of the unfit, particularly as applied to mental defectives. They admitted their complete bafflement over the problem of hereditary transmission. This expert committee of members of the 

British Medical Association admitted that sterilization of mental defectives would have no appreciable effect on posterity, certainly not for many generations. 

Even though it might be easy to prove legally that a person was mentally defective, it would be a tre-

mendous problem, almost impossible to solve, to decide whether the mental deficiency had been inherited or could be handed on by the patient. Mental aberrations resulting from environment are not hereditary. 

Therefore sterilization in such cases is futile. Sometimes however the defect is the result of a mixture of heredity and environment and no doctor is competent to untangle such a snarl. Mental instability still remains an enormous maze. It has been calculated with surprising exactitude that if every albino were sterilized in every generation, the occurrence of albinism would not be eliminated for four thousand years. And even then it would crop up in isolated cases.

In Belgium one entire session of the Société de Médecine was given over to the problem of sterilization of those persons known to be abnormal. While it is a good argument, this desire to produce a better race of mankind, there is no way of forecasting with certitude the exact proportion of the transmission of defects to future generations. At best the laws of heredity are peculiarly uncertain. In the case of the 

Meister family for generations there had been outcroppings of talipes. If some ancestor far back had been sterilized the trail of the deformity would have been stopped. In which case Karl Meister one of the acknowledged greatest surgeons of the World War would not have been born. Countless were the lives he saved. Though he was mentally abnormal, even though he did not suffer from club-foot, as did his

brother, the world could not have spared him. Had his ancestor been sterilized, all those men who were so grievously mangled by the War might have died.

It is cases of this sort that are problems to doctors everywhere. Of course in the case of the habitual criminal, the constant recidivist, it is better to sterilize him because his use to society is negative. But even with criminals it should be definitely ascertained that their criminal tendencies might prove hereditary.

Chide Leyden believed that sterilization can no more benefit mankind to any measurable extent than did enforced prohibition of liquor in Finland and America, or the religious persecution of the Inquisition. You can't make a man sober nor good by legislation. 

Neither can you make him well. Forever he will invent new vices and devices that will again open up the problem. Bertrand Russell in an article entitled "Sci entific Reproduction" suggests that perhaps some day only physically fit women will be allowed to have babies and all the rest will be sterilized. Such women will be precluded from all work except child-bearing and each of them will have to have at least eight or nine children. He suggests that impregnation may be resorted to in a purely scientific manner, much in the nature of a surgical operation, so that the women need not be unnecessarily embarrassed. Perhaps Mr. Russell wrote that with his tongue in his cheek. If so it is clever hypothesis. But many of us still have vulgar minds. Bluntly speaking we believe that if a woman is to go through the agony of childbirth repeatedly she

should be permitted to have the pleasure as well. You cannot control a natural impulse any more than you can persuade a mountain to walk. When the universe was created, and the gods formed man for their own amusement, they created an organism which was a marvel of construction. Then they created woman which was a step nearer to a perfect organism. It is a dangerous thing to tamper with machinery which is so hard to understand. The doctors in Belgium seriously wondered whether even the good effects of moral reeducation could be transmitted to posterity. Many of them believed that a mutilation as serious and with effects as far-reaching as sterilization should not be resorted to in cases that rested only on theory. They con- 

Icluded that if sterilization were only resorted to in acknowledgely serious cases, the resultant help to future generations would be negligible. Their conclusion was that birth control was far more to be encouraged than mutilations.

Chide Leyden believed that this new madness, sterilization, that was sweeping Germany, ought to be curbed. Man should never be trusted with new devices, the power of which he has no way of registering. 

Nancy Hanks was an extremely moody woman, often on the verge of insanity, yet she gave birth to Abraham Lincoln. God help America, if she had been sterilized.

Even at that very moment the possible sterilization of six hundred children who were born to German women and whose fathers were Moroccan, Algerian

and French soldiers, spawned during the Allied occupation of the Rhine country, was up for discussion. 

Certain German newspapers, particularly the Deutsche Zeitung, wrote in favor of such a passage. According to it the "sterilization will benefit not only the German race but the half-blood children who are condemned to be lifelong outcasts in the new Germany."

Thinking men and women the world over should pause for a moment and ponder over this subject. Is this progress? "What we call Progress," according to Havelock 

Ellis, "is the exchange of one Nuisance for another."

CHAPTER 25 ALTHOUGH 

LTHOUGH the sudden death of her baby had momentarily shocked Dessa Dornay, she had soon recovered her composure because she did not believe in death. When Nok had found her she had been babbling foolishly but by the next morning, after a troubled sleep, she was calm once more. There is no death. Death is not like falling into an eternal sleep, rather it is waking. It is 'the key that opens the door to the palace of eternity.' Perhaps 'the gods conceal from men the happiness of death, that they may endure life.'

Dessa walked alone into the forest. There was so much to think about, so much that troubled her. Perhaps little Kurt was happier glorified by what men call death than he had ever been in life. In the material world he was a cripple. He would aways have been lame. And in later years it would have broken her heart to see him hobbling about. But in the spiritual world, he was whole again. He no longer had a club-foot. Perhaps he would romp and play about in the blue fields of the sky, among the star- 185

flowers. Because she believed in the existence of a spiritual world she was no longer sad. 

But the death of Karl Meister troubled her. He had been murdered. Of that there could be no doubt. Even as he fell she had heard the notes of Nok's violin in the distance. She tried to shut her mind to evil thoughts and yet in spite of everything they insisted on seeping in. Nok had prepared the trap so that Karl 

Meister would be killed. He did not think that Dessa would be in any danger. He knew the forest itself would protect her. It would guide her footsteps. But he had not taken little Kurt into his calculations. That 

Karl would be carrying the child at the moment he fell, had never entered his mind. Thus Nok whom she loved had unwittingly killed her baby. She was not angry with him, she knew he had done it for her sake, but nevertheless it rather spoiled her love for him. She could not help avoiding him. It almost broke her heart to do so, yet she felt as though if she remained with him any length of time she would blurt out all the pent-up hurt within her and his heart would be broken. Always Nok had sacrificed everything for her.

As she walked through the shadowy forest trails, she thought that perhaps some day her attitude would change. But now under the circumstances she couldn't stand being with him.

She leaned against a tree and thought of Kurt. All her dreams were ended. If only she had been able to have kept him a few months longer. Her breasts were

hungering for him. They were heavy with milk. They hurt but it was nothing compared to the hurt that was in her heart. Such a little while. She hadn't even had time enough to really know him. How she longed to hear the echo of his cry, his tiny voice. She held out her arms in supplication. "Oh, Hedwig," she said softly, "wherever you are, guard my baby well. Keep him safe until I come to him." 

There came a gentle stirring in the treetops, the fragrance of pine intensified and she distinctly felt the touch of an invisible hand against her cheek, so soft, so gentle. Dessa threw her arms about a small tree and wept and where her tears fell flowers were blooming.

Meanwhile far off in a cemetery in Cologne, Rebekka Werner stood beside the grave of Karl Meister.

Like a gray granite statue she stood without moving. 

She might almost have been a monument set up to his memory, so still she was. Her lips moved, lips that were colorless. She felt as though steel talons were clawing at her heart. Hot irons were searing her soul.

For had it not been for her, Karl Meister would still have been living. Her hatred for Dessa had been so severe, it had sapped all reason. Like a cancer it had grown in her breast until at last it was strong enough to overcome Dr. Meister's mental power over her. For the first time in years she was free, able to do something of her own volition. And that act had been the greatest folly of her life.
