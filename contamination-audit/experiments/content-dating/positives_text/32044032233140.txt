PREFACE 

The purpose of this Supplement is two-fold. First, to emphasize that any estate plan for the transfer of property should not be given effect by the taxpayer until it has been the subject of study and approval by his lawyer.

The second purpose is to set forth a discussion of certain changes in the Federal tax laws, together with headnote references to the con trolling sections of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. 

To the member of the Bar, with his training and knowledge, a review is offered of savings in gift tax, estate tax, and income tax that are possible in connection with certain types of transfer of property in trust. The commentary is more or less limited to those sections of existing law which, in a sense, have been made to measure for the benefit of the parent, grandparent, or other relative or person who wishes to reduce tax liability and, at the same time, give effect and permanence to estate plans.

DISCUSSION 

The Revenue Act of 1948 provided, in the case of husband and wife, for splitting of income for purposes of the income tax, splitting of the value of gifts for purposes of the gift tax, and the splitting of the value of estates for purposes of the estate tax. 

These changes have been reenacted in the Revenue Code of 1954. 

As the design of this Supplement is to focus attention on lifetime gifts in trusts, the discussion is more or less limited to the overall tax-saving possibilities under the gift tax.

On this matter of tax saving there are a number of excellent reasons and purposes to be served that invite the serious attention of the married taxpayer to the inherent possibilities of lifetime gifts. For example: 

1. On gifts made by husband and/or wife during life the splitting of the value and computing the tax separately on one-half thereof is a tax saving that is immediately available. It is existing and substantial. It is not to be likened or compared to the unpredictable promise of a future benefit such as the marital deduction under the estate tax, in which there is always the hazard that the wellto-do spouse will survive the spouse of lesser estate. In such case the marital deduction is no longer available to the larger estate. 

2. An outright gift in trust can do everything that a will can do at a fraction of tax costs. The splitting of the value of gifts made by husband and/or wife will enjoy the benefit of the annual $3,000 exclusion in connection with gifts to certain types of trusts, the gift tax exemption of $30,000 and the lower rates of tax, computed separately in each case on one-half the value of the gift, as compared to the estate tax at the rate applicable to the highest bracket of the particular estate. 

3. Inherent in lifetime transfers is the savings due to the avoidance of state inheritance and estate taxes, probate costs and administration expenses. These taxes, costs and expenses automatically increase with the size of the estate. It becomes readily apparent that the well-to-do taxpayer not only can put his plans into effect during life, but in so doing he avoids excessive liability. The savings enhances the value of the estate. 

4. Where a taxpayer is in a nonliquid condition due to certain holdings like real estate, close-held corporation stock, or similar property for which there is no ready market, his estate is in a most unfavorable position to meet the demands of the estate tax and other charges. It means sacrifice prices and shrinkage far in excess of the amount of the tax. Transfers during life can reduce the liability to a reasonable point so that the taxes and other charges can be taken care without the danger of destroying the estate.

It is in point to mention that the statements in this paragraph can apply to the succeeding paragraphs wherever donative transfers are made by husband and wife, or either of them.

DISCUSSION 

The type of trust described in the headnote has long been a favorite in the planning of estates and the preparation of wills. Because of the many advantages, now the trust for life with a limited power of appointment over the remainder is rapidly taking its proper place as the ideal inter vivos transfer. Through the use of this device the donor is certain of the lifetime protection of the property and the life tenant; the trust guards against personal indiscretions and misfortune.

After the creation of the trust the donor's income tax is reduced as the tax on the trust income, to the extent it is accumulated, is paid by the trus- 13

tee prior to age 21 of the donee, and later, by the donee for his life. The rate of tax applicable to the trust compared to the rate at the highest bracket applicable to the income of the donor before the transfer will show the tax economy of the trust.

Over the years the actual savings can be very material. 

Another very favorable feature from a tax angle is the avoidance of estate tax in two generations. The property will first escape tax in the estate of the donor. The only possible exception will occur where the donor dies within three years from date of transfer and the Government succeeds in proving that the transfer was made "in contemplation of death" within the meaning of the tax law (see Code, Sec. 2035(b)).

The estate tax will again be avoided upon the death of the life tenant when the property passes to the designated beneficiaries free of tax.

Briefly, where the circumstances and family situation of the taxpayer are favorable, this type of trust has definite advantages that make it worthy of study by the well-to-do person who wants to give enduring effect to a proper estate plan that will help to avoid the over-all effect of the estate taxes, while at the same time saving expenses and delay in estate settlement. 

However, care must be exercised in making certain that the power of appointment is a limited power, other wise the Code provision imposing estate tax on property subject to general powers of appointment may operate to make the transfer taxable upon death of life tenant (see Code, Sec. 

2041) or at least open the door to litigation where the power is questioned by the tax authorities.

The Code defines "general" power of appointment by stating that the term means a power which is exercisable in favor of the decedent, his estate, his creditors, or the creditors of his estate, except that- (A) A power to consume, invade, or appropriate property for the benefit of the donee of the power which is limited by an ascertainable standard relating to the health, education, support, or maintenance of the donee shall not be deemed a general power. 

The Code gives other examples of general powers that are not pertinent to this discussion. For further information relative to general powers as defined by the Code, see Sec. 2041.

The Code imposes no tax on property subject to special or limited powers of appointment whether exercised or not. A special or limited power of appointment is any power other than a general power. 

The donor should also make certain that the gift is absolute in the sense that he has not retained any possibility of the remainder interest reverting to him or to his estate (see 

Code, Sec. 2037). In order to avoid such possibility it is customary to have the trust instrument name an exempt charity as contingent beneficiary of all or part of the remainder interest in the event of failure of the primary beneficiaries.

Part II IRREVOCABLE SHORT-TERM TRUSTS WITH REVERSION TO GRANTOR [111] In general- The Internal 

Revenue Code of 1954 has modified the income tax liability of the "Clifford" type trusts. Previously the trust income was taxed to the grantor where he could take back the principal or income within 15 years if he (or his wife), as trustee, had certain administrative powers over the trust property. If he did not possess these powers, the trust income was taxed to him if he could recapture either the principal or income within ten years.

Now, under the Internal Revenue Code, Sec. 673, the general rule is that a grantor will only be considered to be the owner of any portion of a trust and taxable on the income therefrom if he has a reversionary interest in either the corpus or the income therefrom which will or may reasonably be expected to take effect in possession or enjoyment within a period of ten years commencing with the date of the transfer of that portion of the trust.

Subsection (b) provides a special rule under which income of a shortterm trust for benefit of exempt charity is taxable to donor only where the term is for less than two years. 

Subsection (c) contains a further exception to the ten year rule to the effect that the grantor shall not be treated as the owner of a trust and taxable on the income therefrom because of a reversionary interest that will take effect only on the death of a person or persons to whom the income is payable, even though, due to the short life expectancy of the beneficiary, the reversionary interest may reasonably be expected to take effect within ten (10) years.

Subsection (d) provides the rule under which a postponement of the date for reacquisition of the property is considered to constitute a new transfer in trust, for the purpose of determining whether a short-term trust is in effect following the postponement. However, the section contains a qualification where the extension goes into effect before the termination of the original term. 

Briefly, the short-term trust is extremely flexible and can serve many purposes. Income tax savings of the trustor can be increased by setting up separate trusts for each undertaking for which he may be a regular contributor. DISCUSSION 

Frequently it is necessary for some member of the family to provide for the support and well-being of an aged or handicapped relative. In such cases the value of the short-term trust is clearly demonstrated. The donor (trustor) is taking the tax load off his income at the highest bracket, while the income of the trust passing to the beneficiary may escape tax entirely because of personal exemptions and credits. 

For example, the taxpayer had been sending remittances of $200 each month to his wife's mother (it could have been any needy relative or other person). He realized that as his income generally fell within the 5056% brackets the $2,400 he gave his mother-in-law each year was costing him from $3,600 to $4,000 because of income taxes.

His lawyer knew the facts and explained the advantages of an irrevo- 19

cable living trust that would provide monthly payments of income to the beneficiary for the period of her life and upon her death the trust would end and the property would be returned to the donor (or to his estate, if he be not living at that time).

The income that the trust would earn and pay out would be taxed to the beneficiary and not to the donor.

The annual tax saving to the donor is quite obvious. The payments to the beneficiary would probably escape all tax because of her advanced age, low income, exemptions and credits.

The Code, Sec. 673(c), makes specific provision for trusts of this nature and the grantor is not treated as the owner and is not taxed on the income even though the beneficiary is of advanced age and has a very short life expectancy.

The merit of this type of trust would be the annual income tax saving. The income tax bracket of the donor measures the saving to him without reducing the payments to the beneficiary in most cases. 

There would be very little, if any, estate tax saving. The property would return to the donor upon death of the life beneficiary. In the event the donor died during lifetime of the beneficiary, the present value of the reversionary interest would be included in his taxable estate. 

Because of the age of most of the beneficiaries in such cases, there is very little, if any, actual gift tax payable. The value of the right to receive the income for life is based on the life expectancy tables. To illustrate, a trust providing $200 per month for the life of a person 65 years of age has a gift tax value of $23,281.00. 

The gift tax exclusion $3,000 would be applicable. The gift tax exemption of $30,000, if still unused, would be more than sufficient to offset any actual payment of tax. 

And, in addition, if husband and wife, or either of them, establish the trust, the law provides for splitting the value of the gift and determining the tax liability separately as though each made a gift of half the value of the total. 20

DISCUSSION The Revenue Code of 1954, Sec. 

673, reflects a change in the law. 

Subsection (b) provides a special rule under which the income of a reversionary trust, irrevocably payable for a period of at least two years commencing with the date of the transfer, escapes income tax if all the income for the entire term is paid to a single specifically designated beneficiary that qualifies as either a church or religious order, or a hospital, or an educational organization. Under the prior law the exemption would not apply to any trust for a term of less than ten years. 

So that, in instances where the taxpayer is interested in an exempt charity, he can give effect to his purpose with a short-term trust. The two- 23

year term limits the selection to a particular charity. This limitation does not apply to trusts of longer term. But in either case the donor is able to bestow a greater benefit on the charity with less income-tax cost to himself.

The Code, Sec. 642(c), provides that a trust is allowed an unlimited deduction for charitable contributions. Thus, a trust may deduct any amount of its gross income, without limitation, which is paid or permanently set aside for charitable purposes provided for by the trust instrument.

On the other hand, this is not the case with charitable contributions by the individual. The Code, Sec. 170, provides that for purposes of deduction the total of such contributions shall not exceed 20% of the taxpayer's gross income. An additional 10% is allowed for contributions to religious, educational, and hospital organizations.

The difference is well illustrated in an outstanding case. Two sisters created separate irrevocable trusts to run for a period of ten years in favor of an exempt charity. Certain securities were transferred to the trustee and the trust instrument expressly provided that all income was to be applied to the general charitable purpose of the particular charity and was not in any way to be retained as part of the trust corpus. At the expiration of 10 years the corpus of each trust, but no accumulation of income, was to be returned to the settlor.

The income from each trust was in excess of $25,000 per year and this passed to the charity free of income tax.

It would have been otherwise had the income been payable to each of the two sisters who, in turn, would make annual contributions of this amount to the charity. Only the fixed percentage allowed by the Code would have escaped tax. The balance would have been subject to tax at the highest bracket of the particular taxpayer. 24
