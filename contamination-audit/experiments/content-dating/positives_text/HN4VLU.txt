RESOLUTIONS CONGRATULATING THE TOWN OF NAHANT UPON ITS ONE HUNDRETH ANNIVERSARY AS A TOWN. 

Whereas, Nahant was one of the earliest of the many settlements which the Puritans founded along our coast, it was not until eighteen hundred and fifty-three that it was set apart from the city of Lynn and incorporated as a town; and

Whereas, This beautiful town, jutting out into the Atlantic Ocean, has long served as a beacon for sailors returning to the Port of Boston, in fact, historians tell us that the Norseman Thorwald, in the year ten hundred and four, sailing northward from Cape Cod, noticed a bold promontory which projected into the sea, and exclaimed "Here it is beautiful and here I should like to fix my dwelling!", but fate decreed otherwise, for after a battle with some Indians he was slain and his body buried on the spot that he admired so much; and 

Whereas, The land here was so rich and sheltered that for many years after it was first settled it was the source of a great deal of litigation as to who was entitled to possession; and

Whereas, After several years of argument and persuasion and application of the state legislature, it was finally set aside from the city of Lynn and became a separate town; and 

Whereas, Nahant has long been noted for its distinguished citizens who have so well served their country and this commonwealth, foremost being the late Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, Sr., who because of his love for his town forsook his duties as an international statesman to return each year to preside over its town meeting as its moderator; and

Whereas, The year nineteen hundred and fifty-three marks the one hundredth anniversary of its incorporation as a town; therefore be it 

Resolved, That the House of Representatives extend to the town of Nahant its congratulations and best wishes for its continued success; and be it further 

Resolved, That the Secretary of State be instructed to send a copy of these resolutions to the town of Nahant to be preserved among the archives. 

HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES, MARCH 9, 1953. 

Adopted.

LAWRENCE R. GROVE, Clerk.

NAHANT AN HISTORICAL SKETCH By JAMES J. DEVENEY 

1953 

EARLY DAYS 

Any story, or history of Nahant must go back to the days of the Sagamore tribe of Indians whose village spread along the Lynn shore before the advent of any white man. This tribe was said to have been friendly and proved to be helpful to the early settlers.

We have been told many times that Thorwald, a Norseman, visited Nahant in the year 1004, that Gosnold, an early explorer landed in 1602, and in 1614 Capt. John Smith of the Virginia 

Colony while on a surveying trip along the coast also landed. 

In 1622 the Council of England made a grant of land, which included Nahant, to a Capt. Gorges who came over the next year and claimed it.

In 1630 the first settlers appeared in Lynn, coming from Salem where they had found haven the year before after a long, rough voyage from England. From that date on until 

1853 Nahant was considered as being a part of Lynn. 

At that time Nahant was composed of two islands, Great Nahant and Little Nahant. There is however some evidence to indicate that Bass Neck, later known as Bass Point, was surrounded by water also. The name 'Nahant" is said to have been derived from the Indian word "Nahanteau" meaning two.

Great Nahant is composed of six hundred and forty acres or one square mile of land, has a circumference of six miles and an elevation of about eighty-five feet. Little Nahant has an area of eighty acres; thrown in for good measure is Egg Rock with about three acres.

We are told that the islands were well wooded and had many features of natural beauty. Caves and grottoes were found; the beaches between the headlands abounded with clean white sand, shells and stones of many colors.

Following the discovery of this garden spot in the ocean many disputes arose regarding its ownership. One Thomas

Dexter claimed to have traded a suit of clothes with an Indian chief for it, another claimed to have lent money to the same chief, and possessed a mortgage. All claims were finally disallowed and Nahant became public land and was used for pasturing cattle. When a young man of Lynn became a "freeman" he was allotted a plot of land in Nahant upon which to pasture his cow. Immediately the trees were cut down so that by the year 1800 there were few left. Allotments were made without too much order or record and conflict frequently arose until in 1706 a plan was made and each one's holdings were registered. There were two hundred and thirty plots laid out in Great Nahant and twenty-three in Little Nahant. In the future development those at Little Nahant were the last to be given up. Ranges were established at forty rods in width, and in between were the range roads running from water to water and from east to west. Many of these range roads have become the permanent roads of the town. 

ROADS AND COW PATHS 

There was no roadway from Lynn to Nahant until 1848, although several attempts had been made to form one. The storms of winter made all efforts useless. Travel had to wait for the action of the tides which would uncover the hard sand below the crest and make travelling easier. The first road in Nahant was called 

Washington Street, being a continuation of that street in Lynn, and ran in a southerly direction to the foot of Bass Beach Hill, now more commonly known as Forty Steps Hill; land beyond this point was called the Ram Pasture and was used for grazing. 

The second road branched off from the first one at a point near the foot of the great hill and after many twists and turns joined with it at what is now the corner of Nahant Road and Pond Street. This road was named Calf Spring Road on account of its giving access to a spring, still running, near the brick house, which became a common watering place for cattle. 

Nahant was almost entirely given over to the grazing of cattle. 

Cow-paths were numerous, changing direction with every large boulder. Cattle were driven over from Lynn in the morning by men hired for the purpose who returned them at night to pens at the end of the long beach where the owners came for them. 

Some of these shepherds and caretakers lived on Nahant, but their stay was often of short duration and no great notice was taken of their migrations.

The first settler of which there is definite record was Thomas Graves who came to Nahant in 1635. The next settler was his son-in-law, Hugh Alley, who came in 1647. It is not known how long Graves lived in Nahant or when he died, but Alley died in 1684. His land descended to his daughter, the wife of James 

Mills, who died in 1717. There has always been some doubt as to where the Mills house was located. The map of 1706 shows it as being about where the Samuel Hammond house is today. 

There were no inhabitants living on Nahant in 1717. After the death of Mills, his family moved away. 

THE BREEDS AND HOODS 

During the year 1717 Samuel Breed, Sr., purchased land and possibly a house on it, from Dr. John Burchstead, on the southerly side of the road at the foot of Bass Beach Hill. This house is still standing, although greatly changed, and is the only one of the earliest houses that does stand upon the original site at 

369 Nahant Road. 

Samuel Breed, Jr., succeeded to his father's estate and in 

1738 became an innkeeper. Descending through the Breed family it became the property of Jessie Rice in 1819 and continued as an inn. In 1841 Albert Whitney, Rice's son-in-law, acquired the property, greatly enlarged the house, and ran it as a hotel for many years. It later housed the only telephone in the town and the Post Office, and was an "institution" for many years. 

Samuel Breed's other son, Jabez, built a house across the road on land given him by his father and in 1738 sold out to 

Richard Hood, a Quaker from Lynn, who was related to the

Breeds through marriage. Elbridge Gerry Hood, a descendant, was the father of Mrs. Thomas Roland, Sr., whose son Phillips married Marion Bruce, a daughter of Carrie Alley, a descendant of Hugh Alley. The generation of Rolands in Nahant represented by Phillips Roland, 2nd, can lay claim to the earliest settlers as ancestors on both sides.

THE JOHNSONS 

Jeremiah Gray, a son-in-law of Richard Hood, built a house on land directly opposite the Town Hall in 1741 and sold to Jonathan Johnson, the progenitor of a long line, who came to Nahant in 1750. The Alleys, Breeds, Hoods and Johnsons inter-

married, Johnson's third wife being a granddaughter of Hugh

Alley. One of the Hoods married an Alley. 

The Johnsons had three sons, Benjamin, Caleb and Joseph. 

Benjamin early moved to Lynn; Joseph also went to Lynn but returned to Nahant about 1802. Caleb always lived in the homestead and succeeded to ownership as did his son Caleb Hervey who lived until 1901. He is well remembered by some of those now living in the town and was known as "Uncle Hervey” to most of the inhabitants.

The Johnson estate at 333 Nahant Road became the property of Henry Sigourney in 1897. Joseph Johnson, after a try at shoemaking in Lynn, returned to Nahant and built a house on the main road nearly opposite the homestead and this became the third permanent house of record standing in 1802-03. 

The Breeds, Hoods and Johnsons owned most of the land southerly from what is now Summer Street. 

The Caleb Johnson family had ten children and forty-five grandchildren. The Joseph Johnson family had fourteen children and fifty-seven grandchildren making a total of one hundred twenty-six persons all of the same name. Many of these Johnson men became well known in business and professional lines and made enviable names wherever they went. Several served the

Union during the Civil War; one remained in the Navy, and went to Annapolis and became an Admiral; another became a mayor of Lynn; another a postmaster in Nahant and a selectman for several years. One of these men became a shoe manufacturer in Lynn and gave the Chapel in the cemetery to the town. Three were "forty-niners" and participated in the gold rush to California. The early town officers were mostly Johnsons and there has been one or more in town office ever since until 1947, when Charles Cabot Johnson, passed away. He had been Town Clerk and later Treasurer for nearly fifty years, as well as having been a member of the Massachusetts General Court through several terms the second Johnson to have been such. One of the feminine members of the family gained notice as a writer of poetry and another, Florence, taught school in Nahant for nearly fifty years, dying in 1950 at the age of ninety-two. There are only a dozen persons now living in Nahant that are descendants of the original Johnson.

The Breeds, most of the Hoods, and some of the Johnsons moved away from Nahant. Those that remained became farmers,

fishermen and tradesmen of all sorts, earned the reputation of being industrious and possessed of a fine neighborly and cooperative spirit and action that did much to give the town a good name. So peaceably did they live that the street where several of Joseph Johnson's sons lived, was named "Harmony Court.” 

Jonathan Johnson, a son of Joseph, built a house on the site of the present Post Office building, and in company with nearly every householder then living on Nahant Road took "summer boarders." Longfellow was a summer boarder for several seasons and while there wrote "Hiawatha." The room occupied by the poet at that time may still be viewed at the Hotel Rockledge on Willow Road to which point the Johnson house was moved in 1889. 

In 1803 there was another house built in the wilderness of Bass Neck by a Johnson, no relation to the other family of Johnson, which was run as a hotel until 1840 when it was purchased by 

John C. Phillips, the first mayor of Boston, and the father of 

Wendell Phillips, the great anti-slavery advocate of the fifties.

This house was later known as the "Old Castle" and was demolished in 1902 by Edward E. Strout who erected a modern house 

Ellingwood Chapel

on the site. Phillips Road and Wendell Road are reminders of the earlier owners.

THOMAS H. PERKINS 

The fame of this beautiful spot in the ocean travelled far and wide and being so near Boston attracted many visitors in spite of the hardships of travel across the beaches. 

Thomas H. Perkins, who had been a visitor, built a house in 

1816 on the north side of the town overlooking Spouting Horn, a freak of nature. The stone used in building this house was transported by barge and was landed on one of the sandy beaches. 

The workmen lived aboard this barge while engaged in building the house. The same method of transporting stone from Weymouth was used at the time the Public Library was built in 1894. 

A granddaughter of Perkins maried Louis Agassiz, the scientist, and was herself a President of Radcliffe College. Another granddaughter married C. C. Felton who became a President of Harvard College. These two families lived in the same house, still standing on the north end of Pleasant Street. A third granddaughter, Emma F. Carey lived in the homestead for many years and was well known to many of the townspeople. 

Mr. Perkins was enterprising and wealthy. He became interested in erecting a stone building at East Point which was opened to the public as the "Nahant Hotel" in 1823. About this time a 

Dr. Robbins built a house near the site of the present Elliott house and he too became interested in the hotel, also Judge 

Prescott, father of William Prescott, the historian, who was fond of helping the delivery boys up the hills when they were bringing heavy parcels from the store. Next to Prescott's was the summer home of B. W. Crowninshield, a Secretary of the Navy during the administrations of Presidents Madison and Monroe, and afterwards a Congressman from 1823 to 1831.

The Crowninshield house was left to his daughter who had married a clergyman named Mountford. Later the place was acquired by K. W. Sears, a wealthy and dignified business man from Boston; and the house was moved to Pleasant Street near the site of the present Town Hall and still later moved to Spring Road when the new Town Hall was built in 1912. 

The Sears house, an imposing structure with a high tower, was built in 1874 and has recently been cut down in height and remodelled into a modern house by Ralph Lowell and

stands near two great concrete watchtowers erected during World 

War II.

DEAR SIR....We very readily comply with your request to express our opinion respecting the advantages to be derived from a residence on the sen shore. It is well known how highly these advantages are valued in other countries, and we believe that they are duly appreciated among us. If our citizens have not availed themselves of them, as fully as those of Great Britain, it may be attributed to the want of proper arrangements for their convenience. The moment has arrived for making such arrangements, and the only question should be, în what place they should be made. There is not a spot probably on the whole coast of our country, which offers so many natural advantages for this purpose as the peninsula of Nabant. It has all the good qualities of an island; is fully exposed to the sea, has its beach and its elevated grounds, and affords opportunities for the amusement of fishing, while it is easy of access both by land and water. Its temperature is much less subject to variation, than that of the main land; a circumstance of great importance to invalids. And it may be added, that the fine view of the sex, with the various land prospects which it furnishes, must not only give delight to persons of taste, but has also no small tendency to invigorate both the body and the mind. That the public may avail themselves of these advantages, it is only necessary that proper houses should be erected for the accommodation of visitors, and that conveniences should be provided for bathing. But it may be thought necessary that we should rather speak of the medical benefits, which may be derived from Nahant. The medical advantages of sen air and sea bathing may be said to be of two kinds, or to have respect to persons of two descriptions, viz.: to those who are enfeebled by heat, and confinement, and the fatigues of business, and thus are rendered liable to disease; and to those who have already become the subjects of disease. Among our citizens those of the first description are the most numerous. Such persons may often be renovated and saved from illness by a well-timed visit to Nahant for a day, a week, or a month, according to eircumstances. In many instances a retreat from the city to the country will have similar good effects; bat ordinarily more may be effected in one week at Nahant, than in a month in the country. This would be still more true, if conveniences could be provided at the latter place for both warm and cold sea bathing. These are essentially important, and are most loudly called for by persons of every description, who visit Nahant. The diseases for which sea air and sex bathing are found useful, are generally those of debility. Such are many of the summer and autumnal diseases of children, indigestion, nervous complaints, some cases of chronic rheumatism, and many of serophula. To those may be added the cases of convalescents from long and exhausting diseases. It is to be hoped, that the considerations which have been suggested, will not be overlooked by persons now in health. Men of property, when they themselves or their families become ill, are justly regardless of expense in pursuing the best course for the recovery of health. Surely these, who now enjoy this blessing, cannot calculate that they and their families shall continue to possess it without interruption. They should not then hesitate to incur a moderate expense for a most generous and refreshing remedy, of which every man will occasionally feel the need. We cannot believe that those who give to the subject a moment's consideration, will have any hesitation in providing a shelter on the spot, to which, within a few short years, they may look as a refuge, under feelings of the greatest anxiety, for their wives or children, The opinions which we have expressed, are the same which we have often had occasion to give to individuals in our professional business, It has, therefore, afforded us the highest satisfaction to witness the energy and perseverance which you have manifested on this subject. We cannot doubt that other gentlemen of liberality and spirit, will do something for the advancement of a work so important to the community. 

We have the honor to be, Dear sir, your very obedient servants,

In 1843 Paran Stevens, a hotel man of note became manager, enlarged the building until it resembled a huge barracks, large enough to accommodate fifteen hundred guests; six hundred could be seated in the dining room at one time. 

It was the largest hotel north of Boston. Stevens planned to make Nahant a rival of Newport, Rhode Island, as a watering place. Great sums were expended for entertainment, and it is said that the affairs held at the hotel attracted more attention and patronage than the Inaugural Ball in Washington.

The Post Office was first established in the hotel in 1847 and regular transportation to Lynn was started by Capt. Henry 

Dunham, a partially disabled veteran of the Mexican War. 

War clouds of the late fifties caused the hotel business to diminish to such an extent that it did not open for the season of 1858, and remained closed until totally destroyed by fire 

September 12. 1861.

THE LODGES 

In 1823 Cornelius Coolidge, a Boston merchant, bought all the remaining land of the Ram Pasture. He had a flair for architecture and development; built the roads at East Point as they are today, and erected many houses which attracted people of wealth to enhance the welfare of the settlement. Among others was Charles W. Eliot, the father of a President of Harvard University. Henry Cabot who came from Salem and was the fatherin-law of John Ellerton Lodge, father of Senator Henry Cabot

Lodge, Nahant's most notable citizen, who spent his early days at the Villa and the remainder of his life at East Point. Senator 

Lodge was the orator of the day upon the occasion of the celebration of the Town's fiftieth birthday anniversary in 1903. His grandson and namesake, a native of the town, former Senator Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., will likewise be the featured speaker of the day for the forthcoming celebration of the 100th anniversary in July. Another grandson John D. Lodge, is presently Governor of Connecticut.

After the burning of the Nahant Hotel, John Ellerton Lodge attempted to form a company to acquire the property and erect another hotel. With the Civil War being waged, the intended sponsors withdrew and Mr. Lodge was left alone with nearly thirty acres of land which were divided between his two children,

Henry Cabot Lodge and his sister who had married George

Abbott James, who later in life became interested in beautifying the town by having trees planted and who was the sponsor of an organized town baseball team in 1906.

The first house, built by Mr. James, in 1868 and was turned over to Mr. Lodge when he married in 1871. A second house was built by Mr. James, nearby. Much of the material used in the construction was imported from England. It is still on the original site and presently serves the Army as a local headquarters. 

THE RAM PASTURE 

Samuel Cabot built a house near the Robbins and Prescott houses that decended to Julia Bryant and to General Charles J. 

Paine of cup defender fame. With J. Malcolm Forbes, then a summer resident at Bass Point, he successfully defended the America Cup with the "Puritan" in 1885. In 1886 General 

Paine won with the "Mayflower" and again with the "Volunteer” in 1887. After the "Jubilee" of 1893 lost out to the "Vigilant" in the trials, it was a familiar sight anchored off the Town Wharf or sailing in the bay.

B. C. Clark who has been credited with inaugurating yacht racing in Massachusetts Bay also had a home in Nahant, now at 211 Willow Road, and his yacht "Mermaid", appeared in 1832.

The first regatta was held off Nahant in 1845 and aroused much interest. 

Amos A. Lawrence, father of Bishop William Lawrence of the Episcopal Diocese of Massachusetts, later owned the Clark house.

Charles Amory, who also had a summer home in Nahant was the grandfather of Abbott Lawrence Lowell another President of

Harvard University who spent his summers here. 

In 1824 Coolidge built a hotel on Vernon Street and a wharf at which steamboats from Boston landed guests. In 1840 this was discontinued and a house, presently owned by Mrs. 

Dudley B. Fay, was built on the site, for George W. Peabody of 

Salem, whose son-in-law, William C. Endicott, was Secretary of 

War in the first Cleveland Cabinet and a summer visitor to the town.

Coolidge also donated the land on which the Union Church, later the Nahant Church was built in 1832; enlarged in 1868 to what it is today. This church was open to all creeds.

Nahant Church 

Thus the Ram Pasture became developed with large houses, many of which remain on the original sites. 

Activity in house building became noticeable in other parts of the town. Edward Phillips built a house near the wharf in 

1846, now 171 Willow Road, which was later owned by Gen. 

John C. Fremont, and occupied by his family while he was finding a new and shorter route to the Pacific Coast, meanwhile discovering Pike's Peak. Mrs. Fremont caused a new road to be built from the foot of the hill to join Spring Road near the present telephone exchange. This road was called Fremont Street, -now, Willow and Valley Roads. In later years this property was acquired by the Tudors. 

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow built a house, which was burned in the great fire of May 18, 1896, just above the Fremont house on the hill.

John Joy constructed a log cabin on Schoolhouse Lane, now Pleasant Street, with logs imported from Maine. 

In 1829 one Stephen Codman built a house across the road from the present Public Library, and while a barn was being built,

it was struck by lightning and one of the workmen killed. Later this property was owned by James W. Paige who was related to 

Daniel Webster through marriage. Webster became a frequent summer visitor, and it was here he secluded himself after losing the nomination for President in 1852. This property was later owned by one Abbott Lawrence whose sons were interested in breeding horses and conducted horse races on the hard sands of Long Beach. General Francis Peabody, Lawrence's son-in-law, was a personal friend of Governor William E. Russell and served on his military staff, thus bringing Gov. Russell to the town often. 

"COLD ROAST BOSTON" 

The summer colony grew until there were more than one hundred families of the so-called "aristocratic class." Now the descendants living in the town may be counted on the fingers of one hand. Some of these people were not of a friendly nature and Thomas Appleton, himself a member of the summer colony, and a noted wit, characterized Nahant as "cold roast Boston.' 29 The colony consisted of several families of Sears, Motleys, Russells, Gibsons, Warrens, Bradlees, Guilds, Rodmans, Spragues, Loverings, Wrights, Inches, Mifflins, Burrs, Dabneys, Uphams, Merriams, Dwights, Otis', McBurneys, Schlesingers, Parkers, Thorndikes, Hodges, Blanchards, Beals, Curtis', Richardsons and 

Shepleys, Admiral Thatcher and General Devens; three families of other Johnsons, the son-in-law of one being Curtis Guild, Jr., thrice Governor of Massachusetts and an Ambassador to Russia, as well as a comrade in arms during the Spanish American War with President Theodore Roosevelt. 

Robert C. Winthrop a noted writer and statesman was a frequent summer visitor. He succeeded to the seat in the U. S. Senate made vacant by the death of Webster. Mrs. Joseph Storey, the widow of a U. S. Supreme Court Justice, built a house on Pleasant 

Street where her son, Julian, a noted sculptor also lived. This house was later owned by Alanson Tucker, whose schooner yacht "Constellation", the largest of its type in this area, lay off the 

Town Wharf. 

The schooner "Breeze" owned by Samuel Johnson and the "Avalon" owned by Herbert F. Otis were also well-known to the residents of the town.

For speed Nahanters could point to the "Now Then" owned by Henry Addicks, the copper king, who lived on Nahant Road;

and the "Cora" owned by Thurlow Weed Barnes of the Louisiana Lottery, who was then residing in the house which had been built on Coolidge's wharf.

Many of the summer colony and visitors wrote in glowing terms of Nahant-among them were Willis, Curtis, Hawthorne, Whittier, Emerson, Harriet Beecher Stowe, Sarah Orne Jewett, Julia Ward Howe, Frances Hodgson Burnett and Elizabeth Stuart Phelps Ward, the last of whom lived at Little Nahant. 

Cyrus W. Field who was responsible for the laying of the first 

Atlantic cable summered in Nahant for several years, as did Charles Hayden, whose great personal estate now forms the Hayden Foundation.

J. Lothrop Motley came to visit his brother Edward and the older Lodges; Charles Sumner came to Longfellow's and Rufus

Choate was also a summer visitor. 

President Theodore Roosevelt was a guest at the home of

Senator Henry Cabot Lodge in August of 1902. The townspeople turned out en masse to greet him, and he made an address from a stand hastily erected in front of the Library. He returned again to the town, quietly, in 1910. President Taft was entertained at the Nahant Club and by the Lodges, Labor Day, 1910.

Still another President, Franklin D. Roosevelt, visited the town in 1938 on the occasion of the marriage of his son John to Anne Clark.

FREDERICK TUDOR 

While Cornelius Coolidge did much to develop Nahant, another enterprising and energetic man appeared in the person of 

Frederick Tudor, called the "Ice King" because he cut ice on his father's pond in Saugus and shipped it to the West Indies and later to the East Indies and China. He was the owner of a large fleet of ships and his wharf was located at the site of the present Boston Navy Yard. When he first came to Nahant, in 1824, he resided with his mother in a stone house that had been built in 1822 and which afterwards became his home and later the Nahant Club and is now the Thomson Club. 

Upon finding Nahant almost devoid of trees, Tudor caused a double row of elm trees to be planted on each side of Nahant Road, extending from the bottom of the great hill to Summer Street. Trees had previously been planted by the Breeds, Hoods and Johnsons from there to the foot of Bass Beach Hill. Tudor

also created gardens on every side and imported fruit and shade trees. An enclosed garden named "High Ho" with a thirty foot fence was formed in the low lands, the soil being enriched by loam dragged in from Bear Pond. A brick house erected for his gardeners and a stone barn, to store crops including tobacco, were added in 1839; both of these buildings are still located on Spring 

Road. Choice fruit and vegetables were raised in this garden. A cranberry bog was located outside the fence. Tudor also built 

Marginal Road and the sea wall. He created the famous Maolis 

Gardens, a six acre plot lying on the north side between Ocean and Pond Streets.

The name "Maolis" is said to be a reversal of King Solomon's "Siloam Gardens"-"By cool Siloam's shady rills, how fair the lilly grows" has been exemplified by the Old North Spring where cold water has been gushing from the ledge since before the discovery of the town. A hotel was connected with the Gardens, also a large dancing pavilion; the rock temple is still standing with the bear cave below, and there was also a restaurant. Shelters were scattered about for visitors who partook of their own "picnic." Great sheds and a large barn were located about where the 

Kinsman house is presently, and horses were sheltered from the sun. Balm of Gilead trees were imported and planted. 

Witch House Maolis Gardens

Tudor replaced the old wharf with a new one, called at first the "Central Wharf" and later "Tudor Wharf." This wharf was given to the town by the descendants of Frederic Tudor after his death in 1864. Steamboats ran from Boston and passengers were transported to the Gardens by horse drawn barges. The charge for admission was trifling and thousands of people visited the famous spot. The buildings of the Gardens were moved by scow to the grounds of the Bass Point House in 1892, greatly increasing the size and scope of that later day amusement center. 

Tudor acquired numerous parcels of land variously situated. 

Upon his death his widow attempted to carry on, but this proved to be too great an undertaking so a holding company known as "The Nahant Land Company", was formed to liquidate the real estate. The family of Tudor moved elsewhere and no land in 

Nahant is now owned by any of his descendants. 

TOWN OFFICERS 

By this time the year round population had increased in number as well as the summer colony and in 1846 a petition was filed with the Legislature seeking separation from Lynn. Objection was so strong that permission was not granted, but in 1851 the great storm that destroyed Minot's Ledge Light House also ruined the road that Lynn built in 1848 so that when the people of Nahant again applied for separation in 1853 Lynn agreed with the stipulation that Nahant take the roadway and the beaches. Thus Nahant became the smallest town in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts.

The first town officers were mostly of the Johnson and Hood families and remained that way until 1876 when the number of selectmen was changed from three to five. This was changed back to three in 1879.

One of the five elected in 1876 was Joseph T. Wilson who was neither a Johnson nor a Hood. He had previously been elected to the School Board in 1870 and to the Board of Library Trustees in 

1871. Henry Cabot Lodge became a Library Trustee in 1877 serv ing until his death in 1924. Joseph T. Wilson served as a selectman until 1906 and was the foremost citizen of the town for more than a quarter of a century until his death in 1914. In addition to his several other town offices, he was also Moderator of sixty-eight Town Meetings, alternating at times with his life-long friend, Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, Sr. His son Fred A. Wilson was

made a trustee of the Public Library in 1897 and continued in that office, as well as being Moderator for sixty-eight Town Meetings, until his death in 1950. He was the author of "Some Annals Of Nahant," pronounced to be the most complete history of the town ever published. Miss Constance C. Wilson, his daughter now serves as a Library Trustee continuing a family service in that office that began in 1871. 

J. Colby Wilson, a brother of Joseph, served with him on the 

Board of Selectmen from 1882 until 1906; and Harry C. Wilson, his son, was a member of the School Committee for several years and also a member of the Board of Selectmen for twenty-five years, retiring in 1931. His widow, Edith Johnson Wilson, is the oldest member of the pioneer Johnson family now living in the town.

Albert G. Wilson, a brother-in-law of Joseph, was a Library 

Trustee for thirty years and also supervised the workings of the 

Fire Department for many years.

Other town officers elected at various times served many years, being released in most cases only by death. A notable example was that of Alfred D. Johnson who served as Town Clerk from 1857 to 1890. Two others spanned the interval until 1904 when William F. Waters became Town Clerk, serving until his death in 1935.

Leon M. Delano, the present Town Clerk, succeeded Waters, after having served as a Selectman from 1921, and is now the veteran officer of the Town.

In the 1870's there was a cry of "too much Johnson." In the late 1890's a similar cry arose of "too much Wilson." Since 1906 there have been many changes in town office and there has been no such slogan.

LITTLE NAHANT 

The first dwelling at Little Nahant, built in 1847, was used to house the men working on the road from Lynn. In 1850 Dr. William Lawrence built two cottages, one of which remains, though not in its original location, and was occupied by William Dean Howells for a summer or two. The other house stood where the 

Howe house, with the tower, now stands, but was moved from the site in 1880 across Short Beach and set up as the Tremont Hotel, near the corner of Nahant and Spring Roads.

George W. Simmons acquired most of Little Nahant in 1860

and in 1862 built the house now numbered 25 Baker Road. 

George W., and his brother William, for whom Simmons College was named, were in the clothing business as the Oak Hall Clothing Co., on North Street in Boston. This store was the headquarters for uniforms of Northern soldiers during the Rebellion. Simmons established enclosed gardens after the Tudor style, a bowling alley was an added feature much enjoyed by the young men of the town in later years. There was only one road leading to the house from a big gate on the Lynn side known in the old days as the "Black Gate." The road ran diagonally up the hill.

The Simmons family owned considerable land in the town, a large tract bordered by Nahant Road and Pond Street, land across the street and the triangular plot surrounded by Pond 

Street, Spring and Valley Roads. G. W. Simmons died in 1885 and his son in 1896. The estate was sold at auction in 1897; much of the land at Little Nahant, bordering the beach road was purchased by the Carahar Brothers, then conducting a barge and express business at Bass Point. A small hotel called "The Grand 

View" was built on the road side of the property in 1898 and was destroyed by fire in January of 1915 during a severe snow storm.

The town ceded the beaches to the State in 1900 and in 1901 a hotel and other buildings were cleared from the ocean side at the end of the beach. The hotel had been used as a terminus for the 

Lynn & Boston Street Railway. Nahanters took advantage of the railway running as far as the hotel and would then walk the beach road home.

The Carahar Brothers acquired the hotel and other buildings forming Seaside Park, an amusement center adjacent to it, although under different management. The hotel was moved to the top of Little Nahant Hill and afforded a wonderful view of Lynn. 

Harbor. It was badly damaged by fire in 1928 and later demolished.

The remainder of the Simmons land was purchased by the Little Nahant Land Company, a development company headed by Edward E. Strout. Their aim was to promote "sales in an orderly fashion"—but sales did not meet with their expectations, so in 1911 it was auctioned off in individual lots.

BASS POINT 

For several years most of the activity of the town centered about "Great Nahant." The Nahant Hotel, the Nahant House, Whitney's Hotel, the Bay View House, the Hood Cottage, afterwards the Hotel Tudor, Rice's Hotel and the many "summer boarding houses" provided temporary abode for those whose stay involved more than a day's outing. The Johnson House, overlooking Lynn Harbor, went out of business in 1840 and nothing of note took place at Bass Point during the ensuing decade. 

In 1850 Nathan Mower who had been engaged in conducting *parties to the Maolis Gardens, acquired land and two small buildings at Bass Point and started a business of providing sea food dinners for the daily visitors; thus marking the beginning of the famous "Bass Point Fish Dinner." These two buildings were consolidated forming the nucleus of a hotel which was enlarged from time to time until it became of considerable size, but had never received a name. In 1862 soldiers returning on furlough from the battlefields of the South told about a popular road house near Baltimore called "Relay House," so Mower's hotel assumed the name. In later years it reached its peak while under the management of Eugene H. Brann. 

In 1864 Frederick Tudor built a hotel called "Trimountain

House" between the three hills on the south side of Bass Point that was under the management of Sylvester Brown for more than forty years. This property was absorbed by the Federal Government in the forming of Fort Ruckman in 1900. The hotel was demolished in 1922.

In 1868 Mrs. Tudor caused a hotel to be built at the Point and called it "Bass Point House." From a small building its size and scope were greatly enlarged by the addition of the buildings, moved by water around East Point, from the Maolis Gardens in 1892. In later years a wharf was built and the Clyde Steamship 

Company became interested in the hotel with Andrew G. Fuller as Manager, the steamship company supplied the boats which ran to Tudor Wharf as well. The hotel suffered damage from several fires, being finally destroyed in 1938. 

The creation of a "Midway" after a World's Fair attraction, and the installation of many features of entertainment, together with name bands to furnish music made this section of the town a popular place of amusement. A line of smaller boats, sponsored

by officials of the General Electric Co. made frequent trips between the Relay Wharf and Lynn carrying many passengers. The boat service was augmented by trolley cars and vehicles of every description. It was no uncommon sight to see more than forty thousand people at Bass Point on a hot Sunday of Summer or on a holiday, all seeking to partake of the famous fish dinner. 

World War I brought an end to the steamboat service. The amusement business diminished and faded so that there is nothing now to attract the public in the manner of former days. 

SPORTS 

It has been said that the first game of Lawn Tennis played in the United States was at Nahant. James Dwight participated in this sport in 1874 and eventually became a national champion. 

Later a private court was maintained at the estate of Dr. Haven, at the corner of Nahant Road and Pond Street; and while this was occupied by Richard D. Sears, also a national champion, many world famous players appeared here in invitation Tournaments.

The National Championship matches were played for several years on the grass courts of the Nahant Club, then located on 

Ocean Street.

Many of the young men of the summer colony were identified with sports; Thomas P. Curtis, a hurdler was a member of the first U. S. team that took part in the revival of the Olympic games at Athens, Greece in 1896. Louis Curtis was one of the Harvard rowing crew that won at the Henlay regatta in England in 1914. 

Charles Devens was a member of the Harvard football team in 1932. Louis Curtis, Sr. was President of the U. S. Golf Association. 

Two town boys, George and Leo Lermond, spread the name of Nahant far and wide with their running. George was a U. S. National six mile champion and participated in the Olympic games at Paris, France in 1924. Leo took part in the Olympic games at 

Amsterdam, Holland in 1928 and at Los Angeles, California in 1932. He also won races in Ireland in 1928, and in 1929 became a 

European champion by winning the 1500 meter race at the International meet in Stockholm, Sweden.

EGG ROCK 

Egg Rock, one mile easterly from the town derives its name from the large number of sea gulls that laid their eggs there. It was

ceded by the town to the Federal Government in 1856 and a lighthouse built upon it, being superceded by a larger one in 1900. In 

1928 this lighthouse was abandoned and demolished. Various keepers came and went; one, George B. Taylor, settled in Nahant after his tour of duty and became the proprietor of the Bay View House, located at the corner of Willow Road and Summer Street. 

In later years he was a familiar figure as the Custodian of Tudor Wharf.

Another identified with the wharf and yachting was Capt. 

Charles F. Kemp who was the commander of the training ship "Governor Cobb" during the first World War.

GOING AND COMING 

Transportation by water to Boston in the summer was by steamboat. In 1817 the "Massachusetts" was used for excursions then the "Eagle" which ran on a regular schedule, consuming three hours each way. The "Nelly Baker" sailed from Nahant to 

Boston from 1854 to 1861 and in later days the "Nahant" built in 1881 with local capital. Later boats made stops at the Bass Point 

Wharf as well as the Town Wharf. At one time a line of boats ran from Boston to Lynn and another line to Point of Pines, both stopping at a wharf located at the harbor end of Wendell Road. 

BARGE BOARD 

Transportation before 1900

The passenger depot used at Wendell Road from 1866 to 1878 now forms a dwelling at 100 Castle Road. A line of smaller boats ran from a wharf at the Relay House to Lynn from 1894 - 1916. 

The first public transportation to Lynn appears to have been started in 1817 and operated more or less successfully until 1847 when the establishment of the Post Office forced a more regular means of transportation. In 1868 Edmund B. Johnson, son of 

Joseph, started a barge line which he later sold to George W. 

Kibbey. Byron Goodell of Peabody also started a barge line in 

1868. The "White Line" of Goodell, the "Red Line" of Kibbey and the Carahar Line from Bass Point passed out of existence with the appearance of trolley cars in 1905. Trolley cars became non-existent in 1930, and since that time busses have been the chief means of public transportation.

GROWING 

In 1853 there were three hundred residents in the town, thirty of whom were voters, sixty-eight dwellings and one schoolhouse with forty-eight pupils. In 1953 there are two thousand seven hundred inhabitants, one-half of whom are voters, one thousand dwellings, two schoolhouses, with another one pending, a school enrollment of five hundred and twenty-five with seventy-five others going to high schools in Lynn and elsewhere. During the past decade, the increase in the number of people, houses and school enrollment has been tremendous owing to many new people making their homes in Nahant. Some of these people are being frequently changed, likewise the schools are being affected by changing attendance. In the early days every pupil was a permanent resident of the town. In later years there have been pupils from several states registered during one year. Many teachers have come and gone, and it has been difficult to maintain a high standard of scholarship at times, because of the turnover of staff and crowded conditions. A veteran in the list of teachers is Miss 

Olive R. Grover, who was identified with the schools almost continuously from 1907 until her retirement in 1952. 

The Public Library was first formed in 1819 through a gift of William Wood. It was housed in different buildings, including schoolhouses and the Town Hall until the present building was erected in 1894. It now houses nearly fifty thousand volumes. 

Librarians were many, some were the principals of the schools. In 

1892 the first full time attendant became employed. In 1899, Miss 

May W. Perkins became librarian serving until her retirement in 1941.

Among some of the various features that contributed to the interest of living in Nahant was that of the Post Office. First established in 1847 it was housed in the Nahant Hotel during the summer months. That fall it was moved to the Johnson store and until 1902 it was quartered in several stores, as political fortune made the proprietor a postmaster, and in Whitney's Hotel. It was then ranked as a fourth class post office. In 1902 it was moved to its present location and became a sub-station of Lynn; mail delivery started at the same time. Miss Mary T. Cusick served as clerk and Superintendent for nearly half a century, retiring in 1946.

The first Town Hall was built by George U. Perkins in 1868, and had an unique existence. Not only was it the scene of community activities, entertainments and town meetings; but it held several of the town offices, the Police Court and jail, the central station of the Fire Department and was the storage place for everything owned by the town that could not be placed elsewhere. 

The new Town Hall was built in 1912. 

The police force for many years was made up of temporary, intermittent officers until 1878, when Frank G. Phillips was appointed the first regular member, afterwards becoming the first Chief of Police. He retired in 1904 and was succeeded by Thomas 

H. Larkin who had been a "bicycle cop," intercepting speedsters on the Long Beach since 1896, and who retired in 1938 after fortytwo years of service.

The Fire Department began with the hand-drawn, hand operated pumper “Eagle” in 1843. Another larger one called the "Dexter" was purchased in 1871. These two machines were often seen at musters and frequently won prizes. More apparatus was acquired as the need appeared; the hand-drawn carts were succeeded by horse-drawn wagons, and the first piece of motorized 1843 1821 A 

First Fire Engine

kit appeared in 1911. After being housed in several scattered buildings, the department is now quartered in a Central Fire Station and is operated on an up to date and effective basis. Frank 

B. Crocker was Chief Engineer for many years, retiring in 1919.

Nahant enjoyed great prosperity for several years. The building of new houses produced plenty of work. At one time there were more than one hundred building tradesmen living in the town.

The principal industries of the town were hand shoemaking and fishing. Footwear was made in kitchens and in a number of small shops built for the purpose. Several fishing schooners were owned by the Johnsons and the Goves. Worthen Gove was the first of that family coming to the town in the days of the Nahant Hotel, for which he commanded a sailing boat used for the entertainment of the guests. Charles E. Gove, a son, conducted a fish market at the junction of Ocean Street, Willow and Emerald

Roads for many years. This was the "Country Store" of the 18th 

Century; and its proprietor became famous for his naive wit and homely expressions. He often appeared before legislative committees on fisheries at the State House and gained the reputation of being "the man that looks like Abraham Lincoln.”

In times of strife the people of Nahant contributed their full share of men and money for the protection of the Country. The prominence of the inhabitants and their generosity at the time of the Civil War became known to the officials at the National Capital and a ship of the Navy was named "Nahant" in honor of the Town.

PERSPECTIVE 

When Mark Twain wrote "New England Life is filled with variety and vicissitude," he might have had Nahant in mind for both of these attributes have been present in generous measure from the beginning.

In the past three and one half centuries the headlands of Nahant, originally a cluster of islands, have been successively, a primeval wilderness hunted over by the red men, a pioneer settlement of white men, a watering place with the ambition to rival Newport and Saratoga, then a place for wealthy summer residents with an amusement resort at the side, and, since the first World War it has been gradually changing into a residential suburb.

The Town has had great and wonderful days, filled with pomp and grandeur. Nature has done everything for it; man has done much, both for and against it. 

The people of Nahant inherit an illustrious and glorious past, and today look forward to the coming years with the hope of a correspondingly worthy and majestic future.
