New Orleans

My Christmas Journey 

There is so much in the papers now about Trieste, it recalls my very brief, but very happy, stay there. 

To explain how I happened to have ever been there at all, I must begin way back by telling you that my daughter, Maridel, and another New Orleans girl, Ethel Jane Westfeldt, were taking a postgraduate course at Christ Church College in Oxford, England. They planned to go on their eight week Christmas Holiday to Egypt and Palestine, and had "Cook's Tours", London, to arrange their itinerary for them. When they wrote us of this, we supposed, of course, they were going with a larger party, and we were amazed to learn later that these two venturesome young ladies proposed going alone. 

So my husband gave me a seven hour notice to get ready and leave for Egypt, or else he would have the lady with whom they boarded in Oxford to go and chaperone them. 

Needless to say, I accepted the challenge and was on my way to Egypt—which I had never dreamed of seeing—in just seven hours. My daughter secured tickets and reservations for me too before leaving London, and I connected up with the girls in Paris, just a few hours before we left for Marseilles, from where we sailed for Port Said. 

My husband issued another ultimatum about our trip.

The girls had intended "by-passing" Greece entirely, which he considered almost a personal slight to him, as he was formerly a Professor of Greek and had studied in Greece under the famous archeologist, Dr. Doepfeldt, and he thought Athens the most interesting city in the world. So to Greece we had to go, and that change in our itinerary was the remote cause of our stop in Trieste. 3

Cairo- Now back to Port Said, from where we proceeded according to schedule, to Cairo. And what a wonderful time we had in Cairo! We saw not only what all tourists see there, but enjoyed the special privileges and pleasures that only those who have "a friend at court" can have. Our good friend was Judge Crabites of New Orleans who was a member of the "International Exchange Tribunal" in

Cairo-and a cousin of Ethel Jane's. We met many of his delightful friends at a dinner dance at the world-famous Shepherd Hotel. We saw interesting excavations made by some Harvard students in archeology for the National Museum in Cairo. The Judge introduced the girls to these boys and they took them down into the tomb of the "Three Graces" which they had excavated that day. I preferred looking in from the outside. And I must confess I was rather shocked at the lack of respect with which the boys handled the "Graces". One of them quite nonchalantly extracted a tooth from one of the old girls. This "operation crossroad" was out quite near the Giza Pyramids and, of course, the girls had to climb up. 

All these things were wonderful, but none of them did we enjoy more than just "poking" around the shops in the old Muski quarters where since the days of Saladen the Frank merchants have been allowed to live and trade. 

The streets there are narrow and filthy and thick with flies that caused the little children who played there to have fearful looking sore eyes, but the little shops opening into these streets were filled with fascinating things we could not resist, and some of the principal European shops were still to be found there, because that is where the tourists flock.

We bought and bought till we had to buy a huge shopping bag woven by the women down there, into which we put our purchases. Besides this, Ethel Jane bought a huge copper kettle in which she carried her breakable articles. 

Then reluctantly we checked all these at the Shepherd Hotel in Cairo and started out on the second part of our journey to • · 

Palestine- 

My most interesting Christmas was spent in Palestine -the birthplace of all Christmases. My daughter, her roommate, Ethel Jane, and a dear friend of ours, Mrs.

Catesby Jones, from New Orleans, and I had just spent two such delightful weeks in Cairo it was hard for us to tear ourselves away, even for going to Jerusalem (a game 

I had greatly enjoyed when a child), Ethel Jane refusing to go with us as she had made a previous trip there. But 

Cooks, in London, had arranged our itinerary that way. 

So two days before Christmas we began our trip to the

Holyland. We crossed the Suez Canal (which seemed to me a very insignificant little thing for the Nations of the World to make such a fuss about) and there took a sleeper for Jerusalem. 

Over there, as here, the conductor on the train collected your ticket on entering the car, and kept it till just before you left it, when he gave you the stub as a passport for your exit. Just before we were to get off for Jerusalem, the conductor came reeling in our car holding only two stubs in his hand, sputtering wildly, in Arabic, that one of us had gotten on the train without a ticket. Of course, we had not the remotest idea what he was saying. For- 5

tunately for us, there were two English Army Officers sitting in the coach with us, and the elder one said: "Maybe I can help you ladies out in this". I told him we would be most grateful to have him do so. Then he told the old fellow he was a drunken fool and had lost one of the tickets himself. How could three people get on the train with only two tickets? And he had better get us another exit slip, pronto, or else he would be reported. This sobered him down like magic and he produced another exit slip immediately. This was the beginning of one of the most delightful friendships of our lives. year serv The young officer who had just finished a five ice in Egypt seemed delighted to meet a pretty American girl, and the older officer seemed quite as happy to meet the charming young widow who was with us.

When our Cooks' courier came to meet us, the Colonel asked him to what hotel he was taking us, and when he said "The Allenby”, he asked him why should we be taken to that dirty old place, why not to the new "Palace Hotel". 

Our courier, Saint John the Baptist-Little Jasmine Flower, by name, replied it was not completed and he could not get us in there. The Colonel said: "Come with me, I will get them in" and so he did.

After we were settled down there, he said: "We are two lonesome old bachelors, who are out for a pleasure trip and it would be so much pleasanter if you ladies would allow us to tag along with you". Needless to say we accepted their delightful company with joy. And they went everywhere we did as long as their leave lasted.

Bethlehem- 

Our first trip was to Bethlehem, that night, to see the beautiful Christmas Eve celebration, in the "Church of the

Nativity". For that matter, the whole village was celebrating the anniversary of the greatest event in the history of the world.

It was a cold, crisp night and the stars stood out like beacon lights, and while we were driving out we wondered which one it was that guided the shepherds to the manger where lay the infant Christ. The "Church of the Nativity" is supposed to be built around the stall where Christ was born. In one corner of the church there is a creche representing that stall in the manger. There are beautiful lifelike figures of the divine Mother and Child on a bed of straw. And kneeling before them are the Wise Men who came bearing gifts of gold, incense and myrrh. And in the background stood Joseph, and outside the stall stood some gentle looking cows which made it all look very real. 

The religious ceremony lasted till two a.m., but we only till Midnight. It had been a long, exciting day, and Christmas Day was to follow tomorrow. 

Christmas Day we drove out to Jericho-where the walls came tumbling down. On the way there we passed by the Dead Sea and St. John (as we called our courier) told us that many of his tourists had greatly enjoyed taking a bath in the "Dead Sea" which was so thick with salt you can almost stand on it, and as we were out not to miss a trick, of course we stopped and went in. And it really was fun. When you were lying flat on the water it was almost impossible to stick your feet down. Then on to

Jericho,-through country almost filled with orange orchards. I had always thought the best oranges in the world were in Sicily and Louisiana until I ate those in Jericho. 

We had a delicious dinner at one of St. John's favorite hotels and then we wandered around the town to see the things of interest there. One amusing thing that attracted our attention specially was a group of long bearded old men sitting around in a circle, smoking long stemmed old bubble pipes. I guess it would be dangerous to have the fire any nearer their long beards, but their arms could hardly reach the bowls of the pipes. 

Our return trip to Jerusalem was over a different route, and it was a thrillingly interesting one. We passed over the route General Allenby had taken in his Conquest of Jerusalem. And our Colonel had served under General Allenby in this campaign and he described every action taking place all along the way and it was over the most barren, rockiest terrain any army ever had to pass over. He told us about how General Allenby had planned his campaign from reading in the Bible of Joshua's military tactics as given to him by orders from Jehovah. One night General 

Allenby could not sleep, he was so depressed by the terrible responsibility of this engagement, and, remembering that Joshua had traveled that same way, he got up from his bed and read those divine commands and decided to obey them too. And that led to his successful entry into Jerusalem, as it had to Joshua's, centuries ago. 

And our St. John had us to stop by the place where 

Mary and Martha, whom Christ loved, had lived. And showed us the spot where Christ had raised Lazarus from the dead. St. John was such a devout Christian and his

faith so implicit all of this was indeed and in truth holy ground to him. And he inspired us with such reverence we felt Christ's spirit was hovering over the place. 

St. John was a Christian till he spied a Jew-the long bearded, long robed, greasy headed kind we met there, and then his heart was filled with hatred, and he had to turn and spit to express his disgust. I asked him why the Arabs despised the Jews so bitterly. He said: "Suppose you asked a stranger into your home, for awhile to help him out, and after a long time he told you to get out, that he liked your home and wanted to keep it for himself and family. Would you like that?"

When we returned to our hotel that night we were greatly surprised to see there was to be a dinner dance there to celebrate the opening of the "Palace Hotel" which really was not more than half finished. But they had a longtime contract to accommodate a hundred and fifty tourists, who were to arrive Christmas Day, and they had to open part of the house. My room was the only one occupied by our party that had any heat in it, so it was the rendezvous for our little cocktail parties and all other social gatherings which made them more informal and gayer. 

And I assure you we appreciated heat after spending two weeks in a Cairo hotel and visiting in homes which were supposedly heated by a few burning embers in a brass brazier in the middle of rooms which were really frigid. 

I had read and sung of "Egypt's burning sands" and had taken my fur coat along only because I had no place to leave it, and I slept under it every night in Cairo and in it and under eight blankets the nights we spent in a de luxe Arabian tent out in the Sahara Desert, and I believe I would have frozen without it.

So when I struck a warm spot in Jerusalem, I declined an invitation to go sightseeing my first afternoon there.

Instead, I spent it sitting on a radiator, and was so completely thawed out that I regained my voice which had been only a whisper for several days. When our newly found friends returned that night, they were amazed to hear I had a voice and thought a miracle had been performed in that Holy Land. That was true, but performed only by a "hot-seat". Please pardon this silly digression and let us get back to the dinner dance. 

It was great fun-Maridel and the young lieutenant had all the dances together except one courtesy dance with the chaperone and one with the widow, and the Colonel's and widow's program was about the same. But the chaperone loved looking on such an interesting event in such an interesting place. The following days were filled with carefully planned trips to interesting places. Of course, St.

John made us see Jerusalem quite thoroughly as it is undoubtedly one of the most interesting cities in the world. 

It is a city of hills, surrounded by walls with streets that are only narrow lanes. They are arched over here and there and the bazaars and portions of the "Via Dolorosa" are entirely covered. The Encyclopedia Britannica tells us that viewed from the Mount of Olives the most conspicuous object is the Haram enclosure, with the Dome of the Rock in the center, and the Aska Mosque extending to the south wall. Arcades with painted arches stand on the flight of the steps leading to the platform surrounding the Dome of the Rock. Here are the Tower of David and three minarets, and near are seen the two domes of the rotunda and choir of the Holy Sepulchre. 10

St. John took us to the room where "The Last Supper" was held. There are far too many wonderful places there for me to try to mention, or to remember. But there are some I can never forget, but are, to me, too sacred to mention.

Others are not, for instance "The Wailing Wall" which we insisted on St. John showing us. Of course, he turned his back while we saw and heard the wailers.

Never in life have I seen anything more exquisite than the stained glass windows in the Aska Mosque. If they had been made of diamonds, rubies, emeralds and sapphires, designed by the world's greatest artist, they could not be more lovely.

Later he took us to Nazareth, to see Christ's childhood home and Joseph's carpenter shop, and to places where He had performed many of His miracles. And we went to the 

Garden of Gethsemane and to Golgotha where He was crucified, and to the Tomb of Joseph of Armathea where He lay three days before the glorious resurrection morn. All this was soul stirring and we seemed to live over all the lessons we had learned in Sunday school when little children. I spoke of how distinctly St. John pronounced all those Biblical names. Maridel said: "That's because you knew already what he was talking about. Mona and I haven't understood half of what he said". I replied I feared 

I hadn't made my little girl as good a Sunday school pupil as my parents had made me. 11

Damascus- From Damascus to the Hills of Lebanon was more than a Sabbath day's journey in Biblical times. It is less than half a Sabbath day's journey in automobile time. And there was to be no Sabbath day journey on Cook's tours itinerary. But we were tired of Damascus because our hotel was cold and dirty and the food abominable-though St. 

John assured us it was the best to be had in Damascus. So we asked him to see if he could not get us out of there at once, and as he, St. as he, St. John the Baptist-Little Jasmine

Flower, was a very important man and had served Cook's Tours forty years, he pulled some wires and managed to do so. But before leaving Damascus so precipitately, let me mention a few of the highlights of the place which we had seen.

Our traveling companion, Mrs. Catesby Jones, a friend. of ours from New Orleans, was a very intelligent tourist and had read up and digested all important facts about a place before we reached it. And she began demanding, as soon as we reached Damascus, that we go see "The Street Called Straight" that Saul was to take to the house of Annanais (not the liar, but a devout Christian) who would loosen his tongue and explain the vision that had struck Saul down and made him dumb on the road to Damascus, and made him stop persecuting the Christians and begin working with them, and was to be persecuted to death himself for the cause of Christianity. Not only his life but his name was changed-from Saul of Tarsus to Paul. So that was the first place of interest we saw in Damascus. 

Next, St. John took us along that wonderful glass covered street with little shops filled to overflowing with beau- 12

tiful rugs and other works of art. He took us to the home of one of his friends who was a rug dealer and collector who owned the most beautiful and valuable rugs in the Orient. Many were far too fine to put on the floor but decorated his walls like priceless tapestries. 

But to me, far more interesting than that was our visit to the home of an Armenian family who had found sanctuary in Damascus during the persecution of their people by the Turks, in a recent massacre. This man, his wife and a three-and-a-half year old child were all weaving on a beautiful rug. The little girl-almost a baby-was sitting on a low stool and the different colored flosses were hung on a frame by her side. And her little fingers were flying in and out the rug as rapidly as her mother's were and her eye was as accurate in selecting the right colors. 

It was almost unbelievable. The tall father was working on the top of the rug, the wife on the center and the little girl near the bottom. Strong white strings were strung across the frame for the foundation of the rug for the colored flosses to be tied into. A picture of the finished rug stood on the side of the frame as a pattern to be followed. I have never thought the price of an Oriental rug high since I saw that one being made. 

Then St. John took us to a factory where brass, bronze and silver trays and vases were being made. An old man was punching designs in the unfinished brass pieces, which were then turned over to men, women and children of all ages, from the cradle to the grave, to have silver and bronze hammered into the punctures. The finished articles were beautiful. I have a tray which I bought there which I greatly prize. But it makes my heart ache to think of those 13

pitiful people who made only twenty-five cents a day for such hard work.

St. John told us the streets of Damascus are so dirty every one there would die of the plague if the heat, which often mounts to 120 degrees on the glass covered streets, did not act as a sterilizer. That is one reason we were so anxious to be on our way to Baal-beck. Lebanon- 

Never shall I forget the beauty of that drive up there between the Hills of Lebanon. They are very high hills— 

I don't know why they are not called mountains. The road lay in a valley between these "Hills" which were covered with snow. But the setting sun made one side rosy pink and the other being snowy white it was the most beautiful picture imaginable. But the thing that greatly surprised me was there was not a cedar to be seen in all of Lebanon. As wise as he was, King Solomon must not have believed in reforestation, or when he was having all those temples, palaces and things built of the Cedars of Lebanon he would have had more planted and that part of the world would not be so treeless now Baal-Beck Our room in the hotel was supposed to be heated by a little wood burning stove. They brought us up two little bundles of roots of some kind (they didn't smell like cedar) tied up with a little white string, for which we paid an extra dollar. Maridel was self-appointed custodian of the stove. Those two bundles were soon consumed with no appreciable effect on the temperature of the room, so she 14

went down and bought two more little bundles of sticks which did little more about warming us up. So I suggested that we take a hot bath and go to bed to get warm. We had paid two dollars extra for a room with a bath. Try to imagine our reaction when we discovered a solid cake of ice, two feet thick, in the tub. Even now it makes my teeth chatter to think of such a bath. Needless to say, we went to bed without a bath, but we did have plenty of warm cover so we thawed out by morning, but the ice in the tub did not.

After a nice, warm breakfast the next morning, St. 

John took us over to see the world famous ruins of the Temples of Baal-beck. He gave us a very learned dissertation on their history. But there was much too much history, archeology and mythology, all mixed up in it together for my feeble mind to digest then, or my poor memory to recall now, but I will quote a few facts I find in the Encyclopedia Britannica: "These temples were sacred to Jupiter and Bacchus and were built in the Roman period. But some authorities think that the immensity of the stones and magnificent columns indicate that they were built by some primeval race of giant builders. But they are more imposing for their immensity than pleasing detail". (To which 

I agreed.) "It has been suggested that the name Baal-beck was the Baal-gad spoken of in the 16th Chapter, 17th verse of Joshua. Others say it was derived from the greater temple, sacred to Jupiter (Baal) identified with the Sun and to the lesser temple sacred to Bacchus". (Maybe hiccuped into "Beck".) "But whoever they were sacred to, they were fought over for many centuries, and belonged to-at different times -such conquerors as Ghenghis Kahn and Saladen and the 15

Crusaders raided its valleys more than once but never took the city." ways 

St. John had warm friends wherever he went and altook us to see them at their shops, and we never left the shops without having bought some of their wares (maybe that is why they were such "warm" friends). There they made the most beautiful hand embroidered robes.

Maridel bought a green and I a red one, but there too we felt desperately sorry for the poor women who did that lovely needle work for twenty-five cents a day. I asked

St. John how they could live on that. He said they lived on leek soup and had meat once a week. The C.I.O. should try to unionize over there instead of in the Deep South. Luxor- After returning to Egypt from Palestine Ethel Jane joined us and we went down to Luxor and spent several days at that delightful Palace Hotel. It is simply charming! And one of the most fascinating things you do there is to have tea on that lovely terrace and watch the sun suddenly drop from the sky over beyond the Nile and "The 

Valley of the Kings". In Rudyard Kipling's "Road to 

Mandalay” he wrote "The sun comes up like thunder out of China 'cross the bay". That is just the way it goes down across the Nile, beyond the "Valley of the Kings". The sky there is the bluest I ever saw, and there is never a cloud in it "the size of a man's hand". We had a delightful experience there. Our little "foursome" were playing bridge in the lounge of the hotel the first night there and suddenly a man appeared and said: "Maridel Saunders, where on earth did you come from?" 16

They had been guests in a house party at the home of a mutual friend in Alpina, Michigan. He, too, was a Harvard student doing some excavations in the "Valley of the Kings". He conducted our party on a very interesting sightseeing trip over there. We even got a peep into the entrance of the famous tomb of Tut-an-Kamen. It was closed to the public then.

We took a boat trip on the Nile in one of those little boats with sails so enormous they kept the boat lying on one side or the other (and me scampering from one side to the other for ballast) nearly all the time. The little boys there are as great beggars as in Italy. At the Assuan dam we met a cute little beggar who knew some English and when thrown a penny he would say "Thank-yer-Man, Lady man, Gentle man". We had an exciting boat race on the lake back of the dam and the boatmen were chanting a prayer to Mohammed to let them win (a custom of theirs) but Mohammed let the best boatman win the race.

I chant a paraphrase of the song "Now I am too old to dream I have that trip to remember". And such silly things as these I remember instead of all the ancient history I learned there. But you can get a history of Egypt and read all that yourself.

From Luxor we returned to Cairo, packed up our various purchases in Cairo and Palestine, including Ethel Jane's copper kettle, filled to overflowing, and started on our journey to Alexandria to take ship for the Piraeus port of Athens. The friendly gate keeper at the station in Cairo didn't require us to show our tickets but laughed and just waved us through. All this was very pleasant then but caused a minor "Bulgarian Incident" after we left Athens for Paris. 17

Athens I certainly am glad my husband insisted on our including Greece in our itinerary-otherwise we would have missed all of its beauty and grandeur. Though a small country, it has such a variety of scenery with its mountains, bays and islands, its natural beauty is said to excel any country in Europe in that respect-just as Europe excels any other country in the world. 

No part of Greece is more than forty miles from the coast, or ten miles from hills. Though my father was a learned Greek scholar and my husband a professor of

Greek, it was only what meets the eye that I could appreciate. But there was an abundance of that in all the magnificent old temples and other ruins made beautiful by their classic marble columns and magnificent proportions. the Parthenon is conceded to be the most perfect architectural structure in the world. The open air theatre in Athens, where the famous Greek tragedies and comedies, such as those of Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes were played, was dug out of a hillside of stone. And the 

Stadium where athletic contests, like discus throwing and others that made up the famous Penthalion occurred, had seats carved out of marble also. suppose

We had a very highly educated courier who conducted us not only over Athens but other parts of Greece and let us see the things and places we should see. The only thing to mar the perfection of our tour was that we had the most reckless driver I have ever seen-I implored our courier to make him go slower. He only laughed, shrugged his shoulders and speeded up. As we began our drive at 5 a.m. one morning at the foot of our first hill was a completely 18

wrecked car, and by it a man's hat filled with blood-a most disconcerting picture to begin a tour with. 

Greek people are said to be the most eager for learning and greatest lovers of adventure of any other race.

Well-this was clearly demonstrated in our courier and driver of that day. They also love the dramatic and spectacular. Their policemen look almost exactly like Russian ballet dancers-with all their stiff, short white skirts and long white knitted stockings, waist high, and long white knitted caps, with a tassle down to the shoulder. 

Girls are allowed to marry only three times there. But

I don't see how they could marry more times, since they must always bring a dowry. Many unmarried girls wear gold pieces on headdresses to advertise their dowry—as it were.

I was especially anxious to go to Mount Hymettus on this trip to get some honey like my husband had brought home from there when a student. Not that I expected it to convert me into a poetess, as it is supposed to do, but for old time's sake. 

At "Ole Miss" Paul always gave a dinner to his graduating class in Greek. On the next one we served hot biscuits with Hymettus honey as the "pièce de résistance”. 

While this one was in progress, the Junior class built a bonfire of the "Jacks" (translations) of the seniors calling each by name and adding one for "Pete" (what they called Paul) on the campus in front of our house, and in a circle marched around it chanting the "Frog chorus" from Aristophanes.

I still have the program of that "Euripidean Symposium" but now all such as that is indeed "ancient Greek" to us. 19

As a coming home present to my husband, after our trip, I brought a silver loving cup which was a copy of a beautiful alabaster one that was taken from the tomb of a King who was buried at Mycene 2,000 years B.C. and was one of the earliest of modern excavations. The original cup is now in the Athens Museum. Paul was quite pleased with the gift but suspected me of wanting a beautiful, new centerpiece for my table. "Beware of the Greeks, bearing gifts".

Agamemnon, also, was buried quite near this king's tomb-Maridel bought a funny wooden-dog clock as à souvenir from there and named it "Agamemnon". His eyes roll around to point the time. But he ticks so loudly he is not allowed to run.

We have other souvenirs from Greece which I treasure but I need nothing to prevent my forgetting that wonderful trip to that "Birthplace of philosophy, literature and art".

Trieste- The annoying experience I mentioned earlier in this story occurred on the border of Bulgaria, on our return trip from Athens to Paris. I had been sleeping sweetly for hours when I was awakened by wild-eyed Ethel Jane, in her pajamas, and with her long hair flowing, followed by four or five wild looking men, with long beards flowing, gesticulating wildly. Ethel Jane said: "They say we cannot go through". Drowsily I answered: "Through what?" "Through Bulgaria". I said: "They cannot throw us out in the middle of the night, in our night clothes, in this snow covered country". She said: "They say we can pay". 20

Well, that was another serious problem. When leaving a country, we carried with us as little as possible of that currency, so we had almost no Greek money. But as we were to reach Paris Saturday morning and I wanted to do some shopping that day and feared the banks might be closed, as they are here, I had bought several hundred francs and that amount was exactly what it required to get us on our way. It seems that holding people up on the border is an old Bulgarian custom.

While we were trying to find out why we could not "go through" Bulgaria, a kind American man came out into the passageway to see what the excitement was about, and to offer help to his fellow countrymen in distress, if needed. He offered to lend us money, saying he too had been held up there on a former trip without enough money. 

He was so very kind I stopped by his seat the next day to tell him how much we appreciated his proffered help and was quite surprised to learn he was traveling salesman for refrigerators in Russia—of all places! I had always thought of Russia as the snow covered country like pictures of Siberia I had seen in movies. I was just amazed to learn he did a large and thriving business selling refrigerators in that frozen region of the north. 

Last week I was speakng with some friends of ours here about this experience and learned they, too, had been thus annoyed. But he is a man meticulously right about everything, so didn't have to pay anything and was not delayed long. But this confirmed my opinion that trying to hold up people on the border is an old Bulgarian custom. 

The next serious question to be solved was how we could eat the next day. But by pooling the odds and ends 21

of our coins we had left we calculated we could buy one meal. So we decided to sleep till lunch, and eat enough then to last us till we reached Paris the next day. The lunch was a "table d'hote" affair and very abundant. So after satisfying our hunger, I slyly slipped the remaining food in my very commodious purse-which would then not close, and when getting up from the table, I dropped it, spilling rolls, cakes, cheese and nuts in every direction. In all my life I have never been more embarrassed or more amused. The two girls, convulsed with laughter, were on the floor in a flash, retrieving our lost food. When eating it for supper that night we were so full of laughter we almost choked.

On the train Maridel and I occupied one of the compartments that could be connected with another-which 

Ethel Jane shared with a very charming Athenian lady who was going to Paris for a visit. She was so beautiful she could have been the model for one of the famous old Greek artists, so, needless to say, she had many friends and admirers. They showered her with flowers, fruits and candies, and even cookies, as if she were starting off on a long ocean voyage, instead of only a short trip to Paris. When she heard of our sad plight, she insisted on sharing her "goodies" with us.

The nice conductor on the train deeply sympathized with us, and said he would hold the train at Belgrade long enough for Ethel Jane-who spoke French like a Parisian -to phone the Cook's agent there to bring us money and validate our tickets for us. We could not wait quite long enough for that there, but they telephoned the Cook's agency at Trieste to meet us at the train and straighten things out for us. 22

No shining angel from Heaven could have looked lovelier to us than that man from Cook's who reimbursed me for the extra tickets we had to buy and validated the ones the Cairo agent failed to have us validate. 

So from Trieste we proceeded joyfully on our wayto "Gay Par-ee".

The next day we spent shopping furiously in Paris. 

Each of us wanted a new spring outfit to take home from that fashionable mart. On her way to Egypt Ethel Jane had had a first fitting of a dress that was to be finished while she was away and picked up on her return to Paris. 

So she dashed over to get it and found to her dismay that the twelve pounds she had gained in her absence refused to be buttoned into the previously fitted garment. So seams had to be ripped open and other changes made for her to get into it. I could just see the huge bowls of rice covered with chutney she was so fond of eating sticking out in every direction. So the poor kid had to begin dieting to regain her nice, slim girlish figure. 

Maridel came to grief about her Parisian dress too. It never occurred to her to declare it on her customs slip when she returned to London, as she had gone there with a trunk full of new clothes the previous summer without having to pay a cent of duty. But this time the customs officer assessed the fine and duty on the price of the dress. So she told him he could keep it—which he did. It seems that as she had been in England several months she was bringing in foreign goods bought as an English citizen (or some such foolishness). Later they wrote her they had discovered she had not been there quite long enough to be covered by that law and could get the dress for only $20.00. She told 23

Homeward Bound- 

I spent a few pleasant, but busy, days in England. I saw my two young charges safely back to their pleasant quarters in the home of Canon Cook in Christ Church College, Oxford. They lived in a house which was built and occupied by Cardinal Woolsey while he was having Christ Church College built. 

Then, as fast as the old Mauretania could

carry me, hurried back to "The City Care Forgot" and reached there at its most carefree period-in ample time for Mardi Gras. 

This season was an especially interesting one which I could not miss as my husband was REX and reigned as "King for a Day".
