RECOMMENDATIONS SUBSEQUENT INJURIES FUND 

Subsequent Injuries Fund laws have been enacted to accomplish three primary purposes: (a) to encourage industry to employ handicapped workers in order that these workers might continue in gainful employment, sustain themselves and their families, restore their morale and thereby enable them to retain their self-respect; (b) to retain to industry the skills and experience of the handicapped workers; and (c) to accomplish all this without burdening employers with liability for compensation benefits based upon disability resulting from the combined effect of a previous disability and a subsequent injury. 

In drafting a measure of this kind due regard must be given to the benefits provided for physically and mentally impaired persons under other welfare programs. To guard against any overlapping of programs, the great majority of subsequent injury fund laws enumerate the particular disabilities which must have been suffered before occurrence of a compensable injury. Where previous disabilities are not listed in the laws there are limitations of one kind or another, such, for example, as previous disability must be due to bodily injury; or must have resulted from an industrial injury; or must be due to injury other than disease; or if disability is due to disease then the disability must be from one of the occupational diseases covered under the Workmen's 

Compensation law; or if disability is due to disease, excluded or disabilities from aggravation or acceleration by a subsequent injury of pre-existing noncompensable diseases or conditions; or some provision for exclusion of disability from congenital anomaly or natural degenerative processes; or, as in Wisconsin where the previous injury must be such that, had the disability resulted from a compensable injury, the employee would have been entitled to indemnity for 250 weeks. 

As pointed out elsewhere in the report of this committee, initially 

(1945) our California enactment (L. C. Section 4751) provided for the payment of benefits from this special fund in those cases where an employee previously disabled by reason of the loss of, or loss of use of, a hand, an arm, a foot, a leg, or an eye, received a subsequent compensable injury and the combined effect of the last injury and the previous disability or impairment resulted in permanent disability equal to 70 percent or more of permanent total disability. Amended in 

1949, these specifically described disabilities were deleted and in lieu the law now provides only that the employee have some previous partial disability.

Your committee agrees, unanimously and unequivocally, that so far as is practicable obstacles to the employment of physically impaired persons should be removed and that, to this end, employers should be encouraged to avail themselves of the experience and skills of such persons. But your committee is conscious of the fact that only to a very limited degree can any feasible subsequent injuries law serve to encourage employment of physically handicapped workers. It has been conclusively demonstrated that to have the provision for pre-existing diseases apply to more than certain easily recognizable permanent objective disabilities converts the Subsequent Injuries Law into a health insurance plan and in addition thereto, provides life annuity benefits when the combined effect of a previous disability and a subsequent injury results in a permanent disability rating 70 percent or more of total disability.

OCCUPATIONAL DISEASE Under the California elective form of Workmen's Compensation Law (Roseberry Act-Chapter 399, Laws of 1911) effective September 1, 1911 and under the compulsory form of law (Boynton Act-Chapter 

176, Laws of 1913), effective January 1, 1914, benefits were payable for disability or death from "accident." By legislation (Chapter 607, Laws of 1915) effective August 8, 1915 the term "accident" was changed to

WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION "injury." And with enactment of the Workmen's Compensation Insurance and Safety Act (Chapter 586, Laws of 1917), effective January 1, 1918, it was provided by Section 3 (4) that "The term 'injury' as used in this act, shall include any injury or disease arising out of the employment." Through minor amendments (1949, 1951, 1953) this section, recodified as Section 3208, Division 4 of the Labor Code now reads: 

"'Injury' includes any injury or disease arising out of the employment, including injuries to artificial members, dentures, eyeglasses and medical braces of all types; provided, however, that eyeglasses will not be replaced, repaired, or otherwise compensated for, unless injury to them is incident to an injury causing disability.

At the time of this extension in the scope of the law, no statutory definition was provided for the term "disease; nor has any such definition been enacted during the intervening years. In the circumstances, the meaning of the ambiguous term "disease" and rules applicable to various associated problems have, of necessity, been decided by the Industrial Accident Commission and the appellate courts. With changes occurring from time to time in the personnel of the commission, with decisions of the commission being affirmed or reversed by different courts and with decisions governed by the changing legal, economic and social philosophies of these administrative and judicial bodies, there has been a gradual extension in the scope of the law by these extra-law making agencies with resultant confusion and steadily increasing public dissatisfaction. Particularly is this true in respect to cases of disability involving heart conditions, other natural degenerative processes and pulmonary dust diseases.

In the opinion of your committee, additional determinations by these extra-law making agencies can only serve to compound an already serious state of affairs. Besides, your committee holds that it is the function of the Legislature rather than of the commission or courts to correct deficiencies in the law and/or broaden the scope of the law. 

Therefore, your committee recommends that certain fundamentally essential provisions be added to the Workmen's Compensation Law and, concurrently therewith, that certain existing provisions having application to cases arising from either accident or disease be amended for clarification and assurance of more equitable awards. 

Specifically, the committee recommends:

Definition of Occupational Disease 

осси- That the law be amended to include a definition for the term pational disease." Benefits in cases of disability due to nonindustrial illness are provided for under the Unemployment Disability Compensation Act with payment from funds raised for that express purpose. 

The need for a definition of industrially caused disease is required as some assurance that benefits for only disease of this latter class will be assessed under the Workmen's Compensation Law. 

Also, such definition is essential because testimony before the committee disclosed that through decisions of the commission and the courts, the Workmen's Compensation Law has extended coverage into the field of health insurance to the point where employers are adopting methods to protect themselves against what they consider to be an

WORK MEN'S COMPENSATION 

11 unjustifiable imposition upon industry. Evidence presented to this committee disclosed that more and more employers are requiring preemployment physical examination of prospective workers, or are adopting some other plan to screen workers who might have some physical impairment which, in the opinion of the employer, makes the applicant a potential compensation claimant. This tightness of employment requirements operates to deny employment to otherwise employable persons, impairs or destroys their chance for economic independence, their morale and their self-respect and operates to increase the number of persons on public relief.

Laws of some jurisdictions include a list of diseases and provide that those enumerated diseases, and only those diseases, shall be considered to be "occupational diseases." The lists of diseases vary in degree of comprehensiveness. This approach to the general problem serves to simplify administration but at the expense of complete justice to all workers because it is impossible to establish an exact boundary by a list of diseases.

In the circumstances, the committee recognizes that any definition must be expressed in general terms. But the committee is convinced of the imperative need for a statutory definition and directive for determining the compensability of disabilities asserted to be due to diseases arising out of and in the course of employment and recommends that the law be revised by addition of a section to provide, in effect, that "occupational disease" means a disease or infection which is due to causes and conditions which are characteristic of and peculiar to a particular trade, occupation, process, or employment; and that physical deterioration, and ordinary diseases of life to which the general public is exposed outside of the employment, shall not be compensable, except where competent medical evidence shows the physical condition or disease had its origin in a risk connected with the employment and flowed from that source as a natural consequence. 

Definition of Disability 

Although the Workmen's Compensation Law was extended in 1917 to provide benefits to workers who suffer disability from occupational disease, the vague term "disability" has not yet been defined in the law; and no generally acceptable definition has been established by the appellate courts.

Whether disability in compensable form or degree exists and, if so, upon what date the worker became so disabled are questions still left to be determined in each individual case by some one or more of many referees and commissioners acting without benefit of a uniform rule to govern their decision. As a natural result, decisions by the commission and the courts are confusing and the cause for widespread dissatisfaction.

To correct this glaring deficiency in the law, we recommend that the Labor Code be amended by the addition of a section to define "disability" as it applies to the state of being disabled from occupational disease.

WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION Date of Disability Section 5412 of the Labor Code reads: 

"The date of injury in cases of occupational diseases is that date upon which the employee first suffered disability therefrom and either knew, or in the exercise of reasonable diligence should have known, that said disability was caused by his present or prior employment."

This section purports to establish a date as the occasion when the worker first suffered disability but the term "disability" is not defined in the law. Besides, the section is based upon the fallacious concept that a worker having some illness (confining for a day or otherwise is not specified) should, if reasonably diligent, know from the inception of his illness whether the illness is of some nonindustrial kind or is an occupational disease and, if occupational, whether due to his present or prior employment.

Some occupational diseases such, for example, as silicosis and other pulmonary dust diseases-develop so gradually that a worker may be temporarily disabled periodically for short periods of time before even his doctor recognizes the exact nature of the illness. 

Therefore, this Section 5412 is tragically ineffective. It leaves the claim adjudicating officials without a statutory rule. Consequently, the date when the statute of limitations commences to run in progressive types of occupational diseases is decided according to the interpretation of this ambiguous section of the law by, or according to the philosophy of, the particular referee and/or commissioners by whom the case may be considered. This leads to conflicting decisions and accounts for the confusion about which complaints are serious and numerous. 

Obviously it is impossible to fix the date of disability from disease with the exactness of the date of injury by accident. It is equally obvious that development of a practical and equitable plan for establishing the date of disability can only result from a realistic approach to the problems involved.

Consideration of the testimony given before this and previous Senate committees and of the provisions enacted in the laws of other jurisdictions convinces us of the wisdom of the decision in the California case of Marsh v. Industrial Accident Commission (217 Cal. 338). In dealing with the question of when the statute of limitations commences to run in progressive occupational disease cases the court said: "An injury, then, may arise out of, and in the course of, the employment when there is a casual connection between the employment and the injury; but for purposes of compensation the injury dates from the time when the diseased condition culminates in an incapacity for work. It is at that time that the employer's liability becomes fixed; for until then the workman had received no injury in a legal sense, though the seeds productive of the injury lodged in his frame long before."

The committee is of the opinion that the deficiency in the law should be rectified by an amendment which will serve to define and to fix the date of disability in occupational disease cases.

SILICOSIS 

Previous Senate committees have reported at considerable length upon this subject and upon the serious problems relating thereto. The report filed by this committee on March 14, 1955, contains a transcript of testimony which reveals that immediate remedial legislation is imperative in the interest of both workers and employers. This last report also contains a digest of the special laws enacted in many other states in respect to such diseases as silicosis, asbestosis and other pulmonary dust diseases.

Silicosis is described in The Merck Manual, eighth edition, as "a fibrogenic pneumonoconiosis caused by the inhalation of free silica dust, characterized by descrete nodular pulmonary fibrosis and, in its more advanced stages, by impairment of respiratory function. As to the cause and development of the disease, we find it stated there: "The inhalation of dust containing free crystalline silica over a prolonged period, varying from two to 25 years or more, is necessary for the establishment of silicosis;" and "Descrete nodulation does not usually result in any significant symptoms. It is the massive conglomerate fibrosis resulting from the coalescence of nodules that is responsible for the symptoms and signs. In these cases, symptoms develop very slowly over many years. "Silicosis is variously defined in the workmen's compensation laws of many states but in all cases, the disease is attributed to the inhalation of silica dust over a prolonged period of time. And in an August, 1949, bulletin of the Bureau of Adult Health, California

Department of Public Health, it was stated: "The diagnosis of silicosis is made on the basis of chest X-ray findings typical of the disease together with a history of exposure to free silica dust. The X-ray findings, however, do not become evident until several years after the beginning of the disease, which may be present for many years before the subject has any symptoms or feeling of ill health and may increase only very slowly. *** Once present, the condition is always progressive regardless of occupation or removal from dust exposure." 

Because of the slow development of the disease and the lack of legislation relating specifically to it, a man may be exposed to the hazard from silica dust in employment in other states for many years, come to California, take employment where he is exposed to the same hazard to an equal or lesser degree, become permanently disabled shortly thereafter and obtain an award for unlimited medical benefits, compensation for 400 weeks and a pension payable thereafter during the remainder of his life.

The slow development so characteristic of the disease and the problems resulting therefrom have been the cause for special legislation in many states; and clearly, any consideration of the cause and of the clinical course of silicosis compels admission that the disease is in a class so distinctly apart from other occupational diseases that they must be subject to special legislation if justice is to prevail under our workmen's compensation law.

The fact that silicosis is not disabling until many years of constant exposure to silica dust and even that clinical evidence of the disease is not recognizable for some years after such exposure justifies the presumption that unless a worker, who is disabled from silicosis, had been

WORK MEN'S COMPENSATION 

exposed to the hazard of that disease for a period of years during his employment by the employer in whose employ he was at the time of becoming disabled then it must be presumed that the disease was contracted in some former environment and that the disability was precipitated by aggravation of a pre-existing disease. 

The unusual characteristics of these diseases, the muddled conditions due to successive court decisions and the lack of uniform observance of a single rule by the referees of the commission have combined to create a situation which, in our opinion, requires immediate legislative action.

It is the recommendation of this committee that the Workmen's Compensation Law be amended to provide that, subject to all provisions of the law as it presently exists and to all amendments proposed in this report, coverage for disability or death from silicosis and asbestosis be restricted to workers who have been exposed to the hazards of these diseases during the 10 years immediately preceding the disability or death; that four years of such exposure shall have been in employment in California and that no liability shall attach to any individual employer in whose employ the disabled worker was exposed to the hazard of the disease for less than six months. 

LABOR CODE SECTION No. 5500.5 

In the report filed April 12, 1951, the Senate Interim Committee on Workmen's Compensation Benefits reported on complaints filed with the committee about the occupational disease law (or to be accurate, about the lack of an occupational disease law) with emphasis upon the reasons for, and extent of, dissatisfaction among employers and insurance carriers over awards for disability or death from silicosis. The complaints were pretty well summed up in a letter addressed to the committee by Attorney R. P. Wisecarver. He stated: "Successive interpretations by the Supreme Court have thoroughly muddled the whole problem of occupational diseases and especially the disease known as silicosis. By court interpretation of what otherwise was a plain statement of limitation, the period of limitation was extended from six months to any time in the indefinite future that an applicant could claim disability. On top of this interpretation was another, revoking all previous experience and practice, which held that every employer who had any liability for the silicosis had a full liability. In other words, though a man may have worked 30 years in mines elsewhere, he could come to California, work a few months in a mine, claim disability from silicosis which he contracted elsewhere, and receive maximum compensation with a life pension". PRE-EXISTING DISABILITY OR DISEASE 

Workmen's compensation was adopted in California in the year 

1911 by enactment of the Roseberry Act (Chapter 399, Laws of 1911). 

Therein it was provided that liability shall exist against an employer for any personal injury accidentally sustained by his employees. This law was followed by the Boynton Act (Chapter 176, Laws of 1913) which provided that liability shall exist against an employer for any

WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION 

personal injury sustained by his employees by accident. By amendment in 1915 (Chapter 607, Laws of 1915) the words "by accident" were deleted so that the liability of the employer was for any personal injury. The Boynton Act was succeeded by the Workmen's Compensation Law of 1917 and, at this time, the term "injury" was defined to include any injury or disease. Though expressed somewhat differently, it was provided in these several laws that to be compensable the injury (and in the last law, also the disease) to an employee must grow out of and be incurred while in the performance of his duties. And these laws also provide for payment of benefits if the injury proximately causes death.

While the Roseberry and Boynton Acts did not contain a provision for apportionment of liability in cases of pre-existing disability, the commission ruled in favor of apportionment in such cases and at the instance of the commission, an amendment to this effect was made to the Boynton Act in 1915. 

Clearly then the theory from the start was that industry should be assessed only for benefits based upon the degree of disability due solely to the compensable injury. Consistent with this concept of the obligation of an employer, the Workmen's Compensation Law of 1917, which imposed liability upon the employer for disability and/or death from injury or disease, also made provision for apportionment of liability in cases of pre-existing disability or pre-existing disease. Provisions of this nature have existed continuously to the date of this re port. Currently the Labor Code provides: 

Sec. 4663. "In case of aggravation of any disease existing prior to a compensable injury, compensation shall be allowed only for the proportion of the disability due to the aggravation of such prior disease which is reasonably attributed to the injury." 

Sec. 4750. "An employee who is suffering from a previous permanent disability or physical impairment and sustains permanent injury thereafter shall not receive from the employer compensation for the later injury in excess of the compensation allowed for such injury when considered by itself and not in conjunction with or in relation to the previous disability or impairment." "The employer shall not be liable for compensation to such an employee for the combined disability, but only for that portion due to the later injury as though no prior disability or impairment had existed."

Through the years, the obvious intent of the law has been pretty strictly observed in cases of such evident pre-existing disability as the loss of or the loss of use of a major member. 

For some years, the intent of the law was rigidly observed in respect to pre-existing disease. But with changes in the personnel of the commission there developed a tendency to hold that unless prior to injury, there was some evidence of disability or impairment resulting from pre-existing disease, there could be no apportionment of disability on the grounds of such pre-existing disease. Through quite similar decisions by successive commissions and with assists by the courts, Labor Code sections enacted by the Legislature have, in effect, been amended

WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION 

19 1 by these extra-legal agencies to provide that an employer accepts an employee in the condition in which he hires him. Under this rule, if the pathology is dormant, or if it be active and progressive but temporarily visually symptomless, there is no pre-existing disability and in the event the worker subsequently suffers some compensable injury or incurs some compensable occupational disease, award is made against the employer for the degree of disability resulting from the combined effect of the pre-existing pathology and the compensable injury or disease or for death if proximately resulting therefrom. It matters not that the disease or some congenital weakness would alone eventually cause disability or death. This rule has been successfully enforced notwithstanding the statutory provision (Section 3600, Labor Code) that the injury or disease must be proximately caused by the employment and arise while the employee is performing service growing out of and incidental to his employment and acting within the course of his employment and the sections quoted earlier, (1) Section 4663, which provides for apportionment in cases of aggravation of a pre-existing disease and (2) Section 4750 which provides for apportionment in cases of previous physical impairment. 

The construction currently placed upon the workmen's compensation law has brought serious complaints from industry. Employers charge that their failure to obtain apportionment of liability in accordance with the express provisions of the law is imposing a terrific financial burden upon them. While the complaints apply in general to all pre-existing diseases, they particularly stress the experience in respect to cases in the following categories: (a) disability or death from the normal progress of heart disease or other cardiac condition but considered compensable because the seizure occurred while in the performance of regular duties or occurred elsewhere but by some rationalizing can be attributed to some out-of-the-ordinary task or to some excitement at the place of employment; (b) disablement from rupture and protrusion of intervertebral discs of the spine due largely and often wholly to degenerative causes and unrelated to the employment or disablement from other conditions of the spine and back and (c) to disablement from silicosis, asbestosis and other pulmonary dust diseases.

Paradoxically, though this rule broadens the coverage under our Workmen's Compensation Law to the extent of arousing very vigorous complaints from employers over the cost resulting therefrom, it also operates to the serious disadvantage of a far greater number of workers than are benefited.

As a method of escape from awards based upon pathology antedating a compensable injury or occupational disease, testimony shows that many employers are inquiring carefully and exhaustively into the previous history of all applicants for employment and are employing only those who pass the screening tests. And according to information possessed by the committee, the number of large enterprises already following this course of action is steadily increasing. Therefore, for each disabled worker who reaps some benefit under the present interpretation of the law, a far greater number of skilled workers are finding it impossible to obtain employment in the field in which they are experienced. These unfortunate workers must take employment in

some different occupations at some lower wage scale while some are forced to apply for unemployment insurance benefits and still others turn to county relief agencies.

No member of this committee would take from any worker any part of the benefits statutorily provided for him under any of the benefit plans embraced in the social security program of this State. The problem dealt with here stems from the fact that, without appropriate action by the Legislature, the Workmen's Compensation Law has been extended into the field of health insurance with industry not only being held liable for disability arising out of and caused by industry, as of course it should be, but is also required to pay compensation benefits for pathological conditions which, if they cause unemployment before the occurrence of a compensable industrial injury, would be paid under the Unemployment Compensation Disability Act, and if they cause compulsory unemployment following cessation of all payments properly chargeable to industry, should then be paid out of unemployment disability compensation funds.

To assure strict observance of the purpose and intent of Sections 

4663 and 4750 of the Labor Code, the committee recommends amendments thereto which will make it mandatory for the commission to determine separately the nature and extent of the previous disease and the nature and extent of the disability due to the compensable injury when considered by itself and will also require the commission to make separate and detailed findings thereon. Together with the recommendations based upon studies of: (1) occupational diseases, (2) heart conditions and (3) liberal interpretation, this will, in the opinion of the committee, not only clarify the laws in respect to these latter subjects but also serve to assure realistic determinations in cases where pre-existing impairments are a consideration.

LIBERAL CONSTRUCTION The Senate Interim Committee on Workmen's Compensation Bene fits reported, in May, 1951, that many decisions of the Industrial Accident Commission were based upon little more than a scintilla of evidence and that the higher courts had shown a tendency to affirm the commission's decisions if there was any evidence in the record which, taken in isolation, might be considered to support the commission's findings. Convinced of the need for remedial legislation, the committee recommended "that the workmen's compensation act be amended to require that the courts empowered to review commission decisions shall consider the entire record certified to it by the commission and sustain the orders, decisions and awards of that body only if they are supported by substantial evidence in the light of the entire record." Labor 

Code Section 5952 was amended to conform to this recommendation. 

In its report of March 19, 1953, the Senate Interim Committee on 

Workmen's Compensation Benefits announced that while that 1951 amendment proved of some benefit, it was, of itself, inadequate to assure complete observance of statutory intent.

As was explained by these previous committees of the Senate, license for the decisions complained of stem from the construction placed upon Labor Code Section 3202 by some referees and commissioners and some appellate court justices. This section reads:

WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION 

21 "The provisions of Division 4 and Division 5 of this code shall be liberally construed by the courts with the purpose of extending their benefits for the protection of persons injured in the course of their employment."

The purpose of this section seems to be crystal clear, i.e., that the provisions of Divisions 4 and 5 of the code (the law) shall be liberally construed in favor of injured workers. However, over the years, the commission and courts have taken it upon themselves to decide that not only the law but the evidence taken by the commission that shall be liberally construed in awarding compensation. It is this rule by these extra-law-making bodies that leads to injustice because there is no restraint to the observance of this arbitrary rule. 

The law (Section 5952) provides for review of commission decisions by appellate courts but this section prohibits the court from exercising its independent judgment on the evidence. Hence, freedom of referees and commissioners to evaluate evidence according to their respective liberal or conservative philosophy in respect to social security programs, or from sympathy for persons in distress, or even from bias and partnership has been successfully substituted for the statute which restricts liberal construction solely to the provisions of the law. 

In the circumstances, the commission has the power to decide cases contrary to the preponderance of the evidence and, according to testimony before this committee, such decisions are frequently issued. 

It is apparent that to obtain adherence to legislative intent and to assure justice in all cases, the law must be expressed with greater preciseness. In the circumstances, the committee recommends that 

Section 3202 of the Labor Code be amended to provide that only the language of the provisions of Divisions 4 and 5 of the Labor Code shall be liberally construed by the courts and the commission and, additionally in the interest of impartial determinations, require that decisions shall be in accordance with the fair weight of the testimony. 

DESTRUCTION OF LEGAL FILES OF INDUSTRIAL ACCIDENT COMMISSION 

All legal files have been retained from the earliest days of the commission. For speedy and economical access to the more recent files the general policy is to retain such files at the main offices for approximately five years.

A survey made for this committee by the Legislative Auditor disclosed that (1) all files for the earlier years are stored in the Central 

Record Depository, a state-owned warehouse in Sacramento; (2) that as of the time of his report to the committee (November 10, 1954) these files are in 1,806 metal drawers and 602 cardboard containers and; (3) that "new cases are currently being filed with the Industrial Accident Commission at a rate sufficient to fill 275 drawers per year." 

Obviously it is becoming increasingly difficult and expensive to provide storage space.

Following determination from the reference records of the State Archivist of the number of times cases filed in any particular year were withdrawn from the Central Record Depository during each of the

WORKMEN'S COMPENSATION 

last eight years, conferences with the commission, and consideration of an opinion prepared for the Legislative Auditor by the Legislative Counsel on the legal phase of the problem, the Legislative Auditor has recommended that, with the exception of life pension cases and lifetime medical cases the maximum retention period for legal files be 

25 years. The Legislative Auditor estimates that the implementation of this recommendation will eliminate 570 file drawers of material in the Central Record Depository and save the State $52,668 for storage space during the next 30 years.

While there is nothing in the law to prevent establishment of a retention period, commission representatives have expressed a justifiable desire for legislative authority for destruction of these records. 

The committee recommends enactment of authority consistent with the proposal by the Legislative Auditor.
