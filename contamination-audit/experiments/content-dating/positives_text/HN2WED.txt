FOREWORD TO THE TEACHER 

Directed Experiments in Physics is an outgrowth of a series of laboratory experiments used in mimeographed form for many years in several New York high schools. During these years, the experiments have helped prepare thousands of students for College Board and New York State Regents' requirements. They have also served to provide a meaningful interpretation of everyday physics for those students whose formal education ended with the high school. Constant revision during actual laboratory use has produced a manual which is complete, convenient, and instructive. 

The authors believe that the introduction of this new manual into an already crowded field is justified by a number of unusual features incorporated in these experiments. A brief description of these features is offered here as a guide in the use of the manual. 

The Experimental Problem. In keeping with the principles of the scientific method, each experiment is introduced by a problem or question which has arisen in the logical development of the subject. This problem is worded carefully so that each step in the procedure is a step toward its solution, and the summation of observations and data is a complete answer to the problem. 

The Procedure. The directions are concise and logically arranged. The student who is prepared to perform the experiment will have no difficulty with it; but it is impossible for the student to follow the directions blindly, without comprehension, and still complete the experiment successfully. 

The Diagram. The diagram supplies the structural details of the apparatus and an indication of the method to be followed. The student, however, must complete the drawing to show just how he obtained his data. The drawing thus becomes a pedagogical device which teaches principles without taxing the student's drawing ability.

The Data Table. All data is recorded neatly and logically. Emphasis is placed on the use of proper units for numerical values. Where possible, checks and controls are provided for in the data tables. 

The Conclusion. As the final step in the inductive process, the student must present his conclusions in his own words, and with direct reference to his data. Initiative and imagination are encouraged by the complete absence of "fill-in" and leading questions at this stage of the experiment. 

Time Limitations. In every case, the Conclusion of the experiment can be finished in a single 45minute period. The Supplementary and Optional Questions can be adapted to a longer period. During a 90-minute period, every student should be able to work through the Grade C optional question, while the exceptional students will be able to answer all the problems. These questions may also be used for home or class work.

The Correction Box. Common errors in the performance of the experiment can be indicated on the "correction box" at the head of each experiment. This simplifies the teacher's work, and makes it easier for the student to improve in subsequent experiments. 

Textbook References. Complete page references to 13 standard high school texts enable the student to prepare adequately for each experiment, and make the manual convenient to use with any text or set of texts. Additional references to other texts will be supplied upon request. 

The authors wish to acknowledge their indebtedness to Mr. Robert Black, Mr. Thomas Treharne, Mr. Kenneth Smith, Mr. Victor Davis, and Mr. De Forest Brane for testing this material in mimeographed form and for contributing many valuable suggestions. They are especially indebted to Mr. 

William Van Arnam for his constructive criticisms and to Mr. Burnett Cross for his assistance in arranging much of the material for use. They are grateful to Mr. W. Irving Jennings for providing the drawings according to their designs.

TO THE STUDENT 

One of our leading military authorities has stated that the First World War was a chemists' war but that this one is a physicists' war. The statement illustrates the importance of physics in wartime. It is no less true that physics is fundamental in times of peace. It is necessary for successful living in everyday life and as a required background for engineers, doctors, and other professional people. 

This book is designed to help you understand the fundamental principles of physics and to use those principles for interpreting and controlling your environment. Each lesson is a personal experience in physics that will enable you to understand a basic principle. You will see how you are constantly using many of these principles in your everyday living. 

Each experiment begins with a question. This is followed by a list of materials needed to help you find the answer. Then the procedure suggests the method of using the materials. 

Read this carefully, constantly referring to the partially completed drawing. Care exercised in following these instructions, step by step, will enable you to complete the experiment quickly and accurately. Make your observations honestly and accurately. Record these observations in the proper place. (Data may be recorded in pencil and inked in after it has been approved.) Double check all computations and study your results. Make certain that you understand their significance.

Now you should be ready to write your conclusion. This is not solely an answer to the question at the beginning, but shows the steps of thought by which you arrived at your conclusion. Notice that, in drawing your conclusion, you must refer directly to the data. For example, the conclusion for Archimedes' Law might be stated as follows: "Since the weight of the displaced liquid is 18 grams and the apparent lost weight or buoyant force is 18 grams, I conclude that apparent lost weight (buoyant force) of an immersed body is equal to the weight of the liquid displaced.'

Your teacher will be pleased to explain more fully what is meant by the scientific method and how it is used in industry and research. Observe how each experiment illustrates this scientific method.

The supplementary questions are designed to give you a broader comprehension of the principle involved and to show you some of its practical applications. The optional questions can give you added practice. They are a challenge to the ambitious and able student. 

You will find it necessary to make constant reference to textbooks. References to many accepted texts are listed in a table on the preceding pages. You will also find all needed numerical tables in the Appendix.

EXPERIMENT 10. BOYLE'S LAW Problem: What is the relation between the pressure on a given mass of gas and the volume of the gas if the temperature is kept constant? 

Apparatus: J-tube, mercury, beaker, barometer. 

Procedure: 

1. Record the reading of the barometer and the reading at the top of the closed tube. 

2. Add mercury to the open tube until the level in the open tube is the same or slightly higher than that in the closed tube. 

3. Take the reading at the top of the mercury in each arm of the J-tube. 

4. Add 5 to 10 cc of mercury to the open arm, and repeat the readings. 

5. Repeat step 4 until the difference between the two readings is equal to the barometer reading. 

6. Compute the total pressure on the enclosed air (P) by adding the barometer reading to the difference between the height in the open arm and the height in the closed arm. 

7. Compute the volume of the enclosed air (V) by subtracting the closed tube reading from the reading at the top of the closed tube.
