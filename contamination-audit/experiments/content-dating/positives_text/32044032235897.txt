HATTERS FUR Report to the President Under Paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401 of Developments With Regard to Hatters' Fur Since Modification of the Trade-Agreement Concession on February 9, 1952 To the President: 

United States Tariff Commission, 

Washington, February 4, 1955.

This is a report made pursuant to paragraph 1 of Executive Order 

10401 of October 14, 1952 (17 F. R. 9125), 1/ of developments with regard to hatters' fur since the modification, effective February 9, 1952, of the concession which was granted on imports of that commodity in the 

General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (Proclamation 2960; 17 F. R. 188). 

This is the second report under paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401 with respect to hatters' fur; the first report was submitted to you on 

February 5, 1954.

The Tariff Act of 1930 (par. 1520) originally specified a rate of duty of 35 percent ad valorem on imports of hatters' fur. This rate was reduced to 27 percent ad valorem (effective May 1,1935) pursuant to a concession granted in the trade agreement with the Belgo-Luxembourg Economic Union, and was further reduced to 15 percent ad valorem (effective January 1, 1948) pursuant to a concession granted in the General 

Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. 

1/ Prescribing procedures for periodic review of escape-clause modifications of trade-agreement concessions.

---

Hatters' fur was the subject of an "escape clause" investigation by the Tariff Commission in 1951. The report on this investigation was made pursuant to section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 and contained data regarding conditions of the trade through the first 6 months of 1951. In its investigation the Commission found that the injury to the domestic industry resulted largely from increased imports of low-grade hatters' fur and that such imports had increased because of the fall in prices of hatters' fur in foreign producing countries. The fall in the prices of hatters' fur had accompanied a fall in the prices of the fur skins from which hatters' fur is made. The reduced ad valorem rate of duty afforded domestic producers of hatters' fur less protection from the lower grades than from the higher grades of hatters' fur--particularly when prices of hatters' fur and fur skins were low. It was found that the least restrictive and yet effective remedy for the injury was a change in the form of duty which would take into account the differences in grades and the changes in prices of hatters' fur. 

In accordance with the recommendation made by the Commission in the investigation, the trade-agreement concession with respect to hatters' fur was modified by Presidential proclamation, and the present rate of duty of 47 cents per pound, but not less than 15 percent nor more than 35 percent ad valorem, was made effective on February 9, 1952. Under the modified duty the 35 percent ad valorem rate specified in the Tariff Act of 1930 applies to very low-grade and low-priced hatters' fur (that valued at $1.36 or less per pound); the trade agreement concession rate of 15

percent ad valorem applies to higher priced hatters' fur (that valued at $3.16-2/3 or more per pound); and the specific rate of 47 cents per pound applies to hatters' fur at the intermediate prices (that between $1.36 per pound and $3.16-2/3 per pound).

Hatters' fur is the soft underfur obtained from certain animal skins and used as a raw material in the production of fur felt hats. It is cut chiefly from the skins of rabbits and hares, and to a small extent from skins (or pieces of skins) of beavers and other animals. The United 

States industry producing hatters' fur sells almost its entire output of cut fur to the domestic fur felt hat industry. United States exports of hatters' fur are negligible, and sales of cut fur to domestic consumers other than hat manufacturers--such as the domestic textile industry--are small. On the other hand, domestic hat manufacturers have ordinarily bought from independent cutters most of their supplies of hatters fur. 

Those hat manufacturers who cut fur for their own use have probably accounted for no more than 10 to 15 percent of total domestic consumption of hatters' fur in recent years. 

Throughout the 1930's and until 1948, United States imports of hatters' fur were small, and in terms of quantity supplied less than 1 percent of domestic consumption. Imports during this period often consisted of special qualities or kinds not regularly produced in the United States or not immediately available from domestic sources. Imports increased in 

1948 and continued upward in 1949 and 1950. In 1951 imports were slightly less than in 1950, but even so they were substantially larger than in any year before 1948.

Imports declined in 1952 after the trade-agreement concession was modified (effective February 9, 1952); they increased sharply in the early months of 1953 to about the same level as in the corresponding periods in 1950 and 1951, but declined again in late 1953 and in 1954. 

Imports amounted to 282,000 pounds in 1950, to 247,000 in 1951, to 133,000 pounds in 1952, to 248,000 pounds in 1953, and to 103,000 pounds in the first 9 months of 1954 (see table 2 in the appendix). Imports supplied approximately 5 percent of domestic consumption in 1950, 4 percent in 1951, 2 percent in 1952, 4 percent in 1953, and 3 percent in the first 9 months of 1954 (see table 1 in the appendix).

From 1948 through 1952 imports consisted in large part of one lowpriced grade of fur obtained from European rabbit skins and commercially known as Petit Bon. This fur is closely comparable and competitive with the domestic grade Grey Entire, also cut from European rabbit skins; to a less extent it is competitive with the domestic grade BCB Unpulled, which is cut from Australian rabbit skins. Belgium has been the principal supplier of United States imports of hatters' fur (see table 2 in the appendix). 

Beginning in 1953, and continuing in 1954, imports have consisted of increasingly larger quantities of white and mottled fur and of lesser quantities of Petit Bon (a grey fur). White and light colored furs sell at substantially higher prices than Petit Bon or darker colored furs. The quoted price of Petit Bon fur was under $2.00 per pound in 1954 (F.0.B. 

Belgium), whereas the average unit value of imports from Belgium was almost $4.00 per pound.

United States consumption of hatters' fur was slightly lower in 1952 than in the 2 previous years, owing to the decline in production of fur

felt hats. As a result of an increase in the production of men's fur felt hats in 1953, the consumption of hatters' fur increased also. For the first 9 months of 1954, however, consumption of hatters' fur was much lower than in the same period in 1953 and earlier years--approximately 3 million pounds in 1954 compared with 4 million pounds in 1953. Variations in the volume of domestic production of hatters' fur have closely followed the variations in domestic consumption. That is, production dropped slightly in 1952, in 1953 almost regained the rate in 1950 and 1951, but in 1954 was materially lower than in earlier years. 

The domestic industry producing hatters' fur is located in New York City; in Newark, N. J.; and in and around Danbury, Conn. In the New York- 

Newark area there were 11 producers in mid-1951; in late 1953 there were 

5 producers in this area, and as of late 1954 the number had further declined to 3. Employment by fur-cutting concerns in this area ranged between 500 and 550 workers in 1949 and 1950, but declined to. about 400 to 

425 workers in 1951. By early 1952, employment had dropped to only a little over 250 workers, but by October 1953 it had increased to about 370. However, as of October 1954, employment had again declined to about 270.

Since mid-1951 the average hourly earnings of employees in the hatters' fur industry in the New York-Newark area have increased by more than 20 percent. Although the total number of employees has declined, available information indicates that employment was somewhat more stable in 1953 and 1954 than in 1951. Production of hatters' fur in the Danbury area is much smaller than in the New York-Newark area. The Commission does not

have complete statistical data on recent operations in this area, but it is known that conditions there have been about the same as in the New York- Information available to the Commission indicates that since 

Newark area. mid-1951 the Danbury area has had a decrease of about one-half in the number of concerns in operation, an overall decline in workers in the hatters' fur industry, and an increase in wage rates of about 15 percent. 

The financial position of the companies producing hatters' fur has worsened materially since 1952. The Commission has continuous financial data for all of the years from 1951 through early 1954 for 7 companies in the hatters' fur industry. 1/ The aggregate data for these 7 concerns indicate that as a group they lost substantially in 1951, 1953, and early 1954, and showed a profit only in 1952. In 1951 the group lost more than $250,000; in 1952 they made a profit of $32,000, but in 1953 they lost about $80,000. In the first 6 months of 1954 the losses increased to more than $140,000. The combined sales of the 7 firms have been about 8 million dollars annually except in 1952, when they amounted to 4.5 million dollars. 

As pointed out in the original report to the President on the escapeclause investigation, changes in the price of hatters' fur closely follow changes in the price of the rabbit skins from which the fur is cut. Prices of tame-rabbit skins and of hatters' fur, which were low in 1952 and 1953, declined even further in 1954, both in the United States and in foreign producing countries.
