ANNUAL REPORT OF THE GOVERNOR OF AMERICAN SAMOA, 1952 John C. Elliott, Governor INTRODUCTION 

American Samoa, which was administered by the Navy for 51 years, was placed under the administrative jurisdiction of the Department of the Interior on July 1, 1951, by Executive order of the President, No. 10264. In order to carry out this transfer of jurisdiction, governmental positions formerly occupied by naval personnel were restaffed by civilians. The Honorable Phelps Phelps was appointed 

Governor on January 29, 1951, and served until June 30, 1952. He was succeeded by John C. Elliott who had been Secretary of American Samoa. Mr. Leland P. Draney followed the latter as Secretary.

Despite difficulties presented by a curtailment of appropriations and a large turnover in personnel the transfer of governmental operations was satisfactorily concluded. 

The transfer of authority to civilian administration unfortunately coincided with an economic slump caused by several factors, such as a drop in copra prices, a severe drought affecting food production, depletion of wartime accumulated reserves of cash and materials, and the closing of the naval station.

The islands of the territory are for the most part mountainous, and only about one-third of the land is considered arable even in terms of the rudest types of hand cultivation. On the main island, Tutuila, where the people's interest was centered mainly in wageearning activities, little attention was given to developing or even maintaining, through replanting, the coconut plantations. Nearly all producing trees on that island are now overage and should have been replaced years ago. The people of the remote Manu'a Island group, where virtually no naval employment was ever available, have continued to depend on their copra export for cash earnings and have,

Until now, little effort has been made to introduce new cash crops. 

Cacao was extensively introduced in the neighboring territory of Western Samoa more than 20 years ago and has contributed greatly to the sound economy there. In American Samoa introduction un fortunately was sporadic and wholly unsupervised and only a very few mature trees are producing in the territory today. These earn no revenue for their owners. It is significant to note that agricultur development was not an established official activity of the government of this territory until October 1932. The activities of the Agricul ture Department are thus unfamiliar to the tradition-minded Sa moans, many of whom still regard them with suspicion. Crop surveys have resulted in considerable deliberate misinformation and tree marked for yield studies have been chopped down or carelessly destroyed.

Thousands of Samoans, finding the natural resources of their island inadequate, have expressed a desire to go to Hawaii, Guam, or the mainland, where they believe the existence of a wage economy offers better opportunities than in their overcrowded and underdeveloped home islands. Hundreds have already left, among them many of the more experienced and skilled employees of the government. Emi gation, thus, has become one of the chief means of meeting the prob lem of population growth.

During the year more than 1,500 young Samoans offered themselves for enlistment in the Armed Services and asked that recruiting facili ties be established in the territory. Because of the attitude of the 

Defense Department to this proposal, however, there appears to be little hope for most of these volunteers unless they can save the money required for expensive air travel to Hawaii via the Fiji Islands. Not withstanding this obstacle, small numbers of young Samoans have suc ceeded in making the trip and are today serving in the Armed Forces Allotment checks from these volunteer servicemen are one of the fe sources of cash income of the territory's families. Some men have achieved distinguished combat records in the Korean campaign and several have been killed or are missing.

A source of continued hope for economic development lies in the handicraft export trade based principally on floor matting and other items woven of pandanus (laufala) and articles such as bowls and trays carved from local hardwoods. Under the previous naval admin istration considerable encouragement was given in this field through establishment of a producers' cooperative. LEGISLATIVE BRANCH Fono of American Samoa 

During the year the Fono of American Samoa met on five separate occasions. These sessions covered a total of 28 days, 26 of which were regular or customary sessions and 2 of which were special sessions called for the purpose of considering matters of special importance. 

The Fono of American Samoa consists of two houses. The upper house, or House of Ali'i, is comprised of 12 members who are the holders of the 12 high titles which are set forth in the Code of Ameri- I can Samoa. These members are not elected but derive their office from their traditional positions. The lower house, or House of 

Representatives, contains 54 members who are elected. Two of these members are elected by secret ballot and represent that portion of the x population of American Samoa which has never been a part of, or has formally disaffiliated itself from, the customary social system of com- 20

munal life which is known as the "Matai System." The remaining 52 members represent all the main villages or groups of villages in the territory. They are not elected by secret ballot but rather by open meeting in the traditional Samoan manner. Since the fono is elected for a 2-year term in November of every even-numbered year, there were no elections this year.

The office of chairman of the upper house is held by members in rotation; the speaker of the lower house is elected by vote of that body. 

This session, the chairman was the Honorable S. F. Satele; the speaker, the Honorable M. T. Tuiasosopo.

All sessions were conducted in the Samoan language and all records, reports, and resolutions were therefore translated into English before they reached the Governor's office. This is a costly and time-consuming procedure but is made necessary because of the fact that the majority of the members of the Fono are accustomed to using the Samoan language rather than English, and, in fact, many members speak no English at all. JUDICIAL BRANCH 

The judicial powers in American Samoa are vested in the High Court, district courts, and village courts. An appeal may be taken from a village court to a district court and from a district court to the High Court. The judicial branch of the government is completely independent of the excutive and legislative branches. Except in uncontested probate matters no case is decided in which a Samoan judge or judges have not participated.

It has been judicially declared in American Samoa that the fundamental human rights of the Samoan people are protected by the Constitution of the United States. Not only are they protected by the 

Constitution but they are also protected by the American Samoa Bill of Rights contained in the Code of American Samoa. The American 

Samoan Bill of Rights is modeled after the Bill of Rights in the Constitution.

During the fiscal year ending June 30, 1952, a total of 212 persons was tried on criminal charges; 183 were convicted and 29 acquitted. 

Of the 183 persons convicted 26 were sent to jail and 151 fined, while 2 received suspended jail sentences and 4 worked out alternative fines by doing work for the public benefit in their villages under the direction of the pulenu'u (mayor).

Criminal cases are disposed of promptly after filing in court, due regard having been given to the constitutional rights of the defendant and his rights under the American Samoan Bill of Rights. 

Western civilization is making an impact upon the Samoan people and their culture. This was accentuated by World War II and the presence here of many thousands of American troops. New opportunities for education are also having an effect. However, western ideas and institutions are frequently not welcomed. The Samoan is! independent and proud. Most crimes of a serious nature are prosecuted by the attorney gen- Feral in District Court No. 1 at Fagatogo. If a plea of guilty is made, the government is nevertheless normally required to produce evidence of guilt on account of the possibility that the defendant may have pleaded guilty through mistake. If upon the production of evidenceand the defendant may introduce counterevidence—a reasonable doubt as to his guilt appears, the plea of guilty may be withdrawn and a plea of not guilty entered in the interest of justice. Most of the litigation in the High Court involves title to land and the right to matai (chief) titles. The district courts are the courts of general jurisdiction. During the year, the courts disposed of 371 civil cases.

Judge Albert B. Maris, of the United States Circuit Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, spent 3 weeks in American Samoa making a study of the court system with a view to making recommendations to effect a statutory reorganization of the judicial branch. 

EXECUTIVE BRANCH Department of Administration 

This department, headed by the director of administration, provides a wide variety of services for the government in the fields of budget planning and control, accounting, procurement, storage and issuance of supplies, administration of personnel, payrolls, collection of customs, and conduct of communications activities. The department also manages various public services and agencies such as the local marine and land transportation systems, a laundry, a printing shop, the two government-supervised producers cooperatives, the copra fund, and the department of Samoan industries. In the performance of its duties the department employed, as of June 30, 1952, 9 stateside and 

104 local personnel.

These employees were organized into the following divisions: The office of the director, accounts, customs and revenue, supply and purchasing, personnel, communication and management services. A brief résumé of the activities of each follows: 

The office of the director accomplished the complete organization of the department, which did not exist under the previous naval administration, its functions having been performed by several more or less autonomous agencies. In close cooperation with and with the active participation of the Budget and Finance Division of the Department of Interior and the Accounting Systems Division of the General Ac-

✩  

counting Office, procedures were established to control expenditures, to facilitate budgetary planning, and to regulate stock levels and purchasing. A system of accounting for all government activities was installed, a manual was prepared and published, and personnel were retrained in the new procedures. Thus, at the close of business for the fiscal year 1952, a trial balance for the whole government could be prepared which, for the first time in the territory's history, gave accurate and verifiable information as to net worth and obligations. 

A balance sheet for the general fund of the government is printed elsewhere in this report.

The accounts division had the responsibility for putting the new system into effect. Personnel of this office, while learning and establishing the new procedures, worked many hours into the nights to establish and maintain control over the financial affairs of the government. The accounts division deserves special commendation. 

The customs and revenue division, staffed entirely by local personnel, had complete charge, following reorganization, of most of the principal sources of the government's revenue, which amounted to $648,- 

173.55 for the year. Of this amount, $284,204 was derived from duties, licenses, and other revenues of the customs office. An analysis by this office of imports reveals that $606,586 worth came from the United States, $444,086 from Australia and New Zealand, $49,304 from Apia and Fiji and lesser amounts from Great Britain, Japan, Canada, India, Germany, and France. Dollar volume of imports from all sources was more than 50 percent above that of the previous year. This was due to increased inventories and sales by local merchants following disestablishment of the naval commissary and ship's stores. Private sale revenues of the government-owned electrical, telephone and water systems as well as rental of government-owned buildings were also the responsibility of this division and amounted to $71,310.62. 

A boat shed with two diesel-powered launches, a motor cruiser and a marine railway for repair of these and other small craft were also managed. A launch service was inaugurated to serve the northern shores of Tutuila Island and the island of Aunu'u, thereby affording inhabitants of those remote regions a better means of travel and of transport for their products and purchases. Boat service for school children across the Pago Pago harbor was also conducted. Fares for all services were nominal in the general interest of the public concerned.

A land transportation system of eight vehicles was operated by this department during the year with gross revenue of $12,875.80. 

Despite continuous efforts to repair aged equipment, at least two vehicles out of eight were inoperable at all times. A regular bus service was maintained for both Eastern and Western Districts. A total of 31,400 single-fare tickets was sold and 3,208 commuters and

students passes issued. A total of 5,776 hospital patients was given free rides, and much in the way of official transportation provided for all branches of the government. The operation of this bus service has been a particular credit to the chief of this division, who, although it was not germane to the rest of his responsibilities, devoted much of his time and energy to meeting its many problems. At the end of the year this service was transferred to the management services division.

The property and supply division, also entirely staffed by local personnel, found itself faced with many serious problems as a result of the change in administration. No longer benefiting from integration with the large naval procurement system, this division had to establish entirely new procedures for nearly all its activities, buying directly from suppliers in many instances. A complete and continuous reevaluation of government needs has been necessary. Loss of naval shipping has caused severe delay and disruption of deliveries. Nevertheless much progress has been made and, in general, the various needs of the government were met with a minimum of inconvenience. 

An inventory (including goods in transit) of over $850,000 was maintained. This division also was accountable for all government property, which is valued in excess of $580,000. 

The personnel division, faced with many problems, was hampered by a staff shortage due to lack of funds. Originally, it was contemplated that two stateside specialists were needed, but the employment of only one aided by a local assistant and a typist was possible. 

The nearly complete turn-over of all stateside personnel made extensive recruiting necessary for suitable replacements. Policies and programs were lacking at the outset and had to be developed and modified during the year. This made impossible the negotiation of uniform agreements with contract employees and caused much dissatisfaction. A ruling by the Civil Service Commission denying Federal civil-service status to nearly all stateside employees of the government further complicated matters, necessitated several additional replacements of recently arrived personnel, and caused dissatisfaction.

Another problem faced by this department was the necessity of developing a uniform wage system for all local employees. Under previous naval administrations certain persons, deemed "Navy employees," were paid higher wages than "island government employees" in equally responsible jobs. After the limited staff of this division found itself too severely taxed by work to undertake the total revision of wages, services of a private contractor were obtained and paid for by the Interior Department, at a cost of $11,800. This contractor, Research Associates of Honolulu, had recently undertaken similar work in the Territory of Hawaii. Two specialists made field

The personnel division also made all travel arrangements for employees of the government and their dependents. With only tenuous and irregular transportation to serve the territory, this has proved a burdensome assignment.

The communication division is responsible for the maintenance and operation of all radio facilities of the government. As a result of the cessation of naval activities, the schedule of operation, which had been continuous, was reduced to an 8-hour day, 512 days per week. 

Local operators were recruited and trained to replace naval personnel, and they have proved satisfactory. Among these is a 19-year-old girl, first of her sex to qualify for this work in Samoa. 

With the assistance of the Department of the Interior, arrangements were made for the assignment of new frequencies, and details were worked out as to schedules and tolls, etc. A new wireless telegraphic substation was established at Leone village in the Western District, and operation of five other substations was continued. A new shipboard radio station was established aboard the M. V. Samoa, an interisland vessel in the government service. 

The Department also resumed operations of a long-wave broadcasting station, WVUV, which provides listeners in the territory with educational and musical programs in both Samoan and English, several hours each day.

The management services division, with responsibility over a number of activities, is a new unit.

Management was provided for the official ship Manu'a Tele, a former yard minetender of the Navy. This vessel, which replaced a naval-operated AKL, provided most of the local marine transportation services for the government and territory and, on occasion, made long voyages to Canton Island when other transportation arrangements failed. This ship, with high-speed diesel engines and limited cargo space, has proved a costly item for the government; nevertheless, under the management of this division, a gross revenue of $42,374.60 was developed during the year.

The public works department, with three stateside and 199 Samoan employees, completed this year's operations in a creditable manner. 

Its two operating and maintenance divisions (for buildings and roads and for utilities and utility equipment), by frugal, planned management, were able to carry out their assigned jobs within the budget authorized for the fiscal year. When the Navy administration left, officials of this department found themselves responsible for the maintenance of a physical plant for which many of the basic data had never been assembled. For example, much information concerning the total amount of floor space, the water supply piping, and the electric transmision and distribution was lacking. Nor was there any record of unit costs for department activities. 

A substantial part of the buildings and other structures is of wood construction, capable of lasting for only a relatively brief period of time. Maintenance of these buildings, which have seen severe deterioration under tropical conditions, had been at a minimum level, apparently, for the two preceding years. This year, however, maintenance work increased, and the buildings are now considered 80 percent safe and sound.

A severe and unusual surging of the sea during the year caused road destruction in both districts. In the Eastern District over 2,500 square yards of roadway were completely washed out, while the Western District suffered loss of several hundred lineal feet of under-cut roadways. The positive road program was, therefore, disrupted while all heavy equipment was used for emergency road repair. 

This department has resurfaced, with rolled coral, 5¼ miles of primary roads, constructed two bridges and 80 miles of ditching to prevent road erosion, and has completed one-half mile of new road construction and 9 miles of secondary road rehabilitation. Surplus Navy bunker fuel which was left here in storage tanks, was used for waterproofing coral-surfaced roads. This program, which proved to be only participatially effective, was resorted to because of the severe limitation on government funds; asphalt or other suitable surfacing material is, at present, too costly.

The first sanitary system for disposal of unburnable garbage was initiated, in order to control fly and mosquito breeding and cut down the rodent population. Reservoirs have been cleaned for the first time since 1945, springs dug out, brush and trees near the reservoirs removed, filters and pipelines cleaned, leaks corrected, and department personnel trained in more efficient operation of the water system. As a result of this effort, water rationing has been largely done away with. 

A labor dispute resulting in a walkout in the telephone communication department created a problem, but the telephone system is again

operating. The Tafuna Airfield was repaired, at a relatively minor cost to the government, and a United States Air Force C-54 and Pan- • American Airways DC-4 were thereby permitted to make landings; - pilots of both planes commented on the excellence of the field. The @hospital boiler plant and the cold storage and ice manufacturing plant, both very old installations needing replacement, were found in poor condition and were repaired for further temporary use. Skilled laborers from this department were temporarily assigned to other departments which were in need of their services; the Department of 

I Administration used these workers for repairs on the Manu'a Tele and the department of public health used them on numerous hospital € projects. As the year began, many new procedures were instituted and a general intensification of effort was required from all members of the department. Employees, in general showed willingness to carry out the new program, but, in a few cases, employees indicated some suspicion of change, resentment of newcomers, or inertia developing i from unsatisfactory situations. A number of employees resigned because of the downgrading of their jobs, and a few were dismissed for disobedience.

The conditions of vehicular equipment present a continuing problem. However, at the close of the year's program 90 percent of the equipment was operative, although at a disproportionately high unit maintenance cost. Because of financial restrictions, replacement of vehicles was impossible.

In conclusion, it can be said that, by and large, the morale of the department is good; the three stateside engineers and quite a few of the

The mission of the department of public health in American Samoa is to furnish medical care for the people, maintain quarantine and sanitary regulations, carry out preventive medical measures, conduct a school for Samoan nurses, and promote health-education among the people of this territory. 

The policies and practices of a civilian department, being different from those of a military installation, have called for a period of tran- 242125-53-3

sition this past year. The civilian government received a physical plant which was inadequate and has required a great deal of planning, reconstruction, and maintenance to meet the standard required for proper medical care of the people of the territory. The present Director, Dr. James R. Dean, took over the duties of this department August 

13, 1951, and has been forced to devote a great deal of his time and energies to the development of the physical plant to meet these minimal needs. During this period, extensive repairs were done to the heavy structural members, walls roof, etc., of the hospital. A new kitchen was installed with new equipment and a more adequate and efficient food service was set up. In addition, a new tropical type frame building has been provided by voluntary contributions of funds raised by the Women's Welfare Organization of American Samoa, coupled with voluntary labor by residents of American Samoa, for the use of Hansen's disease patients in the hospital annex. 

The problem of maintaining adequate medical service here was further hampered by the lack of professionally trained personnel in the various fields of medicine. The departure of naval medical personnel who formerly operated the hospital with a far larger staff left the recruiting of trained personnel up to the civilian government. A great deal of difficulty was encountered in obtaining this personnel, and the department was forced to operate during the past year with one American-trained doctor, one dentist (also United States trained) and, for part of the year, with three European-trained doctors. The department was further handicapped by the death of one of the European physicians in the latter part of the fiscal year, and by inability to obtain a replacement.

The nursing field was faced with the same difficulties, having only two, and at times three, registered nurses to train and supervise the Samoan graduate nurses in service. As a result, the department was forced to depend upon the services of Samoan medical practitioners and graduate Samoan nurses who were not yet adequately trained to carry the burden and responsibility forced upon them. 

Organization. The department of public health is divided into three main divisions-medical services, public health, and school of nursing. During the year, however, the work of the department was determined more by immediate needs than by the carrying out of the proposed long-range program. As a result, the primary duties of the department were those of medical care, and only such public health measures as largely overlapped these were taken. 

The medical services division operated a 225-bed hospital, with 27 bassinets, for the care of individuals where conditions required more intensive and special care than could be furnished outside. The hos pital has been divided into the following units:

(a) Outpatient clinics, (b) dental clinic, (c) medical wards, (d) surgical wards, (e) obstetrical and gynecological wards, (f) pediatric ward, (g) tuberculosis wards, (h) annex for the care of leper and psychiatric cases.

In addition to the above-mentioned hospital, a 20-bed hospital was maintained at the Ta'u dispensary for the care of the peoples of the 

Manu'a Islands. 

Groupings under the public health division include quarantine and sanitary workers, insect and rodent control technicians, and medical and nursing personnel for the rendering of medical services apart from the hospital. This section is also charged with the responsibility of carrying health education to the people of the territory. 

The division of the school of nursing maintained a school for the training of Samoan girls in the nursing field and to graduate them as qualified nurses for service within the department. This school was operated under the supervision of stateside registered nurses.

Health problems.-The major health problems in American Samoa are tuberculosis, filariasis, malnutrition, Hansen's disease, Samoan conjunctivitis, and a high infant mortality rate. The incidence of tuberculosis is extremely high, being about four times that found in the continental United States and occupying a leading place in the department's work. Early in the next fiscal year a tuberculosis control officer from the California State Board of Health will come to the territory to assist temporarily in the establishment of a wellorganized tuberculosis program. It is contemplated that his services will also be utilized in the training of the medical and nursing personnel in the department. 

The department requested the loan of an officer from the United States Public Health Service to assist in organizing a program to combat malnutrition and infant mortality. Again, it is contemplated that during his stay a better program to meet these conditions will be instituted, and that his services will be utilized for the training of the professional staff.

While leprosy and filariasis are endemic in the territory, they have not reached alarming proportions, and a long-range program was instituted toward the reduction and elimination of these conditions.

Department of Education 

This department experienced little administrative change in the turnover from Navy to Interior. Its usual replacement of teaching personnel involved staff members' becoming acquainted with their new surroundings, making adjustments and eventually establishing a smooth running educational program. The wage study and job classification resulted in an overall increase in Samoan teachers' salaries, which was much needed due to the sharp increase in the cost of living.

Admissions to the first year of school continued to rise, reflecting the rapid increase in population growth and posing problems for the near future of shortages in elementary teachers and school facilities. 

In a stepped-up educational program during the year, 14 new schools and 12 living quarters for teachers were built entirely by village labor. The schools now total 51 in the territory, including 4 junior high schools and 1 high school. The 4,162 students enrolled in private and public schools during the school year 1951 increased to 5,116 in 1952; 3,861 of these were in government schools. It is estimated that 5,675 will be enrolled in the 1953 school year. There are 156 teachers presently employed by the government.

At the beginning of the year the teacher-training program was placed under the active direction of the director of elementary education so that the training program could be closely coordinated with the actual operating program of the village and district schools. This was done in spite of the fact that the students in the teacher-training program were seniors in the high school. The number of course offerings in any one institute is to be reduced and all courses will run for the entire length of the institute. A subject matter reading program for teachers during the school year is being initiated.

Department of Agriculture 

The change from naval to civilian administration brought about only slight adjustments in the Samoan Department of Agriculture.

Basically, the operations have continued without change, with improvement of the present economy, and further development of potentially valuable areas as the main objectives in a long-range program. 

In addition to the planning and development programs, the department has been active in various projects for protection of agriculture from insect pests. A comprehensive series of plant and animal quarantine regulations has been written, with due regard to the recommendations of the South Pacific Commission, and is now awaiting final approval before being placed into effect. As an effective weapon against pests now present in the area, several introductions of insect predators and parasites have been undertaken, notably the Coccinilid Rodalia cardinalis as a predator against Icerya seychellarum a scale insect which has been causing considerable damage to breadfruit and other plants. The Giant Mosquito (Toxorhynchites brevipalpus) 

was introduced as a possible control of various mosquito pests, although without much success to date. Further attempts will be made to achieve a successful introduction. Correspondence has been initiated with various organizations and individuals with the view of further introducing beneficial insects. Experiments were carried out during the period with a bacterial disease Bacillus popilliae against the Coconut Beetle Oryctes rhinoceros with encouraging results.

This of course was a controlled experiment, and the results may not be the same in the field. However, the results are considered sufficient to encourage further trials both in the laboratory and in the field. 

Thanks to the cooperation of the Hawaiian Board of Agriculture and Forestry, this department was able to make an introduction of a number of superior species of fruit trees. These were grafted specimens of several varieties of mango, avocado, lime, and litchee, which were placed at the experimental farm for development and eventual propagation. The department has also made several importations of planting material of superior varieties of coconut, cacao, and yam for distribution to interested Samoan planters. These materials were obtained from Western Samoa and from Fiji.

Considerable progress was made in the building up of the reference library in the department. The most important acquisition was a complete set of the USDA Yearbook of Agriculture from 1900 to 1951, which was obtained through the efforts of the Department of the I Interior. Other books and journals were obtained through purchase and subscription. This reference library is invaluable to the department, since there is no locally available source of reference information on agriculture.

Experimental farm. The experimental farm, operated by the de partment for testing and introduction of new plants, and also for experimental work on local crops and methods, carried on without interruption during the period. The Hawaiian Sugar Planters Asso-

✩ 

ciation, which had utilized a part of the farm area for experimental seedling work for several years, ceased operations in February of 1952. 

The land which they had utilized reverted back to the control of the department and will be used as a banana trial and selection project. 

The living quarters and other equipment used by the HSPA were purchased by the government and the farm and dairy manager has been installed in the quarters.

In order to cut down on damage to farm crops by roving pigs, and also on theft of farm produce, the area was completely fenced during the year. Previously, a three-strand barbed-wire fence enclosed the area, but this was not a deterrent to the pigs. The new fence is constructed of standard stock fencing, and has proven very effective against the pigs.

One distinct improvement at the farm during the period was the acquisition of a small flock of purebred Rhode Island chickens obtained from Fiji. These were obtained as the basis of a project for the improvement of local chicken flocks. It is planned to maintain this as a breeding flock, with sale of surplus cocks and hens to interested persons for improving the strain of their flocks. Considerable interest is shown by Samoan planters in obtaining breeding stock from this flock.

In conjunction with its function of experimentation, the farm acted as a field training area for students from the agricultural classes of the Vocational School of American Samoa. Approximately 100 stu dents each spent 1 day a week working at the farm under instruction and supervision of both American and Samoan instructors. 

The farm produced approximately 17,600 pounds of produce during the year, which was sold to individual customers at the government station. This provided an income of $1,331.75 for the general fund. 

Dairy.-The dairy was maintained successfully during the period. 

Improvements were made in fencing, and attempts were made to improve the pasturage. The pasture improvement programs were not entirely successful, since lack of adequate machinery prevented good preparation of the soil and the local weeds and grasses were able to overcome the introduced forage plants. This, it is hoped, will be remedied in the near future.

Department of Justice

The successful organization and operation of this department was impeded by the fact that during a substantial portion of the year the territory was without the services of an attorney general and, owing to a lack of funds, was without the services of a commissioner of public safety until late in the year. This lack of administrative personnel was particularly critical since the disestablishment of the naval station brought with it the abolishment of the traditional law-enforcement agency of the Government of American Samoa, the famed Fita Fita Guard, and necessitated the creation in its place of a wholly civilian police force.

Moreover, the vast volume of legal research directed toward the revision of the antiquated Code of American Samoa and the preparation and study touching on organic legislation and applicability of federal laws became a burden on the limited staff. However, following the arrival of a commissioner of public safety in January, the department got its work in hand and by the end of the year can be said to have made a modest but impressive beginning. 

The staff of the department included, in addition to the attorney general and the commissioner of public safety, a complement of employees that is exclusively Samoan, numbering 47. 

The policy of the department in administration emphasizes: A balance of the objectives of law enforcement, safety, and maintenance of individual rights; specialized training; and fixing of division, section, and personal responsibility.

Bank of American Samoa 

June 30, 1952, marked the thirty-seventh anniversary of the found ing of the Bank of American Samoa. The transfer of administration brought about many changes in the bank. Principal among these was the change from military personnel to civilian personnel serving on the board of directors of the bank. This change resulted in a differ ent attitude toward banking problems and a more realistic approach to the needs of the people of American Samoa. An outstanding result of this policy, as shown in the balance sheet, is the increase in the loan portfolio from $36,119.82 on June 30, 1951, to $67,254.02 on June 30, 1952, an increase of over $31,000. This increase does not indicate a less conservative loaning policy. The bank's policy is still very conservative, due to the conditions, but there is now encouragement of credit for business firms and individuals who are in need of funds and can comply with the loaning policy. It has been proven, after 1 year's experience under this new policy, that these additional loans have created revenue for the bank as well as having rendered a valu able service to the community.

✩ 

Another aspect of the transfer which affected the bank was the termination of various Navy activities such as operation of the commissary store, the ships store, supply department, and others. These agencies of the Navy had provided a large percentage of the people in American Samoa with foodstuffs and merchandise. With their closing it was necessary for local merchants to provide service on a greatly expanded scale, and larger inventories were called for. This accounts for the decrease, as compared with last year, of approximately $260,000 in commercial deposits in the bank. The funds withdrawn from the bank were used for imports, mainly from the United States. Under these conditions the bank found it necessary, in order to maintain a proper ratio between cash on hand and funds due from other banks, to sell $150,000 worth of United States Government bonds.

Savings deposits showed a sharp increase over a year ago of approximately $96,500. This was due in part to an increase in the interest rate paid, from 12 to 2 percent, and also to an educational program presented to the people of American Samoa. This program of having school children bring in their coins to start savings accounts was initiated in the high school and was extended to include other groups. 

The gross earnings of the bank for the current fiscal year were $36,305.87 as compared with $32,780.53 for the previous year. Gross expenses for the current fiscal year were $24,026.23 as compared with $18,500.75 for the previous year. The increased expenses for the current year of approximately $5,500 are accounted for principally by the increase in interest paid on savings deposits, the remodeling of the bank building, and the substantial loss incurred by the necessity of selling United States Government bonds prior to call date.

A regular dividend of $2,500 (5 percent on the capital) was declared and paid during the year. This dividend brings the total amount paid by the bank of the government of American Samoa in regular and special dividends since 1915, the date of establishment of the bank, to $77,075.

Department of Samoan Affairs 

The original planning for the new civilian government called for the employment of an anthropologist experienced in the Polynesian field to head this department. Because of insufficient funds, however, the services of an anthropologist could not be obtained, and certain administrative duties which this position called for were performed by the Secretary of American Samoa, who is not an anthropologist. 

These duties consisted of direct supervision and coordination of the three levels of local government, namely, the 3 districts, the 14 counties, and the 54 primary villages and village groups. Officials at these levels are as follows:

Three district governors, 14 county chiefs, 522 village chiefs (pulenu'us), 52 policemen (one for each of the above), and 3 district clerks (one for each district governor).

Only a very few local matters proved to be too complex for the local officials to solve, and even fewer local solutions had to be reversed or overruled by the Secretary. There are strong indications, developing from this experience, that the traditional Samoan system of matai leadership has retained much of its original capacity for self-government despite the unsettling intrusion of modern civilization. With patience and a good deal of self-restraint, it is believed that an increasing amount of self-government can be developed along these traditional lines, particularly as the effects of an ever-widening school system makes possible the selection of better educated matais. 

One county chief was removed for inefficiency following the recommendation of his district governor and an inquiry by the Governor. 

Numerous replacements of pulenu'us, police, and clerks also were required. The decisions of local officials and their various councils governed all action taken in these cases, provided their selections were found to be qualified under the terms required by law.
