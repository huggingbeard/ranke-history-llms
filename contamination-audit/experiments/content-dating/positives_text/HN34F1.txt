PREFACE 

The Strathmore Plan is an educational program based on the use of the formula test-teach-practice-test-reteach. It will supplement any course of study in English or Arithmetic which you are using. Grade levels are not indicated on any of the materials, so that a seventh-grade or an eighth-grade pupil who has failed to master any of the skills which are taught in the fifth or sixth grades, for instance, can work on them without feeling that he may be doing work on a lower grade level. 

The Teacher's Manuals of the Strathmore Plan contain a copy of each test, exercise, and practice sheet which the pupil will use for the testing, learning, and practice of English and Arithmetic skills. The areas of subject matter in the Teacher's 

Manual are divided into units of work, each of which is preceded by an inventory test to determine what skills in English or Arithmetic the pupil has not mastered. 

The pupil's record on each of these inventory tests reveals the skill on which he needs further study and practice. The pupil's work sheets which provide this necessary practice are indicated on the inventory test, and may also be found by consulting the Table of Contents or the Suggested Grade Placement Sheets in the Teacher's Manuals. 

Since the practice exercises are keyed to the inventory test, the pupil studies and practices only those skills on which he needs help. Following the practice exercises, there are reviews and a self-test to enable the pupil to determine his progress and his readiness for the mastery test. The mastery test completes the final check of that unit for both the pupil and the teacher. The program is thus flexible and timesaving. It meets the needs of all pupils on any grade level, and pupils with exceptional ability do not spend time on what they already know. 

How to Use the Strathmore Plan 

There are eight procedures suggested for each unit of material: (1) Give the inventory or pre-test (2) Teach to obtain understanding (3) Use appropriate practice material (4) Use review exercise (5) Use additional practice sheets if necessary (6) Use the self-test (7) Use additional practice sheets if necessary (8) Give the mastery test

Each teacher will find the best way of presenting the material to her group. It iii

Since a new skill should not be attempted until such understanding and mastery have been accomplished, the mastery test is keyed to definite exercises which makes it possible for the pupil to find the exercises on any learning step that he needs to study and practice again.

Accuracy should be emphasized. Before the pupil starts to do an exercise or a test, he should be sure that he knows exactly what is expected of him.

EXERCISE 30 The Library and the Card Catalogue

A card catalogue in a library lists all the books that the library has. It is called a card catalogue because each book is listed on a card. The cards are kept in little drawers. The cards are arranged in alphabetical order. 

Instead of going around the shelves of books looking for a certain book, you can go to the card catalogue, or card index, and see if the book is in the library and where it is on the shelves.

Rhythm

In music, there is a division of time or accent, as three beats to the measure or four beats to the measure. The first beat is accented, or sounded, a little stronger than the other beats. This gives a swinging movement to the music. 

Like the musician, a poet must think of repeating the strong beat at a regular time, as soft, hard, soft, hard, soft, hard. 

Example: 

"I'll tell' thee all' I can'; 

There's lit'tle to 'relate'. 

I saw' an ag'ed man', A-sit'ting on a gate'.

In the above example, each strong syllable is marked by an accent. Notice that the accent falls on every second syllable.

The regular repetition of an accented syllable is called rhythm.

EXERCISE 20 Singular Forms of Nouns 

You know that a noun is the name of something. Sometimes the noun is the name for one thing. Sometimes it is the name for more than one thing. "Single" means one. That makes it easy to remember that a singular noun names only one thing. 

A noun that names only one person, one place, or one thing is called a singular noun.

In the story below, draw a line under each singular noun. 

Did you ever have a burr that clung to your stocking? A burr is a strange-looking seedbox. It has many prickles, and each prickle has a little hook so that a burr can stick to things that touch it.

Once a tiny burr grew on a burdock by the side of the road. It grew and grew. 

It became very prickly and had sharp little hooks on it. 

Nearby was a tall milkweed. One day the little burr saw the milkweed seed open the door to its house and sail away on snowy sails. "Oh, dear!" thought the burr, "I'm grown up now. I will get ready to travel, too."

But the burr had no sail. It had no wings. "What shall I do?" it cried. "I know. My hooks will help me.' 99 So the burr watched and watched. And when a sheep came along, it caught on the wool with each little hook and clung there.

The sheep carried it on and on, away to another town. There the little burr fell off and lay on the ground.

The little burr grew drier and drier. "My! I feel ready to burst," it thought. 

Suddenly it did pop. Little black seeds hopped out and went in every direction. 

The next spring Mr. Frank, a good farmer, said to his son, "What brought all these burdocks to our pasture?" 

Perhaps you can tell Mr. Frank.
