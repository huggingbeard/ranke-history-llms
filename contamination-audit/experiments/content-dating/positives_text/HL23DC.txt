In the dying hours of the 1959 Wisconsin legislative session, May 27, 1960, the assembly by a vote of 80 to 1 killed a bill intended to gear Wisconsin income tax to federal income tax. The measure itself (Substitute Amendment 1, S., to Bill 621, S.) only that morning had won last minute passage in the senate by a 25 tó 3 vote. Its declared purpose was "simplifying state income tax by using federal income as the basis for levying such state tax...' 

Thus Wisconsin, which pioneered state income taxation in 1911, rejected the first attempt to join what appears to be a slowly spreading trend toward "federalization" of state income taxes, RISE OF THE CONCEPT 

There are now 8 states whose income taxes are tied explicitly to federal income tax. These include Vermont (1947), Alaska (1949), Kentucky (1954), Iowa (1955), Montana (1955), Hawaii (1957), Idaho (1959) and North Dakota (1959). Even as new states have been added to the list, however, others have fallen away. Prior to 1959,

Georgia, South Carolina and New Mexico revoked former state income tax laws based on the internal revenue code. Ptax In none of the jurisdictions currently embracing federal income tax standards is the income tax quite so vital a cog in state finances as it is in Wisconsin--but some come close. A table showing state tax collections for 1959, published in the "Book of States 1960-61", based on bureau of census tabulations, discloses that state income tax collections made up 44.3% of all Wisconsin state tax revenues in 1959. In Alaska, 39.6% of state tax revenue is derived from state income tax, 30.9% for Idaho, 29.7% for Vermont, 29.3% for Kentucky, 20.4% for Montana, 15.4% for Iowa, and 10.2% for North Dakota. No figures are available for Hawaii.

ORIGINS OF THE WISCONSIN PROPOSAL 

Substitute Amendment 1, S., to Bill 621, S., was essentially drafted for introduction by the state bar simplification committee-a group of 8 lawyers plus 5 advisory certified public accountants. 

The measure was grounded on the majority report and recommendations of the Governor's 11-man committee on income tax simplification, appointed May 14, 1957.

Purposes of the Governor's committee were stated thus: "The objective of this committee is to determine the feasibility of adopting in Wisconsin a simplified income tax based on the taxpayer's net taxable income as determined by federal law. "While there are some difficulties (differences in deductions) to be resolved before such a proposal could be adopted in Wisconsin, it has been found in other states greatly to facilitate the preparation and filing of state tax returns by eliminating the conflicts and duplications of effort resulting from differences between federal and state law.

CHANGES PROPOSED 

Fundamentally, Substitute 1, S., to Bill 621, S., proposed to: 

1-Base all Wisconsin income taxes, both individual and corporate, on federally defined "taxable income" and "adjusted gross income 2-Use a series of adjustments to reject federal exemptions of interest on state and municipal securities, federal treatment of capital gains and federally allowed capital loss carry-overs while preserving state exemption of federal bond interest, state exclusion of income with an out-of-state situs and adding the comparatively new federal authorization to small business corporations (not more than 10 stockholders) to report as partnerships. 

3-Offer a special tax break to wage earners with no more than $100 in reportable income from interest by allowing taxpayers whose affairs are uncomplicated by capital gains, capital loss carry-overs, property entitled to depreciation or out-of-state income to apply state tax rates to 90%, instead of 100%, of their adjusted gross income.

Among the changes contemplated by this bill, one of the broadest in scope and most easily understood is the benefit which homeowners would enjoy because they now may deduct property taxes on residences from their current computations for federal tax purposes but not for state income tax. This proposed bill, of course, would pick up that federally authorized deduction for state purposes too. 

An opposite result would be reached in treatment of federal income taxes paid. Wisconsin now permits individuals to deduct up to 

3 per cent of net income, and corporations up to 10 per cent, prior to subtracting charitable deductions. This deduction would be wiped out by the proposed bill, since there is no federal deduction for past federal income taxes paid. 

Other changes which would be effected by this switch to federal definitions include repeal of Wisconsin-excluded alimony and the first $1,000 of armed forces income, revocation of state exemptions for retirement benefits received under state teachers' retirement and several Milwaukee retirement plans, and repeal of Wisconsinallowed accelerated amortization of farm milk houses and water pollution treatment plants.

Delegation of Power 

The Wisconsin line of demarcation on delegation of legislative power was laid down in State ex rel Wisconsin Inspection Bureau v. 

Whitman, (1928) 196 Wis. 472, 220 N.W. 929, as follows: "The power to declare whether there shall be a law, to determine. the purpose or policy to be achieved, and to fix the limits within which the law shall operate is vested in the legislature and may not be delegated; but when the legislature has laid down the fundamentals of a law, it may delegate to administrative agencies authority to exercise such legislative power as may be necessary to carry into effect the general legislative purpose. 

The Whitman case involved a statute delegating to the state commissioner of insurance the power to review rate schedules fixed by insurance rating bureaus and to veto portions thereof for the purpose of establishing reasonable insurance rates. 

Powers which may be so delegated include the power to "fill up the details". Gibson Auto Co. v. Finnegan, (1935) 217 Wis. 401, 259 N.W. 420. 

Note that under proposed s. 71.015, quoted above, the "purpose" of the bill was explicitly set forth, along with a general statement of the limits of the bill. 

Whether the Wisconsin view of the power of an agency to "fill up the details" could be stretched to include the bureau of internal revenue as that agency cannot, of course, be certain without a court test.

"An income tax law so drafted would eliminate biennial consideration of an action upon each federal amendment, although the legislature could consider any particular amendment and, if it is not desirable for Wisconsin, appropriate action could be taken. In the Committee's study of comparable legislation in other states, it was noted that Kentucky and Iowa, in 1954 and 1955, respectively, adopted income tax laws based upon the federal law in effect at the time of the enactment of the new state law. On the other hand, Vermont and Montana, in 1947 and 1955, respectively, adopted the more practical approach approved by this Committee. Going one step further, Alaska adopted in 1949 an income tax which is a percentage...of the federal income tax. 

The Committee does not favor the dependence of state revenue on federal tax receipts which results from the Alaska method. WHAT HAVE OTHER STATES DONE? 

The questions which are merely implicit in the defeated Wisconsin bill proposing to tie state income tax to federal definitions have become concrete in states where such proposals were enacted. 

Alaska is the only state which unequivocally ties its state income tax to the actual federal tax paid, currently levying upon individuals a tax of 14% of their federal tax and upon corporations a tax equalling 18% of their federal tax.

The Alaska statutes not only make federal tax collected under the internal revenue code "as amended or as hereafter amended" the controlling base for Alaska income taxes but also specifically incorporate by reference "rules and regulations promulgated by the United States Commissioner of Internal Revenue, or hereafter so promulgated...unless and until the (Alaska) Tax Commissioner promulgates specific regulations in lieu thereof...

In what is one of the most frequently cited decisions concerning - 15 -

LRL-IB legislation by reference, Alaska Steamship v. Mullaney, (1950) 12 A 

594, 180 F 2d 805, the ninth federal circuit court recognized the right of local legislatures to incorporate federal law "now in effect" as beyond question, and further declared that the words "as hereafter amended" are a valid method of insuring the future validity of future incorporations by reference in Alaska income tax law. 

Aside from considerations of constitutionality, there are practical economic reasons why other states have rejected the Alaska method of designing state income tax to be a mere tail on the federal income tax dog. By avoiding use of the federal dollar tax as a base and tying state income taxes merely to the "core" of the federal tax, i.e., "adjusted gross income" and "taxable income", legislatures have retained their independence of federal control of state purse-strings.

A sharp cut in federal income tax rates during an off-legislative year, for example, could result in a sudden and unwelcome drop in state revenue for Alaska--but not for those states which have steered clear of such a close relationship. 

A sort of wary arm's length uniformity was first espoused by Kentucky and Iowa and is the type of relationship between federal and state income laws which has been adopted by the newer converts to state income tax "federalization"--Hawaii, Idaho and North Dakota. 

Iowa in 1955 switched from an income tax base roughly resembling that now in effect in Wisconsin to one tied to "adjusted gross income as defined in the internal revenue code of 1954. Iowa and Kentucky make no attempt to include any federal legislative afterthoughts by reference. Instead, both legislatures update their references from time to time. At present, Kentucky defines the internal revenue code as meaning the IRC "in effect on January 1, 1956." The 1959 Iowa legislature updated the reference in Iowa income tax law so as to refer currently to the internal revenue code of 1954 "as amended to and including December 31, 1958.

Idaho in its new 1959 income tax revision followed the Kentucky- Iowa method and even chose the same December 31, 1958, cutoff date that Iowa selected.

Hawaii's sweeping 1957 tax act, which became law by the hard route of overriding a veto, explicitly bars inclusion of future federal changes. The Hawaii act states that "internal revenue code" means "the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, as amended as of the date of enactment of the (Hawaii) Income Tax Law of 1957 (June, 1957), as it applies to the determination of gross income, adjusted gross income and taxable income... Subsequent amendments to the code shall not be operative for the purposes of this chapter.

Whatever the dubious value of incorporating optional versions of definitions in the internal revenue code, both past and present, which was attempted in Substitute 1, S., to Bill 621, S., it is pretty clear that delegation of legislative power and its handmaiden, legislation by reference, are firmly embedded in the Wisconsin legislative process.
