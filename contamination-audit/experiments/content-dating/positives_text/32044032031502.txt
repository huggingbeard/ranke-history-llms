EXPEDITING THE PUBLICATION OF STATISTICS OF INCOME INTRODUCTION 

The compilation of Statistics of Income is required by section 6108 of the Internal Revenue 

Code. This section is as follows: 

Sec. 6108. Publication of Statistics of Income. The Secretary or his delegate shall prepare and publish annually statistics reasonably available with respect to the operation of the income tax laws, including classifications of taxpayers and of income, the amounts allowed as deductions, exemptions, and credits, and any other facts deemed pertinent and valuable.

Under the above authority, the Commissioner of Internal Revenue publishes part 1, which relates to statistics from individual, fiduciary, gift, and estate tax returns, and part 2 which relates to statistics from corporation returns. Statistics of Income has been published by the Commissioner of Internal Revenue since 1916 in accordance with section 21 of the 1916 act. 

The purpose of this study is to find ways and means of expediting the publication of Statistics of Income in order to enhance its usefulness.

A. USERS OF STATISTICS OF INCOME DEPARTMENT OF COMMERCE 1. The Office of Business Economics 

The Office of Business Economics relies on Internal Revenue Service tabulations as a principal statistical basis for its estimates of many important business indicators, including key components of the national income and product accounts. Among the latter may be mentioned the following series: 

Corporate profits.

Net income of unincorporated businesses and professions. 

Rental income of persons. 

Business inventory change. 

Depreciation on business property.

The Office of Business Economic's Industry Survey series on business sales and book value of inventories, and its estimates of the sources and uses of corporate funds, and the size and State distributions of personal income also rest in greater or lesser part on Statistics of Income. 

While these cases involve extensive use of other source material in combination with the tax return data, the latter are essential for the present level of accuracy and usefulness of the OBE estimates mentioned. These estimates, it may be noted, are basic not only to OBE's own analyses of business conditions and trends but also to studies of great practical importance by other public and private agencies. Substantially all of them are used by the Joint Economic Committee, Council of Economic Advisers, and many business corporations in appraising the general business situation and outlook. In addition, the particular series are standard statistics for the subjects they cover, and as such they have specific uses of considerable significance; to cite two examples, the series on corporate profits plays a material part in the current projections of Federal revenues for budgetary purposes, and the distributions of personal income geographically and by size are widely used in market studies. 

2. The Business and Defense Services Administration Statistics of Income are used by the Business and Defense Services Administration as a frame work for estimating the probable level of changes in tax revenue which would result from revisions of the Internal Revenue Code contained in proposed legislation or in the tax revision proposals of the Department; e. g., changes in corporate tax rates, as an aid to small business, changes in capital gains rates, changes in the exemption level for individuals, etc. These data are also used as a base for the preparation of estimates of the probable level of revenue receipts during a mobilization period. 1

The Office of Distribution has a vital interest in the data assembled in Statistics of Income, in connection with its program of collecting, analyzing and disseminating information on distribution costs. Because of the importance of the marketing function in sustaining a high level of production, business activity and employment, accurate information on distribution costs is essential to those engaged in manufacturing and distribution, in reducing costs and so strengthening the entire system of distribution.

In its studies on distribution costs and in answering numerous inquiries directed to the Department on this subject, this Office has frequent occasion to make use of and reference to the data contained in Statistics of Income, part 2, pertaining to operating statistics based on corporate income tax returns.

The value to business firms of this type of statistics contained in Statistics of Income cannot be overestimated. This need finds constant expression in the many inquiries received by the Depart ment of Commerce. 

4. The Bureau of the Census 

With respect to Statistics of Income the Census Bureau has a general interest. Many millions of dollars would be added to the cost of the 1958 economic censuses if the current names and addresses of FICA taxpayers were not readily available to the Census Bureau, as they were in connection with the 1954 censuses. Together with the FICA lists, the business income tax returns filed by companies with no paid employees made possible the complete elimination of a field canvass in connection with the 1954 economic censuses. 

THE BUREAU OF THE BUDGET 

From time to time Bureau staff uses the Statistics in preparing the Bureau's reports or in reviewing proposed agency reports to congressional committees on pending tax legislation. Also, the Statistics furnish information which is sometimes incorporated in descriptive materials written on various aspects of Federal finance, such as speeches by Bureau officials, Bureau publications, internal studies, and replies to letters received from the public. 

As the Federal Government's statistical coordinating agency, the Bureau uses the Statistics of Income in assessing the reliability of the current economic indicator series, notably manufacturers' sales and inventories and corporate profits, that use Statistics of Income as a benchmark or are based in some other way upon Internal Revenue Service materials. Statistics of Income also provides a basis for comparing and integrating statistics based upon enterprises as the reporting unit with statistics based upon establishments as the reporting unit. 

The Bureau has been aided considerably in its efforts to minimize reporting burdens on business by the fact that these statistics provide a comprehensive body of annual enterprise financial statistics that need not be duplicated. Relatively low cost to the Government and no additional reporting burden on business are occasioned in producing these statistics. An outstanding illus tration of efficient byproduct use occurred in 1955 when the tabulation of information from some small business income tax returns for 1954 made it possible to conduct the 1954 Census of Business for $1 million less than it would have cost otherwise and with no reporting burden on those small business firms.

THE FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION 

Executive Order 10544, dated July 12, 1954, authorizes the inspection of corporation income tax returns by the Federal Trade Commission "as an aid in executing the powers conferred upon such Commission by the Federal Trade Commission Act of September 26, 1914 (38 Stat. 717).' 

The Commission's statistical staff subsamples the samples of corporation income tax returns which flow through the Statistics Division of the Internal Revenue Service. The Commission's subsample of 8,000 manufacturing corporations (out of a grand total of 120,000) is used by the Commission to obtain quarterly financial statements from a scientifically selected cross-section representing all the various operating circumstances present in manufacturing industries and all sizes and types of manufacturing corporations. To these are added the quarterly financial statements compiled and collected by the Securities and Exchange Commission from 1,500 manufacturing corporations listed on a national stock exchange. These statements, in summary form only, are published jointly each quarter by the Federal Trade Commission and the Securities and Exchange Commission.

The summaries contain quarterly estimates of selected income and balance sheet items-sales, costs, tax liability, profits, liquidity, operating ratios, and the like-for all manufacturing corporations, for each of 23 manufacturing industries, and for each of 7 sizes of manufacturing corporations.

They are designed to meet the needs of all Government agencies and are used, among others, by- 

(1) the Senate and House Select Committees on Small Business and the Small Business 

Administration for evaluating the current financial position of small business, (2) the Joint Committee on Internal Revenue Taxation, the Treasury Department, and the Bureau of the Budget for estimating current tax liability of and future tax receipts from corporations, (3) the Joint Economic Committee and the Council of Economic Advisers for evaluating current economic conditions, (4) the Department of Commerce for estimating national income and gross national product, (5) the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System for measuring corporate working capital and related financial factors in its analyses of money flows in our economy and in its evaluation of the general financial condition of corporate enterprise. 

The published quarterly summaries are also used by many executives- (1) to measure efficiency and appraise cost, price, and selling policies by comparing a company's operating results with the average performance of companies of similar size or in the same line of business, (2) to determine whether or not to undertake new ventures by comparing the profitability of various types of business activity, and (3) as a guide to the relative movement of sales and profits in order to reduce controversies in wage negotiations. 

THE DEPARTMENT OF HEALTH, EDUCATION, AND WELFARE 

The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare makes use of the Internal Revenue Service Statistics of Income and related tabulations for a wide variety of purposes. The usefulness of these data would be increased greatly, in many cases, if the tabulations were available more promptly. 

Certain uses of Internal Revenue Service data 

Medical expenses and voluntary health insurance.-Data on medical expense deductions by gross income class have been used as one source of background information on variations in the burden of medical care spending.

Information on the total number of returns and number of exemptions have been combined with information on returns on which medical deductions are shown in order to analyze and evaluate various legislative and other proposals for subsidizing expanded health insurance coverage through amendment of the income tax.

Revenue estimates.-Data on number of returns, adjusted gross income, exemptions and salaries and wages have been used from time to time as a basis for rough approximations of revenue loss or additional revenue take involved in specific tax law changes of concern to DHEW. 

Earnings of physicians, dentists, etc.-Statistics of income when classified by occupation, provide useful indicators of variations in both gross and net income of physicians, dentists, etc., if returns for individuals are supplemented by data from partnership returns. 

Expected operations and status of old-age and survivors insurance trust fund.-Internal Revenue 

Service statistics are one of the series used in preparing and checking short-term forecasts of the operations and status of the OASI trust fund, even though the concepts are not strictly comparable. 

Private philanthropic contributions. Internal Revenue Service data on contributions reportedboth individual (on itemized returns) and corporate-provides one gross measure which is useful in estimating expenses for health and welfare from philanthropic contributions. 

Studies of State by State variations in Federal tax burdens.-Internal Revenue Service data on wages and salaries and tax liability by States have been used to approximate the inter-State variations in Federal individual income tax burdens. Tabulations of dividend and of interest income have also been used in such studies. 

Tax collection data by Internal Revenue districts.-Information now released by the Service on tax collection data by internal revenue districts are used fairly extensively in discussions of problems of intergovernmental relations.

THE DEPARTMENT OF AGRICULTURE 

The Department of Agriculture makes annual estimates of the Federal income taxes paid by the farm population and maintain a series showing the amount of such taxes paid each year beginning with 1941. The procedures for making these estimates are involved and, because current data have not been available from the Internal Revenue Service, the Department has been unable to use the Statistics of Income directly in making annual estimates. However, the Department

THE DEPARTMENT OF LABOR 

Statistics of Income of the Internal Revenue Service is used in the Bureau of Labor Statistics as a general source reference in the design and analysis of its survey data on family income, expenditures, and savings. The area data have been used in conjunction with Department of Commerce 

State per capita income data and OASI county data in the stratification of urban places for the selection of the area samples. The distributions and averages of income, sources of income, and tax liability are used to appraise the distributions and averages obtained in the family surveys, and, in conjunction with other data, as the basis for adjustments in the use of the survey data to estimate aggregate income, before and after taxes. The ratio of wage and salary income to total personal income serves also as a check in evaluation of survey reports. The information on tax liability and exemptions is of help in the development of concepts and procedures for the city worker's family budget and similar studies.

State data from the Statistics of Income would be extremely useful if available early, especially to the Bureau of Employment Security. For example, at the present time consideration is being given to problems concerning unemployment insurance for dependents. Data on the number of exemptions tabulated by States would be valuable in estimating the probable impact and costs of such programs. Furthermore, it would be desirable to be able to relate gross income to wages and salaries by States.

THE CONGRESS 

In addition to the use of the Statistics of Income by the above Government agencies and departments, the staff of the Joint Committee on Internal Revenue Taxation uses these statistics as the basic material in making revenue estimates for the taxing committees of Congress. Revenue estimates prepared by the staff pertain not only to changes in tax laws but also to the total revenue receipts that may be expected by the Federal Government. The revenue estimates also serve as a guide to the committees of Congress responsible for appropriations of Federal funds. The Treasury 

Department, moreover, is responsible for making estimates of anticipated Federal receipts for the President's annual budget and these Statistics of Income data are an important element in the preparation of such estimates. These statistics are also used by the Treasury in making revenue estimates of the Administration's tax recommendations to the Congress. Any additional expense incurred in expediting these data will certainly be justified by their increased value to the Congress in deciding revenue measures.

The Statistics of Income data also serve a broad public interest. Many business concerns, trade associations, State and local tax administrations, private research organizations, and educational institutions use these data in formulating tax policy, business decisions, and studies of income and wealth. The many letters and requests for summary industrial detail received by the Internal 

Revenue Service are an indication of the frequency and range of this use. Thousands of the completed publications are printed and sold to the general public each time they are released. 

In view of the above, it is hoped that the Congress can see the importance of an early compilation of the Statistics of Income data and the urgent need for these data as soon as possible after the eturns are filed.

1. Multiplicity and variety of the work

The publications include information from hundreds of items appearing on the various tax returns: individual income, forms 1040A and 1040; sole proprietors and farmers, schedules C and F; corporations, mutual savings banks, life insurance companies and personal holding companies, forms 1120, 1120L, 1120M, and schedule PH; partnerships, form 1065; fiduciaries, form 1041; gift, form 709; estate, form 706; and exempt organizations, forms 990, 990-A, 990-C, 990-P, and 990-T. 

The complex tabulations showing the relationships of these items require the annual publication of about 500 pages of statistical tables. 

2. Byproduct of an administrative function 

The Internal Revenue Service produces Statistics of Income as a byproduct of its regular tax enforcement work, thereby substantially reducing the duplication of burden on both the public and the Government compared with any other method of obtaining comparable data. By adapting the statistical work with minimum disruption to administrative processes, the resulting data are obtained at relatively low cost. In doing so, however, the statistical processes and time schedule are necessarily dependent upon factors such as a form designed for tax enforcement and district office procedures designed to service the taxpayer. 

3. The volume of work 

The Internal Revenue Service uses probability sampling in assembling the data. Even with this economy the number of tax returns processed each year in the National Office for statistical purposes exceeds 500,000, drawn from over 60 million returns filed in the 64 district offices throughout the United States. 

4. The processing cycle

The production of each publication covers a span of three years despite the use of modern statistical techniques, such as electronic processing, sampling, and careful planning and phasing of all compilation steps. Striving for quality within the limited resources available for the purpose, the 

Internal Revenue Service has built its procedural steps around the normal flow of work in the district offices and the need for an even workload in the Statistics Division. The long processing cycle as described in detail in appendix A results primarily from the foregoing conditions. 

C. PROBLEMS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 1. Coverage of the present publications 

The staff recognizes the broad public interest, Government and private, that is served by these publications. A number of tables, however, have limited value to the staff in its work. There is no doubt but that certain portions of the publications are more urgently needed than others. 

The staff recommends that the Internal Revenue Service initiate a thorough-going review of the uses of the data, consulting with users in and out of the Government to establish priority with due

consideration being given to each major class of user. Unnecessary tables should be eliminated. 

Low priority tables may be published at less frequent intervals. A limited number of selected items shown to be essential to major uses of Congress and the executive departments should be given top priority for early release. 

2. Accelerated release of selected items 

The staff recommends that the Internal Revenue Service explore the possibility of very brief, quick tabulations of selected items. It believes that such tabulations would be of tremendous importance and help to the taxing committees of Congress and to the Treasury Department if these data could be compiled within 6 or 8 months after the returns are filed. Such tabulations should be limited so that only one punch card would be necessary, as a means of expediting the processing. Further, to make tabulation easier and less time consuming, the items should be selected from the first page of the return, if possible. The items selected for this program could vary from year to year as determined by representatives of the Treasury Department and the Joint Committee on Internal Revenue Taxation. Concerning items of individual income tax information, the staff would suggest at this time, for example, the following items as being very helpful immediately: (a) Number of returns. (b) Amount of adjusted gross income. 

(c) Number of exemptions. 

(d) Amount of salaries and wages. (e) Amount of tax liability. (f) Amount of sick-pay exclusion. 

Amount of dividend credit. (h) Amount of retirement credit.

Such a tabulation, no doubt, would increase the cost of operating the Statistics Division of Internal Revenue Service which, therefore, should study and recommend how these data can be readily and economically tabulated. Early availability of returns for this kind of statistical processing might involve photographing or tabulating in district offices. 

3. Changes in methods of statistical processing

The staff recommends that the Internal Revenue Service continue to seek new methods of assembling data so as to reduce the time period between the availability of the returns and the publication of the complete reports. In the past statistical processing by the Service has been spread over an entire year because of the economies which this procedure permits with respect to maintaining a constant supply of trained employees and a constant amount of equipment and space. While economy of processing should, of course, be maintained, it is not the only objective to be considered. It is recommended that the Internal Revenue Service set a goal for the completed publication to be available within 18 months after the filing period covering a tax year. This may require the development of new procedures, such as one to meet the peak workload that occurs soon after the returns are filed, further use of electronic data processing equipment to speed summarizing, and expansion of the use of Internal Revenue Service service centers for special processing may be helpful in solving the peak workload problem. 

4. Resources to meet costs

A substantial speedup in the processing of statistical data does require spending of more money for this purpose. In response to requests from_executive agencies for both new items of information and accelerated delivery of the data, the Internal Revenue Service has included in its budget request additional funds for fiscal 1958 to meet a part of this program calling for the tabulation of selected items of information relating to corporations, partnerships, and sole proprietorships. The Internal Revenue Service should continue to consult with both the executive and legislative branches of the Federal Government in order to plan, budget, and meet the urgent needs for information available from tax returns.

5. Modified statistical procedures throughout the Service 

The Internal Revenue Service should take steps to recognize the official needs for statistical data from tax returns and give this work sufficient priority at all levels of operations to permit successful accomplishment of the objective. The Service has already taken a few steps looking toward shipping of returns from district offices at an earlier date. The design of tax forms and the filing requirements have been governed principally by considerations of tax administration and enforcement. The staff recommends that the Service give careful consideration in these matters to changes that would improve timeliness of the statistics. The staff recommends, for example, that the Service determine methods of obtaining corporation data for earlier tabulation. In conference with interested parties, both inside and outside the Government, the conclusion was reached that a vast majority of corporations now make carbon copies or duplicates for their own records and therefore it would not impose too great a burden to require the filing of an original and a duplicate of their income-tax returns. This was the policy some years ago. Because of the recently liberalized provisions for extension of time of filing by corporations, an increasing number of such returns are not available for inclusion in an early tabulation of corporation returns. The 

Service should also seek a solution to this situation, possibly by requiring a limited amount of information to be provided by such corporations on their application for an extension. Such changes can be accomplished because authority under the Internal Revenue Code is presently given the Secretary of the Treasury, or his delegate, to request of taxpayers such data as he deems necessary to carry out other provisions of the law that he must enforce.

APPENDIX 

The following is a brief outline of major steps involved in the preparation of Statistics of Income by the Statistical Division of the Internal Revenue Service. 

In this description a hypothetical tax year, 1957, has been used for illustrative purposes. The dates and estimates of elapsed time represent goals based on resources and procedures that are now available and assume a minimum amount of delay that might be caused by program changes or operating difficulties.

I. The major expenditure of funds for processing 1957 tax returns will occur in fiscal year 

1959. The budget request for the fiscal year 1959 must be explored in the spring of 1957 for formal submittal to the Budget Bureau in midsummer 1957. If changes proposed for tabulation of 1957 returns have budgetary implications, their nature must be sufficiently definite at this time to permit adequate justification for budget consideration. 

II. Discussions with users of the data must take place in July, August, and September of 1957 to determine specific changes which are recommended for the tabulations to be derived from 1957 returns, even though such changes do not involve additional costs. 

III. Samples must be designed in September, October, and November 1957. Based upon the description of the proposed tabulations, the sample must be designed to provide adequate data and make use of administrative procedures proposed for use in district offices for the processing of 1957 tax returns. A proposed change in tabulations or district office processing frequently changes the sample design. 

IV. Instructions to the district offices must be written in November 1957 to be issued no later than December 15 stating how to select the tax returns beginning January 1 of 1958 for transmittal to the Statistics Division. 

V. In November and December of 1957 the final specifications for the proposed publications must be prepared. These specifications consist of table outlines, with titles, colmun headings, and stubs completely described. 

VI. In December 1957 and January and February 1958 all materials to be used in the processing of the tax returns for statistical purposes must be prepared. For instance, punch cards must be designed and ordered in December 1957 in order to be available for use in connection with 1957 tax returns which start to be processed in February of 1958. Other materials must be prepared such as transcript or abstract forms and operational manuals for use by editors, coders, and punch operators. Orders for new, changed, or additional equipment must be placed for delivery in time for use. Methods for recording and reporting the receipt, progress, and disposition of work must be devised. Costs of changed procedures must be translated into a proper allocation of manpower to the several functions such as coding, punching, tabulating, and typing.

VII. From January to April of 1958 operating personnel must be trained. 

VIII. The bulk shipment of 1957 tax returns may begin as early as February 1958. The operations within the Statistics Division on these tax returns are as follows: 1.

Mail room opens the shipment and verifies the contents against the invoice, February 1958-March 1959. 

2. Each return is examined in a "segregating" operation to reject returns that have been erroneously selected, to separate returns requiring editing from those that do not require editing, to make other classifications that either simplify processing or are required to meet tabulating specifications, and for the preparation of route or control slips used in subsequent operations, February 1958-June 1959. 

3. The industry coding unit assigns an industry code to those returns involved in business, March 1958-June 1959. 

4. The editing unit prepares transcripts or abstracts of those returns for which a permanent record is desired or which are filled out in such a way that direct punching is impossible, April 1958-June 1959. 

5. The statistical coding unit assigns statistical codes either to the returns, abstracts, or transcripts to translate name or quantity designations into numeric codes, April 1958June 1959. 

6. The punching unit prepares and verifies punch cards, May 1958-June 1959.

7.

The punch card files unit receives the punch cards and files them in groups in preparation for tabulation, June 1958-June 1959. 

8. The returns must be released at various preceding stages to the mail room which prepares appropriate invoices and ships the returns back to the district offices, March 

1958-July 1959. 

9. The flow of returns from the district office varies by type of return. For instance, since the estate tax returns are filed relatively uniformly over the year, they flow into the Statistics Division at a relatively constant rate throughout the year. Since the majority of the individual income tax forms 1040 are filed in the first 4 months of the year, they tend to flow into the Division beginning in February 1958, peaking in the summer, with final shipments arriving in January 1959. The corporations returns form 1120, on the other hand, are involved in fiscal year and extension of time of filing problems, so that those for 1957 flow into the Division starting prior to February 1958, peaking somewhat in the summer of 1958, with the last shipment received in June or July 1959. 

IX. Specific tabulating plans, such as sorting machine instructions, wiring diagrams, and 

Univac instructions should be devised in the months of July to December 1958 so that they are ready for application to the completed punch cards in the spring of 1959. Because of the difference in the flow of returns from the district offices by type of return, the date for completion of punching will vary by type of return and, therefore, the date for starting the tabulating or summarizing process will vary. Depending upon tabulating capacity and the availability of completed punch cards, tabulation of parts should be completed between April 1959 through December 1959. Tabulating steps are spread over a number of months because they usually are sequential. For instance, there must first be a sample count tabulation upon which is based the expansion factor. Following this the weights are applied to the individual records and the weighed records obtained are summarized. Frequently two summaries involve crossclassifications of the same records. In this case one summary must be prepared after the other. 

X. By this time there might be quite a spread in the timing of the output of tables from various types of documents. The manual compilation unit, therefore, tends to be occupied from May 1959 through February of 1960 in reviewing the tables for internal consistency, making corrections and translating machine table outputs into tables with titles, column heads, and stubs that are readable by the users of the data. 

XI. The tables are next reviewed by professional statisticians to assure conformity with original specifications for coverage, sample design, and table design. Figures subject to excessive sampling variability are suppressed and footnotes and text are written. These processes are subsequent to, but relatively concurrent with those in step X and, therefore, some tables are completed in the fall of 1959 permitting limited release of data to some executive and legislative agencies.

XII. The final step consists of (1) preparation of copy for the printer, and (2) printing. 

Printing cannot take place until the most laggard of the tables has gone through the previous steps and been prepared. Submission of copy to the printer, therefore, is scheduled for the spring of 1960 and delivery of the printed copies expected in the summer of 1960.
