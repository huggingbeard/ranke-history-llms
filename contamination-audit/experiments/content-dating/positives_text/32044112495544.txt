FOREWORD 

Before beginning the story of the New England People, it may be well to glance at these first settlers and their surroundings as they appeared in the Western World, weary with watching for the seemingly never appearing land, and worn with strife against the ever rushing ocean waves.

When their sad eyes saw for the first time the land they approached, it was a barren shore of wave-lashed sand dunes against a background of dark, towering trees that met their view-a desolate wilderness, a landscape not pleasing to the eyes. But to those weary wanderers, who, for seventy days had stood on the rolling deck of a small sailing vessel tossed on the restless ocean waves, it was alluring; it was a haven; it was home; and it may be truthfully stated that there is no spot on American soil so sacred to Americans as that which those pilgrim feet first pressed. Miles Standish, or any of his companions may, or may not, have stepped on Plymouth

Rock or touched the sacred stone, but it is there today as a memorial of the place where that "little band of exiles moored their bark three hundred years ago." 

Inspired by that love of freedom inherited from their ancient ancestors-the "free-necked men" who dwelt on the banks of the Elb-those intrepid people, left friends and native land, not on a voyage of enterprise or discovery; not with any hope of gaining wealth or dominion; but for the determined purpose of establishing a government of freedom in the little-known land of America.

FOREWORD 

This people, descended from the hardiest stock of history, to accomplish their high purpose, deemed no hardship that a three-thousand-mile voyage across the Atlantic could bring to them too severe to be borne: no dangers of a desolate wilderness too great to be faced. 

Thus this fearless people, fired by the noblest passion of the human race-love of liberty-expressed in the immortal words of Patrick Henry "give me liberty or give me death"-that same spirit manifested by our fathers, yours and mine, at Concord Bridge-and again at Gettysburg, overcame all obstacles, reached the shores of America, and laid the cornerstone of freedom in that compact drawn up in the cabin of the Mayflower, on which they have built here a nation among nationsfirst in war, first in peace-mightiest in wealth and power, that justly merits the proud title "Land of the 

Free and Home of the Brave."

The New England People Builders of America CHAPTER I THEIR ORIGIN 

Let us trace briefly the course of this sterling people, these nation builders, descendants of the Saxon and the Dane. As we glance back from this end of the line we are led to exclaim, "What a people! and what a course!" Can we tell the story? We fear not fittingly; but we will do the best we can, promising to tell the truth, though not the whole truth. No one individual could know or tell the whole truth of the trials and triumphs of this mighty people in its upward and onward progress of three hundred years from Plymouth 

Rock to present America.

Lord Macaulay in a speech in the House of Commons spoke of the New England Colonists as "those men of high and ungovernable principles who preferred leaving their native country and living in the savage solitude of a wilderness, rather than to live in a land of prosperity and plenty where they could not enjoy the privilege of worshiping their Maker freely according to the dictates of their own conscience."

These words of Macaulay provide a suitable text for this book and at the same time describe briefly and fittingly the character of this people. 

By "New England People" is not meant the rank and 13

file of the inhabitants of the New England States as at present constituted. As here used, the classification will refer particularly to the pioneers of this part of our country-the Pilgrims and the Puritans as they landed on American shores, principally at Plymouth and Boston in the early part of the seventeenth century -and their descendants.

There is another classification that might be made, viz; those people composed of the many nationalities that have come to America in more recent years, largely from the countries of central and southern Europe, usually termed foreigners, and their descendants. 

The distinction may be illustrated by the story of an Englishman, who, after spending some time in this country, remarked that he was greatly surprised at the large number of different nationalities represented in 

America. He said, "Now you know in England we have only four: the Scotsman, who is proud of his religion and careful of his cash; the Welshman who prays on his knees on Sunday and on his neighbors during the rest of the week; the Irishman who never knows what he wants and is always fighting for it; and we Englishmen, who are self-made men, and worship our Maker!"

To get a true perspective of New England people, it is necessary to trace their origin far back into the dim past, even beyond the dawn of recorded history-back to the stone and bronze ages.

In the words of the historian Montgomery, "all the progress which civilization has since made is built on the foundation which they (these prehistoric men) slowly and painfully laid during unknown centuries of toil and strife."

The first appearance in history of the English people from whom the New Englanders descended were three

15 tribes, the Jutes, the Angles and the Saxons, living between the Baltic and the North Seas along the Elb. It may be stated briefly that these tribes, joined by common blood and a common speech, united into a confederacy and mingled into one race. They entered 

Britain about 500 B.C. They took the name of the strongest tribe, Angles, pronounced "English" by the

French; hence the English people. These tribes were offshoots of the Celts who have been characterized as a "large limbed, fair-haired, fierce-eyed people." 

They were an agricultural people who enjoyed small communities but hated cities. Their leader was the free land-holder, whom they called the "free-necked man," whose long hair floated over a neck that had never bowed to a lord. These people loved freedom and independence.

They were later joined by the Danes, a hardy race much like themselves. The Danes' courage and independence is expressed in their song: "I trust my sword, I trust my stead 

But most I trust myself in need." 1 The mingling of these hardy tribes, reared in a rigorous climate, enured to toil and hardship, developed the best and strongest qualities in mankind, and produced a stalwart race that was the foundation of the English people. It is not surprising that from such a source should spring a race of strong hand, clear brain and resolute purpose, unsurpassed by any other people on the pages of history. 

The traits of every race of people have remained constant characteristics in their onward march down through the ages. Just as the Negro has retained the distinctive characteristics of his forebears who dwelt

on the banks of the Nile, even to the color of his skin, his woolly hair and his mirthfulness; so has the New 

Englander retained the physical and mental characteristics of the aboriginal race from which he sprung who dwelt on the banks of the Elbe. 

Wherever he appears he exhibits the same characteristic energy and enterprise as was shown by his sturdy ancestors of the north.

Wherever you find the New Englander or his descendants, whether in Maine or Minnesota, in Tennessee or Texas, you will find the same spirit to investigate, to invent, to achieve. To his energy and initiative is due, in no small degree, the opening up and development of the rich areas of the Middle West, the great states of the Pacific coast and the mighty Southwest. 

In every state of the Union you may find his familiar face and hear his Yankee drawl. You will note also the work of his head and his hands.

The plow that turned the first furrows on Kansas' fertile fields was a New England plow, held by New 

England hands. The first gold coined from California's mines jingled in New Englanders' pockets. The first screech of a locomotive beyond the Rockies was a New 

England battle-cry, calling to arms the captains of industry to rally their forces and invade the Golden West to gather the wealth thereof.

In fidelity to their religion, the English people compare not unfavorably with the Hebrew race. In mental development, if they have not attained to the culture and philosophy of the ancient Greeks, they have progressed far in education and reasoning. If they have not shown the war-like spirit or thirst for world dominion of the Romans, they have founded a nation whose domains the sun never sets on, and that has won the proud title "Mistress of the Seas"; whose soldiers march in

17 every clime and whose "morning drum-beat, following the sun, and keeping company with the hours, circle the earth with one continuous and unbroken strain of the martial airs of England." The English people, though they have been stern, and, sometimes, cruel in the administration of law, have developed a constitutional government more efficient, just and liberal than any other people of the Old World. Though shrewd and exacting, they are social, kind and courteous. They are typified by Scott's characterization of King James: 

"Blue-eyed Saxon, proud of your liniage."

From these people, proud of their liniage, (and well they may be) came the pioneers that landed at Plymouth and Boston in the early seventeenth centurythe progenitors of the New England People. 

The sterling qualities of body, mind and heart of their ancient ancestors who dwelt on the shores of the Baltic and the Elbe; and the characteristics of industry, enterprise and energy of their more recent ancestors in Britain, transmitted to them down through the centuries have produced in the New England People a race predominant among the nations of the world; and these qualities inherited and imparted to succeeding generations, have brought forth here the American people, unsurpassed, if not unequalled, alike in the sphere of industrial achievement and in exploits on the field of battle by the people of any other land or time. 

In all the annals of human history no people had a humbler or hardier origin or were destined to produce so far reaching results on the progress and well-being of humanity as that little band of pioneers who, after a voyage of seventy-two days in a small sailing vessel across the stormy north Atlantic, landed on American soil in Massachusetts Bay on a bleak December day in 1620.

Those people as leaders and their descendants have produced here in America more wealth and developed a greater power in three hundred years than have any other people anywhere else on the earth's surface in three thousand years; and they have established a freedom that has unleashed powers of the human mind never before known or suspected. Listen to their story -a story of toil and tragedy, of wit and wisdom, of humor and humanity-a story of action and achievement.

CHAPTER II THEY REACH AMERICA 

"What sought they thus afar? 

Bright jewels of the mine,

The wealth of seas, the spoils of war? 

They sought a faith's pure shrine."

These brave adventurers, these stalwart sons of a hardy race, came to a wild and unknown land, not seeking wealth or dominion, although they were destined to gain both. That love of freedom and independence that characterized their ancient ancestors led them forth, especially the desire for freedom of worship. 

In England they were persecuted for worshiping according to their own belief and compelled to pay a tax to support a creed they did not accept, and denied the privilege of their own faith.

These people, armed with a fortitude that had come down to them from their fierce-eyed forefathers, undiminished through the centuries, and fired with the same love of independence that distinguished the old freeholder, whose neck never bowed to a lord, determined to find asylum in a foreign land and establish a government of free men where they could worship according to the dictates of their own conscience without let or hindrance.

Therefore in 1608, about a thousand of the more self-reliant and resourceful among the many who were suffering religious persecution in England, crossed the North Sea to Holland, to join a people having a reli- 19

gion like their own and where they believed they would have more religious freedom than in England. 

These were not of the lords and nobles, nor were they of the peasants. They were from the great middle class the class that has constituted the strength of nations everywhere in all the world's history. 

After twelve years sojourn among their religious friends in Holland, they found themselves circumscribed in too narrow limits. They wanted more room; room to exercise their energies, their enterprise and their industry. They realized, too, that their children were learning to speak a foreign language, acquiring foreign habits and customs, and saw that they would grow up to be Dutchmen, not Englishmen; and they decided to emigrate to the then little known land across the wide Atlantic-America.

Consequently, in July, 1620, a company of some two hundred of the most enterprising and venturesome arranged to establish for themselves a colony in America. 

Two ships were to make the voyage—the Speedwell and the Mayflower. After much delay the Speedwell was found to be unseaworthy and was left in Plymouth,

England; but the crew of the Mayflower, although they knew that at this season they faced the fury of the 

Atlantic's equinoctial gales, were ready, and, on September 16, 1620, the Mayflower spread her sails to the wind and headed for the shores of the New World!

That small ship of only 180 tons burden, bearing a company of one hundred as intrepid and enterprising souls as ever set foot on any ship, inspired by that old Danish spirit still flowing in their veins, began a voyage of three thousand miles across a tempestuous ocean, a voyage that was to take them, not to waiting friends, but to an unknown wilderness. 

What a people! and what a mission! A people

21 nobler than they thought, and on a mission destined to produce results beyond their wildest imagination—an enterprise that meant more to the world than did the sailing of Æneas' ships from the coast of Troy.

This company was composed of artisens and farmers, mostly poor men, workers, as distinguished from the merchants and adventurers not brought up to manuel labor, who had emigrated to America and begun a settlement at Jamestown in Virginia in 1607.

For more than two months this little band of fearless, determined people, buffeted by wind and waves sailed on their solitary way across a little known waste of waters, until on November 21, land was sighted!

Let us, you and I, imagine ourselves members of that company of anxious watchers, who, for sixty-six days had been tossed on a stormy sea and for many days had been looking patiently, and, finally, almost hopelessly, for sight of shore, suddenly roused by a cry from the lone lookout at the mast head, "Land ho!" What a thrill must have run through that throng of weary wanderers at the sound of that long hoped for call! We can imagine those pious people falling upon their knees, and, with tears of relief and gladness streaming down their stern faces, offering up thanks to Him whose guiding hand had led them safely across that trackless, boisterous ocean.

These people on the Mayflower had displayed an indomitable courage and an unfaltering faith unsurpassed in all recorded history. 

But hardship and danger still awaited those patient, persistent people that would still further try their courage and their faith.

They had crossed the ocean and they knew their weary eyes now saw America! What part of it they did not know. They had intended to land near the mouth

of the Hudson River and join friends from Holland who had already established themselves in the New World. But adverse winds and currents, and, who shall not say, Destiny, had swept them in their frail craft from their course two hundred miles to the north of their intended destination; and the land they hailed was the stern, rock-bound shore at the point of Cape Cod, where Provincetown is now located. 

They were unable to make a landing there, but remained anchored for a month while their leaders in a rowboat explored the shore. Finally they made a landing at a place across the bay which they named Plymouth from the town that received their last lingering glance as they sailed out of sight of their native land! 

Their landing place was a bleak austere shore in the wilderness where: "The breaking waves dashed high on a stern and rock-bound coast, And the lofty pines against a stormy sky their giant branches tost, This was their welcome home." 

No friends to greet them, no human habitations in sight. no sound save the monotonous roar of the waves, and the melancholy moaning of the pines, in a rigorous climate at the beginning of winter. The prospect was not alluring. A less persistent people would have immediately returned to Holland, if not to England: it is easier crossing the Atlantic from west to east than from east to west. They were a people generous in victory and unconquerable in defeat and no obstical could stop them or turn them back.

Among them were wise William Bradford and redoubtable Miles Standish. These were people of stout heart and determined purpose who could not be dis-

CHAPTER III THEY FOUND A GOVERNMENT 

In the cabin of that wave-battered ship, the Mayflower, these determined, devoted men drew up articles of agreement for a government and chose one of their number, John Carver, for Governor. They established then and there the all important principle of American Government, that public office should not be determined. by inheritance, wealth or social position, but by a free and untrammeled electorate. "Here struck the seed-the Pilgrim's roofless town, Where equal rights and equal bounds were set, Where all the people equal-franchised met; 

Where doom was writ of privilege and crown; 

Where human breath blew all the idols down: 

Where crests were nought, where vulture flags were furled, And commen men began to own the world!"

Thus there on that desolate strand in a wilderness of woods and water was laid the foundation for a great nation-a nation destined to become wealthier, more powerful, more benign than the world had ever known; a government that for the first time in history recognized that "all men are created free and equal." 

How well the superstructure has been built upon that foundation let this great nation, the United States of America, bear witness. A government that guarantees to all its citizens absolute freedom in the exercise of all 25

their just rights, protects them in their property and in their person; that protects and fosters enterprise and industry; that aids in developing all natural resources that help in harmonizing the forces of nature, and applying them to human use, to make easier and happier the lives of all mankind; that provides every child with a common school education and fits all who care to enter the higher institutions of learning. 

That little ship, Mayflower, must have brought across the ocean a considerable cargo, not only the more than one hundred persons, but there must have been much merchandise. If we are to believe that all the household furniture was aboard that is now claimed of historic value as having come over in the Mayflower, the Mayflower would need to have been as large as the 

Queen Mary! But there must have been no small amount of household goods-beds, bedding, clothing, cooking utensils. They must have brought large supplies of provisions, for it would be nearly a year before vegetables could be grown.

The artisans brought the tools of their trade; the farmers their farm implements. They came prepared to build houses, to clear away the forest and cultivate the soil. Did they bring live stock?-chickens, sheep, cows, hogs and horses? All of these would be needful, and if they were brought there would be need of fodder to feed them until forage could be grown. 

They brought guns and ammunition that they might supply themselves with meat from the game-deer and wild turkeys.

This was no idle or pleasure excursion. It was a voyage of discovery that meant the building of a nation and was undertaken by men of sound sense and good judgment who well knew it meant hard work and sturdy endurance. We may be sure it was well and carefully

These hardy pioneers built a huge hut to shelter themselves during that first winter. That building had to be hastily constructed, for the weather was cold, as we, the present people of New England, well know, who have felt the penetrating east wind along the 

Atlantic coast in December.

That building was by no means an apartment house, such as we have today. It was built of rough logs, not of brick, but of trees felled in the forest; heated by a big open fireplace, not by steam radiators. It must have been quite commodious to accommodate one hundred persons. But however much it may have been crowded at first, it must be sadly recorded, there was plenty of room before spring, for half their number died that winter. This fact bears solemn evidence to the suffering and hardship born by those indomitable people. 

It might reasonably be expected that the Puritansthat is, the remainder of them, after suffering so many hardships during that first trying winter, would have returned to England at the coming of Spring. But, embued with those sterling characteristics; independence, love of liberty and determination that had come down to them from their ancient ancestors, the Angles, the Saxons and the Danes, they remained in the land to which they had immigrated; undismayed by the rigorous climate and forbidding shore, determined to establish a free government under a free sky; and no tears dimmed their eyes when they saw the good ship Mayflower sink from sight beyond the horizon on its return to their native land. They had no regrets, no hardships

brought tears; only when a benign Providence smiled upon them and brought relief to their suffering did tears course down their faces, and then they fell on their knees and gave thanks to Him who notes the sparrow's fall. 

They had come to stay! And I believe, if but two had survived, a man and a woman, they would have remained, a second Adam and Eve, and undertaken the peopling of the New World. And they wouldn't have done a bad job either. Those early settlers were a prolific people. It is recorded that some years later, four families in the Kennebec Valley, in Maine, raised sixty children, and one family in that region reared twenty-five children!

The extent of the land upon which these-the hardy pioneers in a new world-had settled, and its boundless natural resources were entirely beyond their wildest imagination. The early English settlers believed the distance across the land from the Atlantic to the Pacific to be only about two hundred miles, and an expedition was sent out from Jamestown to discover the Pacific Ocean!

These intrepid people, who had established themselves on this side of the Atlantic, had not the minutest conception of the vast region extending three thousand miles over a wealth of woodland, fertile plains and "o'er mountains of gold" to the Pacific Ocean. Of course, only a small part of that vast area was then controlled by England, and thus available to them. But with all its boundless wealth in millions of acres of fertile soil, in deposits of the precious metals; of iron, coal and oil, and great water power, it was destined to become a possession of theirs and their descendants, embracing within its limits every variety of healthful and productive climate; to become under their control

Despite the tenacity and courage of the first settlers and the desire of others in England for independence of government and freedom of worship, at first people came slowly from the motherland. In 1630, ten years after the arrival of the Puritans at Plymouth, the 

Colony numbered only three hundred. But immigration increased from that time, so that in 1634, the number in Plymouth Colony had reached 4,000, and, in 1670,-fifty years after the landing of the Pilgrims -there were 8,000 people in Massachusetts, occupying twenty towns.

They had established the township where in townmeeting all public questions were discussed and settled; where the rate of taxes was determined; all money for public expenditure was raised and appropriated; and where all citizens voted in the election of public officers. 

Here in Massachusetts for the first time in history was established that government epitimized in the immortal words of Lincoln, "Government of the people, for the people and by the people," and thus here early in the settlement of New England was established the town-meeting, still an important institution in government of these six states.

It is not so much what these early settlers did that renders their record brilliant. It is their intrepid spirit that adds lustre to their everlasting glory. They possessed the stuff that men are made of: it is that fibre, strengthened and toughened by the union of those three ancient tribes, and that old Celtic spirit that never bowed to a dominating power, which we have inherited from them that makes New England People what they are-the most adaptable, the most indominitable people in the world.

They are like that "Noble Roman in Rome's imperial day, Who heard a coward croker before the castle say, "They are safe in such a fortress, there's no way to shake it,' 

Replied, 'I'll find a way or make it.'

The New Englander has always found a way or made it.

CHAPTER IV THEY ESTABLISH THE SCHOOL AND THE CHURCH 

One of the leading characteristics of the New England people has ever been a love for learning, and, realizing the importance of education in a free government, in 1635 the people established the public school, providing that every child should be guaranteed an education; and, realizing also the essential need of higher education, three hundred years ago Massachusetts

General Court voted 400 pounds, equivalent to $10,000, to establish a college in what was then Newtown, now Cambridge, and, on receipt of 750 pounds from John Harvard, named the institution Harvard College, the oldest institution of higher education in America.

Today Massachusetts, the first colony established by Puritans, contains within its limited boundaries more institutions of higher education than any like area on the earth's surface!

This system of public schools begun by the early settlers in New England has had an important influence on public morals and the maintenance of law and order. 

It has produced a greater number of readers than any other country has ever known; as many newspapers and magazines are published in the United States as in all of the rest of the world. In no other nation is education and culture so widely diffused, thanks to those early settlers in New England!

The public school system, established by the early 31

settlers in New England, now extends to every state in the Union, and has produced an intelligent, educated electorate that resists every insidious attempt at dictatorship, but insists on a free representative government which it has maintained for three hundred years, and which is now stronger than ever. 

The religious instinct has been a strong propelling power in all peoples during the whole world's history, leading to disputes, often to beheadings, to burnings at the stake and sometimes to armed conflict. It was the prime motive that actuated the persistent Puritans in the settlement of New England. The English, as we have seen, were descended from a people who loved liberty and independence and who were possessed of a determined purpose. These attributes are characteristics of all English people, but they were especially strong in the Puritans who had suffered severely on account of their religious belief, and who were impelled and sustained by these sterling qualities of mind and heart that had lost nothing of their force in transmission through a long line of ancestors, and, who, like their ancient forebears, had themselves been enured to toil and hardship.

It was that religious instinct, coupled with that other natural instinct implanted in the human breast, desire for liberty of thought and action, possessed by a people of unconquerable spirit, that brought the founders of a nation to the shores of Massachusetts Bay and held them there despite any impelling power of adversity tending to influence their return to England, when the 

Mayflower made her return voyage; and that failed to dim the eyes of those who remained at Plymouth when they saw the good ship that had brought them return without them!

CHAPTER V THEY WIN THEIR INDEPENDENCE 

Instead of following the well-known history of the early settlers of New England it may be more interesting to take a retrospective view of the first one hundred and fifty years of American life, from the landing of those sturdy pioneers at Plymouth in 1620 to the Revolutionary War.

A wilderness, inhabited by savage beasts and still more savage men, had given place to thirteen colonies -extending twenty-five hundred miles, from the Bay of Fundy, occupying a space along the Atlantic coast, two hundred and fifty miles wide, to the Gulf of Mexico, inhabited by 1,200,000 white people, owning 800,000 Negro slaves-under organized government. 

Where wild waste and savagery had abounded, now fertile farms spread their broad acres to the sun, and busy industry attested the prosperity of an intelligent, contented people. A degree of prosperity not contemplated by the early settlers.

Forests had been felled, and potatoes, corn and wheat had been grown on the burnt land. In later years that land had been cultivated and yielded bountiful crops! 

Pleasant farm houses adorning the hill tops, flocks and herds grazing in the valleys, and waving fields of grass and grain, attested to the contentment and happiness of an industrious and intelligent people. Water power had been developed, villages had been located at convenient community centers, cities had been built along 35

water ways that provided power and transportation. 

There was little poverty and want; all were well-to-do; some had accumulated wealth. Fortune smiled on a vigorous, law abiding, peace loving people : but a people jealous of their just rights, resolute and ready to defend them. "Law abiding and peace loving," yes-but unwilling to obey arbitrary British orders and unafraid of 

British bullets.

The planters in the South, especially in Virginia, the first settled English colony in America, owned extensive estates and many slaves. They had accumulated for tunes, lived in stately mansions and rode in coaches. drawn by a span, sometimes by four, fine horses. They, those Southern people, were then, and ever have been, a gallant, courteous, hospitable people; but brave, independent, unwilling to yield to any arbitrary domination. They were staunch friends but bitter and dangerous enemies. Though accustomed to leisure, nevertheless, like their New England neighbors, they, too, were alert and ready to defend their just rights.

In the New England large towns and cities were people who had become wealthy in manufacturing industries, commerce and shipbuilding. They, too, lived in fine houses, furnished with expensive furniture. 

Both these classes of people, those of New England and of the South, though different in their recent ancestry, and in their business activities, and in their social relations, were alike active, alert, energetic, enterprising and equally ready to resist any encroachments on their rights, among which they regarded as preeminent their sole right to levy and collect taxes on themselves and their property, and they strenuously resisted the efforts of a government to do so in which they had little interest and no voice.

These two peoples, though differing in modes of life

The history of the world is the history of war. From the days of the Pharaohs to the present time, great changes in national life have been marked by war. Or perhaps it is better to say war has caused great changes in national life. On the pages of history are stamped the names of great generals-Cyrus, Alexander, Cæsar, Napoleon, Grant. As the Revolutionary War was followed by important changes in the progress of the United States, so also did the Civil War produce great changes. 

War is sometimes a creature of slow growth, the fundamental cause preceding by a long period the immediate occasion or actual outbreak of hostilities. 

Such was especially the case in the war between the states. The seeds of civil strife were sown when the first settlements were made in America. Not that there was any hostility between the people of the North and South at that time, but the peoples of these two sections were distinctly different and were destined to take opposite views of social and public affairs. 

Those who settled at Plymouth were from the great middle class of the English people, who, through a long line of hardy ancestors, had been inured to earn their bread by the sweat of the brow; while those who formed the first settlement at Jamestown were from the nobility, and not accustomed to manual labor. 53

Therefore the Southern people, almost from the beginning of their settlement in America, bought Negroes imported from Africa and held them as slaves, and regarded them as their rightful property. But that any human being, white or black, should be held in bondage, religious, political or personal, was abhorant to every instinct of the liberty loving people of New England. 

Thus slavery became a matter of controversy in the early colonial days. But in those early days these two peoples were widely separated, with scant means of communication and knew little of what each were doing, had little in common, and thus the question of slavery, prior to the Revolution, while a matter of dispute, was not of paramount importance. Indeed, some people in New England then held slaves. 

Under the Constitution state rights and rights of private property were more definitely defined than they had been under the Articles of Confederation, and the Southern people claimed that under the clause that protected the citizen "in his person and in his property" they were authorized to hold slaves as their rightful property. But New England people believed that human beings could not be regarded as property and thus slavery became a matter of serious controversy.

An adequate food supply is essential to all living creatures and the human family requires not only food but clothing as well, and the farmer must supply the essentials for both.

From the days of ancient Israel the producers of food and raw material for clothing-the tillers of the soil-have been the most essential, if not the most honored, branch of human society. 

Whether in the valley of the Nile in the days of the Pharaohs, along the Merrimac in the days of the Puritans, or on the broad prairies of the West in our own times, the growing of grain has ever been an honorable occupation and the most important to mankind. 

It has, indeed, been essential to the existence of humanity.

From the early days in New England, when the 'farmer planted his grain on the burnt land, and harvested it with the sickle and threshed it with the flail, to the present time when he plows the prairie with the gang plow, driven by a power engine and cuts and threshes it in one process with the great reaper, the farmer has fed the nation, as he has done since the days of ancient Israel./

While the farmers have generally been a prosperous part of society, they have not become its wealthy members. There has been no piling up of great fortunes by the tillers of the soil. There are no Carnegies, no 61

Rockefellers or Astors among farmers. The amassing of millions is accomplished by the money changers in the market place, not by those who toil on the land.

The farmer has ever been a living example of industry and frugality, since the days of ancient Egypt, toiling unceasingly, and saving in time of plenty against the time of famine. Especially is this true of New 

England farmers. They have always been an honest, industrious part of the community; never "reaping where they have not sown, nor gathering where they have not strown.' ""

Their sons have gone to every state, not only developing farms and establishing industries in new sections, but they have provided journalists, lawyers, physicians, teachers, ministers and statesmen in the new states they established as well also as in the older settled communities. 

In the early days the New England farmer had little money and little need for it.

He had no rent to pay, furnished his own fuel, and produced most of the food for the family. His wife took the wool as he sheared it from the sheep, cleansed it, carded it, spun it into yarn, wove it into cloth, and, with her own capable hands, converted it into garments for the family and blankets for the beds. How could the mothers of large families perform all the duties imposed upon them! They prepared the food, kept the house and clothing clean, and sent the children to school. Their capable, willing hands were never idle: from early morning till late at night, they were never idle those loving, willing hands! You and I can remember how they bathed us and, when we deserved it, beat us: and how they soothed our aching heads when they were placed gently on our temples throbbing with fever! Oh, those mother's hands!

There were no holidays on the farm, save sometimes the Fourth of July, and even on that patriotic day regular farm duties had to be performed indoors and out. 

Sunday services were sometimes held in the nearby schoolhouse-there were churches only in the villages which were a dozen miles or more apart.

New England farmers have always been a religious people, and in some localities they were accustomed to hold what were termed "quarterly meetings," to produce religious revivals. Those meetings were held during slack periods of farm work, between spring planting and haying. They were held in some farmer's barn and usually lasted two or three days, always including a Sunday. The barn floor was swept and dusted clean. Seats were provided by placing rough boards on blocks of wood, and a crude pulpit was constructed of rough lumber. Several different preachers, known for their eloquence and ability to arouse religious fervor, delivered sermons at the services held during the meetings, which drew the people from a considerable region around. And thus was religious interest maintained in the rural communities. 

The farmers' implements were inexpensive, although they included carpenters' tools (for the farmer, in the early days, was a carpenter as well as farmer). He often had a workshop in which he constructed his sleds and sometimes his carts. He built his barn, his shed and often his house.

New England farmers are the most independent class of workers in the world. They do not work by the day or by the month. They work by the season: the planting season, the haying season, the harvesting season; and during the winter season they get out lumber for building purposes, and replenish the wood pile for the coming year's supply of fuel.

They are their own masters; while always busy they never hurry-except when they have hay ready to go into the barn and a shower is coming. Then look out!

They know how to manipulate a hayfork and then they make the hay fly!

The farmers, accustomed to management and to planning and directing affairs are intelligent, thoughtful people. They read the news, keep themselves informed on political matters and vote intelligently. When unprincipaled politicians get into office it is by the city's vote, not the farmer's.

While there are undoubtedly some dishonest farmers, as there are bound to be in all classes of humanity, there are no criminal organizations among them-no gangsters, no racketeers, and, outside of the regular parties, there have been no political organizations. There have been no Tweed Rings, no Tammany Halls, among farmers. Every man has gone to the poles and voted independently according to his own unbiased judgment. 

The farmers have produced some of the ablest statesmen, and more than once the chief magistrate of the nation has come up from the soil.

The farmers have always been cooperative in their work. In building a barn the raising of the frame was a festal occasion, in which neighbors from the region joined, wives as well as their husbands. A big dinner was provided that required many capable hands to cook and serve; and when heavy hewn timber had been fastened together with inch-sized wooden pins for a side of the huge frame, a crowd of sturdy men, some with pick poles, lifted one edge of a side of the barn frame and with many a shout of "All together! Up she goes!" that ponderous frame-work of timbers rose slowly, steadily into position. 

Many of those barns, sheltering stock and fodder to

feed them, have withstood the storms of one hundred and fifty years, and still serve their purpose and bear testimony to the thrift and industry of their builders. 

In the beginning those first settlers had to be farmers. 

Food must be grown to feed their families, the first essential to which was plowing the land. From that day to this farming has been an important part of New England life, as indeed, it has always been everywhere. 

And so of all the implements devised by man's inventive genius, none have been more conducive to human welfare and human happiness than the humble plow. 

In all ages of recorded history the plow has been a recognized implement of worth and importance. It has been used in prose and poetry as a symbol of industry, persistency and peace: as when it was said, "He who puts his hand to the plow and looks back is not worthy to be the ruler of a nation." And in the Scriptures, "When peace on earth shall come and the nations shall beat their swords into plowshares"; and in our own New England in the early days of the Revolution the farmer "left the plowshare in the mould, their flocks and herds without a fold, and mustered in their simple dress, for wrongs to seek a stern redress."

This is a poetic description of plowing: and plowing is poetic. A six-ox team, marching majestically across the field, directed not by bit and rein, but by word and goad, and the plow guided by strong and steady hands, throwing up the rich brown mould—is a picture and a poem-a picture by Rosa Bonheur, a poem by Holmes. 

The author may be pardoned for relating here a personal experience that illustrates life among New England farmers. 

In my younger years, it was my duty, I may saymy privilege, indeed, my pleasure, to hold the plow behind six sturdy steers, to break up pasture land. I say pleasure, for, although it required strength and endurance, it was a pleasure to follow the furrows behind that sturdy team day after day turning under the green. sod and laying the brown soil in smooth round ridges. 

I was interested in it all: the field, the teamster, J.

"The lord of earth, the hero of the plow."-Holmes.

W. Otis (a farmer of sturdy New England stock), and the steers.

I lived in the Otis family the preceding winter attending school, earning my keep caring for the cattle. On 

Saturdays when school was closed, I yoked those steers, hitched them to a sled, and drove them about the neighborhood breaking them for work. Now it was fall, and the crops were harvested and it was time to do the plowing for next spring's planting. We yoked the steers and proceeded to the job. But driving steers hitched to a sled over the highway was very different from teaching them to draw a plow in the field—the steers found it was-and so did we. 

The first day our team consisted of only four steers, as many as we believed could be successfully managed until they were somewhat trained. We began on a half acre in the field, that was not as tough as the six acres of pasture land that was to be plowed later. Otis was a good teamster and I was no novice at the plow handles. The steers were acquainted with him and knew his voice. But they were green in the yoke, they knew nothing about pulling a plow or following the furrow. We had good material, but it must be trained. 

Our performance was like taking husky college boys, ignorant of football, and turning out a team to win games. 

That first lesson down there on that half-acre was some performance! The steers were not used to pulling a load, but there wasn't much trouble in getting them to put their shoulders to the bow and pushing hard. At first they were a little frightened at that strange thing they were drawing behind them, scraping in the ground, and Otis was more concerned in keeping them from running away from it than getting them to draw it. 

But when they began to go too fast I put on the brakes

by thrusting the plow deep into the ground. However, we managed to keep our team on that half-acre, and between us-Otis, the steers and myself-we managed to plow that piece of ground-after a fashion! I don't know whether doing the job should be charaterized as a circus performance or a rodeo. When it was finished it was difficult to determine whether the furrows ran lengthwise of the field or crosswise! 

The next day we took the team out to begin breaking up the pasture land. This was tougher plowing, and we used six steers. Otis hitched a span of horses on ahead of the steers to keep them in line. With one hand he held the reins to guide the horses, in the other he wielded the goad to drive the steers. With the horses leading the way, the steers soon learned to obey the word of command and the motion of the goad. 

Three yokes of oxen and a span of horses made an imposing, and rather unwieldy, team. After the first day we left the horses in the barn. The steers had learned when Otis shouted, "Gee Star!" and waved his goad to the right, to turn to the right; and, the leaders would go round until the off one stepped into the furrow, and then, to follow it. After plowing round a few times turning to the right-or "back furrowing"on reaching the end of the furrow, Otis commanded "Whoa hish, Star, Haw to!" and waved his goad to the left. The team then turned to the left, and the leaders came round so that the off steer entered the furrow and again followed it.

When we got to that stage, the plowing was rhythmic, it was poetry.

This brief description of plowing is typical of work on New England farms in the early days, and was much the same for many years.

It illustrates also New England rural life. The

country boys worked on the farm in summer; attended the district school in winter; and some of them later went to college.

It was a life that has produced a race of men, strong physically and mentally, industrious, enterprising—a race unsurpassed for the task of world development and progress by the people of any other nationality in history.

The New England farmers are not peasants or serfs as is the case in many countries. They are captains of industry, independent in thought and action. They own their farms, and manage them. No more intelligent, independent, industrious, reliable class of people are to be found in this, or any other country of the world. 

The New England farmers have become the nation's farmers. New England plowshares have turned the turf in every state from Massachusetts to Oregon, from 

Maine to Mexico.

Since the early days of New England settlement, the farmers have been the pioneers. They led the way along the Mohawk Valley to the Ohio and opened up the 

Northwest Territory. They crossed the Mississippi and established the nation's hold on the Louisiana Purchase; they crossed the Rockies, and opened the Golden Gate.

The New England farmers, in the words of Lincoln, "Brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created free and equal." 

THE NATIONAL MEMORIAL 

Henry Ward Beecher, in his story of the history of our flag, said: "When a nation's flag is unfurled we see

not the flag merely, but the nation itself." True as that statement is, a nation's monuments tell the story of its people more truly and more impressively than does its flag.

Every nation in all ages of the world's history has erected monuments to commemorate its eminent men and memorialize their illustrious deeds. 

The Pyraids of Egypt were repositories of the mummified bodies of its heroic dead, and that strange monument, the Sphinx of Gizeh, bearing a human head on a beast's body, illustrating both intelligence and physical force, bears, as does the pyramids, unmistakable evidence that hero worship was a chief characteristic of the Egyptian people.

The famous Roman Amphitheatre, having a seating capacity for 87,000 people, adorned with Doric, Ionic and Corinthian columns, constructed to provide a place for men and beasts to fight to entertain the populace, whose massive masonary after two thousand years, still stands, provides ample proof of the warlike spirit of the Romans; while the many Grecian "monuments in the dust yet beautiful in ruin," render solemn testimony to the Grecian people's love of art and learning; and the Golden Image, that towered ninety feet tall, set up on the banks of the Tigris by King Nebuchadnezza, told the story of ancient Babylon's fabulous wealth and greed for gold.

As the monuments of other nations have symbolized their peoples, so does the majestic National Memorial, carved on Rushmore Mountain in the Black Hills of 

South Dakota by Gutzon Borglum, the greatest work of that eminent sculptor, in even a more perfect manner symbolize the American people, as it pictures there in granite a mile above the surrounding landscape, in incredibly vast size, the faces of the four presidents,

The National Memorial 

Here, engraved in granite, you see the faces of America's Master Builders, under whose direction was reared the structure of this nation on the foundation laid by the first New Englanders, with freedom as the corner stone.

Washington, Jefferson, Lincoln and Theodore Roosevelt, within the period of whose administrations of 130 years from Washington to Theodore Roosevelt, occurred the development and expansion of this nationthe period that produced American glory and American greatness.

Mr. Borglum at one time contemplated carving on the face of the mountain a five-hundred-word history of America, but abandoned the idea. There is no need to write that history in words. The faces, of Washington who won American Independence, of Jefferson who wrote that immortal document, the Declaration of Independence, and Lincoln who preserved the Union, emblazon up there on the mountain top in characters that all may read, "that sentiment dear to every true

American heart, Liberty and Union," and the face of Theodore Roosevelt, whose untiring energy and enterprise constructed the Panama Canal, that lifts huge battleships, eighty-five feet aloft and bears them over the ridge of the world from one ocean to the other, displays in equal glowing characters the story of American energy and industry.

It is the most gigantic sculpture ever made by the hand of man. The heads are carved on the scale of a man 465 feet tall, each face from chin to crown is more than sixty feet. The immense size of the figures is essential to give a due proportion to the faces, as they are shown at the great height they appear above the surrounding country.

In producing the monument, a force of 50 men, using modern methods and machinery in rock removal, have been employed 14 years, and have removed 400,- 000 tons of granite from the mountain. The sculptor, in his artistic work, has so accurately proportioned the features that those familiar faces are not only readily

recognizable but they bear a natural and life-like expression. 

It is an important and remarkable feature of the monument that a mountain should be located near the north central part of the country, so that the likenesses of these illustrious men appear to show them standing there, at a height of a mile, facing in a southerly direction, gazing in quiet dignity and calm complacency out over the broad land that is the United States of America, contemplating, as it were, with pardonable pride, the mighty nation they have builded. 

This Memorial does not mark the spot of an important battle, as does Bunker Hill Monument. Nor does it commemorate the name of an eminent statesman like 

Washington Monument in the city of Washington.

Nor does it memorialize an historical event as does the Eternal Torch that flames continuously at Gettysburg: but it symbolizes that noble sentiment of Liberty and Union that stirs and unites the hearts of all true Americans. And thus this mighty National Memorial visualizes the future as it memorializes the past, as those illustrious faces seem to be looking forward rather than backward.

When one views the National Memorial on Rushmore Mountain in South Dakota's Black Hills, he sees written there in enduring stone the stirring story of this great Republic; and a picture of that monument may well illustrate this book that tells the story of the New England People, who laid the foundation and whose energy and industry, diffused throughout America, have developed here the wealthiest, the most powerful, most benign nation the world has ever known.

The result of the lives and labors of these illustrious persons is beyond calculation if not beyond imagination. 

Their influence has extended beyond New England; it has been felt in all America, and in all the world. Their benign influence has spread to every civilized people. 

An orator like Webster whose eloquence and pathos could sway an audience as did his Bunker Hill address and his speech in the United States Senate in reply to Hayne, exerts an influence that extends far beyond those who hear his voice and beyond the country and time in which he lived.

The United States of America, cradled between the two great oceans of the world, lying almost wholly within the north temperate zone, containing a wealth of natural resources greater than is bestowed elsewhere on any like area of the world's surface, has become, during its one hundred and fifty years of existence, the most powerful, the wealthiest, the most benign nation the sun shines on.

But extent of territory, clemency of climate or wealth of natural resources, do not make a nation great or enduring. Nations in the past, existing under most favorable climatic conditions have gained great wealth and power, only to fall in decay and leave their sad ruins strewn long the corridors of time.

The only qualities that can render a nation strong and enduring are the spirit and character of its people. 

Love of peace and desire for justice; and power to enforce these principles, are the strong and enduring elements of a nation.

While this country is composed of many peoples from many lands, the dominating race in its founding and development has been the New England People.

CHAPTER XI CLIMATE 

The effect of climate on man is very remarkable, in both his physical and mental constitution, and has exerted the most important influence on the development and progress of the human race of any single element in the world; and its effect is quite as distinctive in man's mental as in his physical characteristics. 

The white-skinned, blue-eyed, flaxen-haired Saxon of the North differs from the black-skinned, dark-eyed, woolly-haired Negro of the tropics, scarcely less in physical appearance than in mental qualities. Thus has the different climates of the tropical and temperate zones developed in the human race the difference between the slothful, black people of the South, and their energetic, enterprising white brothers of the North.

As the high temperature of the tropics enervates the human being both mentally and physically, so also does the severe cold of the frigid zone, drawing, as it does, upon the vitality to maintain the necessary heat of the body, in like manner, debilitate the human being both mentally and physically; and thus peoples who have become most vigorous in mind and body are those whose native habitat has been in the north temperate zone, midway between the two extremes, under the stimulating effects of a moderately cool climate; and more especially, where there have been seasonal changes of temperature-principally between the parallels 30 degrees and 50 degrees north latitude. 85

Hence the nations that have accumulated the most wealth, developed the greatest power, and achieved the highest positions in philosophy, art and literature; that have produced the world's most eminent scholars, orators, generals, inventors and captains of industry have been those nations located in the north temperate zone, principally between the parallels 30 degrees and 

50 degrees north latitude.

Chiefly within this belt have been located the great nations of the ancient and modern world. Oceans and ocean currents have exerted an influence, by changing to some extent the climatic conditions; as on the western coast of Europe, where a warm equitorial current, flowing northward, gives to England a temperature not greatly different from that of the eastern coast of 

North America, ten degrees farther south.

Much has been said about the changeableness of the New England climate; but variety and change, sometimes sudden, and occasionally so great and sudden as to be regarded as almost violent, are conducive to both mental and physical vigor; although it is alleged that such sudden temperature changes cause colds. The New England People, at home or abroad, will be found also actively interested in every legitimate, profitable enterprise.

The sudden changes of temperature, caused by a cold northeast wind that blows down from the old Atlantic's arctic region, or a driving heavy snow storm that sometimes arrives unannounced, though unpleasant, like some bitter medicine, may, like the medicine, be regarded as blessings in disguise for the resistance to these sudden, and, at the

Another important factor in the development and progress of the human race is a water highway, that provides a means of trade and commerce. When the tide of empire turned westward and reached America it found the most ideal conditions in these two important factors of human advancement, viz., a favorable climate and the ocean, which, while it separated this land from the European nations, gave them commercial connection. Add to these favorable natural advantages a people whose energy and industry, inbred down through a line of hardy ancestors that date back beyond recorded history, and whose strength of mind and body has improved by that climatic influence that has produced the physical and mental vigor and energy that have been the essential elements of human progress from the dawn of time, and it is not surprising that the spirit of such a people, added to a love of freedom and independence, should have produced the wealthiest and most powerful nation of ancient or modern times. CHAPTER XII  

Industry may be broadly defined as employment in any legitimate, gainful occupation. 

It is the alchemy that has changed the raw materials of the earth-iron, copper, lumber, wool, cotton, into useful commodities for human use and adornment. Industry has produced the necessities, the comforts, the luxuries of civilization, and the wealth of the world.. 

When the first New England settlers landed on American shores and set up housekeeping, they at once engaged in gainful occupations. In clearing their land, in building their log cabins, and cultivating the soil they were as truly engaged in industry as were their descendants in later years, when they engaged in manufacturing cotton and woollen goods in the big factories they built to use the water power of their rushing streams. 

Every honest, human effort should be rewarded. The reward, however, can not be quite equal to the value of the result of the effort. There must be a profit on the product of the effort, an increment of gain, above the labor cost. This profit, this increment of gain produced by industry makes individual and national wealth. 

Transportation and service that place the products of industry at the convenience of the consumer are part of industry that add value to every product of the earth and they are entitled also to a reward.

The real estate broker who brings together the seller and buyer of a property gives an added value to the 93

property, and is entitled to a share in that increase. 

The clerk behind the counter who serves customers in the purchase of goods, by his service, adds value to the goods. The physician, who protects the health of his patients produces wealth in promoting health and strength for industrial effort. In brief, every worthy effort, mental or physical, not only earns a reward for service, but produces an increment of profit above the payment for service, which constitutes wealth. 

The raw materials of the earth,-base metals in the mines, timber in the forest, wool on the sheep, cotton in the field,—have no intrinsic value until touched by labor's magic hand. But when the alchemy of Industry lays its creative hand on these base materials they, as also the crude chemicals of the soil, are transmuted into commodities of worth-homes, clothing, golden grain and fruit for food.

In the busy hives of Industry have been produced the moderate incomes of the millions, the fortunes of the individuals, and the wealth of the nation. 

Huge fortunes-Rockefeller's in oil, Mellon's in steel, Ford's in automobiles, Hearst's in journalism, were produced by this increment of profit, resulting from physical and mental effort, and from the industry of millions of workers in all kinds of industries. In like manner has been produced this great nation, equal in extent of territory to the Roman empire, in the days of its greatest prosperity under Julius Cæsar-and greater in power and wealth than any nation of ancient or modern times.

Thus have the early settlers of New England and their descendants and those who have followed in their train under the stimulus of their industrial energy, transformed a wilderness into cultivated farms, busy workshops and thriving cities; and by their initiative

95. and enterprise produced the most powerful and richest nation the sun ever shone on.

This miracle of prosperity has been accomplished in the brief space of three hundred years-brief as compared with the thousands of years of old world history. 

It is the result of industry and the reward of industry: fostered and aided by a beneficent government that encouraged and protected the industries of this country by wise tariff laws from the cheap labor of Europe. 

But now has come a change in governmental administration. The government is providing unemployment compensation. Instead of protecting and rewarding industry it is paying for, and encouraging idleness. 

This is contrary to every law of civilized society, from Abraham to F.D.R., and in violation of the divine injunction "in the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread."

It is well that a grateful nation should provide for the aged and infirm and reward those who have rendered valuable service and endured personal sacrifice in their country's cause, but to compensate strong, healthy young men for doing nothing, is a serious injustice to the taxpayers who provide the compensation, and worse for the recipients of that unearned reward. 

It discourages industry and enterprise and is demoralizing to the youth of the land. There never has been a time in the history of this country when a young man who wanted to work need be long out of a job. This statement is made by one who has been through every business depression since the Civil War, has raised three sons, and has had a wide acquaintance with young people. 

From the days of the earliest settlers of New England the prosperity, progress and power of this nation has been built, and maintained on the industry and

energy of its young manhood. The youth of the nation as they have approached maturity, with zeal and energy, have engaged in gainful occupations. And with laudable ambition to provide for themselves and establish homes, they have engaged in new enterprises and developed new industries.

Paying men to do nothing stiffles ambition and encourages idleness. There are many able-bodied young men, who, if they can receive small pay for doing nothing, will make little effort to secure jobs, and the longer they are taken care of without working the less they want a job. Note examples: a young man, long out of a job, was advised to apply for work in a large manufacturing industry; he declined to make the application on the ground that the place of employment was too far from his home, although it was within a ten-cent carfare. A young man was offered a job at a salary of twelve dollars per week, but declined saying it was too small pay!

It is true that "labor conquers all things" and it is equally true that idleness is the parent of poverty. Paying for unemployment injures the individual, and strikes a blow at national prosperity.

It is authentically stated that the New England People have established within six closely knit states "more diversified industries per square mile than are to be found in any other like area on the earth's surface." Not only is diversity of New England industries unequalled, but their value is probably unsurpassed. 

Excepting automobiles, New England industries supply nearly every article of use and beauty that contributes to the comfort and welfare of humanity, from brass tacks to battleships, and from farm implements to the finest watches in the world.

The rugs that cover our floors, the sheets and

blankets that cover our beds, the clothing that covers our bodies, are products of the looms of Lewiston, Lowell, Manchester and many other New England cities and villages located wherever waterways produce power. 

To her manufacturing industries New England owes largely her material wealth, and her high educational standing as well, for her manufacturies have provided the funds for her public schools and contributed largely to her higher institutions of learning. 

In every city and hamlet of New England may be heard the busy hum of industry, providing employment for thousands of workers and producing millions of dollars in valuable commodities.

The New England People, descended from a hardy stock that had its foundation in a rigorous climate, have ever been, from the nature of their origin and the climatic character of their habitat, industrious, energetic, enterprising. Realizing the need of large capital in conducting big business on a profitable basis, they have combined their capital in cooperative efforts and thus have developed the natural resources of a land of vast material wealth. They have brought the forces of nature under control, and made them subservient to human use. With huge dams they have harnessed the force of rushing waters, and, not only used that force directly, but they have transformed it into electric power, transported it many miles and compelled it to turn the wheels of industry, changing raw materials into useful products, and thus multiplying many times their value, providing employment for many hands and producing profits for enterprise and capital. 

That every village has its factories is not because factories have been built in every village, but because

along every stream and river where water provided power to turn the wheels of industry, that power has been harnessed and factories have been built to use the power, and cities and villages have thus grown up around the factories.

Among the multifarious industries of New England the textile industry has been most potent in producing prosperity and wealth. It has been a great aid to the building industry, also, causing the construction of many mills, and building villages and cities, that contain the major part of the population and wealth of this section of the country.

The textile industry has been carried to other parts of the country largely through New England enterprise, especially to the South, where it has proved an important factor in providing employment and producing prosperity.

The New England People, ever on the alert to find better ways of performing every constructive operation, have made many useful inventions. Eli Whitney, although living in Georgia when he invented the cottongin, was a native of New England. 

Jacob Perkins, of Massachusetts, invented the first nail-cutting machine. Previous to the invention of this machine, all nails had to be pounded out by hand; but 

Perkins' machine could cut two hundred thousand nails a day. This machine was an important aid to the building industry. Perkins also invented a machine for calico-printing which greatly increased the demand for calico and enhanced also the use and value of cotton. 

Asa Whittemore invented at this time a machine for making wire cards for carding wool "which operated, and still continues to operate, as if it had a soul." 

These inventions and many others, including the application of steam power to locomotion, greatly

facilitating production and transportation, placed in the hands of an energetic, industrious people, have developed an expansion of industrial wealth and national progress and power such as the world had never before known.

Under the achievements of science the blind see again, the deaf hear and the dumb speak.

Among New England's many and varied industries, none have been more important and perhaps none more profitable than the fishing industry. 

In the days of the first settlers on New England's stern and rock-bound coast, the fine fish caught along the shore constituted an important, if not an essential part of the food supply, and the fine fishing fleet that has for three hundred years not only provided New England with an important food supply, but it has been especially conducive to the wealth and well-being of New England. The early Puritans found that fine food fish, the cod, so abundant in the waters along the New England coast and so valuable that they named that great hook of land that forms the eastern part of Massachusetts, Cape Cod. In the early years of New England spruce and pine lumber, cut in the forest and driven down the rivers, found a ready market in countries overseas, and its shipment proved a profitable business. CHAPTER XIII  

Trade, the exchange of commodities-that is, commerce, was carried on in the early days of the world's history-first by caravan over land, among the peoples of antiquity—the Babylonians, the Egyptians, the Chaldeans and other tribes.

We learn in the Old Testament Scriptures that Abraham "was very rich in cattle and in silver and gold." He might have acquired his flocks and herds by raising them in the fertile fields of Canaan, but his silver and gold he must have gained in trade with the Chaldeans on his journey from Er to the Promised Land and with the Egyptians during his sojourn in Egypt.

This overland trade, or commerce, conducted by the use of camels, was carried on with considerable profit between peoples as widely separated as Babylon and Egypt.

It was in this caravan commerce that traders from Babylon bought David from his brothers and took him with them in their long journey into Egypt and sold him into the hands of Pharaoh. Thus it is seen that trade in human souls, selling people into slavery, was a part of the commerce among the nations of the ancient world. 

The traveling traders took long journeys of six months or a year, carrying their merchandise hundreds of miles on camels and donkeys, buying and selling as they journeyed from place to place over regular routes 107

of travel. These nomadic merchants realized large profits exchanging the commodities of widely separated countries and many of them became rich as did Abraham.

But with the building of ships, commerce assumed a vastly more important aspect, and was entered into on a larger scale, and conducted by companies formed of merchants combining their capital in large enterprises that took the place of the ancient itinerant traders, and the sea became the great highway of trade and travel, and an important source of individual and national wealth. 

Carthage, the wealthiest and most renowned city of antiquity, advantageously situated on the southern shore of the Mediterranean Sea, that gave it sailing access to all the nations of the then known world, acquired its wealth and prestige largely through its maritime commerce. 

The Phoenicians, the Egyptians and other seafaring peoples acquired immense wealth in maritime commerce two thousand years before the Christian era.

The Romans gained the commercial supremacy which passed successively to the Phoenicians, the Dutch, the Portuguese, the Spaniards, and lastly, and naturally, to the English. We say "naturally" because the English people are closely related to-indeed descended fromthe Norsemen, those sea-kings whose roving vessels in the eleventh century entered every European port; and who are believed to have explored the coast of North America from Labrador to Manhattan Island; and 

Englishmen inherited from those sea rovers a love of the sea, and naturally early acquired a mastery of it, and gained a commercial supremacy through which they have established an empire that the sun never sets on, and have won for their country the proud title "Mis-

tress of the Sea," a title they are determined to retain, a prerogative that makes England great among the nations of the earth. 

The shrewd, enterprising, adventurous Yankees, descendants of those English merchant princes, in the early settlement of New England, were quick to note the many fine harbors in their long extended coastline, and lost no time in making valuable use of their sea privileges. 

Before they established manufacturing industries, which they later did so extensively, they built ships and began maritime commerce; at first in lumber, which, in the early days, they found in abundance and of best quality close at hand.

They built saw mills, sawed their logs, and loaded vessels with spruce and pine lumber, and sent it to the West Indies and to European countries, receiving in return the much needed commodities of those nations. 

Of special importance to them at that time was sugar and molasses from the West Indies and silver from Spain.

Large profits were realized and a valuable maritime commerce was thus early established in America. 

That commerce, begun by New England in its early settlement, has continued and increased until the sails of New England ships have whitened every sea, and the prows of New England vessels have entered every port. 

Her sea captains have visited every land, and her commerce has carried the products of New England industries to all peoples of the civilized world, bringing in return valuable goods from other lands. 

New England's commerce has been commensurate with her multifarious products and thus, as she has led the nation in industry, so has she also in commerce. 

The commerce of a nation is valuable to all other

nations with which it trades. Especially was this true of America in the early part of the nineteenth century, when American industries were being developed, and their products were rapidly increasing in variety and value.

In those days the theory that might makes right was pretty generally held, especially by nations on the other side of the Atlantic, and still is to a considerable extent. 

To gain their desired ends nations were prone to use compulsion instead of cooperation. To gain the monopoly of American trade England in 1812 seized American ships of commerce and impressed American seamen into English service. American merchant vessels and their cargoes were owned largely by New England merchants. The vessels had been built in New England shipyards and loaded with New England products. 

Therefore, while America as a nation was aroused to defend her merchant marine, New England was chiefly effected. But New England, though suffering more than any other part of the country, because she had more ships on the ocean and more seamen being seized and forced into British service, objected to engaging in a war with Great Britain, fearing such action would ruin her trade with England, which was valuable, if not indeed essential to the operation of New England industries. 

But when Congress declared war the New England People took up the sword with characteristic energy and determination and were the leaders in the conflict, realizing they were waging a war to defend American commerce and to protect American seamen, which were, primarily, New England commerce and New England seamen.

The war was fought chiefly on the water in vessels launched from New England shipyards, and manned by

New England seamen. Battles were fought on almost every sea, some even on the Pacific Ocean, and resulted generally in favor of America.

That New England did her full share in bringing the war to a successful issue is attested by Perry's victory on Lake Erie, in the defeat of the Gueriere by the Constitution, "Old Ironsides," under Captain Hull; in the 

Gulf of St. Lawrence; and Marlborough's victory on 

Lake Champlain.

The result of this war not only secured American commerce but gained the respect of the nations of the world for the young Republic across the sea, and caused them to realize that the new nation was ready and able to protect its merchant marine whose flag, in the words. of Webster, was "now known and honored throughout the world."

The value of a nation's commerce depends in no small degree on the character of the captains who command the vessels carrying the commodities. 

The captain is supreme aboard ship; he is attorney, judge and jury. His word is law to his crew and is accepted without question by the owners of both ship and cargo. His dealings with merchants in the countries he visits must be honest, businesslike, and his integrity above reproach. 

New England sea captains have been all this and more. They have been brave, resourceful and able to inspire confidence in the merchants whose merchandise they carried. Before the days of the steel steamers the hulls of New England sailing vessels met the typhoons off Hongkong, and were tossed on the mountainous waves they encountered in rounding stormy Cape Horn.

From the deck of their wooden vessel the sailors often looked into a wall of water that seemed destined to bury their light craft beneath its might. But the gallant

ship, under the control of the calm courage and strong hand of a trained helmsman, rose on the crest of the wave, as it broke in spray over the deck, and bravely pitched to meet the next huge sea. There were some shipwrecks, not many. Since the building of the great steel steamships, American maritime commerce has been immensely increased, as has that of all nations. But it should be remembered that those sturdy New England sea captains of earlier days in wooden vessels established 

American commerce.

To compare the commerce of our day with that of antiquity is to compare that great leviathan, the ocean steamer, with the camel, the ancient "ship of the desert."

The commerce of a nation is an important factor in its wealth, progress and power, as appears from this brief review of the world's commerce from the days of ancient Carthage to modern England, especially has it been true in the sea-carrying trade. As those monadic merchants in the days of the Pharaohs acquired wealth in the exchange of the products of different countries, so have modern merchant princes and nations gained wealth in the exchange of goods between modern nations; and that wealth of trade has been greater in modern times, even in proportion, than the improved facilities of traffic would indicate. 

New England sea traffic opened new markets for her ever-increasing industrial production, and this increase

in manufactured goods, at the same time, gave an added stimulus to her seafaring trade, so that the one greatly aided the other.

The most potent factor in producing the world's wealth, next to industry, that changes raw materials to valuable commodities, is commerce, that exchanges those commodities between nations; and has been ever since the halcyon days of Roman and Cartheginian competition for commercial supremacy on the Mediterranean Sea.

Through the Straits of Gibraltar has passed the commerce of the nations bordering on the Mediterranean 

Sea ever since the days of the first Phonecian ships, down through the years when those ancient rivals, Rome and Carthage, were contending for the treasured title "Mistress of the Sea"; and on, until England, through her ever expanding merchant marine, won that proud title. And for one hundred and fifty years England has controlled the Straits of Gibraltar, and thus has acquired much of her maritime supremacy. 

New England, with her industries, and her early shipbuilding gave an impetus to American commerce that has made America one of the foremost commercial nations of the world, and in three hundred years has developed here a nation whose wealth is nearly equal to that of all other nations combined. 

The sea, the restless rolling sea! For four thousand years it has been the world's highway of trade and travel. Ever since the first Phonecian ships spread their sails to the wind, and the ancient merchant princes from the rich cities about the Mediterranean traded in Tyre and Sidon, this international highway has been the greatest single source of the world's wealth; and the cities located on the shores of the sea have won supremacy and power.

Occupying three-fourths of the earth's surface, the sea has been as essential to man's welfare and progress as the solid earth on which he dwells. But with all its benefits to mankind, in its might it remains uncontrolled and, with its own strong hand, still continues to reap its annual harvest of human lives. 

What scenes of tragedy and triumph have been enacted on its surface! What journeys for pleasure and profit!

It has been the amphitheatre where "grim-visaged war" and productive peace have performed; where war has persued its terrible trail of destruction and death; and on which peace has borne, to all nations, the bountiful products of the good earth.

Here Freedom and Tyranny have contended, while right and wrong swayed in the balance.

Manila Bay. 

The "gray old Sea," at once man's beneficient friend and uncompromising enemy, though still unfettered, it has been compelled in recent times to serve man in a new and unexpected manner.

In 1869 the French government under direction of M. D. This artificial gateway, opening a passage between the Occident and the Orient, has proved of vast value to the world's commerce. It is said to be the most profitable project in the world, having paid its entire cost eight times over since it was opened seventy years ago.

The Suez Canal proved so successful and profitable that interest in a canal across the Isthmus of Panama, that had been under consideration for many years, was revived and after competent engineers had carefully considered and surveyed the two contemplated routes at Panama and Nicaragua, a canal was finally commenced at Panama by the French under direction of De Lusseps.

Shortening the sailing distance between American Atlantic and Pacific coastal cities eight thousand nautical miles!

Pacific coast-line.

Thus the New England People, descended from the merchant princes and sea kings of the old world, began a maritime commerce on their side of the Atlantic, and have continued with its development until it has borne the world's ships of commerce across seas and lifted them over mountains and carried them from the Atlantic to the Pacific, exchanging the products of New England's industries for the fruits of California's fertile fields. 

The sea, the mightiest monster of earth, bears man's burdens from coast to coast and from city to city. 

It is the great scavenger that purifies the air and water. It gathers up the water that flows into it from a thousand rivers, purifies it, carries it back over the land and pours it in copious rain to fertilize the earth and returns it again in streams rushing down the valleys, providing power to turn the wheels of industry: and stores it also in pure springs and fountains to quench the thirst of man and beast.

The winds that blow across its surface are wafted over the land in ever refreshing currents purified by their contact with the sea. And with all its other blessings, last but not least the beneficient sea provides a bountiful food supply to feed the human family. The annual supply of fish from New England fishermen reaches the enormous value of $125,000,000. No tax -the bane of humanity-is levied on this valuable pro-

duct of the sea. And there is no accumulated inventory on which must be marked down a manufacturing loss. 

An abundance of this nutritious food is always on hand, that requires only harvesting, and this gracious gift of the sea has been bestowed upon the human family ever since the days when He who gives every good and perfect gift directed the apostles where to cast in their nets on the sea of Galilee.

This mighty monster takes its toll of human life, but bestows with more lavish hand its bountiful blessings, and humanity, mindful of its power and likewise of its manifold gifts as it looks upon the rushing rolling sea, exclaims, "Roll on, roll on, thou dark blue Ocean, roll!"

CHAPTER XIV THEY MINGLE WITH THE RED MEN 

When the first white settlers landed in America they found here a people so different in both physical and mental make-up as to mark them a race distinct from any other people in the world.

In the language of an eminent author, "here lived and loved another race of beings."

This strange people-and they were strange-are described as having a skin the color of old copper, and hence have been called "red men." They accepted this term and later applied it proudly to themselves as distinguishing them from the white race.

They possessed other physical characteristics quite as peculiar to themselves, to distinguish them from any of the dark-skinned tribes of Asia, and that precludes the theory that they may have been descended in the remote past from people who might have crossed Bearing Straits from Asia.

The best conclusion seems to be that the North American Indians, whom we term Red Men, are indiginous to America and developed here with the flora and fauna of the continent.

These two peoples, the White Men, whose origin we trace back in antiquity to time before recorded history, and to place, the land along the Elbe between the

Atlantic to the Pacific.

The White Man, endowed by nature with the power of production of property and the disposition to preserve and store for future use, and possessed of a creative mind, active, alert, ready to be trained, that could be stirred to high impulses and noble motives in the early ages of the world's history, learned to apply the forces of nature to his uses; to cultivate the soil, to domesticate some of the wild creatures about him and compel them to serve him; to mine and use metals, and thus early learned the value of the land and the animals about him. He learned the importance of individual ownership, learned to gather in communities, acquired administrative ability-in a word, the White Man became civilized; began to own the earth, built cities, established nations, and became rich and powerful and possessed the comforts and luxuries of life. 

The Red Man, on the other hand, possessed little creative ability. He had neither the creative power of wealth production nor the will for provident saving. 

Food and shelter for his present needs were all that concerned him.

These two races, as unlike as different members of the same species could well be-the Red Men and the White Men-came into contact with each other here on the shores of Massachusetts Bay. 

When the Red Men first saw the White Men, clothed in flowing robes, coming over the big water in great white winged canoes, they thought them people from another world. They were people from another world; but not from another planet. They were from a civil-

Finally in 1878, General Custer, with an army of soldiers, was attacked by an Indian force in the Black Hills, that outnumbered him ten to one. Every white man was slain.

Then the Indians, though they had won a signal victory, knowing they had avenged themselves against their long time enemy, and realizing that it was useless to continue further the contest against the ever increasing number and superior weapons of the white man, gave up the strenuous struggle they had waged for two hundred and fifty years against overwhelming odds, and retired to the reservations the Federal government had allotted to them in the Indian Territory. It was, however, no sad or shameful surrender. The dusky warriors retired with dignity and honor, not as a defeated foe, but as a people who had at last decided to give up the unequal contest and unite with their white brethren in peace.

Every mountain and plain, the forest and glen, every lake and stream was familiar to him and dear to him.

CHAPTER XV THEY ARE SPORTSMEN 

There are three proper definitions of a sportsman. 

Webster gives two: "One who pursues sports, especially of the field"; "One who is a good loser and a graceful winner." We add another, "One who never knows defeat."

The New Englanders (and the Americans in general) qualify under all three of these definitions.

An evidence that the first definition applies is the fact that Boston supports two baseball parks and provides the best attendance to the great national game of any city in the big league circuit, except New York, which has a much larger population to draw from. 

New England's interest in the time- and worldhonored sport of horseracing has developed here the famous Wilkes and Hambletonian families of trotters, eminent among the world's famous families of trotting horses: and the Morgan horses, noted roadsters and the finest cavalry horses in America. And that New

Englanders are good sportsmen in the hunting grounds is shown by the record of an average of 2500 deer a season brought out of the Maine woods during the past twenty-five years, and so honestly have the state's well-framed hunting laws been obeyed that the number of deer in the forest has not been diminished. 

But the highest type of sportsman is not found on the field of sports or in the Maine woods; but in the war game. 131

Here where victor and vanquished face each other in the terrible struggle of life and death are enacted scenes of the highest sportsmanship; and here appear the noblest sportsmen of the world. Here are found men who qualify as true sportsmen under every definition of sportsmen-men who are good losers, and graceful winners, and men who never know defeat. 

The highest achievements of every people that have adorned the pages of history have been inspired by the spirit of sportsmanship that prompted the adventure. 

When those first New England people embarked in that small sailing vessel, the Mayflower, from England on a voyage of three thousand miles across an unknown ocean to an unknown shore, they manifested a spirit of sportsmanship unsurpassed in the annals of history. 

And again when half their number had perished because of hardship and the severity of exposure during that first winter in the American wilderness, and those invincible people saw the good ship that had brought them sail out of sight on its return to the homeland, with indomitable courage and unfaltering faith, they remained here to found the wealthiest, most powerful, the most benign nation the sun ever shone on, they displayed the supreme spirit of sportsmanship.

The New England People have carried this same splendid spirit of sportsmanship in their onward march westward across the great plains, over the Rockies to the Golden Gate.

Those few solemn-faced people who never thought of sports or sportsmanship, engaged in an enterprise that became an example of supreme sportsmanshipthe establishing of a nation of freemen, who have produced wealth, power and prestige unequalled by any other people or in any other period of history. 

They were certainly engaged in a good cause. They

During our long struggle for freedom, Lafayette was in deep sympathy with the American cause, and receiving the news that Washington was in retreat, mournfully exclaimed, "Washington is retreating across the Jerseys and the cause of Liberty is lost." This maneuver was not in the bag of military tactics; it was a shrewd move by a great sportsman.

He did advance, and that advance, begun by that American division in July, 1918, did not stop until the Armistice was signed in November. 

That was sagacious sportsmanship. It may have produced the turning point of the war, and perhaps the final result-victory for the Allies. That American officer, aware of the importance of his action, and sure of his success, dared almost to disobey a military command. That was sportsmanship, that called for wisdom, resourcefulness and courage. 

The highest kind of sportsmanship means initiative, courage and resourcefulness. The most remarkable and finest example of true sportsmanship was that shown by the Pilgrims when they set out in a frail vessel on a voyage across an unknown sea to an unknown shore. 

And every movement of the American pioneers to push the bounds of civilization westward whether by steam. train or the covered wagon, made to possess and develop this great land, was a fine example of good sportsmanship.

Sportsmen are pioneers and pioneers are sportsmen. 

Sports and sportsmanship date back to the Olympian

Games, established in Greece eight hundred years before the Christian era. The Olympian Games were the most important festivities of the ancient Greeks. They were held every fifth year, and were regarded as of so much importance that the year in which they occurred was called the Olympiad, and was regarded as a sort of calendar by which other historical events were marked -referring to these festivals as the Third Olympiad, the Fifth Olympiad, etc.

THEY ARE SPORTSMEN 

137 So much importance was attached to the events in the Olympian Games that the judges, at first two, later ten and sometimes twelve, who were to serve in deciding the victors, that they might be competent to render just decisions in the contests, were trained and directed for ten months before the games took place. 

These games and the careful manner in which they were conducted, together with the great importance attached to them, developed fine athletes in the Greek youth who became exceedingly proficient in all tests of strength and skill. They excelled especially in foot races. 

Phidippides, a Grecian runner, dispatched from

Athens to Sparta for aid in the battle of Marathon, covered the distance of one hundred and fifty miles in thirty-six hours. It seems almost incredible that the human system could be developed to such a degree of strength and endurance as to be able to produce such a performance. A Grecian runner ran from Marathon to Athens to announce the victory of that great battle with such remarkable speed that the event is celebrated here in Massachusetts in our time by the Marathon race, run every spring, in which runners cover the same distance as did that noted Greek athlete twenty-five hundred years ago.

That the Roman interest in sports was as keen as was the Greek, is shown by the Coliseum, one of the famous buildings of antiquity, erected by the Romans in which their games and athletic contests were held. 

But the Roman contests were of a very different nature from the Greek events. Mere exhibitions of strength and skill did not interest the Roman populace. They wanted to see the shedding of blood, exhibitions of danger and death; and thus combats between man and man armed with swords, and between man and beast

were instituted. The nature of Roman sport is shown in Sparticus speech to the gladiators, as follows: "Ye call me chief, and ye do well to call him chief who for twelve long years has met in the arena every form of man or beast the broad empire of Rome could furnish, and who never lowered his arm," and added, "hear ye yon lion roaring in his den, 'tis three days since he tasted meat, but tomorrow he shall break his fast upon your flesh."

These were the sports indulged in by the Romans; these and the chariot race, equally exciting, because equally dangerous.

All peoples since the dawn of history have been interested in sports and each nation has produced sportsmen, typical of their sports, and the character of their people is typical of their sportsmen. The Greeks produced scholars; the Romans, soldiers; the Americans, statesmen.

CHAPTER XVI THEY ARE  

We have traced the New England People back into the dim past to their ancestral origin along the Elbe in northern Europe, and on down to their signing of that compact in the cabin of the Mayflower in Massachusetts

Bay in 1620-an agreement entered into by those honest, earnest men to protect them from within and from without-that immortal document that is the corner-stone of the great American Republic. We have noted their slow and painful progress in possessing and developing the land that is now the United States of America.

Now it may be well to take a broader, more general view of this remarkable people. In order to gain a better knowledge of their characteristics, and to more fully appreciate their achievements, it seems well to compare them with another people, widely separated from them both in time and place. For this comparison we have chosen the Athenians. Not that the New 

England People can be properly compared with the illustrious Athenians in art and architecture or in language and literature. No people in the ancient or modern world are worthy of such a comparison. An eminent historian in commendation of Athenian art has said, "Their monuments are in the dust yet beautiful in ruin." And no other language is so perfect in its formations, or so well adapted to express the different shades of meaning as the Greek. But in the formation 139

of their government and the laws under which they lived, the Athenian people constitute a fair parallel with which to compare the New England people. 

The Athenians founded a republican form of government, before the Christian era, that endured for a thousand years.

It was formed of twelve Attic tribes in a confederacy called "Attica"-ancient Greece. Three thousand years later, and three thousand miles away, on the shores of the western world, was founded by the New Englanders from thirteen states another republican form of government-the American nation, destined to surpass the Attic nation in wealth and power, if not in art and literature. 

The Athenian republican form of government, for the first time in history, provided free schools, and maintained freedom of thought and action, and freedom of speech. It is authoritatively stated that under the stimulous of the free exercise of the mental powers made possible under the Athenian Republic during Athenian supremacy, "Athens gave birth to more great men-poets, artists, statesmen and philosophers-than all the world besides has produced in any period of equal length." "Never before had any people enjoyed such perfect political liberty as did the citizens of Athens at this time, and never before were any people, through so intimate knowledge of public affairs, so well able to direct the policies of a state. Every citizen, it is affirmed, was qualified to hold civil office." "Never before had there been such a union of the material and intellectual elements of civilization at the seat of empire. Literature and art had been carried to the utmost perfection possible to human genius."

But the greatness of the Athenians was not solely in art and literature. "Her maritime power and her

reputation as a center of wealth and refinement and the home of art and literature, were secured by the address and genius of a succession of statesmen, artists and writers such as perhaps no other city in ancient or modern times ever produced."

Let us repeat that this marvelous development of human genius produced in ancient Greece under the stimulous of the Athenian people was due in large measure to a form of government that gave free play to the mental powers, over a period of a thousand years. 

And here in America, under a similar form of government that has permitted and fostered the free exercise of the mental powers by an energetic, enterprising people, has, in three hundred years produced quite as amazing results, though of a very different kind. Here the mental activity has tended not so much toward art and literature as toward research, science and invention. 

Especially is this true in the last half century. For fifty years America has produced no orators like Webster, no authors comparable to Longfellow and Lowell, or 

Hawthorne and Holmes. But it has produced eminent scientists, mechanics, and captains of industry; and thus

America, under the stimulus of the early New England People, like the Greek nation under the early influence of the Athenians, has developed remarkable mental powers. Here it has produced vast improvements in methods of transportation and communications, and in the construction of machinery and the development and use of the powers of nature, entirely unknown and unthought of by those cultured peoples of the ancient world. It has applied the powers of nature to driving new and marvelous forms of machinery, in the processes of industry, that have revolutionized the production of all kinds of commodities useful and needful to humanity-making machines to manufacture the necessities

and luxuries of life at an ever reducing cost-and thus now in modern times have been produced and performed what would have been miracles in the days of the Grecian nation. And the men of today have so improved on their own methods of construction that, to use a well-known example, an automobile is made today vastly better than one of twenty years ago, and sold at less than half the price, and still produces a good profit to the manufacturer.

What would the Athenians have thought of conversing in audible tones across the Atlantic; or crossing it on the wings of the wind!

The development of the modern mind has not produced artistic monuments or constructed beautiful buildings like the Athenian Parthenon, or the Doric temples, but it has thrown huge bridges across wide rivers and erected skyscraping business blocks.

Here massive structures for utility and profit have taken the place of art and beauty-and speaking over the air to immense audiences has taken the place of oratory, couched in beautiful language.

America, started and stimulated by the New England People, has produced wealth and power instead of promoting art and literature, with the result that with only about 7 per cent of the world's population, it possesses nearly as much wealth as all the other nations combined.

The mental development and achievements of these two peoples-the Athenians and the New Englanders -are due and similar in no small degree, to their similarity of origin and characteristics. 

Says Myers in his Eastern Nations and Greece, "Thousands, driven from their homes, like the Puritans in the time of the Stuart tyranny in England, fled overseas, and under the direction of the Delphic Apollo laid

upon remote and widely separated shores the basis of 'Dispersed Hellas,' and thus was laid the foundation of Greece, much after the fashion of the foundation of 

America by the early Pilgrims."

Mr. Myers also correctly says, "It is not the absolutely pure, but the mixed races, like the English people, that have made the largest contribution to civilization." 

It may well be stated that the mixed origin of these two peoples, the Attic people by the union of the twelve Attic tribes, and the New England People through their English origin and later by the mixing of many races here in America, accounts in large part for the versatile and enterprising character of them both; and that accounts to a large degree, also, for their unparalleled mental progress-the Athenians in art and letters, New Englanders in the production of wealth and power. 

Climate exerts an important influence on man, and the latitude of the homes of these two peoples is not greatly different. In both the climatic conditions produce a stimulating, not an enervating, effect; and accounts in no small degree for the mental and physical activity and energy of these distinguished peoples, both of whom have produced a most important effect on the civilization and progress of the world. 

But here in America science and research have not stopped with the development and use of the powers of nature as applied to their industries, transportation and communication; but has gone far in the control and prevention of disease. It has greatly reduced, if not eliminated, the dread and often deadly diseases, smallpox, yellow fever, and diphtheria, and has greatly lessened the suffering from other malignant maladies.

These two peoples, of similar origin and under similar forms of government, that stimulated instead of stifling mental activity and energy, rose to heights of

intellectual peace and power not achieved by any other people on the earth.

But the Athenians knew nothing of investigation or invention. They were ignorant of the vast stores of coal and oil beneath the earth's surface that should provide heat and power to warm mankind and drive the wheels of industry to make materials for his use and furnish transportation for himself and carry his goods to the uttermost posts of the earth. They knew nothing of the wonders of electricity that enables man both to heat and to freeze, to turn the wheels of machinery, to talk in audible tones across continents and across oceans, a mighty force that provides light and force.

In a word, the vast powers of nature that the peoples of this age have discovered, harnessed and made to serve human use, were a closed book to those ancient people who could construct the beautiful in art and architecture and write and speak the wonderful in literature and language. Freedom that had developed thought and language had not in the days of ancient Hellas touched the imaginative mind and released the then unknown miraculous powers of the human intellect. 

They had learned to express their thoughts in oratorical and poetic language; they had trained their hands to construct the beautiful in building and to ornament their constructions, but they had not learned to search out the hidden laws of nature and make them subservient to their use.

As we of this age wonder at the display of the beautiful in Ancient Greece, so would the people of that age wonder at the production of the useful in our time. 

Freedom in Attica was very different from freedom in America. While in America every person is free; in 

Greece, more than half of the population were slaves. 

Thus freedom released very different powers in the

Ancient and in the modern mind. Greece was termed "land of scholars" and it was. Released by their slaves from all manual and menial labor, the native Greeks had time for thought and study. They observed the natural objects about them-the forest, the sea, the sky, the clouds, the grandeur of the ocean waves, the glow of the sunset, the graceful in the forms, and the glorious in the colors of nature. They delighted alike in the madness of the storm and the mildness of the sunshine -the majesty of the mountain and the pleasant plain. 

Thus the Athenians under the most favorable conditions of opportunity and scenic surroundings produced beauty in construction and grace, and oratorical and poetic expression unequaled by any other people in any age or time.

An eminent writer has said, "There is a dignity in honest toil, that belongs not to the luxury of wealth or the display of fashion," and there is also an energy and mental and physical strength that comes from honest toil that nothing else can bestow. A person performing tiresome toil to produce desired results is ever trying to find an easier way, a better method to accomplish the task before him.

This desire to lighten the burden of toil has amounted to a compelling force that has driven those who toiled to search out the hidden laws of nature to discover her mighty forces and harness them to human use.

Thus has the powerful force of nature been compelled to aid the puny hand of man; and thus have come the achievements of science.

The development of beauty by the Athenians is marvelous to us; the achievement of science by us would have been miraculous to them. Under the stimulus of the freedom founded by the Athenians, Greece became the "Land of scholars and nurse of Arts." Under the

stimulus of that freedom established and maintained by the New England People, America has become land of science and of wealth and power. 

But "Greece, lovely Greece, land of scholars and nurse of arts" founded and cherished by the Athenians, fell because of tyranny and treachery within that rendered her weak against attacks from without. 

What will be the destiny of America? Will America, founded by the Puritans and advanced by their descendants, the New England People, to a civilization, to a place and power even surpassing the Attic nation, finally fall as did her ancient illustrious compeer? Or will this nation, now the pride of her people, under the stimulating exercise of free mental action for individual energy and initiative, gained through wise and patriotic statesmanship, rise to still loftier achievements in civilization and power? No one can predict the future of this great Republic; but we know that America's danger is from within, not from without. This nation, cradled between the two great oceans of the world, with friendly neighbors on the north and on the south, and with its wealth and power guarded and defended by a people superior in initiative, enterprise and energy, is impregnable by any other national power. America's danger is from her politicians. Those who seek public office, not to render honorable service to their constituants, but for personal profit; those who would use storm destruction and disaster as a means of graft, and for private gain; who would spend public funds to a point of bankruptcy of city, state and nation. The unscrupulous, incidious politician is America's foremost enemy. 

This danger is one really to be feared, a danger that comes from the people, and what safeguard have the people against themselves?

This hydra-headed monster has already shown itself:

Boston, because of the origin of its Puritan founders and because of its culture, called the Athens of America, has in recent years been exploited by grafting politicians to the point of bankruptcy. And here in Massachusetts, the cradle of liberty, the occasion of a destructive storm has been made the means to mulct the State by officials in high places. 99 Will this comparison we have been pursuing continue down through the coming years until America, "land of the free and home of the brave," follows the sad fate of ancient Atticus, "land of scholars and nurse of arts,' and finally fall, not because of tyranny and treachery, but, bankrupt by her politicians, be forced into a dictatorship and lose the freedom that has made America great?

Or will America, in the coming years, produce statesmen instead of politicians? Men like Hamilton, Washington, Webster and the Adamses; men who guided this country in the early days of its development and progress; men who regarded public welfare above pelf; men of honesty and economic wisdom, who will lead the way out of the threatened bankruptcy into which grasping graft, and wanton waste of public money have placed city, state and nation; men whose aim shall be to restore this country-not rob it?

There is unquestionably now such men available, and it is incumbent upon the electorate to put such men in office, especially in high office.

In the early part of our country's history under the founding and stimulus of the New England People, the new nation in its progress to place and power followed the path of Atticus under the guidance of the Athenians, but now it is pursuing the way of the Roman Empire. 

The great American Republic has attained a wealth and power surpassing the Roman Empire in Rome's

imperial day. Rome fell, not through an enemy from without but because of the profligate expenditure and wanton waste of public funds. And so it will be with this nation, unless there is a change in the present trend of the conduct of the administration of government. 

In 1837, after this then young republic had laid its foundation as a nation and had won its freedom through the prosecution of a costly seven years' war, John Quincy Adams said "the country was without a dollar of national debt."

But in recent times, after twenty years of peace and plenty, the nation is encumbered with a debt vastly larger than at any previous time during the three hundred years of its existence. This great debt has been incurred not by any sudden calamity, like war or the preparation for war, that called for unusual expenditure of public funds, but by the cost incurred in the ordinary conduct of the government. 

How then can the government continue to pay a like cost of administration and also pay the interest on this huge debt, and the debt itself? A continuance of the present plan of administrative expenditure must continue to pile up this enormous debt, until it becomes overwhelming and national bankruptcy ensues. This is a natural conclusion. It is history. Such has been the fate of other nations. up 

Horance Mann said, "Our greatest danger will grow from our own bosom." And we find that statement true. We need fear no foreign power. But here in our midst is a real danger that already threatens our prosperity, if not our perpetuity.

Municipalities and states, following the trend of the national government, are hastening into bankruptcy. 

Some municipalities have already become bankrupt and their administration has been taken over by the state.

The only safety for this nation from the headlong haste with which it is following the fatal path of desolated Rome is in the wise heads and stout hearts of men, inspired by patriotic duty who shall thrust aside scheming politicians, actuated by party politics to gain public office and plunder the public treasury.

There are plenty of men worthy and well qualified to fill the high offices of government. But those men do not seek office; the office must seek them. It is such men that are termed "captains of industry." They hold high positions of trust and responsibility in big business concerns, but actuated by a high sense of patriotic duty, if called upon, those men of superior intelligence and high character, would accept public office and use the same ability and devotion in directing the affairs of state that they do so effectively and profitably in the prosecution of business.

Thus the welfare, indeed, the safety of the country, depends upon the electorate-upon the intelligence and conscience of the mass of the people. 

In a republican form of government, like that founded by the Athenians three thousand years ago in

Atticus, and by the New England People three hundred years ago here in America, its success, its safety, must depend upon the intelligence and patriotism of its people. 

The people of Atticus, as a people, possessed those qualities of mind and heart essential to national strength and safety, and so do the American people of today. But treachery and treason lurks within our borders as it did in that illustrious land of long ago; and watchful guard must be maintained to protect the nation against the insidious machinations of unscrupulous plotters for self. No truer aphorism was ever

uttered than this: "Eternal vigilance is the price of liberty."

In the words of an eminent statesman, "Let corruption and intrigue triumph over honesty and intellect and our liberties will depart forever." This great nation, with all its vast and varied interests; with its multitudinous industries, its homes in town and country, andworth more than all else, its liberty-without which all else would be worthless, is not to be credited wholly to the New England People. 

Those sturdy pioneers of the seventeenth century and their descendants laid the foundation, and with their intelligence, their energy and their enterprise, that has permeated every state in the Union, have reared the superstructure. But every nation in the north temperate zone has contributed of its bravest and its bestsending leaders, pioneers-enterprising, industrious people, who came and have given their intelligence and energy in developing here a nation of freemen. And these mixed races have contributed in no small part to the virility, strength and stamina of this powerful people.

CHAPTER XVII  

The appreciative sense of wit and humor and the aptitude to produce them differs not less in different peoples than do other racial characteristics, even to the color of their skins.

A keen intelligence and a quick active mind are essential to an appreciation of wit and humor, and even more essential to produce them.

The American Indian possessed no sense of humor. 

He was morose and sullen. Though "his untutored mind saw God in the clouds and heard Him in the wind," he never saw or heard anything amusing in the world about him—nothing to laugh at, and he never laughed except when he hit his enemy on the head with his tomahawk! He had little appreciation of music.

He never sang, and his only musical performance was the beating of the tom-tom in accompaniment to his weird war dance around the campfire in calling his braves to battle, or celebrating victories over his enemies.

The character of the Indians, like that of every people, was moulded by their origin and environment. 

Shrewd and crafty, like the other wild creatures among which they lived, the Indians did not possess the keen intelligence of the white man, essential to appreciate wit and humor, much less to produce them.

The Negro, whose origin and environment required little effort to provide food and shelter, is as mirthful 151

as the Indian was stern; and his face is as smiling as the 

Indian's was grim. He is fond of all kinds of music from that produced by the big bass drum in the band, to the jew's harp that he trills at the corner of his mouth. He sings and laughs; he laughs at everything, whether humorous or not. In fact, the laughing, singing black man has little more appreciation of wit and humor than the silent, stolid Red Man.

Our early Puritan ancestors had little more appreciative sense of wit and humor than the Indians and the expression of their faces was about as austere and solemn as was the Indians', and, whether they met in council or in war, each looked into the face of the other with the same stern aspect. Pestered and persecuted in 

England, they took their religion very seriously, as they did everything else. Compelled to meet adversity and hardship in the early days in America, they had little occasion for quips of wit or sallies of humor. Like their new neighbors, the Indians, they seldom laughed or enjoyed humorous conversation.

But, as time went on, and those stern-faced Puritans cleared and cultivated farms, and constructed pleasant homes, their expression became less austere, and as they engaged in trade and traffic and discussed the favorable prospects of the corn crop and came to enjoy the amenities of social intercourse, they began to sometimes smile, and when the younger generations found themselves possessed of freedom of worship, freedom of thought and action; and, as an appreciative understanding of their independence came to them, they realized that they began to own the world, or a part of it, and a very good part. They began to enjoy life, and they began to laugh. They laughed even at adversity, which they were replacing with prosperity; and their keen

intelligence began, not only to manifest an appreciative sense of wit and humor, but to produce them.

As the colonies increased in population and wealth, those intelligent, industrious people, established free schools, opened institutions of higher education and developed all the characteristics of a rich and powerful nation. They produced orators, statesmen and authors of high rank. And some of those writers have produced keen wit and high humor. Josh Billings, Artemus Ward, Bill Nye; and of a higher order, Franklin and Holmes. 

A part of Billings' humor was in his ridiculous spelling. Here is a sample of his spelling and homely wit: "The muel is haf hoss and haf jackass, and there kums to a full stop, natur diskovering her mistake. Tha weigh more, akording to their heft than any other kreeture, except a crowbar."

Billings sometimes acted as auctioneer. On one occasion, when officiating in that capacity, he was indulging pretty freely in profanity. A man, wearing a long-tailed black coat that distinguished him as a minister of the Gospel, elbowed his way through the crowd to Josh's side and admonished him to use less profanity, telling him that he would sell more if he swore less. Billings replied, "My friend, you and I are very much alike. I swear a good deal and you pray a good deal, neither of us means anything by it." 

Both Josh Billings and Artemus Ward depended, to a considerable extent, on their ludicrous spelling for their humor. While that might be acceptable as humor sixty years ago, we of today spell so badly, the best we can do, that we are not much amused by that kind of humor. 

Strictly speaking, Bill Nye, though born in Shirley,

Maine, can perhaps hardly be regarded as a strictly

New England humorist, for his people moved to Wisconsin two years after his birth and he was reared and educated in that state. But as he was of New England origin he may justifiably be classified among New England humorists. He was, however, a quite different type of humorist from Billings and Ward. He depended for his humor on what he said and the way he said it, not on ridiculous spelling. For example, he says regarding his marriage: "Considering that I had more poverty than one person was entitled to, I made up my mind to endow some deserving young woman with a part of it." 

His birthplace was on the fringe of New England. 

After his maturity he visited the place and thus briefly describes the town as "a barren and inhospitable waste of rocks and cold," and his son writes: "In 1850, the year of Nye's birth, it was a red sawmill and a cluster of houses." It may be noted here that the place hasn't changed much in the nearly ninety years since, and instead of progressing, now appears to be reverting to a wilderness. Young Nye adds: "Nowadays it is best known to hunters who go to the Moosehead region of Maine."

Bill, humorously, says further of this backwoods Maine town, in connection with himself: "Here on the banks of the raging Pircataquis where winter lingers in the lap of spring till it occasions a good deal of talk, there began a career (referring to himself) which has been the wonder and admiration of every vigilance committee west of the turbulent Missouri." 

Bill Nye was once chatting with Senator Shirley of

Maine, and remarked upon the fact that he (Nye) was born at Shirley in the Senator's state, adding that the town had doubtless been named for one of the Senator's ancestors. "I didn't know," said the Senator, "that there was such a town in Maine as Shirley." "I didn't

know it either," said Nye, "until I was born there."

Some years after Nye's last visit to his native town he was told by a humorist friend that the town fathers had placed a tablet on the house where he was born. 

Nye diffidently inquired, "What did they put on the tablet?" His humorist friend replied, "Greenville, six miles !"

While wit and humor and courage and loyalty are widely different sentiments, they are not wholly unrelated. A brave man sometimes smiles when he faces danger.

On an occasion in our Revolutionary War, a division of the American Army under Washington and a division of the British forces were encamped not far apart. 

The British general courteously invited Washington to visit him, and appointed the day and hour of the visit. 

Washington graciously accepted the invitation, but, suspecting some possible treachery, wisely provided for any eventuality.

He went to the appointment somewhat earlier than the time set. On his arrival the British officer remarked on Washington's punctuality. Washington knew he was in the enemy's camp and realized that he might be in grave danger, and shrewdly, and perhaps significantly, replied: "He who is not punctual with a friend may doubt his own integrity."

As the two generals conversed, a troop of horsemen were seen approaching over the crest of a neighboring hill. The British general, as he viewed the oncoming soldiers, smiled grimly and placing his hand on Washington's shoulder announced, "General, you are my prisoner." As the cavalcade drew near, Washington observed that they were his own troops whom he had wisely provided in anticipation of a trap. Now, know- 167

ing he had outwitted the Englishman, he looked calmly into the other's eyes and replied: "I think not, but, General, you are mine."

This incident affords a good illustration of both sportsmanship and courage. There is also grim humor in the incident. The Englishman was caught in his own trap. But

The latter developed large estates, built mansions, and, accustomed to having servants in England, bought 

Negroes imported from Africa and used them as slaves, to serve them and perform the manual labor on their land. They became the Southern planters.

Those who began settlement at Plymouth possessed little wealth; acquired small parcels of land, cleared and developed small farms, built homes and became New England farmers. Later, with characteristic energy and enterprise they established industries and became manufacturers and traders.

The Southern people did not extend their national development much beyond the Mississippi; while the 

New Englanders, endowed with energy and enterprise, developed by generations of mental and manual effort, pushed their nation building across the continent to the Pacific.

Thus these two peoples, having a common origin, had, in succeeding generations, under different living conditions, the one in affluence and ease; the other in toil and hardship, developed markedly different characteristics, but both still possessed that indomitable spirit of their ancient ancestors "whose neck never bowed to a lord.”

Both were firm and unyielding in purpose, and untiring in their efforts to attain desired objects. Both possessed defiant courage in defending their rights and, devoted loyalty to an espoused cause. 

These two peoples, blending their interests and their aspirations, have been the chief instruments in founding and developing the United States of America.

In the development of this country to a power and prestige that make it pre-eminent among the nations of the earth, two cumulative forces have been most potent in producing that result, viz: The indomitable spirit of those founders who, together with their descendants, down through the years of its growth, have imbued its inhabitants with that spirit until it permeates all the people in every section of the country and has become a dominant characteristic of the nation; and, second; the kind of government they founded-a "government of the people, for the people and by the people.' 99 Here, for the first time in human history, the common man has a voice and a vote in the affairs of State, and is eligible to hold public office. That indomitable spirit that knows neither defeat nor retreat has aroused in American youth an impulse of adventure, and stimulated an ambition in young men to make the most of themselves; and not a few sons of poor parents have earned their own way through the higher institutions of learning and fitted themselves for places in the learned professions, and for high positions in the public services. 

It is that unconquerable spirit of our forefathers transmitted down through the generations, combined with the freedom they established, that permits the unlimited action of all the mental powers that has made this nation proficient, wealthy, powerful-pre-eminent among the nations of all time.

History is the story of peoples, recording their struggles, their triumphs, their failures; relating how they have formed governments, and risen to positions of power and prestige, and then gone down to obscurity -sometimes to oblivion. Those that are not forgotten, leaving behind them memories and monuments of their greatness and their culture. Among the most eminent of these are Egypt, Greece, Rome and Babylon.

As nations have succeeded one another on the world's stage of action, what a story of war and bloodshed history has written! That story has told more of strife, destruction and death than of prosperity, and peace. 

Indeed, the history of the world is the history of war, of "rumors of successful and unsuccessful war." 

In the ancient world, war followed war; new nations rose on the ruins of preceding people. Babylon and 

Persia, and Greece and Rome! What amphitheatres for bloody struggles in wars of aggression and conquest! Each striving to rise on the ruins of another. 

Here in America not so. The foundations of this nation were not laid on the ruins of a decayed empire or conquered state; but on what might properly be termed "primeval ground." Here America did not rear the structure of its state on the ruins of departed glory. Here no organized form of government had preceded our own. Here "lived and loved another race of beings," but they did not constitute a nation. 

Those people that we superceded were scattered savage tribes that had roamed the wilderness from time immemorial without subduing it. They had no constitutional form of government, no recorded language or literature. They had little more notion of

constitutional government than had the wild beasts among which they lived. 

Here was planted a nation, as it were, on virgin soil, founded on a principle hitherto unrecognized, if not unknown in the world's history. The principle that all men are created equal; not equal in mind and body, but equal in their just rights-the "rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness."

In establishing this nation those rights were recognized for the first time, made paramount and guaranteed by a written constitution and properly formulated laws that have made them secure secure to you, to me, and to every American.

What a heritage has thus been bestowed upon us of this day and generation!

If in the days of Imperial Rome, "to be a Roman was scarcely less than to be a King," what is it in this day and generation to be an American! We fail to appreciate the value of our birthright. To be permitted to use all our mental powers, free and unrestrained; to be privileged to attain our heart's desires and the will of our conscience is more than was ever secured to potentate or king. We are not only privileged to enter upon our daily duties, moving along our accustomed courses of action with none to "molest or make us afraid," but we are protected everywhere and at all times. We do not see the strong hand stretched out to restrain the wayward, to protect the weak. Solomon with all his wealth and power did not enjoy such privilege, such protection.

But more important than all else as affecting the eminence of a nation is the character of its people and the form of government under which they live. This was true of wealthy Babylon, cultured Greece and Imperial Rome. And it is doubly true here in the United

The character of this people has been formed from the invincible spirit of their sturdy ancestors, who laid here firm the foundation of freedom on which their descendants and their followers have built this Democracy-the first great experiment of self-government by the people.

The star of national supremacy rose first over the land of Egypt and shone in the Egyptian sky for a thousand years then it passed in turn to Babylon, Greece and Rome. It glittered spasmodically and for varying periods of time for fifteen hundred years in the skies of the eastern nations; and was lost during the Dark and Middle Ages.

In 1700 it appeared in the sky of the British Empire where it shone for two hundred years. It has now appeared in the American sky and shines with more brilliancy than ever before. American supremacy has not been acquired by aggression or conquest. This nation has not made aggressions on other peoples. It has not coveted its neighbors' goods or anything that is its neighbors. It has obeyed the divine command to "seek peace and pursue it."

The United States of America has gained its preeminence of wealth and power among the nations of all time by intelligent enterprise, establishing industries in the production of everything of use and beauty that supplies the needs and satisfies the desires of humanity.

Second only in importance to freedom secured by the founders, was the provision they made for public education. They realized that an electorate must be educated if it was to function wisely or even safely. Therefore, they early established the public school, securing to every child a common school education that every citizen might be conversant with public affairs. And naturally followed the founding of higher institutions of learning. Thus the public school system and the college have been established throughout the Union. Every child, regardless of race or color, not only has the privilege of a common school education but is required by law to attend school and every state has its colleges and universities.

Here in greater Boston, within a radius of fifteen miles, are located not less than ten institutions of higher education. These unprecedented educational advantages have qualified this people as no other people have ever been qualified to carry on the administrative offices of government; and to conduct the multifarious forms of industry, commerce and culture, to the end that here has not only been developed an electorate worthy and well qualified to conduct the affairs of state, but has produced highly trained people prepared to serve in all the walks of life: skilled mechanics, eminent scientists, noted inventors and captains of industry.

New England initiative and energy diffused throughout the nation contribute an important part of national character that pervades and motivates every enterprise and onward movement that have made the American people superior to any other people of any land or time.

Freedom has bestowed upon American statesmen the power to determine justice and procure peace. They have reduced the distance across the Atlantic from two months in the Mayflower to twenty-four hours in those great flying boats that wing their way from America to Europe; and across the continent from six months in the covered wagon to from sunrise to sunset in the airplane.

FINAL WORD 

It is hoped that the record of the events as narrated in the preceding pages have convinced the reader that the attributes of the New England People, transmitted to the American people, have been an important, if not a paramount, influence in shaping the character of this nation. 

The following characteristics: confidence, courage, patience, perseverance, sound sense, and untiring energy, have been dominant qualities in the New England People, transmitted to them from their early ancestors who dwelt on the shores of the Baltic and along the Elbe, coming down through the centuries to our time,—and still strongly manifested in the American people, continue to contribute largely to the wealth and strength of America. 

Their characteristic confidence, as expressed in that old Danish song already quoted:- "I trust my sword, I trust my steed, But most I trust myself in need,"

was the impelling force that sent those indominatable (Angles) English from their home along the Elbe to the British Isles, and enabled them to conquer and capture Britain in the fifth century; and that actuated the 

Pilgrims to found a nation in Massachusetts Bay in the seventeenth century; and that also drove forward the pioneers when they pushed the frontier across the continent to the Golden Gate in the nineteenth century, and developed here the Great Republic! It was that courage that caused those farmers with their flintlock guns to face and turn back the trained soldiers of Europe at Lexington Green, and Concord Bridge; and that patience that sustained those same Pilgrims in that perilous voyage across the Atlantic-the most momentous voyage in history! And that persistence also that held the survivors of that first winter at Plymouth and laid there firm the foundation of a mighty nation; and the sound sense inherent in their descendants that wrote the Declaration of Independence; and the energy of those early people still dominant in their descendants that developed here the richest, most powerful nation of all time.

These qualities of mind and heart, and what they have produced, that have been bequeathed to us—you and me-constitute a legacy more valuable than was ever bestowed on any other people. An inheritance not only of vast property value-but what is worth much more-constitutional liberty and its bulwark, the public school.

When we contemplate this priceless heritage that has been transmitted to us we are led to exclaim, "Lives there a man with soul so dead that not to himself has said, 'This is my own, my native land.' 999 Direct descendants of the early New England People are now to be found in every state of the Union and are

While it is only fair to say that the New England People are subject to failings that the peoples of the world are heir to, and, undoubtedly possess many of them, they possess, also, all the high qualities here attributed to them; and which, imbued in the American people, have caused the star of national supremacy to rise higher and shine brighter in the American sky, than it has in the sky of any other nation in history.
