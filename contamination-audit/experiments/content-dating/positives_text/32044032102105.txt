PREFACE 

In the preparation of instructional material for the training and upgrading of police officers there are three important phases: 

1. The cataloging of all the duties performed by a policeman and the knowledge required in performing these duties. 

2. Rearranging these duties so that they are listed in the best instructional order; listing the operation points in each job; and analyzing the instructional procedure in each type job. 

3. Compiling material in each block of duties for reference and study material for the learner.

The work involved in the first phase was completed during the summer of 1932 and the results have been compiled and published as 

Job Analysis of Police Service, Department of Education Bulletin 

No. 4, February 15, 1933. The bulletin contains this list of duties performed by the policemen in the various divisions of police work, and a statement of the knowledge required in performing the various jobs. The work involved in the second phase was completed during the summer of 1933, and is presented in this bulletin. The third phase of the work-the compilation of study and reference material involves a long period of research and the writing of a considerable amount of new material. This material would form the practical textbook for police department schools.

The instructional analysis has been confined largely to the Patrol Division because it offers the greatest field for training, and also because it would not be practical to include an instructional analysis of all divisions in one bulletin. With the Patrol Division complete, the same plan of analysis can be used by instructors in completing other divisions as they are needed. A number of executives in the 

Police Department have assisted in the construction of this analysis and have thus had the experience which will enable them to continue the work. The Bureau of Trade and Industrial Education of the State 

Department of Education will continue to make the resources of the Department available to officers and instructors with a view to the final completion of instructional material in all branches of police work.

INTRODUCTION 

An analysis of duties performed in the various divisions of the Police Department of the City of Los Angeles was made during the summer of 1932 by the Bureau of Trade and Industrial Education, State Department of Education, after a series of conferences had led up to the recognition of the need for such an analysis. 

In making this analysis conferences were held with groups of executives representing each division. Three or four executives were selected from each division and met with the representative of the Bureau of Trade and Industrial Education and after discussion listed the jobs performed in the division represented by their group. When all divisions had been covered, more than three thousand distinct types of jobs had been listed and as many items of knowledge required to perform these duties.

The mere listing of jobs or duties that policemen may be called upon to perform is wholly inadequate from the standpoint of determining what should be taught. For example, it may be a duty of a policeman to testify in court, but the mere statement of this duty does not indicate what he will have to be taught in order to properly testify. 

This list of duties, however, is valuable as a source book for constructing an instructional analysis. In order to have proper instructional material, each job in the original analysis must be analyzed for teaching purposes and each analysis must list the things one must know and be able to do in order to perform these duties. 

The technique used in making the instructional analysis was similar to that used in making the original list of duties. Conferences were held with several police executives who furnished the necessary technical information for use in the construction of teaching units and lesson plans.

The nature of the work and the purposes in each division of it may be stated as follows: 

1. The list of duties was rearranged into groups or units which were considered effective for teaching purposes. This was done by considering each job according to the character of the work performed, the difficulty involved in performing the job, and the amount of knowledge required in its performance. There was no attempt to list the duties absolutely according to the "learning difficulty." The importance of the work and the frequency of occurrence was given consideration along with the so-called instructional order. 1

2. A statement of the objective of each teaching unit was evolved. 

It is the answer to one of the most important questions a teacher can ask himself: What do I expect my students to be able to do as a result of my teaching in this unit? There has been an attempt to state the objective in simple and exact language so that there can be no confusion from intangible and high-sounding phrases. 

3. Each block of work has been divided into checking levels, or into smaller groups of jobs grouped together in such a way that the knowledge of the learner may be tested. In this way the instructor can check on the ability of the learner to proceed to the next group of jobs. 

Each checking level has its definite objective, just as the whole block has its objective. Each checking level is divided into a number of type jobs which form the basis for an instructional analysis sheet. 

4. On the instructional analysis sheet the job listed under the checking level is analyzed in all the operations and the knowledge required to perform each operation in the job. The knowledge required is classified under various headings-mathematics, science, drawing, special police terms, care of equipment, safety, ethics and law. The analysis made on this sheet enables the instructor to know when the lesson is complete. He is now prepared to present the lesson on this job since he knows every operation and the knowledge required in its performance. If the required knowledge is given in connection with the job to which it belongs, the learner is much more likely to retain it. For example, if the laws relating to the arrest of a perpetrator of a misdemeanor by a civilian are given in connection with a lesson on the jobs performed by the officer, he is much more likely to fix these laws in his mind than if he attempted to study all the laws of arrests as a separate and distinct problem. In the one case it is a concrete definite thing-in the other case, it is more or less abstract. 

5. The next step is the construction of the instructor's operation sheet. 

On this sheet the instructor outlines his procedure in giving the material which has just been analyzed on the instructional analysis sheet. 

He states the objective of this particular lesson by answering his own question, "What do I expect these students to know or do after I have taught the lesson?" He notes the procedure for each step in the lesson. In the first step, he usually asks questions to determine the previous knowledge of the learner and also to build up interest in the learner in the material which is about to be given in the lesson. In the second step he actually presents the lesson by illustrations or demonstrations or experiments, according to the character of the lesson. In the third step he makes application of the lesson by having the learner either perform a job or describe the procedure in doing a job

and setting him right on all points in which he may have been in error. And finally, in step four, he uses some device for testing the knowledge of the learner and his ability to perform the job without assistance from the instructor.

When the instructor has his job of teaching analyzed up to this point and has an understanding of the proper procedure in presenting the lesson, he has only to possess the information required of wellinformed police officers to be able to complete the instructional process. II Various arrangements may be made in grouping duties or jobs in instructional order, in the number of checking levels in a block, and in the number of type jobs that are listed under checking levels. 

The arrangement should be made to suit the conditions of instruction. and the amount of information already possessed by the learner. 

The instructional material within this bulletin is meant to be suggestive in character. The first block or unit in the patrol division is headed "Patrol and Observation of Things." This unit has been completed in all details so as to give a suggestive example of a complete unit. The unit is divided into four checking levels, the checking levels are divided into five or six type jobs, and lesson sheets are prepared on all of these jobs. In other units it was not thought necessary to complete every lesson sheet. The unit objectives have been set up, the checking levels have been suggested, and three lesson sheets have been set upon each checking level. In many cases this covers the entire checking levels-in others, there will be three or four lesson sheets to be completed by the instructor. He will find this comparatively simple to do since the remaining lesson sheets will be the same in character as the ones already set up. The operations and the knowledge required are suggested in the preliminary statement of the entire unit in each separate checking level. 

The first unit has been set up in greater detail than would be necessary for experienced men. For new learners without experience this unit would furnish twenty-four hours of instructional work. 

For experienced men, several jobs may be combined and given in one lesson. The procedure would be essentially the same in either case. 

In the case of new learners, the hour would be given over to one lesson. 

In the case of experienced men, two, three, or four lessons might be covered in the same time.

In other units in the Patrol Division the work has not been carried out in such great detail. It has been kept in mind in these units that the work is being given to men of experience. Some instructors may find it desirable to carry out the work in greater detail. In this case the plan of Unit I may be followed.

An example is given here of two ways of setting up the same group of lesson plans. Unit II, Checking Level II, as given in this bulletin, sets forth one plan. Four type jobs are grouped under this 

1. Observing persons suspected of being about to commit a crime. 

2. Observing persons whose actions indicate a locality where unlawful acts are committed. 

3. Observing persons answering descriptions of persons wanted as missing or for crimes committed. 

4. Observing persons under the influence of alcohol, drugs or mental disorder.

Another executive of the department set up this same checking level under Unit II and named seven type jobs and seven lesson sheets, instead of the four stated above. He listed the type jobs as follows: 

1. Observe persons loitering between buildings, in alleys, and dark places. 

2. Observe persons tampering with automobiles or loitering around automobiles. 

3. Observe people in gas stations, drug stores, candy stores, and cafes (especially if car outside with motor running). 

4. Observe places where many people are leaving and entering regularly, especially at unusual hours. 

5. Observe people answering descriptions of persons wanted. 

6. Observe persons who do not appear to belong in the locality observed. 

7. Observe persons under the influence of alcohol, drugs, amnesia, and mental disorders.

It will be noted that the second example goes into much greater detail and would probably be more desirable in the case of new or untrained men.

The main purpose of giving these examples is to show different ways of setting up the work, and it is suggested that the instructor may carry out any of the units, checking levels, or lesson sheets given in this bulletin in greater detail. Special forms are included in each unit, except the first, for the use of the instructor in completing the instructional analysis sheets and instructor's operation sheets for lessons which have not been included.

POLICE PATROL UNIT I. PATROL AND OBSERVATION (THINGS) Unit Objective 

Ability to: Patrol an assigned area within the city; observe and note all factors tending to endanger the safety or well-being of the people in that area, as a preliminary step to the making of necessary changes by the proper persons; recognize the different classes of civil negligence or criminal intent present in each case; estimate the amount and kind of service needed to remedy hazardous conditions; make notations that will form the basis of proper oral and written recommendations in each case. 

Knowledge of: Public and private safety standards as required by statutes of the state and the ordinances of the city; factors which cause unsafe conditions; and the factors which determine the extent of probable public or private liability involved in each case observed.

CHECKING LEVEL I 

Jobs involving: Observing and noting the presence of faulty conditions tending to endanger public safety, where found in sidewalks, streets, fire hydrants, street signs, storm drains, and underground mains as a preliminary step to facilitate the making of necessary repairs by the proper agencies. Type Jobs-

Ability to: Recognize and make notes regarding the faulty conditions of sidewalks, streets, fire hydrants, street signs, storm drains, and underground mains; recognize kinds of repairs necessary and amount of police or other service required to make conditions safe.

Knowledge of: Types and kinds of unsafe conditions; degree of criminal intent or civil negligence involved; amount of detail to include in notes; probable time required to make necessary changes; temporary means usually employed to reduce hazards pending permanent repairs; and the agencies responsible for repairs.

Mathematics: Make linear measurements to determine distances or lines of direction. 

Science: Principles of public safety; the methods of lessening danger from broken sidewalks, holes in streets, leaking fire hydrants, obstructed storm drains, leaking gas or sewer mains; reasons why police are used to report unsafe conditions found on public ways.

Care of Tools and Materials: Protect notebook from becoming unnecessarily soiled or pocket worn by using leather case, protect notes from impeachment by maintaining solid bound and appropriately dated notebook. Protect tape so that it is clean and free from rust. 

Safety Precautions: Exercise care in cases of cave-ins or leaking sewer or illuminating gas.

Law and Ethics: The degree of care required by the municipality in maintaining or controlling public ways; functions of Police Department, Department of Public Works and Department of Water with regard to maintenance of safe conditions on public ways; the extent of reports to be made and the proper procedure and channels used in making them.

FOREWORD 

The preceding unit has been given in great detail. 

It was worked out largely by one member of the Police Department. The detail is so great that several lessons can be covered in one class session with experienced police officers. The following units are given in less detail. Each lesson will cover a greater range of new material and, in general, will take a greater portion of the class session time. 

The unit objective and the general statement of knowledge required is given for each checking level and the instructional analysis is complete for the first three jobs in each level. The instructor will not find it difficult to complete the other jobs.

The following units have been partly covered in the preceding units, but they are of such nature that lesson sheets should be worked out for each one separately. 

While reports have been mentioned in almost every unit, the subject of police reports is so important that several lesson sheets should be worked out covering the various kinds of reports. 

Unit IX, Testifying and Presentation of Evidence, is also an important unit. 

The inability of many police officers to present evidence in the right way has caused failure to convict in many cases that seemed to the officer almost an open and shut case. This unit deserves separate treatment.

The other two units: Unit X, First Aid; and Unit XI, Care of Equipment, have been covered largely in other units, but many instructors believe that there should be a special course in First Aid, and a special course in Care of Equipment. 

In the units that are to follow only the Unit Objective and the Checking Level Objectives are given. The instructor's operation sheets can be worked out by the instructor as suggested by the lesson sheets that have been worked out in other units.

In addition to the duties which are listed under the Patrol Division, other duties are listed in the original analysis under other divisions which all policemen have to perform. Due to lack of time these were not taken up during this series of conferences. All of the lesson sheets have been confined to those duties listed in the Analysis of Police Service under the Patrol Division. 

UNIT IX. TESTIFYING AND PRESENTING EVIDENCE 

Unit Objective

Ability to: Present evidence in court in such a way as to gain the confidence of the court and jury and to confine testimony to material and relevant matters pertinent to the issue, and to maintain a poise and bearing which will gain the respect of the court and jury towards the officer and the department.

Knowledge of: The manner of giving evidence that will gain the confidence of the court and the jury; what constitutes the proper presentation of evidence and the relative value of different kinds of evidence.

UNIT IX. 

CHECKING LEVEL I 

Jobs Involving: The giving of testimony in court; preparing legal foundations for introduction of exhibits of physical objects in evidence; introducing confessions, dying statements, and physical objects. 

Type Jobs: 

1. Testify in court. 

2. Prepare proper legal foundations for introduction of exhibits of physical objects. 

3. Introduce physical evidence by establishing the material nature of the object to be introduced. 

4. Introduce confessions, dying statements, and objects as evidence.

Ability to: Give testimony; maintain an impersonal attitude when in court; speak in a straightforward manner with direct diction and emphasis; repeat conversations verbatim; confine testimony to material and relevant matters pertinent to the issue; prepare proper legal foundations for introduction of exhibits and physical objects in evidence; introduce confessions and dying statements as evidence. 

Knowledge of: What constitutes the best admissible evidence in criminal cases and the proper manner of giving such evidence so as to gain the confidence of the court and jury and the respect of the community toward the officer and the department.

Mathematics: Read dimensions from drawings in notes; estimate distances from photographs; make estimates asked for by the court. 

Science: What constitutes the best evidence in criminal cases; what constitutes admissible evidence in criminal cases; the relative value of direct, circumstantial, expert, hearsay, documentary, cumulative, corroborative, and presumptive evidence; dying declarations, confessions and opinion in insanity cases; how to maintain a bearing and an impersonal attitude while in the courtroom; how to speak in such a way as to describe properly what has been seen or heard; how to use notes in presenting testimony.

Care of Equipment: Notes should be kept intact so that notes on the evidence may be preserved.

Law and Ethics: Maintain an attitude so that there can be no suspicion of persecution on the part of the officer; the general laws relating to admissible evidence; the legal foundation for the introduction of exhibits, confessions, dying statements, and physical objects; the general rules and regulations governing the giving of evidence in court. 

Drawings: How to read drawings so as to give proper evidence from them while on the stand.

PATROL DIVISION UNIT X. FIRST AID

Unit Objective

Ability to: Maintain and use uniform, badge, cap piece, buttons, notebook, pencil, city guide, sap, handcuffs, ammunition, revolver, and flashlight, complete and in good order according to specifications set forth by the department; operate weapons of department arsenal and motor equipment in accordance with department regulations; report to the garage on defective conditions or repairs needed on police cars; report on defects in arsenal items to officers issuing such items for use. 

Knowledge of: The department regulations regarding the care of the uniform and equipment; the best procedures in caring for equipment; operating shotguns, rifles, and other arms; operating automobiles; and the standards and specifications set forth by the department for the care and operation of this equipment.
