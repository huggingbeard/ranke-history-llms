COMMUNIST CHINA 

Population: 700-740 million

Capital: Peking (Peiping) 

Communist China occupies a land mass of some 3.7 million square miles in eastern Asia, an area comparable to that of the United States. It shares a common border with North Korea, the U.S.S.R., Outer Mongolia, Afghanistan, the Pakistan and Indian zones of Jammu and Kashmir, India, Nepal, Bhutan, Burma, Laos, and North Viet-Nam. Two-thirds of

China's area is mountainous or semidesert; little over one-tenth is cultivated. Ninety percent of the 

Chinese live in one-sixth of the country, primarily in the fertile plains and deltas of the east. 

Mainland China lies almost entirely in the temperate zone. Only portions of the three southernmost provinces-Yunnan, Kwangsi, and Kwangtungare within the tropics. Monsoonal climate is a major influence, with summers hot and humid throughout much of the country and winters dry and unusually cool or cold for the given latitude. The concentration of rain in the summer months frequently results in torrential downpours and is a major cause of the floods which often afflict China. 

THE PEOPLE 

Although no recent statistics have been issued, the Chinese population now approximates 700-740 million. This represents an increase from 583 million counted in China's first modern census in 1953. The rate of increase appears to have been some 2 to 2.5 percent per year until 1959, when it probably was affected by the succession of bad harvests. The regime has shown an ambivalent attitude to the growth of an already crowded population, having first endorsed birth control, then played it down in 1958, and again actively promoted it starting in 1962. 

Non-Chinese groups number 35 to 40 million and are concentrated mainly on the Chinese frontiers. They include Uighurs and Moslems in the northwest, Mongols and Manchus in the north and northeast, Chuangs in the south, and Tibetans. 

Although Communist policies have weakened the hold of religion, the vast majority of the population continues to believe in the popular Chinese religion, which consists of an eclectic mixture of Buddhist, Confucianist, Taoist, and animist elements. 

HI STORY 

As with most ancient cultures, the beginnings of Chinese culture are obscure. It is accurate to

say that the outlines of a Chinese culture are visible about 2 millennia before the birth of Christ. Under successive dynasties Chinese culture prospered and advanced to a point where Chinese achievements in literature, philosophy, art, and material objects were among the highest attained by man. 

The advent of the West had profound consequences for traditional China. Weak in the scientific field and untouched by an industrial revolution, Chinese power was no match for 19th-century Western expansionism. A series of military and political humiliations at the hands of the West in the 19th century slowly awakened Chinese intellectuals to the need for drastic changes in the traditional society if China were to be preserved as an entity. The process of change in a society possessing over 3,000 years of cultural continuity has not been an easy one, and China of the 20th century has been rent by political, economic, and intellectual chaos and revolution in its search for accommodation with the outside world. 

"People's Republic of China" 

at The Chinese Communists proclaimed the "People's Republic of China" on October 1, 1949,

Peking. The Communists took over a country that had been exhausted by nearly a generation of conflict and war and social upheavals; a country whose economy had been disrupted and many of whose industrial centers had been damaged or destroyed; a country whose people had become disillusioned by inflation and by the apparent inability of the government concurrently to solve China's economic problems and meet the political challenge of the Communists. As a consequence, substantial numbers of the population were ready for a change and willing to take a chance with any political organization that gave promise of establishing order and restoring the economy.

The Chinese Communists promised this and much more, including "freedom of thought, speech, publication, assembly, association, correspondence, person, domicile, religious belief, and freedom of processions and demonstration." It was also promised that the "People's Republic" shall "in a systematic manner transform the feudal and semifeudal land ownership system into a system of peasant land ownership. It shall protect the economic interests and private property of workers, peasants, the petty bourgeoisie, and the national bourgeoisie.'

The promised freedoms of thought, speech, publication, and the like fell before such mass political indoctrination campaigns as the "Three and Five Anti" campaigns of the early fifties and the attack on the intellectuals following the "hundred flowers' campaign of 1957.

The Chinese Communist leaders initially proclaimed that it was their objective to transform a weak and traditionally backward China into a militarily strong, modern, industrial state. Economic progress of the Chinese Communists in the years of rehabilitation following 1949 was impressive. They succeeded in curbing inflation, restoring the transportation network, and rebuilding many of the industrial plants destroyed during World War II, although the strains of the Korean war created serious financial difficulties. 

GOVERNMENT 

Peking has a typical Communist government, similar to that of the U.S.S.R. The authority of the 

Chinese Communist Party (CCP) Central Committee reaches into every phase of Chinese life through an extensive organization extending down to the village and city block. Strong military and security forces supplement the approximately 18.5 million members of the CCP who, together with the usual Communist women's, youth, and labor organizations, form a tight control web over the entire country. 

In the Communist jargon the Constitution of 1954 proclaimed the government to be "a people's democratic state led by the working class and based on the alliance of workers and peasants." The "people's democratic state" represents a transitional stage on the ill-defined road to "socialism, and the government ostensibly includes other“democratic" classes and certain rump political parties. 

In reality all power resides with the Communist Party.

ECONOMY First Five-Year Plan 

The first 5-year plan (1953-57) represented the beginning of the attempt to create an industrial 

China, following the period of rehabilitation. National income during these 5 years reportedly increased by an average of 9 percent per year. 

An annual increase of 18 percent in industrial output was claimed. (Western specialists believe that these rates probably were closer to 7 and 16 percent.) From 1953 to 1957 Peking's systematic and complete controls over the collection and distribution of foodstuffs and materials forcibly prevented a significant rise in per capita consumption and enlarged the state's share of China's total resources. The lion's part of the state's take was channeled into heavy industrial and military plants. 

But mainland China's leaders were dissatisfied with the progress of the economy during the first 5-year plan. The average increase in agricultural output (4.5 percent a year) was just sufficient to provide for an expanding population but not enough for impressive industrial growth. Moreover, the successive steps toward collectivization of the land may have caused difficulties in collecting food grain from the peasantry. Consequently, in 1958 Peking launched its second 5-year plan (1958-62) under the slogan "A Great Leap Forward," a program designed to speed the construction of a modern heavy industrial base in part through the reorganization of the countryside into "communes," which were expected to permit the mobilization of labor on an unprecedented scale.

The "Great Leap'' 

The "great leap" was inaugurated with an immense fanfare of “Socialist competitions” and campaigns to elicit pledges to work harder and achieve more. Production targets throughout the economy were raised and raised again. Even statisticians were told that "statistics must serve politics,' and became reluctant to report true production figures which failed to meet astronomical targets. 

Initially, in 1958, a bumper crop and wildly inflated production statistics convinced the regime that the "leap forward" was a great success; they even convinced themselves that grain production had doubled in one year. 

Catastrophe soon followed. The failure of experimental agricultural techniques, inadequate management, and lack of technical skills, coupled with adverse weather, led to agricultural failures in 1959 and 1960. Food rations sank to subsistence levels, and deficiency diseases were general. There may have been outright starvation in scattered areas. 

Agricultural raw materials almost disappeared, and industrial equipment and raw materials became scarce as foreign exchange earnings were diverted to the purchase of food. Workers and farmers, driven beyond their endurance by malnutrition and by the mass labor drive of the "leap forward" period, became apathetic and listless. The excessive concentration of labor and capital on heavy industry, the loss of centralized control over the rate of investment, and the breakdown of statistics led in turn to a breakdown of centralized economic planning. 

By 1960 it was manifest that the "great leap forward" was a fiasco. The great schemes were silently set aside. At the outset of 1961 Peking's planners laid down the new watchwords: priority to agriculture, more attention to light industry, retrenchment of heavy industry, and a widespread cutback of capital construction. The commune system was abandoned in fact, if not in name, and the regime reverted to the primary producer cooperatives of the early 1950's. Despite these measures, grain output in 1961 continued below normal and, while slightly improved in 1962 and 1963, food rations continued to be extremely tight. By 1962 industrial production had declined to the 1957 level, and through 1963 little capital construction was accomplished. 1124434

The actual degree of Communist China's economic setback can only be estimated since almost no statistics have been published by the regime since the fanciful "leap forward" claims of 1959. However, grain production in 1964 increased and the food shortage eased. Capital construction in key chemical fertilizer and petroleum industries also increased. Even if the regime can secure an increase in crop yields and gradually reestablish industrial growth rates approaching those of the first 

5-year plan, the failure of the "great leap forward'' represents an enormous loss in terms of economic growth, self-confidence, and faith in the regime's wisdom. 

Peking announced the beginning of a third 5-year plan (1966-70) in January 1966 but did not indicate publicly any of the targets of this plan. 

Efforts at improving the economic picture have been hampered by Communist China's extensive allocation of resources to the development of nuclear weapons. This program, aided at an earlier stage by the Soviet Union, resulted in the detonation at Lop Nor in western China of a nuclear device on October 16, 1964.

A second nuclear device was detonated on May 14, 1965, and a third detonation took place May 

10, 1966.

Soviet assistance to China has been relatively meager. The Soviet Union provided only two loans totaling U.S. $430 million to Communist China in the early 1950's. Military loans were also extended during the Korean War. Prior to 1960 Russia supplied China with technicians and complete sets of plants and equipment for Peking's heavy industrial buildup, but Peking paid for these technicians and supplies on a regular commercial basis. In mid- 

1960 the Soviet Union withdrew its technicians and cut back gasoline shipments for political reasons engendered by the Sino-Soviet split. 

Since 1960 Peking's pattern of trade has shifted from the bloc to the West. In 1960 some 70 percent of China's trade was with the bloc and 30 percent with the free world. By 1965 this ratio had reversed itself and approximately two-thirds of Peking's foreign trade is now with the free world. 

Communist China's recent purchases from the free world have in large part been huge grain imports needed to supplement poor crop yields. The overall shift in the pattern of trade away from the bloc and toward the free world may persist, for both political and economic reasons.

Though acutely aware of its own needs, Communist China nevertheless for political reasons has extended economic aid to other countries. Through 

1965 it had committed some U.S. $2.1 billion of such aid, $840 million of which was to non-Communist countries. However, not much more than 

25 percent of this latter figure had been utilized by the end of 1965.

CURRENT PROBLEMS Failure of the Great Leap Forward 

Popular support for the Communist regime has been affected by hunger, by Peking's failure to improve living conditions, and by disillusionment and loss of the hope that prosperity lies in the foreseeable future. Even the leaders must again recognize that mainland China represents a poor agrarian economy and that the prospect for growth into a modern industrial state is a matter of decades. There has been much disaffection, and reports are heard again of corruption, theft, and bribery, signs that lower cadres have lost their elan. Nevertheless, the regime's stability does not seem to be threatened, largely because of the far-flung and comprehensive control apparatus it has established in the past 14 years and because the army and party members, despite disillusionment, are still loyal to the regime.

The regime has, however, increasingly exhibited concern over the commitment and devotion of the younger generation to the ideals and goals which have been set by the present leaders. A massive indoctrination and political campaign was inaugurated early in 1966 under the slogan "the Great Cultural

Revolution" to restructure all educational and cultural institutions and programs and obliterate all "revisionist" and "bourgeois" influences. This campaign was accompanied by a major upheaval in the Chinese Communist Party affecting several Politburo members and numerous lower ranking party and government officials. 

Sino-Soviet Dispute 

In the past the keystone of Chinese Communist foreign policy had been the preservation of Sino- Soviet solidarity. Since early 1960 sharp differences of opinion and a strong Chinese bid for leadership in the Communist camp have split the monolithic structure. Basic differences between Peking and 

Moscow continue over both ideological and national issues. The post-Khrushchev Soviet leaders have been unable to resolve these problems, and the Chinese Communists have publicly branded the Soviet leaders as 'traitors to Marxism-Leninism."

FOREIGN POLICY 

The international objectives of the Chinese Communists, like their domestic policies, are in part Chinese-inspired and in part Communist-inspired. 

The Chinese objectives essentially are to bring under Peking's influence all territories that it regards as historically Chinese and to exclude the influence of rival powers, particularly the United States. 

The international Communist objectives are to promote communism in Asia and ultimately to communize the world.

Peking's leaders have assumed for themselves a major responsibility to press the cause of communism in colonial areas and among newly independent countries in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. 

They offer their own experience as a pattern for backward countries to follow in seeking transition from an agricultural to an industrialized state, though this model has undoubtedly been much tarnished by recent failures. 

Few of Peking's leaders, however, have had an extensive experience abroad or have meaningful comprehension of the world outside of Communist China. As a result their policies frequently reflect ignorance of the countries to which these policies are directed.

In 1965 Peking suffered a series of foreign policy setbacks, and its relations with a number of Afro- Asian countries, particularly Ghana and Indonesia, as well as with Cuba deteriorated sharply. 

SINO-U. S. RELATIONS 

In the area of Sino-United States relations there are two major factors to consider: U.S. strength and policies in Asia are the major obstacle to the Chinese Communists in the pursuit of their ambitions; furthermore, for ideological reasons the 

United States, as the major capitalist power, represents the arch-enemy in Chinese Communist demonology. Therefore, Peking's leaders have sought to associate the United States with all developments in the area adverse to their interests. Thus the United 

States is portrayed as the cause of border troubles with India and as behind Japan's reluctance to normalize relations with Communist China. We are made synonymous with "imperialism" and warmongering, and we are alleged to have suppressed independence movements in Asia and Africa. But above all, the United States is charged with the occupation of Taiwan, which the Chinese Communists claim as theirs and which they demand as the price for serious discussion of other issues between us, such as the imprisonment of Americans in Communist 

China or the visits of newsmen.

The U.S. commitment to Viet-Nam has also increasingly become a target of Chinese Communist attack. Peking has made clear that it considers the defeat by force of the United States in Viet-Nam as critical to both its national and ideological policy goals. 

U.S. POLICY 

U.S. policy toward Communist China takes into consideration the fact that, as President Johnson stated on July 12, 1966, "lasting peace can never come to Asia as long as the 700 million people of mainland China are isolated by their rulers from the outside world," and that, "a hostile China must be discouraged from aggression. A misguided China must be encouraged toward understanding of the outside world and toward policies of peaceful cooperation."

The essence of current U.S. policy was summed up in the following 10 points made by Secretary of State Rusk before the House Foreign Affairs Subcommittee on March 16, 1966: 

1. We must remain firm in our determination to help those allied nations which seek our help to resist Peking's direct or indirect use of the threat of force against their territory. 

2. We must continue to assist the countries of 

Asia in building broadly-based effective governments, devoted to progressive economic and social policies. 

3. We must honor our commitments to the 

Republic of China and to the people on Taiwan, who do not want to live under communism. We will continue to assist in their defense and to try to persuade the Chinese Communists to join with us in renouncing the use of force in the area of Taiwan. 

4. We will continue Our efforts to prevent the expulsion of the Republic of China from the U.N. or its agencies. So long as Peking follows its present course it is extremely difficult for us to see how it can be held to fulfill the requirements set forth in the charter for membership. The 

Chinese Communists will not consider membership until: the U.N. resolution of 1950 condemning Chinese 

Communist aggression in Korea is rescinded; there is a new resolution condemning U.S. "aggression"; the U.N. is reorganized; and the Republic of China and all other "imperialist puppets" are expelled. 

We believe the U.N. must approach this issue with the utmost caution and deliberation. 

5. We should continue our efforts to reassure 

Peking that the U.S. does not intend to attack mainland China. There are, of course, risks of war with 

China. But we do not want war. We do not intend to provoke war. The Chinese Communists have acted with caution when they foresaw a collision with the U.S. 

6. We must keep firmly in our minds that there is nothing eternal about the policies and attitudes of Communist China. We must avoid assuming the existence of an unending and inevitable state of histility between ourselves and the rulers of mainland China. 

7. We should continue to enlarge the possibilities for unofficial contacts between Communist China and ourselves. 

In this connection, we have gradually expanded the categories of American citizens who may travel to Communist China. American libraries may freely purchase Chinese Communist publications.

American citizens may send and receive mail from the mainland. We have in the past indicated that if the Chinese themselves were interested in purchasing grain we would consider such sales. We have indicated our willingness to allow Chinese Communist newspapermen to come to the U.S. We are prepared to permit American universities to invite Chinese Communist scientists to visit their institutions. 

We do not expect that for the time being the Chinese Communists will seize upon these avenues of contact or exchange. But we believe it is in our interests that such channels be opened and kept open. 

8. We should keep open our direct diplomatic contacts with Peking in Warsaw. These meetings play a role in enabling each side to communicate information and attitudes in times of crisis. It is our hope that they might at some time become the channel for a more fruitful dialogue. 

9. We are prepared to sit down with Peking and other countries to discuss the critical problems of disarmament and nonproliferation of nuclear weapons. 

10. We must continue to explore and analyze all available information on Communist China and keep our own policies up to date. We have said, in successive administrations, that when Peking abandons the aggressive use of force and shows that it is not irrevocably hostile to the U.S., then expanded contacts and improved relations may become possible.
