CHAPTER I 

"Mama goes here and mama goes there; mama's got diamonds stuck in her ear," sang Merle Jennings playfully as she pushed her protesting mother away from the candle lighted dining table and across the broad hall into the living room. "Now you stay there," she continued with mock sternness, forcing the elderly woman down into the cushions on the divan, "and just as soon as you have rested and the others get here you must cut your birthday cake."" "Stop it, stop it!" the mother begged and sighed in exhaustion as she relaxed among the pillows. "You forget I am a very sick woman and that I can't run about like I used to do. 

Now you children go along and let me rest. I've already overdone my strength and Dr. Norwell will be positively provoked when he learns of my foolish capers tonight. You must help me back to bed daughter. I should never have stayed up this late.” "No, no, not yet mother. And then there is Lottie and her friends who will be here any minute. What would they think if they came to your birthday party and found you in bed? Shame on you. Wouldn't that be something terrible and more than discourteous? No, you see, mumsy dear, it just can't be done, for it isn't nice at all. Now be a good little mother and stay up with us." Mrs. Jennings lived in the great, rambling old southern house with her only child, Merle, and the servants. Judge Jennings having died the previous winter. Always a good liver and leader in his community and descendant of a wealthy and aristocratic family, with many traditions for hospitality and fashionable customs to preserve, the Judge's salary and other incomes just about met the monthly demands of his expensive household. His death left his widow wholly ignorant of business affairs and unprepared to manage or conserve his rapidly dwindling estate. Reared among the most luxurious surroundings all her life and protected from the problems of the outside world by a doting husband she could not be expected to exercise the same wisdom that might be done by some other woman with better training. Little did she know or care about housekeeping or its economical management, much less the administration of a doubtful real estate business consisting of many mortgages, rents, taxes and kindred items connected therewith. It was hard enough for her indulgent husband, with his good salary coming in each month, to keep pace with the necessary expenses to say nothing of the high cost of maintaining appearances among the fashionable set they were accustomed to. Were it not for the servants who had been in their employ for the past several years the helpless woman would have been at a complete loss. And, to add to her other worries and difficulties, she suffered a severe nervous breakdown within a few weeks after the burial of her husband. 

But Merle Jennings was now twenty-two and had finished

her college only the past spring. Having more of her father's disposition and the independence of her own generation she was much better equipped to govern the household than was her idolent and incompetent mother. Immediately after returning home from school commencement she took upon her young shoulders the duties of administrator and housekeeper without suggestion. The girl inherited all her mother's love of society, gay parties and many friends together with her father's practicality and devotion to physical exercise and sports. Before leaving for college she had always owned a horse and might be seen daily cantering about the streets of the small southern town of Hampton, a municipality of some ten thousand people. 

Almost over night Hampton seemed to have blossomed into a small city from the bud of lazy village. With the advent of automobiles the easy going citizens lost a great part of their characteristic disregard of the future and the town discarded its antiquated customs. Paved streets and harsh concrete sidewalks replaced soft dirt drives overhung with gigantic elms. 

Gasolene service stations broke up the continuous symmetry of large and shaded lawns of proud homesteads.

The Jennings house occupied at one time a large knoll in the very heart of the city. Its spacious yard was bounded on each of its four sides by a street, thus comprising an entire square or block. Large oaks and shapely elms once proudly waved their branches over the velvety lawn below and witnessed many happy parties there. Originally the homestead was inclosed by an iron picket fence. It was one of the saddest moments of the Judge's life when he was compelled to begin to draw this fence in, closer to the house, to make room for other dwellings. 

The ever increasing expenses of his small family and his wife's unrelenting determination to retain the splendor of the old days forced him to sell off a lot from time to time until now

the home was flanked on either side by bungalows and other less imposing structures. 

The dwelling occupied by the Jennings was a large colonial mansion typical of the slavery time homesteads. Four huge and fluted columns reaching from the ground level of the front porch passed by a small balcony on the second story and supported a projecting gable above. The broad veranda covered the entire front and stopped half way down each side of the main body of the building and a door from either side opened out at these ends. The ancient structure seemed to stand aloof from its less pretensious neighbors surrounding it. The principal part of the once great yard which was left undisturbed was the sweeping gravel walk running from the three steps at the entrance down the slight incline to the iron gate at the sidewalk. The snowy whiteness of the house was perpetuated during its owner's lifetime by a yearly coat of paint and it was now beginning to show quite noticeably the need of another dressing. 

Today was Mrs. Jennings' birthday anniversary and Merle had wished to surprise her mother with a little dinner party. 

But the joy of the surprise had to be eliminated because it required several hours' pleading and coaxing to persuade the elderly woman to put on her evening gown and join the few invited friends. Part of each day she might be up about her room for a short while but for months she had not taken any of her meals in the dining room, but was served in bed. Therefore, Merle was compelled to dine alone except when company was present and this proved to be quite frequent. After much petting and begging Mrs. Jennings finally consented to have her dinner with her daughter and one other guest, Cameron Doran, lifelong friend of the family and Merle's fiancee. Later there would be other visitors who would call

to pay their respects to the feted woman and while away the better part of the evening dancing.

The lone dinner guest at the birthday party, Cameron Doran, was Merle's childhood sweetheart and their devotion to each other was much like brother and sister. The girl's parents had for many years taken it as a decree of destiny that these young people would some day be man and wife. The couple had long since settled this highly important matter. And so thoroughly did their friends understand the situation that even the most ardent suitors for the hand of the pretty, black-eyed brunette soon conceded the impossibility of ousting Cameron from the woman's affections. When either was invited to a function the other went along as a matter of course. 

Cameron had never known his parents, having been reared and cared for by his Aunt Lucy, his father's sister. This good woman had done her best by her nephew and strained her limited finances to the point of giving him two years in college before the boy voluntarily gave up his studies to take work on the morning paper as a cub reporter. His indulgent kinswoman would doubtless have gone to further extremes and denied herself much to have allowed the young man to receive his diploma. But Cameron, knowing of her limited means and insecure future, refused to permit her to spend any more on himself, altho it was one of the bitterest disappointments of his life to forego his education. He endeavored to compensate for the loss of his college training by copious reading and study at home.

Cameron was called from his duties on the newspaper by the entrance of his country into the World War and for twentyfour months he wore the uniform in France. Entering the army a healthy speciman of manhood, tall and slender, eager blue eyes, elastic step and an abundance of boyish energy, he returned before the Armistice a broken veteran with a bullet

in his shoulder and suffering from a gas attack which bid well to follow him the rest of his days. His unfortunate war experience furnished him another great ambition-to get well again. The doctors held one ray of hope, absolute rest for several years and possibly treatment for a long period in a government hospital. This advice he declined to heed. His greatest yearning was to be a writer and he felt that if he had but a few years to live he must make use of every minute. And he also thought that he could never regain his once robustness by mere loafing. No, the chances were too great that he might not be able to realize his lifelong dream of authorship.

The government allowed Cameron a small monthly pension in return for his sacrifice and this just about covered his actual living expenses with little left over for extras. What time he did not spend in the open air in the interest of his diseased lungs he used in writing. These efforts of the muse urge proved a great disappointment to the ambitious boy, for as regularly as he submitted his writings just as consistently were they returned unaccepted. It was true that he was discouraged but he kept doggedly at it, hoping that the time would come when his work would be appreciated. Write, write, write, this was his main theme in life. Just to pass the dull time away, if nothing else, he would continue to write. His melancholy days were interspersed with occasional bright hours in company with Merle. To her alone he had confided his secret and he had begun to realize sadly that it was only the ultimate success in his chosen profession could he ever hope to call her his wife. 

His government allowance was much too inadequate to support them both and he was ill-fitted for any business position. At first quite as enthusiastic as Cameron, the girl later tried to dissuade him and tactfully encouraged him to apply his efforts along some more lucrative channel. In this her father's practical mind asserted itself, to the extent of trying to advise her

childhood chum. The two discussed his affairs quite freely but the woman's admonition to seek other employment went for little and only served to spur him on to greater efforts. To her above all others Cameron would rather prove that he could really write something of worth.

Judge Jennings had, during his lifetime, tried to interest his prospective son-in-law in the study of law, even going to the extent of promising to help finance his education and perhaps later taking him in as a junior partner in his own practice. It was a secret disappointment to Merle when the boy declined the kind offer of her father. His pride might have had a great 

Ideal to do with his reluctance to accept a seemingly good proposition from the parent of the woman he hoped some day to marry. But the greatest of all reasons for his refusal to accede to the man's wishes was that he thoroughly detested the thought of being an attorney. And time proved that the decision of the boy was right when the Judge died, leaving little legal business and less money.

Owing to the fact that he kept his writing a secret from all except Merle, his friends conceived the idea that Cameron's chief trouble was pure laziness or indifference. And the girl, too, began unconsciously to share in this opinion and it was with much effort that she drove these thoughts out of her mind. 

She had by much pleading prevented her mother from calling the young man to account for his seeming negligence concerning the very important subject of making a living. 

Cameron was far from being slow witted and quite naturally sensed the opinion being formed of him by his friends, tho no one was rude enough to actually hint his feelings in the boy's presence. Many sleepless nights he tossed from side to side on his single bed and debated with himself the wisdom of following his own inclinations instead of the apparently sounder advice of his acquaintances. At times he felt like a veritable

slacker as he noted the rapid progress being made by other men of his age, men who had been less studious in school than he and perhaps less capable. Some of these men were now well established in business, married and really taking responsible places in the world of commerce. Quite a few of them were already practicing young physicians and lawyers and were becoming well and favorably known in their chosen professions. 

Cameron proved the lie to the suggestion of laziness as far as his own conscience was concerned by writing steadily every available hour, sometimes entirely thru the night and into the following day. This was the unfortunate part about it that he did keep his tongue and none knew of his industry, with the possible exception of Merle Jennings. Suffering in silence he kept everlastingly at it, solacing himself with the belief that other writers before him had experienced worse difficulties and disappointment than perhaps he was encountering. The weak condition of his body only added to the general unsuccessful appearance of the young man and altho he was always neatly dressed it seemed to most people that he was not making the best of his opportunities. His one great asset was a charming personality and the ready ability to make and hold the friendship of whomsoever he met. This happy attribute somewhat offset the usual tendency of his associates to mentally criticize his evident efforts to avoid work and to drift along with the current of life.

Tonight Cameron was secretly more melancholy than usual. 

With a forced smile he tried to seem unconcerned and gay for Mrs. Jennings' sake and to enter into the festivities of the birthday party in earnest. But his laughter sounded terribly flat and unreal to his own ears, even the tone of his voice being foreign. Merle noted this lack of sincerity in his efforts to be good company and mischievously reprimanded him for it. She loved him dearly and it was only for his good and

their future happiness that she had ever hinted at the idea that he might make more progress in something other than writing. 

However, she longed for him success in his chosen work and really believed that some day he would achieve it. 

Mrs. Jennings pleaded in vain with her daughter to allow her to return to her bedroom immediately after dinner. But the girl hoped that by insisting upon her mother staying up she might get tired enough to gain a good night's sleep without drugs, and that she would later be benefitted by the little dissipation. CHAPTER II 

The dance in the Jennings home broke up near midnight in difference to the mother who had already gone to bed. The half dozen couples took their leave of Merle and Cameron, the latter by this time having become use to assuming the role of host along with the hostess when any function took place in the old house. Together he and Merle walked to the gate and there lingered for some time before saying goodnight. 

The brilliant summer moon looked down approvingly as hand in hand both lovers remained silent for a few minutes. "You don't seem like yourself tonight, Cam. Are you feeling all right?" "Yes, I'm all right I guess. It's probably one of my usual blue spells that I have so often when I think of the slow progress I am making and the possible postponement in our marriage date. It looks as tho I'll never get one of my stories accepted and when I read some of the stuff that is on the market it makes me want to quit and give up for good." "I'm sorry Cam. I think your work is wonderful and some day the editors will think so too. But, Cam dear, don't you believe that it would be better to make writing a side line until you get started? You see it must be terribly disappointing to you to labor so hard and then not realize the results you so richly deserve. Why not get into something else for a while, something that will pay until you can get your stories published?" "What, for instance, Merle? I'm really interested." "Oh, anything dear. It doesn't make so much difference. You see with your pension and any ordinary job we could live

splendidly and you could put in your spare time writing and maybe I could help you. You see we have tentatively announced our wedding for sometime next spring, perhaps in

May and that's but a few months away. We will have to eat. 

That is one of my many small weaknesses. And you know

Daddy's estate is nearly eaten up with taxes and interest and there's a huge mortgage on the home now and it isn't long until another payment is due on it. We have no idea what we will do if they should foreclose the loan. The shock would simply kill mother. She doesn't understand about those things and can't see why we can't keep on in the same happy go lucky manner we did before Daddy died. Now Cam, I am not thinking about my own comfort and happiness. I'd like nothing better, dear, than to marry you now and live in a single room and help you with your work." "But Merle, you don't imagine for a moment that I could even dream of living on your father's estate or in your house unless I could pay for it, do you?" "Of course not, Cameron." She always called him Cameron when really serious; Cam being the usual name and the one he liked best for her and his friends to know him by. He could always tell how serious she was by the manner she addressed him. "Mother will never be well again. Dr. Norwell said so last week. He thought I should know her true condition. He thinks that she is gradually growing worse each day and that it is a matter of but a few months with her. So you see it is more than just a nervous breakdown. But, of course, she isn't aware of it and Dr. Norwell thinks it better never to advise her. It is my hope and prayer that we may find some means of keeping the home place as long as she lives and that she will never fully realize just how really destitute we are." Tears stood in the big black eyes of the girl as Cameron embraced her passionately and somehow he felt that the long lingering kiss she returned to him had something pathetic in it. 

There was a sort of clinging reluctance to leave in the warm

lips as tho they held on for some kind of protection from the man they caressed.

The girl's words were ringing in his ears as Cameron walked slowly back to his room. The power of wealth and the possession of riches, how quickly could they wipe away the tears and cancel out every doubt and anxiety in the heart of the woman he loved. A full grown man, a veteran of the World 

War, a man called upon by his country in its extremity as a willing patriot, an able fighter for the great flag, yet he now stood incompetent to relieve the petty distresses of the girl he worshipped. Never before had Cameron realized the value of money. To him it had always been a mere means to an end and only a medium of exchange for bankers and merchants. 

Perhaps it did assist in keeping body and soul together occasionally by supplying the bare necessities of living. But it had never been considered as indespensible to existence to Cameron. Tonight money had suddenly become the greatest desire of his heart. Just a little of the mountain of gold in the world could now make of him a hero in the eyes of the poor helpless Merle. It would clear those deep dark eyes of the tears that came when she thought of facing the uncertain future. Added to his own already intelligent mind it would make of Cameron a power to be reckoned with. Yet without it he was helpless as a child. He must stand idly by and allow some tightlipped money lender annoy and aggravate the soul of the girl he had promised to protect and wished to shield from even the smallest ills of the earth. All his life Cameron had detested the thoughts of personal riches as the meaning of the word is usually accepted. He had never desired any more than enough to procure the actual demands of his physical body. But now that he had listened to Merle's confession of their inevitable crisis which must shortly come to her his heart ached for her and made him feel like a cowardly

drifter in his own near poverty. Her words had awakened in him a new craving. Heretofore all his ambitions were centered on making a name for himself in literature, one that would live after he had passed on, to remain as a monument to his work on earth. Now the paramount wish in his heart was for the mercenary value of his compositions and that alone, not for his own comfort but that he might obtain that with which to make his darling happy. How proudly would he pay off that mortgage and with a justified oath send that money lending Jew on his way.

Jingling the few coins in his pocket, the last of the previous month's Government check and the sole means of his subsistance for the remainder of the week, he became angry with hmself. These few pieces of silver constituted practically his entire material fortune. For a moment he shook with inward rage and in a fit of temper hurled the small handful of money as far as his strength would send it. Audible curses escaped his lips as he heard the metal discs ring together when they landed several yards away. If they were all he had they were not worth keeping and as for himself he had just as well be done with trying to get anywhere.

The reaction to this sudden display of temper seemed to revive Cameron's sagging spirits and silently laughing to himself he searched for the coins he had just thrown from him. 

With a quickened step he continued then to his room and to bed without touching his typewriter.

CHAPTER III 

Merle had much to do for her mother's comfort and consequently she and Cameron were the last guests to arrive at 

Doris Ormand's party the following week after the birthday dinner for Mrs. Jennings. It was some of her escort's fault also, since he had no car and Merle had refused to allow him to get a taxi, saying that she preferred to walk when the night was cool and moony. He knew that she did this to save him the slight expense but there was nothing to do but accede to her wishes in the matter.

The dancing had already started when the final couple entered the hallway of the Ormand home. Dodging the gliding pairs of merrymakers in the rather small house as they danced in and out of the living room and around the staircase, Merle and Cameron went to the designated places to dispose of their wraps.

Several of his friends were in the men's room when Cameron entered and he was greeted cordially for he was popular with all who knew him. They chatted for some little time together and one or two took a sip from Bob Squire's pocket flask. 

Cameron declined the whiskey but indulged in a cigarette. 

He was not in much mood for dancing either. As a matter of truth he was never fond of the pastime and his last few years of experience had not improved his taste for parties of any kind. His chief and perhaps only reason for attending them was that Merle was always there. The passing of autumn and approach of the Christmas season proved Richard Wall to be more of a personage to be reckoned with than either Merle or Cameron had ever dreamed. 

His passionate wooing of the girl was fast and furious and acknowledged no opposition. For weeks and months she had fought off his advances and endeavored to remain loyal to her childhood sweetheart but in the end she succombed to his hypnotic love making.

Mrs. Jennings became the immediate captive of the handsome suitor for her daughter's hand, seeing in the possible match and subsequent marriage of Merle to this man the ending of her financial difficulties. She could see in the hazy future a glorious renewal of the splendor and prestige of the old mansion, which were in vogue many years ago. CHAPTER VII 

Cameron Doran pulled the heavy overstuffed leather chair to the corner of the balcony in the Merchants and Manufacturers' Club and disconsolately took a seat in the shade. From his position on the second floor of the building he could look directly down upon the traffic of the main thoroughfare of the city and yet be quite hidden from view himself. All stores and other establishments were closed for the occasion, May 30th, Memorial Day. The world seemed light hearted and merry, and the bubbling laughter of the jostling throngs reaching the ears of Cameron only added to his intense misery.

Maybe it might contain a short note suggesting certain minor changes which possibly would make it suitable to some other editor.

did possess real merit and was equal to much of the material he had already seen in print. He even doubted that the editors had read one word of his book and with resentment and despair in his heart he thought of consigning the whole thing to the fire when he returned to his room.

Each trip the big brown envelope made away from the city it left Cameron with a heart full of hope, which gradually increased in fervency until a couple of weeks later he found the manuscript stuffed back in his mail box. And again the entire incident would be repeated. For more reasons than one and far more than merely seeing his writings in print, he was bitterly disappointed when he found the book returned again this morning. An acceptance by the publisher might have meant the prolongation of life itself in his frail body, to say nothing of the great inspiration to further efforts. His meager income allowed by the government was beginning to fall behind in keeping pace with his expenditures; unexpected doctor bills and similar items having lately exceeded the monthly stipend. 

The future held out bleak prospects indeed, with the added fear of a possible nervous breakdown to top off all his other troubles. His physician had ordered Cameron to the country or to some resort where he could spend his entire wakeful time in the open air, and instructed the discouraged writer to abstain from work of any nature. But the well meaning advisor had not taken into consideration the limited finances of his morose patient and therefore his orders were not carried out. 

But after all, these were minor miseries to Cameron compared to the great tragedy which would take place in his life next week when Merle, his lifelong sweetheart and the only girl he had ever loved, a girl who for years was pledged to him, would become the bride of rich, reckless, domineering, handsome Richard Wall. CHAPTER IX

Santa Claus." "Golly, it's good to know that I was dreaming for I was having a terrible time a few minutes ago," laughed Cameron, throwing his arms about his pretty wife and kissing her.

CHAPTER XVII 

The first happy weeks in the Blue Ridge slipped by rapidly and another Christmas was almost here. Cameron's former habitual paleness was gradually being replaced by a weather beaten bronze complexion. Of late he looked more like the athlete he had been before his unfortunate experience in the war. Daily he and Merle took long walks thru the hills and among the jagged rocks near their little home. They loved to follow the steep beds of the mountain streams when there was no water in them. Both had found perfect contentment hidden away to themselves in the great outdoors. They almost forgot that they once belonged to a world of dances and parties, country clubs and evening clothes, taxicabs and trolley cars, servants and steam heated houses.

Cameron's third book was beginning to sell and he now spent many hours of the day on his fourth and should complete it long before the summer days arrived again. While his royalties had not been large, sufficient money had been realized from his writings to assure him that the proverbial wolf would find little encouragement at their humble door. The great improvement in his physical condition had done wonders in brightening up his serious disposition, quite as much as his final marriage to Merle. At the rate he was regaining his health they might begin to prepare to go back to the city in the late spring.

On several of their many trips to the mouth of the pretty valley, where the couple were fond of going to see the view that lay beyond and of which they never tired, they stopped at

Hogue Stevens' cabin. Vainly they endeavored to make friends with the silent man. Each time they passed his home he was invariably standing in the doorway and the dog was always propped against the fence and growling defiance as he glared out of one eye at Merle and her husband. The cheery greeting to the old man was never answered by more than a short grunt. This reply, they could not decide, should be taken as an intended salutation, or an expression of disapproval of their presence. All their well meant efforts to break the ice between themselves and the strange mountaineer were futile. 

Each night they could see a light in the lonely cabin below them. It came on shortly after sun down and went out promptly at the same hour later in the evening. It was more like the reflection of a pine knot thrown in the big fire place than that caused by a lamp or candle. They concluded that the man cooked his supper at this time and that the fading of the light in his window was a sign that he had gone to bed and let the fire die down of itself. 

For the past two or three nights the couple in Buck Davis's cottage failed to see the light in the cabin at the mouth of the valley. Since the weather had been rather unsettled and wet they had temporarily abandoned their usual hikes down the steep slope. The absence of the light in Hogue Stevens' window caused Merle and Cameron to speculate between themselves as to the probable cause. They were sure that they would not have missed it, for the leaves were still off the trees and the cabin was plainly to be seen, even during the day time. 

Perhaps, they thought, Hogue had made one of his periodic trips to the lowlands to trade apples for coffee and sugar. But still, if this was true, he had remained away much longer than was his habit. Five days had gone by and still no light. It was also possible that the old man was sick. Fearing the latter, Merle stated to her husband at the breakfast table that she

was going to visit the lower end of Vengeance Valley that very morning and make an investigation. If the old hermit was in need it was their duty to help him. In this decision Cameron fully concurred and they rushed thru the job of washing dishes and put on their outer coats and prepared to go. "We'd better take along some food for both old Hogue and his dog," suggested the woman. "If we knew that he was sick and what the trouble is we might take some of that medicine in your first aid kit. I suppose we had better leave that off until we find out whether or not he needs it." "Sure, don't bother about medicine. That old fellow is off on a spree somewhere. Maybe the dog is hungry. We can throw something over the fence for him."

An hour and a half of hard walking and partially sliding on the damp earth brought the couple near their destination and they slowed up a bit when just in front of the cabin and made a preliminary survey from the outside. The door was closed this time and no sign of life within. The dog, however, was in his accustomed place at the fence and more vociferous than ever. "I'll be willing to bet that the old man is sick and that the poor dog hasn't had a bite to eat this whole week," said Merle. "Let's knock at the dor and see if any one is at home."

After a second and more vigorous rapping on the simple pine door and which could easily have been pushed open by a child, a coarse but weak voice came from somewhere inside the house. "Who's there?" "It's your neighbors from up on the ridge," answered 

Cameron. "We hadn't seen your light for several evenings and thinking that perhaps you were sick and needed help, we have come down to find out."

“Well, I am sick if the news is any good to you. But I don't want any of your help." "But we have some nice warm food for you and some for your dog too. May we came in and leave it? You want us to feed your dog don't you?" encouraged the woman. "You can come in if nothing else will suit you but your man stays out. I know what he wants and I haven't got any."

Cameron smiled at the suspicion entertained of him by the old man and replied, "why I am not a revenue officer, Mr. 

Stevens. I'm just a poor writer who has come up here to recover his health. I've been sick too. It's all right. May we come in?" "I don't care who you are you stay out. Do you understand?" "Let me go on in, Cameron. I know it's perfectly safe and you will be right here at the door. You know how suspicious the old fellow is." "I am a little afraid for you to go in there alone for there is no telling what he might do. For all you know he may have some contagious disease. But he does sound like he needs help of some sort. You can tell that from his voice." "Oh, don't worry about my safety. He hasn't anything that's catching and if he did I wouldn't be afraid. I'll leave the door open when I go in so that we can see what we are getting into. Here, give me the basket of food."

Pushing open the door ever so gently and which was only fastened by having been hung so that it would swing together of its own accord when released, Merle heard the man move in one corner of the room. She stood for a moment to allow her eyes to become accustomed to the semi-darkness inside the cabin. When she could see she was surprised to note that it was remarkably clean and well kept. What little furniture there was appeared to be of a good quality and in

excellent repair. There was a rather ancient carpet on the rough board floor. The fire was out in the huge grate and there was no evidence that one had been lighted for several days. The cold dank atmosphere that struck the woman's face was remindful of that which one notices on opening a stale refrigerator. It really felt much colder and more unpleasant inside the house than it did out in the cutting wind. 

In one corner of the large room stood a double wooden bed, a valuable antique, no doubt. Its head piece reached quite to the top of the low ceiling. On this bed lay the owner. The linen looked as tho it had been recently changed. Evidently unpressed after laundering it was put on the mattress clean only a few days ago. The sick man turned his face toward his feminine visitor and viewed her darkly for some minutes thru his piercing black eyes. No sign of welcome or appreciation was apparent on his brown and grizzled countenance. He spoke to her first. "Well, you are here. Are you satisfied? What do you want?" "We were afraid you were sick and since we hadn't seen your light for several days we thought it best to run down and see if there was anything we could do for you. Let my husband come in and make you a fire for I know you must be dreadfully cold." "No. No man comes in here. If you want a fire build it yourself. There's plenty of wood over there in the corner." "All right, I'll build one. But aren't you hungry and don't you want some nice cool water to drink? I bet you haven't felt like fixing a thing for yourself." "No, I don't want anything to eat but you can bring me a glass of water, if you are bound to do something. Your man can step over to the spring and get it."

"Fine, and while he goes to the spring for us do you mind if I feed your dog something?" "I don't care a hang what you do with him. It would serve him right if he starved to death. But look out when you open that door or he'll chew you up sure as sin, and don't let him get by you. Watch out now!"

Merle smiled sympathetically at the man's, evident serious injunctions and cracked the door ever so slightly and peered out thru the slit. She then cast several pieces of bread to the animal. The half starved beast greedily snatched the morsels of food from the air, letting one or two only strike the ground. 

The woman went back for more food and this time she opened the door a little wider and the man on the bed rose to a sitting position and screamed out his previous warnings, his face showing that he was actually frightened. "Shut it, shut it. What's the matter with you? Want to be strangled to death by that brute?" "Oh, you are just nervous today, Mr. Stevens," answered 

Merle soothingly. "The poor fellow is just so hungry he doesn't know what to do. He doesn't want to come in. See, he nearly wagged his tail when I gave him that last piece of bread. Now that's all I am going to let him have for that's enough until we can run back in the morning. Here's your water and its so fresh and cool. Let me straighten up your bed a bit before I go. And your pillow needs beating up so badly. It must be awfully uncomfortable that way. You are going to rest so much better after I fix things.

Cameron waited on the outside impatiently while his wife ministered to the sick man in the foreboding looking log cabin. 

He could hear the woman talking to her patient and his monosyllabic answers. It brought a sigh of relief when Merle returned to his side and after she had closed the door behind her. 

The big dog shoved his long muzzle over the high fence and

turning his head to one side he gave his recent benefactors a close canine scrutiny. His growling had ceased but after the couple had gone some two or three hundred yards back up the trail they could hear short barks of satisfaction, sounding something like the yaps of a puppy that is left behind when he had rather go along. "Gee, I was some relieved when you came out of that shack a few minutes ago and I was uneasy the whole time I was going and coming from the spring. What do you make of the situation?" "Nothing strange. He's just a timid old backwoodsman," replied Merle, "and doesn't know what it is to be treated kindly. He wasn't very profuse in his thanks for our efforts for him but still I believe that he was glad we came." "Seemed to drink that water like he was sure enough thirsty." "Yes, and he probably has some fever tho his forehead and his hands seemed cool to me. We must run down and see him again in the morning. I promised him that I would, or rather 

I just told him that we'd come," she laughed.

CHAPTER XVIII 

The daily visits of Merle and her husband to the sick mountaineer's cabin became a part of their regular routine. At the distance the man was required to keep from the inside of the house it was impossible for Cameron to diagnoze his case and Hogue would not hear of their sending for a doctor. The old fellow intimated that he would promptly shoot the first male who crossed his threshold. The woman brought to his bedside what few and harmless remedies they possessed, hoping that in their absence he might make use of one or more of them in the treatment of his illness. The woman alone was ever allowed to come into the room. 

Hogue's dog began to look for the kind visitors who brought him his daily ration of bread and meat scraps. But each time 

Merle fed the animal she blocked the door with her foot to keep him from getting out, while the man on the bed looked on with fear in his eyes. And when she set the pan of water on the single log step she always threw several pieces of bread to the other end of the small yard. This lured the dog away from the door and gave the woman time to leave his water without affording him an opportunity to escape. 

Buck Davis, the mail carrier, had made one or two trips to the cabin with his two friends since the old man had become ill. But, like Cameron, he had not been allowed to come into the house for closer examination of the patient or to offer his assistance. All the information he received was from the woman. It was his opinion that Hogue was merely recovering from or enduring a protracted drunk and he expressed very little sympathy for him. 

The sixth or seventh day after Merle had regularly closed

the door to the cabin at the foot of Vengeance Valley she whispered to her husband when they had gone some few hundred feet along the trail, "You know, Cameron, I don't believe that old Hogue Stevens is really as sick as he pretends to be. But I can see no earthly reason for his letting on." "What makes you think that? And why doesn't he prepare himself some food and build a fire these cold days?" "I think the man is slightly unbalanced in his mind. Every time I go into his little home I find slight changes about the place that shows me that he has been up some time during the day or night. He has even been to the spring, for a fresh bucket of water was on the shelf this morning. He keeps mumbling about going away soon and tells me in his gruff manner that I am wasting my time trying to help him get well. Yet he always seems to feel better when I go in, for he has such a relieved expression on his face. And another thing that shows that he has been up, as much as he pretends to fear that dog he or some one else has put a leather collar about his neck and tied him to the fence by a short rope which is just long enough to allow him to reach the doorstep where 

I leave his water." "I think those actions show signs of intelligence. Perhaps he has a good day now and then and while he felt like it he tied the dog, fearing that he might do you some harm." "No, I don't think that. I really believe he is afraid of the beast for he lies there on his bed in terrible suspense the entire time I have the door open, even today after he knew the dog was securely fastened outside. I wish I knew all about that queer man and what he thinks about all the time. He isn't nearly so illiterate as he would have you to believe. 

There's something worrying him and I think that he is losing his mind over it." "Seems to me you would have learned all there was to know about Hogue by this time."

"Swell chance to find out anything from him. But I do know that he likes to have things clean and neat around him. 

When he talks about her while I am fixing his bed I have a vague feeling that he is somehow referring to me and gives me the queerest little creepy sensations. What do you think he can mean?" "Maybe he is referring to his wife who they accuse him of having murdered," said Cameron with a slight shudder. "No I really don't think so and I doubt very much if he did what they accuse him of. Today he actually grumbled a reluctant 'thank you' when I bathed his rough old hands and face. But I know that he is getting weaker every day for he doesn't so much as touch the food we leave for him. I always throw it out to the dog and put the fresh we bring beside his bed. I wish we could get Buck up here now in time to visit the cabin tomorow. Maybe you and he could just go on in whether he liked it or not and make him talk. You see he has known Buck for several years and he might be persuaded to have some confidence in him. Maybe he will bring the mail early enough in the morning to come with us." "Your suggestion sounds all right and perhaps we can carry it out. We'll try anyway. I still get terribly uneasy when you go in there alone. And especially when you are feeding that brute of a dog. Those deep growls he utters when he is munching his food sounds as tho he'd like nothing better than to sink those old teeth into some one's flesh. I always stand so I can see all the way thru the house to where you are and 

I keep my pistol ready to shoot." "Oh, I don't think all that precaution is necessary," laughed 

Merle. "You needn't be so uneasy for my safety. You know, it's a funny thing and perhaps foolish too with the knowledge of the reputation the animal bears, I am not the tiniest particle afraid of that dog. Of course, he doesn't wag his tail like

other dogs when they are pleased but still he has a sort of doggy thankfulness in his eyes when I feed him and talk to him. I bet the poor fellow has never heard a kind word before, so how could he know how to wag his tail? It it weren't against your wishes I wouldn't hesitate to go out there with him and pat his head. I know he wouldn't try to bite me." "Listen honey, don't you ever dare to do such a foolish thing," cautioned Cameron in alarm at the woman's suggestion. "Every one knows that he is a killer and as treacherous as a panther. Why, he is said to have actually attacked humans, including his own master, and that is why he is kept confined. Don't you remember what Buck said about that hunter who stopped there to get a drink of water and to enquire his way last winter? How he tried to pat the dog's head by reaching his hand over the fence and then how the animal nearly chewed his fingers off?" "He had been teasing the dog before that. Buck says so himself. And his master had already given him a cruel beating the same day. How could the poor beast know that the man wasn't trying to hurt him? I really don't blame him. 

The poor fellow has scars all over his body. I'll be willing to bet anything that his shaggy old head has never received a friendly pat in all his life." "Well, just the same you are not going to be the one to initiate that caressing business with Hogue Stevens' dog. And if you ever do try it don't forget that I have warned you when you find you have lost a hand. For myself I had just as soon cuddle a cross tiger. Much safer I think."

The remainder of the day the chief conversation between 

Merle and her husband centered about Hogue Stevens and his dog. All their requests for information concerning their strange neighbor fell upon deaf ears and little did they learn thru his telling.

CHAPTER XIX 

The couple in the cottage alternately read and discussed the sick man in the valley below them until bed time. Merle went out on the back porch to get a drink of cool water and presently called to her husband, "Come out here, Cam. What is that?" "Sounds like a dog barking to me. Probably some hunter down in the valley, but then he wouldn't be hunting tonight unless it is for 'possums and it's too rough for that. It doesn't sound like a dog that has treed something but more like one frightened or angry. Listen, that's Hogue Stevens' dog. It's coming from that direction. And look, there's a light in his cabin. I wonder what could be happening? If he is as weak as you said he seemed to be today he must be growing worse. 

He has never had a light on this late. It's nearly eleven o'clock. Everything isn't exactly right according to that dog's yapping." "Cam, perhaps the man is worse and is making that light to signal us. I couldn't go to bed feeling that he needed our help. Let's go down there and see what's wrong. We can peek in quietly and if everything is all right we can slip back and he'll never be the wiser." "Not with that dog about, you can't get away with that slip stuff. And you are not going in that cabin tonight. I'll tell you that much before we leave, honey. If any one waits on 

Hogue it will be me if I have to follow my gun in there." "Scarry cat," laughed Merle, "we can decide on that after we get down there. Come on and let's hurry. We can make

it in less than an hour if we walk fast. I feel like a hike tonight anyway."

The couple went down the precipitous sloping valley in a half trot. Both being well hardened to mountain walking by this time the distance wasn't a difficult feat for either. They were over three fourths of the way to the little home when they turned a short curve in the trail. Merle, clutching her husband's arm in excitement exclaimed, "Cam, the house is on fire and may be he isn't able to get out by himself! Hurry, we must get to him! Oh, if this rain will only come down a little harder it may help some! We haven't a chance to put that fire out. Look, the flames are coming out thru the roof now! And yonder is an automobile away down on the side of the mountain and it's coming this way, see those lights over there? It may be Buck and I surely hope it is. Come on, Cam, we must hurry! There's not a minute to lose."

The cabin was a roaring furnace when the breathless couple ran into the tiny yard. With a feeling of horror they realized that nothing could be alive in that inferno. Cameron sheltered his face from the terrific heat with his hat and crept near and peered thru the single window. His face blanched even in the red reflection of the fire and Merle knew only too well what he had seen." "Oh, Cameron, he is in there isn't he?" she screamed. "Yes and he has done this himself. That is what he meant when he said he was going away. I am glad we didn't build him a fire in the grate today or I would feel that we were in some measure responsible for it. We thought it was too warm and it was. Poor fellow, his worries are over now." 

Meanwhile the dog was baying loudly and plunging against the rope which held him prisoner, endeavoring with all his prodigious strength to escape from the creeping flames that were steadily getting nearer and hotter. The fence would soon

burn down to where the rope was tied, but long before that the animal would suffer the same terrible death experienced by his master.

While Cameron and his wife debated the rescue of the dog Buck Davis stopped his car and leaped out to join them. Few words were passed in useless salutations. "I just saw this a little while ago. I had gone over to take a package to Neal Thomas and was starting for home and reflection of the fire caught my eye. I first thought it was your place. It's too bad for old Hogue. I am sorry for the dog but I don't see that there is anything that we can do about it. He would tear you to pieces if you tried to go in there and let him out. Maybe it's better to let him go along with his master." "Oh, we can't do such a cruel thing, Buck. You know we are not going to stand here and watch him burn to death. 

Let's turn him loose and some one will shoot him later because he will always come back to his old home," begged 

Merle, wringing her hands in distress. "Who's going to untie him?" asked Buck with a grim smile. "Me," replied the woman without a second's hesitation. "Don't kid yourself," said Cameron. "I wouldn't let you go in there for a Million and besides there is no way to get to him." "You and Buck can tear down part of the fence."

After much pleading by the young woman and her serious declaration that she was going alone to the relief of the animal, the men began knocking off boards on each side of the small enclosure and out of reach of the dog's rope. Cameron stood on one side at an opening and Buck guarded the other, each with pistol in hand ready to shoot, while Merle ran to where the dog had tangled himself in his leash. It was unbearably hot and the hair had already been singed off of one

side of the animal when in his desperation to escape he had lunged too near the burning building. With nervous fingers the woman's small hands buried themselves in the heavy mane on the dog's neck and unfastened the wide leather collar which encircled it. Once Cameron started to shoot when the huge beast reared upon the girl in its efforts to assist her to release his bonds. With a screaming and undog like cry, when he found himself free, he bounded for the opening where Buck Davis stood guard and disappeared down a deep ravine, howling as he went. Merle had almost been upset by the dog's eager dash to liberty. Buck took a couple of blind shots in the direction of the fleeing animal but, of course, did not stand a show of striking him in the black darkness. The woman rejoined the men, her face red and stinging from the exposure to the hot fire. "It may have been an act of kindness to a dumb brute to turn him loose but I am afraid it's going to prove disastrous to some of the sheep along this ridge," remarked the mail carrier. "But even I am too chicken hearted to stand by and watch a lamb killing dog burn alive. It's too bad we couldn't save old Hogue Stevens. Poor devil."

The cold drizzle which accompanied the couple down the mountain had now turned into a regular deluge and for the first time the three people discovered that they were drenched to the skin. The wind had increased in velocity and added a weird note to the roaring flames which had now nearly consumed the cabin. The roof was caved in and the fence was burning itself out down to the openings made by the two men when they released the dog. To make doubly sure that the remaining shack would not catch fire they destroyed another panel of the fence and pulled the debris to one side and out of the path of the flames. Thus the conflagration was confined to Hogue's original living quarters. The man's body

was now merely a blackened length of charcoal in the smouldering cotton mattress and by tomorrow would be just so much ashes along with the rest of the structure. "It looks as tho Vengeance Valley has once more passed judgment," remarked Buck, as they turned to go, having done all in their power and there being no reason to remain out in the disagreeable weather. "If Hogue really did set fire to his wife like people claim he did, he is only paying the price," continued the mail carrier to his solemn faced friends. "Kind of looks like divine justice to me if it is a hard thing to say." "Oh, it's too horrible to think about," said Merle with tears in her eyes, "and I really believe he burned his house and himself on purpose. And Cameron do you know what that dog is? It's Pharaoh!" "Pharaoh? What do you mean, honey?" "It's Richard's old puppy that he kicked about so that day when you interfered and you and he came near having a fight over it. Hogue Stevens is that man who was on that farm wagon and the one Richard gave him to. Don't you remember? I know it's the same dog for the little silver ring is still in his ear. Richard put it there so we could always identify him in case he was stolen or lost. I saw it tonight when I untied him. Poor fellow, he has had a terrible life." "Gosh!" ejaculated Cameron in surprise, "I have been thinking all along that there was something strangely familiar about that man. You know, when he stood in his doorway and looked at us it was the same cold stare he had when he watched your husband beat that puppy. I felt that I had seen him, somewhere before when Buck pointed him out to us when he brought us here from the station." "Poor old Pharaoh. It's no wonder he had a disposition like something wild," murmurred the woman. "He has been kicked and cuffed about unmercifully every day of his life

with never a moment's pleasure. I am so glad he is free and yet he shouldn't run at large for there is no telling what he may do. I am afraid that he will hurt some one who doesn't understand him." "I hardly think so now," consoled Buck. "Some farmer will probably suffer the loss of a sheep or a few chickens but sooner or later somebody will shoot him. One of these little black bears may chew him up before he gets far. But now folks we have done everything we can. We had better be making tracks for home. It's way past midnight and I'll bet the madam is in hysterics. I would offer to take you back to the cottage but I don't believe I could make it up there after this rain. We could hardly stay on that steep grade with chains and no passengers. So I'll ask you to pardon my impoliteness this time." "Oh, we'd rather walk anyway after the experience we have had tonight. We couldn't sleep a wink thinking of the poor man burning to death. We can talk it over on the way back and it won't seem so far. Please come up to see us soon for we will be dreadfully lonesome for the next several days," said Merle. "I'll have to come tomorrow or next day because I have some mail for you. I would have brought it tonight if I had known that I would see you."

It required more than two hours hard walking and slippery climbing to get back to the cottage. The rain was coming down in sheets and the couple felt drenched to the bone and they were cold. But their recent experience diverted their minds from their discomfort and even after they had arrived home and changed their dripping clothes they sat up and talked of the disastrous fire. And before the two people had gone to bed the first streaks of dawn were beginning to show behind the heavy moving clouds in the east. The skies looked

a little less foreboding than they did a few hours ago when Cameron and his wife stood and watched the flames consume Hogue Stevens' home. A dull red glow was still visible at the mouth of the valley, showing that the conflagration was now well subdued and the rain would soon finish it.

CHAPTER XX 

All was peaceful in Vengeance Valley for a few days after the fire which destroyed the Stevens' cabin and which cost the owner his life. Cameron was working hard every hour. His books continued to find a market and his health was very nearly perfect. If things continued to break as favorably for him he would return with his wife to the city much earlier than they had first planned. However, they were anything but dissatisfied with the serene life in the mountains. Both expressed themselves as having been the happiest since their childhood.

The smaller cabin belonging to Hogue Stevens had been spared by the flames which destroyed the larger house and which was now nothing more than an unsightly black splotch of ashes among the rhododendrum. With the help of Buck 

Davis, Cameron finished a gruesome job and buried the charred bones of the victim of the fire. Under a great oak nearby they interred all they could find of the remains of the queer mountaineer. Cameron carved the name of the man and the date of his death on a wooden board pulled from the fence and this he erected as a marker for the lonely grave. 

When their frequent hikes thru the rugged hills carried Cameron and his wife down the valley Merle always placed some brightly colored leaves entwined in a wreath on the grave of the man she had tried to help.

Nothing was heard of the whereabouts of the dog released by Merle for some little time after the burning of Hogue 

Stevens' home. Late one Saturday afternoon, two or three weeks after the fire, Buck Davis drove into the little yard and

delivered a bundle of magazines, newspapers and a few letters to the couple standing on the porch. They had heard his grumbling motor far down the mountains while he was still many miles away and they had come out to welcome their friend. "Oh Buck, where in the dickens have you been for the past three or four days?" asked Cameron. "Not that the mail makes any difference but we have been expecting you every day and the weather hasn't been bad enough to keep you away. We haven't heard a word of news." "That so? You couldn't guess in a month what I have been doing the last three days. Spent most of it hunting with several others for that dog we nitwits turned loose. Cap Harris lost a lamb last week and Bill Smith reports finding parts of ten or twelve chickens back of his barn. Tom Simpson actually had a shot at the beast yesterday while he was in the very act of killing a sheep. We have searched high and low for that animal thru this whole section and can't get within range of him.” "You won't like my saying so," laughed Merle, "but I hope you don't get him, Buck. You know he is an old friend of ours. He belonged to me when he was a puppy. My first husband gave him to me for a present on my birthday. Hogue 

Stevens passed thru the city on an apple wagon and asked for him, we let him take him away." "With due apologies to his wife," replied Buck with a wink, "and from what I have heard of your first love, the animal absorbed some of his old master's disposition." "I wouldn't doubt it in the least," commented Cameron dryly, "you can hardly blame the dog for he had a competent teacher." "Wish I could stay longer but I haven't time to discuss the old fellow now," said Buck. "I haven't been home since night

before last and the madam is alone with the kids. I just ran up to bring your mail, thinking perhaps you might be about ready to complain to the postal authorities. Keep a sharp lookout for the dog. You will make Fifty Bucks if you get him, so the sheriff says." "We don't need the money," replied the woman smiling, "not that badly anyway." "Hurry back old timer," called Cameron as the mail carrier allowed the car to roll forward of its own momentum down the steep incline from the yard, "it's getting near the end of the season and we haven't had that hunt yet." "We'll get to it before the laws out. I'll be seeing you, so long."

Thereafter a minature reign of terror prevailed along the Blue Ridge from Bonnerville to the top of the peaks west of Vengeance Valley. The finding of a partly eaten carcass of a sheep or calf was not infrequent. Wild stories were prevalent in the hills of a cunning and vicious dog of heroic size. 

Many of the farmers had seen the animal at a distance, usually at sundown or just before day. Rarely during the daylight hours did anyone get a glimpse of the beast. In one or two instances it was claimed that the dog had attacked children and even women when unaccompanied by armed men. 

Pharaoh seemed to know when a person carried a weapon and kept plenty of distance between himself and danger. Many hunting expeditions in quest of the life of the animal terminated in utter failure. The other dogs in the vicinity refused to trail this strange cousin of theirs. More than one unfortunate canine had crossed muzzles with the big animal and met a quick death from the peculiar plunging attack of the outlaw.

Buck Davis had probably seen more of the wary dog than any of the others but the alert animal seemed to sense that

the man was armed and prepared for him and therefore he gave him plenty of room. He was even impudent enough to gallop off a great distance and then stand silhouetted against the sky on some knoll and watch the mail carrier out of sight. 

Taking advantage of this rather strange trait in the dog, Buck had caused one or two hunters to follow him on short trips in the neighborhood where the animal had been seen, hoping that they might trap him. But Pharaoh was ever on guard and the creeping of the men toward him was always discovered in ample time for him to perform his deerlike stanch and then dash wildly to safety. After this nothing more would be heard from him for several days. 

Apparently Pharaoh killed only for food or in anger when he was molested by other animals of his own specie. However, his uncanny cleverness in avoiding capture caused much elaboration in the tales told of his prowness.

"That'll be all right, honey. Just leave it alone for the present. I've enough in the bottle to serve all the purposes I want until our little deal is over. I may need your liquor later on in the day worse than I do now. May need it badly for all we know. Oh, you needn't be looking at the clock. 

Those birds won't be coming home for an hour or two yet.

Come over here and cuddle your old long lost husband and love him up a bit. It's been a long time since I have held your fiery little body in my arms. Remember that first time I kissed you? After the speed cop caught us? While we sat out there on the highway and smoked? Recall how flustered and happy you were then? Come along now and don't mind these whiskers, they'll only add to the pleasure. Let's see if you can still give me one of those devil may care soul kisses again. We have plenty of time for a half hour's loving, and maybe something else more exciting before that little hubby returns, damn him. You used to be a red hot mamma when we were first married. Come on and let's see if you still have the same old courting days pep." 

In terror Merle shrank away from the powerful hand that reached out and snatched her by the arm. In a wild effort she tried to dash from the room but the man was watching her and anticipated her move, blocking the door against her exit.

Mentally she repented that she had ever allowed Cameron to leave her today, and especially before investigating Hogue

Stevens' cabin. Her womanly intuition had told her that everything was not all right there. Men were so funny that way and made such little to do over things of that kind, things that cause a woman more worry and mental anguish than greater difficulties. This semi-madman was driving her crazy. 

He wasn't insane and oh how she wished he was, for then there might be some means of outwitting him. Yes, chances with a lunatic would be far easier than with Richard. He still pos-

sessed all his old time cunning, backed up by a will of iron and a heart that never claimed sympathy as an attribute. 

By brute force Richard took his former wife into his arms and staggered to Cameron's big arm chair and sat down with her in his lap. He hugged and caressed her with the stiffling embraces of an infuriated bear. Again the woman felt the old hypnotic inifluence of the man's superior physical force, the same alluring influence which dazed her the first night he took her to ride and the officer halted them for speeding. Fighting like a wounded tiger she tore his hair and scratched his face, fighting against that sinister unseen power of his personality as much as against the man himself. 

In one heroic effort she flung herself free from his clutches and dashed blindly from the house, her persecutor in close pursuit demanding her to stop. Scarcely thirty feet from the door his stronger limbs overtook the trembling feet of the frightened woman. Passionately he enfolded her again in his arms and lifting her face to his with one hand he kissed her lips roughly, holding her slim body tightly against his own. 

Again the woman wrenched herself from Richard's hands and struck him wildly in the face, causing the man to momentarily release her. In anger he drove his clinched fist to her cheek, knocking her to the ground. The blow might have resulted more seriously had not Merle been too close to the fist that struck her. As it was the blow was more of a hard push. 

Richard snatched the girl to her feet again and in a semiconscious daze she tried to strike again. 

Thru blurred eyes Merle saw the man drop his hands and stagger backwards, releasing his hold on her and directing his gaze over her shoulder. His right hand went to the coat pocket and drew out the pearl handled pistol. Before he could level it at the object of his stare a huge shaggy form flashed by, brushing Merle's body with its wiry hair. Two

great paws went over Richard's shoulders, one on either side of his neck. The burly chest of the animal met the man's with such force that he dropped his revolver in his desperate efforts to avoid the plunge of his attacker. But the great momentum gained by the leaping beast, its body weighing nearly as much as the man's, carried Richard several feet backward and his head and back struck the earth with a sickening thud. A pair of tremendous jaws had already clamped on the unshaven throat in a death grip and dagger like teeth ripped open the jugular vein. Horrified by the scene being enacted, Merle stood for a moment as tho petrified and then made a futile endeavor to reach the pistol which had fallen some feet away when the man lost his grip on it. Her weak knees, however, would not support her weight and she sank into the snow unable to remove her eyes from the tragedy taking place before her. The great dog lifted half the body of the struggling man clear of the ground and shook violently. 

A second time he repeated the shaking and after a single gurgle in the throat of the prostrate man Richard lay still. 

A rifle cracked and shattered the crisp winter afternoon calm and startled the already terrified woman. She saw the hindquarters of the dog slump down and the long gaunt body quivered. The iron jaws relaxed on the bleeding throat and the animal's eyes never opened again. The fangs of Pharaoh had buried themselves so deeply that they still clung to the torn flesh when the beast rolled loosely to one side. 

Cameron and Buck dashed upon the scene in wildeyed amazement. With a cry of joy Merle swooned in her husband's arms. Buck pulled the body of the gigantic dog off of the dead man and dragged it a few feet away. He knelt down and felt the man's pulse and listened for his heart beat. 

He heard none, for once Pharoah secured his peculiar grip

nothing but death could result. He lived and died true to his name, a killer. "What does it all mean?" cried Cameron, shaking the girl gently to bring her to her senses, still staring in bewilderment at the two bodies lying there in the snow. "Oh Cameron, I thought you would never come," answered 

Merle, choking back a wild impulse to scream. "It's Richard and he meant to kill us. Pharaoh came to my rescue."

Buck Davis removed his hat and stood over the prostrate form of Merle's first husband and they heard him murmur two words between his tightly closed teeth, "Vengeance Valley."
