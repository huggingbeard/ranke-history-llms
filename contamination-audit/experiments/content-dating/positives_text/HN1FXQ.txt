PREFACE 

S the North American Indian goes his quiet way, we are apt to forget that the title once bestowed upon him by early voyageurs, who knew him in his halcyon days, before the white man taught him most of his vices and few of his virtues, was "Nature's Gentleman." 

There was much in the natural Indian to commend itself to those who took the trouble to really know, and understand him. 

As "Barry Dane" so neatly puts it: "Thar's good and bad in Injun, An' thar's good an' bad in White;

But, somehow, they is allus wrong, An' we is allus right. "But jes' believe me, onst for all, To them that treats him fair,

The Injun mostly allus waz, And is, and will be, square."

This story is presented merely to give a picture of some of the customs and tragedies of the early times from 1649–75, during the time of the French régime. The tribe chosen is the Petun Nation, or 

Tobacco Growers, who lived in the Blue Hills, south of the Georgian Bay, in Ontario, Canada. 5

PREFACE 

They were scattered during this time, and later were known as the Wyandots. In 1751, a band went to Sandusky from Detroit, where they increased in numbers and, according to Charlevoix, became the leading tribe of the Ohio region, and the privileged fire-lighters of the confederated tribes. In 1842, all the Petuns in the United 

States were settled on a reservation in the state of Kansas, and in 1892 were removed in a body to the Indian Reservation at Oklahoma, where they now live in growing numbers. In all their wanderings they retained their tribal identity and their hereditary chieftaincy.

The missionaries and the places mentioned are based on historical fact, and it is the hope that those who read this book will acquire at least a sympathetic interest in this once splendid people, the history of which has been largely buried in the folds of oblivion.

The years sped quickly in the Blue Hills, where the lodge fires of the Petun nation sent up blue smoke, like wreathes of incense in the brooding forest. Their hunting grounds were in the hill district south of the Georgian Bay, in Ontario. They got their name from the fine tobacco they grew, and were the western branch of the great original Huron nation, and of all the branches of this once powerful people, the Petuns were the bravest and noblest. As a boy, Red Eagle heard tales of the lazy Algonquins, the erratic Hurons, and wretched 

Montagnais, and his heart thrilled that one day he should be the leader of a people far greater than any of these.

In his play, he learned all the tricks of the for-

est. He could shoot the straightest arrow, and no boy could throw him in wrestling, nor outrun him in a race. Old and gnarled warriors watched his lithe body darting along the trails, and smiled with satisfaction. He learned much from his father, and much also from Grey Fawn, his watchful, shrewd, little mother.

So he lived happy and secure in the protection of his home till the testing time came, which every Indian boy in the old times had to face; the time when he must go into the forest alone and fast and wait till the Great Spirit would give him a dream or sign intended to influence the rest of his life as a warrior. He would leave home a mere boy; he would return a young man; he had settled on his career, and would become a mighty hunter, a great warrior, or whatever determined his future life according to the interpretation of the sign or dream.

The Jay or Moose-bird scolded from the tall maples, while the squirrel defied him shrilly from the elms. Ducks winged their busy way across the sky, and beneath him the tiny wren trilled gently from the grassy glades.

Two children were born to her, Tsesta the Good One, who brought love, kindliness and respect into the world, and Taweskare, the Evil One, who brought meanness, wickedness and evil into life. 

Always they quarrelled with each other as little boys, and it grieved the Good One that his work should be constantly upset.

He it was who taught the animals all sorts of tricks, and showed them to be kind to their young. 

He it was who taught his Red people to make the birch canoe, to fashion a tepee, to catch big fish and birds, and smoke meat before the coming of the snow blanket. But Taweskare, the Evil One, was constantly upsetting his work, making trouble between the animals and causing hunger and strife. 

Red Eagle thought about this all again. "I shall be like Tsesta, the Good One," he promised himself inwardly. "I shall make my people strong and fearless and bind them with thongs of peace."

HAT evening Red Eagle did not go to sleep early, but sat before his tepee thinking about the Senecas. It was many years since his people had been to war, and he hoped they would help the Hurons, that he might have a chance to go with them. The moon came out full and golden, casting a flood of pale light over the quiet wooded scene. The dim shadows of the trees, vibrating with the echo of night voices all about him, brought a sense of loneliness. In the valley were slim white poplars, and he loved their lithe beauty, for they stood like warriors. 

Indeed it was said that white poplars were once young braves who were so elated when they took the war trail, they talked so much the enemy heard them and killed them all, so for an example the Great Spirit turned them into trees where they whisper and talk together to this day.

He would not do that when he took the war trail. Again his mind went back to the Iroquois. 

It was said that a Senecas maiden, who was very beautiful and much sought after, caused the war between her tribe and the Hurons, by having a 19

Chief of her people slain by a young Huron. 

This Chief had withheld his consent to her marriage with his son, and before she would promise to marry the Huron she insisted that he bring her the Chief's scalp. He tried in vain to dissuade her from insisting on the condition, but she was adamant. Since then the Senecas and their allies, the Mohawks, periodically wreaked their vengeance on the whole unfortunate Huron race.

Across the hills came the weird cry of the Lone One from the marshes where the loons gathered. 

Up the hills it was answered by the derisive " Hooooh ooh " of the Great Owl, which made the wood rabbits tremble in the moonlit glades where they played. The scent of tamarack and spruce, the perfume of pines and fresh musk of grasses damp with dew, soothed the lad till very soon Red Eagle went to his tent heavy with sleep and weary with waiting.

He awakened with the first rays of the sun in the morning, and remembered that a blurred dream had come to him in the night, but try as he would, he could not recall it. Well, he reasoned, perhaps there would be another-there was time yet. He had been away only two days.

Washing himself at a little stream, he ran through the woods for exercise. When he returned clean and dry he strutted about in his new buckskin shirt and leggins which his mother embroidered

for the occasion. Thoughts of food now haunted him constantly, but he turned away from the berries on the elder bushes and contented himself with drinking water. Red Eagle had a will of his own. He was there to fast, so fast he would.

As he went back to the tepee he was startled by a very unusual sight. From the decayed remains of a bit of basswood almost covered in last year's dead leaves there had sprung up a cluster of Death Flowers (The Indian Pipe). Three pale stems with bell-like flowers of waxen white stood together, while near them a tiny one had just pushed its way through the ground. A cold feeling struggled at his heart. His father's sign was three wolves' heads. There could be no connection-and yet-three flowers of death-he could not get the thought from his mind. The Death Flower seldom grew in his country; the hills were too clear for it, and in the valleys instead of deadwood were the fertile fields of tobacco, which his people grew for all the surrounding tribes. 

That afternoon he heard a rustle in the bushes far up the trail, as though someone were walking carelessly. Red Eagle had sharp hearing, and hoping it was his mother or father coming to talk with him, he went to meet them. That night dark clouds rolled up from the west.

The last traces of the sunset were blotted out by their flying columns, and the wind sprang up, and ran crying through the forest, as if it had been rudely wakened from a quiet sleep and was angry. 

Red Eagle watched the storm approaching. The wind grew from a startled cry to a loud whistle. 

Then the clouds opened and torrents of rain fell.

Growing strong, the wind howled through the forest, lashing trees before it, and bending them almost double.

Taweskare, the Evil One, was angry, and his voice could be heard thundering through the sky and reverberating over the hills. Red Eagle watched the battle between wind and trees. He was not alarmed, for his tepee was beneath an hawthorne tree, and Indian boys know if they walk through the woods after such a storm they might find the oak or the ash tree splintered, but never the thorn. 

Fascinated by its fierceness, he continued to watch the storm. There had been many such before, but he had not faced them alone. In the absence of the comforting proximity of the bark lodge and the presence of his people, he was brave. The elements were struggling; perhaps he would have such struggles for his people. He looked up to the mysterious sky in awe.

There were the same trees about him, the same hills, and the same lonely voices of the wood dwellers giving their calls on every side. Eagle heard footsteps. He peered out, and saw the Black Robe passing. Red Eagle liked the kindly countenance of Pere Garnier, the missionary at his village of Etharita. He wondered what had brought this Pale Face into the forest, and believed for some unaccountable reason that the Black Robe would not have lied about spruce roots. For a few minutes the priest stood, wrapt in admiration for the whole scene, then he turned and retraced his steps back to the village. He

Who could tell the heart-aches and longings of these missionaries as they strode in the van of the voyageurs, following the star of service-with unwavering devotion to the cause they held high. Watched, studied and held in respect or ridicule, as it suited the mood of the Indian, only the hills and forests were kindly, only the solitudes yielded peace. 

In his tepee, Red Eagle thought much about it. 

That night he dreamed a wild but vivid dream of many cats crashing through the forest and leaping on dozens of Indian Pipes growing around his tepee. Over it all flashed the spirit fires, and he found himself being lifted and borne toward them, and held aloft by a huge eagle-and directly before them flashed a cross of fire, such as he had seen during the storm. The signs were woven together and he was given his dream.

ED EAGLE hurried home soon after daybreak, hunger and eagerness being keen spurs. As he neared the palisades, dogs ran to meet him, but he did not wait to greet them, for he saw smoke issuing from the roof of the council house, and heard the voice of a crier summoning the people to come and meet. 

Passing through the palisades, he made his way between the rows of bark lodges. Around the council house were squaws jabbering excitedly, gaily-dressed maidens, toothless old men, smokeblinded old women, and young warriors strutting in their best warpaint, and dressed in buckskin shirts and breeches. "What is it?" Red Eagle asked a lad his own age. "A wampum bearer had come from the Hurons," he replied.

Late that night, when the fires were burning low and Red Eagle was invested with the office which he held for more than thirty years, keeping his people safe from the raids of the Senecas, he walked through the wigwams of his people with White Fawn to the home of the great Marquette, where the good father was waiting to give him his blessing. 

It was the Blossom moon, when the Great Spirit gives all the world of nature new life. "You will not go to search for this magic water of life again?" asked White Fawn softly. "I have found it," said her husband proudly. "It is what a man has when he gets power and courage and his woman loves him."

The eyes of White Fawn glistened proudly. 

Catching his arm, she pointed northward and said, See! 

Above them the blossom moon had drenched the world with silver, and the spirit fires were kindling in the north.
