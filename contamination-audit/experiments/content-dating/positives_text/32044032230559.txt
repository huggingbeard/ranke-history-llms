Paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401, of Developments with Regard to Dried Figs Since Modification of the Trade-Agreement Concession on August 30, 1952 

1/ This is a report made pursuant to paragraph 1 of Executive Order 

10401 of October 14, 1952 (17 F. R. 9125), of developments with regard to dried figs. The tariff concession granted on dried figs in the 

General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was modified and the import duty increased from 2 cents per pound to 4 cents per pound, effective 

August 30, 1952 (Proc. No. 2986; 17 F. R. 7567), as a result of the

Commission's escape-clause investigation under section 7 of the Trade 2/ Agreements Extension Act of 1951. This is the fourth report with respect to this matter under Executive Order 10401. The first report was made pursuant to paragraph 2 of that order and was transmitted to the President on June 3, 1953; the second and third reports, pursuant to paragraph 1, were transmitted to the President on August 24, 1954, and 

August 9, 1955, respectively.

The 1953 report contained the results of a formal investigation instituted, pursuant to the Fresident's request dated March 5, 1953, for

the purpose of determining whether, and, if so, to what extent, the modification of the tariff concession on dried figs remained necessary in order to prevent or remedy serious injury or the threat thereof to the domestic industry concerned. As a result of that investigation, the Commission found that the modification, by Presidential proclamation dated August 16, 1952, of the concession granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, as supplemented by the Torquay Frotocol to such agreement, remained necessary in order to prevent serious injury to the domestic industry concerned. 

The 1954 and 1955 reports to the President under Executive Order 10401 dealt with developments in the trade in dried figs during the 1/ marketing years 1953/54 and 1954/55, respectively. In the letter of transmittal accompanying each of those reports, the Commission expressed the opinion that the conditions of competition between the imported and domestic dried figs had not so changed during the respective marketing years as to warrant the institution of a formal investigation under the provisions of paragraph 2 of the above-mentioned order. 

The Commission's 1952 report of its escape-clause investigation on dried figs contains information concerning the structure and condition of the dried-fig industry up to July 1952; the subsequent reports, under Executive Order 10401, contain information with regard to developments up to July 1955. 

1/ Dried figs begin to be harvested in California about July 1.

In this report the marketing year for the crop covers the period from 

July 1 of each year to June 30 of the following year.

There are several commercial types of dried figs, and each type embraces many varieties. Only two types, Adriatic and Smyrna, are of commercial importance in the United States. The domestic production consists of three varieties of the Adriatic type (Adriatic, Kadota, and 

Black Mission) and of one variety of the Smyrna type (Calimyrna). The dried figs imported from Turkey and Portugal are Smyrna type, and those from Greece and Italy are Adriatic type. In United States markets domestic Calimyrnas and the Smyrna figs imported from Turkey generally command higher prices than any other figs. 

Most domestic dried figs are converted into fig paste, and some imported dried figs also are converted into fig paste in the United States. In recent years imports from Turkey and Portugal have included 1/ fig paste as well as dried figs. The conversion of dried figs into fig paste consists of the simple operation of putting the dried figs through a food grinder, one pound of dried figs making one pound of fig paste. Imported figs are like or directly competitive with domestic dried figs, and imported fig paste is like or directly competitive with domestic fig paste.

The most important use for domestic dried figs in the United States is in the manufacture of fig bars; virtually all of the imports of fig paste and a small part of the imports of dried figs are also used in the production of fig bars. Tho preferences of individual fig-bar bakers for particular varieties of figs are based on many factors, particularly the comparative prices of the different varieties of figs, and the type of outlets in which the bars are to be marketed. The higher priced fig bars, sold mostly under nationally advertised brand names, usually contain fig paste of domestic Smyrna-type figs (Calimyrnas), Turkish Smyrnas, and the highest quality of the domestic Adriatic variety. The lower priced fig bars, such as are generally sold in the dime stores, are made of varying blends of three domestic varieties--Adriatics, Kadotas, and Black Missions--and the Smyrna figs imported from Portugal.

Medium-priced bars are made of blends of the same varieties as those used in making the cheaper bars but with a higher proportion of the lighter colored fig paste. The medium-priced bars contain no Black 

Mission paste because its coloring is too dark, and they contain little or no Calimyrna or Turkish Smyrna paste because such pastes are generally too costly for use in medium-priced bars. 

As indicated in the Commission's earlier reports on dried figs, commercial drying of figs is confined to California where 85 to 90 percent of the fig crop is marketed by growers as dried figs; the remainder (consisting largely of Kadotas and Black Missions) is distributed to the fresh and canned fig markets. Until March 1955 the marketing of California dried figs was controlled by a State marketing program that prescribed regulations for the handling of dried figs by growers and packers. In March 1955 the United States Secretary of 

Agriculture, in response to a request by the California dried-fig industry, issued a Federal marketing order for California dried figs

pursuant to the provisions of the Federal Agricultural Marketing Agree- 1/ ment Act of 1937, as amended. The Federal marketing program is operated in conjunction with the California State program; the control committee for the Federal program and the advisory board for the State program have the same membership. The Federal program prescribes quality standards for the regulation of packers' acquisitions and shipments of dried figs and provides for volume control, while the State program is concerned with sales promotion, research, and the control and disposal of the substandard fruit.

The fig-bearing acreage in California has declined almost steadily since the early 1930's (table 4). Among the factors contributing to the decline have been more profitable returns from alternate crops, increasing costs of production for dried figs relative to average prices received, and urbanization of portions of the areas in which figs have been principally grown. Notwithstanding the decline in acreage devoted to fig trees, the trend in output of figs was almost consistently upward until 1948 because of the increase in average yield per acre that resulted primarily from the removal of the least productive acreage and improved control of pests and diseases. In each year from 1948 through 

1955 the domestic output of dried figs was less than the average annual output of the preceding 12 years. The overall quality of the 1955 crop, however, was exceedingly good, so that the merchantable output amounted to about 45 million pounds, the largest since 1951, and the nonmerchantable cullage amounted to less than 6 million pounds, the smallest for any crop in the period 1931-55 (table 5). In the United States about two-thirds of the total consumption of domestic dried figs is accounted for by biscuit manufacturers for the production of fig bars (table 8); the remainder is sold in retail markets as dried figs. Reports to the Commission from biscuit manufacturers who produce 70 to 80 percent of the total United States output of fig bars (from imported as well as domestic dried figs) indicate that the consumption of this important fig product has increased in recent years.

In many years before 1952 about two-thirds of total United States imports of dried figs also went to fig-bar manufacturers, so that the keenest competition between imported and domestic figs was in the fig-bar trade. In later years much smaller portions of the imports of whole dried figs have been converted into paste in this country for the domestic production of fig bars. Most of the imports of whole dried figs in the 1955 marketing year have been distributed to final consumers in the form in which they entered; about the only imported whole figs used for fig bars were from Portugal. Imports from that source amounted to 0.8 million pounds (about 20 percent of the total imports of dried figs), virtually all of which were used by figbar producers.

It appears unlikely that the growers' experience with the 1956 crop of dried figs will be as favorable as their experience with the 1955 crop, because the movement of the 1956 crop from field to packing plant will be influenced by the deterioration in the situation of the packers during the marketing year just ended, as described in the following paragraphs. On July 1 of every marketing year since 1950, the packers' physical inventory (including amounts under contract and held for future delivery) has been larger than the amount considered sufficient to supply the market for dried figs until September, when large quantities of dried figs from the new crop become available. In 1956 the inventory on July 1 of dried figs in the hands of packers was somewhat larger than on July 1 in each of the four preceding years. The carryover of oldcrop domestic figs into the new marketing year is now (in 1956) about 50 percent larger than the corresponding carryover in 1955 and nearly 20 percent larger than that in 1952, when the modification of the concession on dried figs was proclaimed by the President as a result of the Commission's escape-clause investigation. 

On the basis of information received from packers who handled 90 percent or more of the domestic output of merchantable dried figs in 1953-55, it appears that the increases in average prices paid by them for each variety of dried figs from the 1955 crop (table 15) were accompanied by considerably smaller increases in average prices received

by thom (table 16). For Calimyrnas, which comprised nearly 40 percent of their purchases and sales, prices paid were 20 percent higher in 1955 than in 1954, but prices received increased by only 11 percent and for Adriatics, which comprised about 30 percent of their purchases and sales, prices paid increased by 3 percent, but prices received increased by less than one-half of 1 percent. 

Prices received by packers for their 1955/56 shipments to fig-bar manufacturers (table 16) reflect sales made in the early part of the marketing year. Since early spring, packers consummated very few new contracts with fig-bar manufacturers. Although there was some interest in Calimyrnas, packers considered their unsold supply of that variety of too high quality to grind into paste and move at the prevailing price 1/ for Calimyrna paste. With respect to the other varieties of domestic dried figs, the packers' sales were sporadic during the latter part of 1955/56. It may be noted, however, that some of the contracts made by fig-bar producers in the fall and winter of 1955 provided for shipments up to September 1, 1956, and even beyond that date.

On the basis of all the facts available to the Commission, it appears that the situation of the domestic dried-fig industry (including both packers and growers) deteriorated during the 1955 marketing year. The threat of lower farm prices for the oncoming crop induced the domestic fig industry to apply in April 1956 to the Secretary of Agriculture for an investigation under section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act, as amended, to determine whether dried figs and fig paste are being imported into the United States under such conditions. and in such quantities as to materially interfere with the Department of Agriculture's market-order program for dried figs. The industry has directed the attention of the Secretary to the changed character of the import trade in dried figs and fig paste and particularly to the substantial increase in imports of fig paste subsequent to the President's escape-clause proclamation increasing the rate of duty on dried figs. 

The industry alleges that the increased imports of fig paste nullify the effectiveness of the escape-clause proclamation with respect to dried figs and that they are responsible for the present large inventories of domestic figs in the hands of the packers. 

In anticipation of a depressed market in 1956/57 for domestic dried figs and fig paste resulting from the abnormally large carryover from the 1955 crop, the Dried Fig Administrative Committee, on June 27. 

1955, recommended to the Secretary of Agriculture that 20 percent of the 1956 merchantable output of each of the four varieties of dried
