INTRODUCTION 

HOMAS W. LAMONT II was born in New York City in 1924 and was the eldest child of Thomas Stilwell Lamont and Elinor Miner Lamont, as well as the first grandchild of Mr. and Mrs. Thomas W. Lamont and Mr. and Mrs. Edward G. Miner. From 1930 to 1937 

Tommy attended the Buckley School in New York. During the winter of 1937-38 he went to a school in Switzerland, Ecole Nouvelle de la Chataigneraie, near Geneva. 

Then for four most fruitful years, from 1938 to 1942, he was a student at Exeter, where he was prominent in extra-curricular activities of an intellectual nature. 

T. W. L. II became one of the school's best debaters, as a member of the G. L. Soule Debating Society, the 

P.E.A. Senate and the Academy Debating Team. At his graduation exercises in June, 1942, he delivered the Class 

Oration, making an address of singular depth and maturity. On the literary side Tommy was a member of the 

Lantern Club, Feature Editor of the Exonian and President of the Phillips Exeter Review. He was also on the 

All-Club Soccer Team for two years.

In June of 1942 Tommy entered Harvard College and remained there for the full Freshman year. It was of course a Harvard on a war basis, with normal academic and extra-curricular life drastically curtailed. None the less, Tommy very much enjoyed his months at Cam- 

VII

bridge and felt that he got a great deal out of his brief experience of college. One thing that must have particularly pleased him was his election to the Delphic Club, to which his father belonged.

In April, 1943, Tommy transferred to Colgate University, at Hamilton, N. Y., to start his training as a naval aviator. His career from then on is recounted in the 

Naval Record of Thomas W. Lamont II, compiled by his father for this book.

T. W. L. II was the first of that large family group centering around the Thomas W. Lamonts and the Edward 

G. Miners to lose his life while serving in the armed forces of the United States. When the Second World War began in the fall of 1939, Tommy was only fifteen years old. Yet he saw through to the fundamental issues involved far more clearly than most of his elders. 

As one of his friends at Exeter wrote: "He understood this war, from the Spanish Civil War on, better than anyone else my age that I have ever known. He never for one moment mistook the Wave of the Past for the Wave of the Future." Tommy knew that he was taking part in a World War revolving around world issues. Essentially it was a battle between democracy and Fascism with the whole earth as its arena and the future of humanity as its stake. And Tommy constantly insisted that victory would be in vain unless the war resulted in a better world in which, among other things, the United States fully cooperated with other nations to establish enduring peace. So he consciously fought and died not simply to defend America but also to further its highest ideals VIII

and to advance the liberty and well-being of all mankind. 

Tommy's awareness of the deeper implications of the greatest war in history did not come as a sudden awakening. For many years, and even before he went to Exeter, he had been keenly alive to the big problems, national and international, of the day. In 1937, when Tommy was thirteen, he saw in London, during the family trip to Scotland and England, a news reel of Italian troops marching through Spain, and Japanese guns firing into Shanghai. This picture affected him strongly and he put in his diary this revealing observation: "What a world,' meekly murmur some. 'What a world! And something has to be done about it!' say I." Here, too, in his 1939 diary is a typical entry: "Had lunch with Grandma

Lamont. We had good political conversation." 

In his letters, English themes and debates Tommy was forever discussing the fundamental social and political issues of our time. And in the broad, tolerant democracy of the Lamont family circle, at the dinner table at Palisades and North Haven, he took up the cudgels on many a burning question with aunts, uncles, parents, grandparents, cousins or anyone else who might be present.

He was not always consistent and he was sometimes impatient of views he thought wrong. But he was steadily working toward a sincere and intelligent appraisal of the confusing social currents in the world about him. 

Tommy made a lasting impression upon those with whom he talked seriously, on both teachers and fellowstudents at school and at college. Thus Mr. George D. 

Williams, an instructor at the Buckley school, writes: IX

"My memories of Tommy are very vivid. In current events classes, especially, he was a delight. He knew how to participate in discussions. and whenever he spoke he really said something. Really, I used to marvel at that 12-13-year-old boy." And Delmar Leighton, Dean of Freshmen at Harvard College, says: "No youngster who has gone off from Harvard to fight this war has given higher promise or had a more eager spirit to find out what was wrong with the world and to do something about it." John Crocker, Jr., a close friend of Tommy's at Harvard and North Haven, remarks: "Talking to him was real fun because he was always so quick.... I can honestly say that conversations with Tom prompted more thinking in me than those with any other guy and it was really stimulating and helpful."

It was primarily because Tommy understood so well the far-reaching import of the Second World War that he was so eager to get into the fight and strike a direct blow against the Axis enemy. Naturally there were other factors in his desire to be in the thick of things. Youthful love of excitement, the example of school and college friends, impatience with the routine of formal education in such a stirring time, all played their part. But I think that Tommy's major decisions regarding his role in the armed forces were based on careful and serious reflection. He wanted to serve in a great cause to the best of his ability and to make that ability count as quickly as possible.

In enlisting first in Naval Air and later volunteering for the Submarine Service, Tommy well realized the dan- Xm

gers he was facing. He knew that he would be encountering fearful hazards again and again. And the final sacrifice of his life at the early age of twenty, with all the bright, long years of his manhood before him, was a fully conscious and meaningful act. With unassuming heroism Tommy went down to his death, in the great tradition of American sailors and fighters of the sea. 

When a person so young and vigorous and beloved as Tommy suddenly dies, it is almost impossible to accept in one's heart the fact of his going. The news first came through on May 14, 1945, in a telegram to Tommy's father and mother from Washington, D. C. "The Navy

Department deeply regrets," it began, "to inform you that your son Thomas William Lamont II Seaman First

Class U.S.N.R. is missing following action while in the service of his country." At the time, I simply refused `to admit to myself that Tommy was dead. Indeed, for months I held out private hopes that somehow, somewhere, he would turn up alive and well; that he would call long distance from a remote Pacific island and make a merry wisecrack over the phone about the situation. 

Most of us, I am sure, cherished some such secret dream. 

No one will ever know, probably, exactly what happened to Tommy's submarine, the U.S.S. Snook. His father gives all the available information about the Snook and the last months of its career at sea. There can be no doubt about the important and gallant part this submarine played in the Pacific war, a fact recognized by the Navy Department in awarding the Submarine Combat Insignia to all members of the Snook's crew. Nor XI

can there be any question as to the invaluable role of U. S. submarines in general in the defeat of Japan. A total of fifty-two of them were lost, almost all in the Pacific theatre, of which the Snook was one of the last to go down. Secretary of the Navy Forrestal gave the American underwater fleet primary credit for the destruction of Japan's merchant marine. And it shared the honors in the annihilation of the Japanese navy. 

In this book on T. W. L. II it has been our intention to let Tommy speak for himself. This is Tommy's own book for his friends and family. Here are printed a fair sampling of his letters, his various stories and essays, and his poems. In editing the letters, I have left out the passages that were too personal or confidential for reproduction in this record. If the letters seem rather serious in tone for a boy of Tommy's age, that is chiefly because in most of his correspondence he did discuss significant topics rather than the trivia of school and college life. 

As his mother has said, "Tommy hardly ever wrote about people; his letters were always about big, vital subjects." 

I myself carried on a running correspondence with 

Tommy for years. And we covered most of the prime issues of the day, with special attention to Soviet Russia and Spain. I was constantly impressed by Tommy's intellectual awareness, his maturity of thought and his ability in argument. But even I was surprised, in reading through the vast amount of material from which I made the selections for this book, at Tommy's far-ranging mind and his genuine literary promise. In some of his later letters Tommy expressed the hope of becoming a XII

reporter. He would have made a first-rate one, in my opinion.

The bulk of Tommy's poems are published here for the first time. He sent about ten of them from the Snook in 

December, 1944, having worked over them during the submarine's exciting seventh patrol in the fall of that year. Habitually deprecatory of his own work, Tommy remarked: "I know this stuff is bad, myself. The rhyme is often obvious; most of the sentiment is hackneyed. 

Also you will probably trace my wailings to the infinitely more profound cynicism of A. E. Housman, who, incidentally, is my Bible nowdays; yea, my rock of salvation.”

I thought the poems quite good for a beginner who made no pretense of trying to be a professional poet. 

I am convinced that Tommy would have gone on to outstanding achievements in the field of writing. And it is fitting that an Exeter classmate, Bill Sharon, has established in his memory an annual prize for literary excellence at the Phillips Exeter Academy. It was during his four years at Exeter that Tommy, to a major extent under the stimulus of his work on the Exonian and the Review, especially blossomed forth as a writer. “No one in our class," states Sharon, "wrote more forcefully or with more vision than did Tommy."

Tommy's venture into the sphere of poetry was characteristic of his many-sidedness. As his mother has told me, you could buy almost any sort of a present for Tommy, because his interests were so diversified. This is apparent all through his letters. He was a great lover of music and spent long hours playing records from his XIII

considerable collection. As his diaries in particular show, Tommy early developed an interest in birds as part of his general enthusiasm for the outdoor life. He was an ardent skier and a keen sailor. And after he entered the 

Navy he would grow nostalgic for those marvellous summers in Maine that are such an integral part of every Lamont's life. From Colgate, where he was in training as an aviation cadet, Tommy wrote: "North Haven is my 

Tivoli, my Mandalay, my favorite place on earth."

Though Tommy passed through moods of youthful irritation and despondency, he was essentially a healthy, happy individual and full of the zest of life. Sensitive and discriminating in temperament, yet he had a fine, invigorating gusto for experience. And he possessed a wealth of friends among both boys and girls. I liked this passage in his diary in 1942 while he was at Harvard: "Went up to Dublin for a girl's coming out party. Everybody was there. Got to know a bunch of new guys. Swell party. Champagne, women, wow! Mighty glad I went."

In personal relationships Tommy was always frank and sometimes intolerant. His Exeter friend, Norman James, put it just right: "He was a rare person - intelligent, attractive, with an entertaining scorn for those he disliked and genuine affection for those whom he liked. He was occasionally irritating, in the way in which all fascinating people are, but he was usually immense fun." 

That August day in 1945, at the Island Church in North Haven, showed how people felt about T. W. L. II. 

The Memorial Service was crowded, by both summer and native residents. It was a great outpouring of love XIV

and affection for Tommy, of admiration for his courage and devotion in giving up his life that all of us might live in peace and happiness. It seemed to me that everyone there must have been thinking in his heart what one letter expressed so well: "It is unbearable to think of boys so young and tender and full of promise crushed in this mad world struggle; but they rise to heroic stature by the nobility and maturity of their sacrifice."

I am sorry, but I feel practically the same about the boys as I did the first day. They are a pretty tough mob. 

Most of the time they fight and tease. They have all sorts of tricks to make you unhappy. At night a gang comes down the hall and bursts into either mine or Norman's room. They hang around for a few minutes; mess up the room, you and run away. I have taken your letters to heart and am trying to make them like me. It is very hard because they are so fickle and ask so much. Dear Daddy, La Chataigneraie, Oct. 17, 1937 I have received many letters from the family during the week. A couple from Mother, a couple from you and one from Teddy. Teddy wrote me all about school..

He mentioned that Mr. Cameron read part of my letter to him to the class. Please ask Ted just what he read and why? Tell me sometime. I also got a letter from

Mr. Roper. He has been travelling around Europe this summer. 

Your long, type-written letter of Oct. 6 was exceedingly interesting. I want to make myself clear on Spain. 

I believe the most important thing in the world today is the maintaining of peace. I sincerely think that Communism or Socialism typifying Russia and maybe France does more toward peace than Fascism typifying Italy, Germany and Japan. Fascism has already proved itself a symbol of war to me. Japan is now at war with China. 

Who started it? The Japs. What's the reason? Greed for territory and power. Italy has just finished conquer-

ing Ethiopia. The reason is the same: lust for land and power. Hitler has cruelly treated the Jews and broken numerous treaties. I admit some were unfair. And what has Russia done? As you said, innocent people have been shot. But the number is very small compared with the victims of Il Duce and Hitler. Think of all the people white and black who died in the conquest of Ethiopia. 

A few weeks after the fall of Addis Ababa a couple of 

Ethiopians threw a bomb at Marshal Graziani. Nobody was killed. That night squads of Italian soldiers prowled through the native quarter of the city and any native found owning any kind of firearm was immediately shot with his family. About 1000 were killed.

In 1934 Hitler purged his army because of a small revolt!

In the last month thousands of innocent Chinese civilians have been killed by Jap air-raids!

Italy and Germany have helped the Rebel cause in Spain tremendously, thereby prolonging the war. Have 

Russia and France done the same? No! At the beginning numerous Russian and French volunteers entered the ranks of the government. But soon Russia and France fell in with England and upheld strict non-intervention. 

While all the time Italian government troops poured into Spain. Italy and Germany are always the ones who don't cooperate. They're the ones who withdraw from the nonintervention patrol. They're the ones who won't attend conferences on non-intervention and peace. Why Mussolini's excuse for not attending the conference at Nyon was the most stupid, biggoted, small headed, little 10

trouble-making note I've ever read. Can't that man see what he's doing? But the U.S.S.R. attends the parleys and cooperates pretty well.

I ask with Uncle Corliss why is Russia always referred to as a warlike, aggressor nation? Why is Stalin, though a dictator, put in a class with Hitler and Mussolini? 

What has Russia done to warrant these accusations?

Why do people always take Russia as an example of dictatorship and despotism?

At the present I think it is a struggle between the right and left in Spain. I favor the left for those good reasons mentioned above. I favor a government duly elected by the people. The Madrid government was such before the revolution. Also it was not Communist. 

There were a tiny number of Communists in the Chamber of Deputies. It was a weak government because the Fascists wouldn't stop making trouble. At last the 

Fascists, who were beaten in the elections, knowing they could count on the support of Mussolini and Hitler, broke into open revolt. Now the Madrid government is 

Communistic I admit. It has changed hands a few times and is unstable. But anything is better than a Fascist 

Spain. If Franco wins, it will be one more feather in the

Fascist hat. Franco is just a puppet in the hands of 

Mussolini. When Franco wins Spain will be an Italian colony. Mussolini will say, "Come on now, Francisco, if it wasn't for me you wouldn't have beat the reds. Now

I'll put an air-base here, and a naval base here, and guns here so we can blow up Gibraltar in 5 minutes." 

Now please don't get me wrong. I don't agree with All

the principles of Communism at all. If I had the choice of a government like that of Russia or a government like that which Franco intends to set up or they have in Italy or Germany, I would pick Russia. Communism and Fascism are both evils, I believe the lesser of which is Communism. Peace is all that matters now! 

One of the two Spaniards in the school is Uruttia, who speaks good English. If he is typical of those supporting

Dear Lansing, La Chataigneraie, Nov. 7, 1937

I have received two letters from you. Now this is one from me. 

This school isn't as nice as Buckley or even St. Bernard's. Thanks for feeling sorry for me I feel sorry for myself. 

A few nights ago a very strange thing happened. All over Switzerland there was "Air Raid Precautions." In the next war the most people will be killed by terrible bombs from air-planes. Mothers and fathers and children and babies will all die. In China they are being ruthlessly bombed right now by the Japanese. It is all very horrible. That is why everyone should do all they can to prevent another great war. But many people think there might be a war in spite of all we can do for peace. Dear Pa, Base Camp, High Sierras, Cal., July 4, 1941

This will probably be my last letter. We will definitely leave Base Camp within 3 or 4 days and proceed to Kearsage Pass and the "interior." Yesterday was about the toughest day of work and walking I have ever experienced. The point of the expedition yesterday to Kearsage Pass was (1) to break trail for jackasses; (2) to see really how much snow there was in the basin over the pass. I started with Bole at eight o'clock. We motored down from Base Camp to 

1000 ft., then swung on to the Onion Valley Road. This is one road Mother couldn't stand. I remember ascending the Eiffel Tower by elevator with her. She hated that and she would hate the Onion Valley road. It is tortuous, steep, unfenced mountain road with the most breathtaking drops and god-awful hairpin turns. The road climbs 6,000 feet into Onion Valley where it ends at the packer's camps at 8,800 ft. above sea level. I 36

didn't feel the altitude at all. At the road-head we started to hike carrying trail tools. After two weeks in the dusty furnace of this high desert Base Camp, the air, the snowfields, and the trees and water of the high Sierras seemed pretty swell. 

We found one big snowfield across the trail at one place. Although some of the packers had taken horses over it, Bole doesn't like to risk his stock especially when the snow is over boulders or scree. We dug out fifty yards of snow six feet deep. After that we came to a couple of lakes and at the second lake we camped for lunch. Lunch is very good and specially prepared on march days - chocolate, biscuit, dried apricots and figs. 

I slept awhile. Then on across snowfields, out of the woods up above the timberline. No attempts were made to shovel trail across the snowfields. We are going to lead the jackasses over those long stretches at night. The last mile to the top of Kearsage Pass was pretty tough going just wading uphill through snow. The top of the pass is 11,823 feet. I looked down into Center Basin.

There was a lot of snow down there but Bole says that if the hot weather continues we will be able to feed the stock in the Basin. A couple of guys got lost on the way back. This caused quite a mixup and we didn't get back until 7:30. I was tired as hell and the loss of altitude coming down fast made me feel lousy for the first time....

Dear Pa, Cambridge, Mass., Oct. 14, 1942

This is probably the most important letter I have ever written you. I have waited for a clarification of the situation before I attempted to explain to you my position in regard to the war.

It became evident to me some time ago that I did not want to stay in college more than through my sophomore year at an extreme. When I was taken into the Naval 

R.O.T.C. some weeks ago I was not, therefore, particularly happy, because although the R.O.T.C. assures technically that you will remain in college and provides that you cannot be drafted, the circumstances under which you stay at Harvard are hardly to my liking. I find myself taking Math C, Physics B, Nav. Sci., and Anthropology

B. Frankly I did not come to Harvard to do this type of work which affords me little profit and no pleasure. I intend to be a writer or journalist, if I get through the war.

I decided that I did not want to stay at Harvard indefinitely, concentrating in these fields even if I was preparing myself for a Deck Officer in the Navy. I believed it would be better for me to do one thing or the other. I wanted to fight, and fight soon. I wanted to fly instead of being on a ship. Therefore on October 1, I enlisted in the Naval Air Force. 

Now, at present, I am awaiting my parents' permission.

They cannot call me until I get it. But if the draft goes down to 18, as it will probably within some days, the 

Naval Air Force can call me any time, parents' permis- 44

sion or not. Mother did not want to give me permission.

I did not want to bother you, but now that I shall probably go anyway, you ought to know.

I hope I have made the situation clear. My membership in the R.O.T.C. is entirely separate from the Naval 

Air Force enrollment which I did independently. I shall continue with the Naval Science or R.O.T.C. as long as possible, as long as I am in college because the course is valuable to me as a future flier. But if the draft goes down - this is what will happen to Harvard. 

The University will be turned into a huge officer training school. (There are already 3000 officers from the various services training here.) The members of the 

R.O.T.C. will continue at Harvard as cadets, taking naval training, and living under naval discipline. The R.O.T.C. will in effect be disbanded. Now if I stick with the 

R.O.T.C., I will probably be commissioned a line officer in the Navy in a year and a half at the least. I would then have to apply for transfer to Naval Air Force. 

But I want to fly soon. Staying with the Regular bunch would be waste effort. Lt. Donahue, P.E.A., Harvard, who was very kind to me when I enlisted, said, “If anybody gets to stay in college after the draft is lowered it will be you. We want you for Naval Air Force, but you will probably and tentatively be able to finish your present year, probably to February, perhaps to June."...

The Naval Air Force, I feel, is the finest outfit in any service. At 1st Naval District Headquarters, I was introduced to Commander Brewer, who knew you. All the officers seemed like peaches. Up until some months ago 45

the N.A.F. was the only service which required that its officers be college graduates. Recently that requirement has been lowered, but the calibre of Naval pilots is very high.

First I would go to Pre-Flight School at Squantum or 

Chapel Hill. Then I would go to Pensacola. The whole thing takes upwards of seven months. The course is tough. I need toughening. Lt. Donahue said I ought to make a good flier. Of course, I may easily wash out, but that's a game chance to take.

I passed my physicals without great difficulty. They are the toughest in the world. All sorts of balance, eye, ear tests. 

Now that I am definitely fixed I've never felt happier.

My happiness is also due to other things, but it would be premature to discuss them here and now. I realize how confused all this war service information is. Boiled down it means that T. Lamont wants to fly a plane soon. 

He doesn't like going to Harvard and working hard at Math and Physics. If he is qualified for Naval Air (and he is) he wants to go in February.

Well, Pa, don't think I haven't put thought into this. 

I've talked with Mother, Grandpa, Grandma, Uncle 

Corliss, etc. Nobody says it, but they are afraid of flying.

But the draft lowering puts an entirely different complexion on things. They've got you now.

Of course, the prospect of only a few more months of education is a somber one. It makes me realize how little and ignorant I am, when I think that if I live I may have to go through life with my present fund of 46

knowledge. But the present program is so militarized and will continue to be increasingly so, I really might as well be in action where all good men are - you see I hope Harvard really isn't an education for me now. Education means History, French, Spanish, Fine Arts courses. 

I'll never get them. Well, the war comes first, but I'd rather do my drilling at Pre-Flight school than in Memorial Hall with a big section in Anthropology afterwards, just to remind me that I'm missing the broad outlook. 

It's such a tantalizing taste. I sit and look at the catalogue sometimes, counting all the fascinating courses I would love to take. I never can.

Harvard's just drudgery now - really it is. It's not your Harvard, Daddy. Can't you see why I want to go?

My education can wait until after the war when I'm coming back to good old Harvard and really learn something in a settled atmosphere. Right now it is impossible to learn anything around here, and nobody is in consequence, except a few studious draft dodgers who are trying desperately to make medical school or become engineers. The boys who leave now or in February to fly are going to be just miles ahead of the herd. Just wait and see. And if we get this Harvard Unit going boy, what a hell of a good war it will be.

Well, I was thinking the other day that even without an education I could always write well enough to write the comments under pictures of bathing beauties in magazines....

Dear Pa, 1943

U. S. Navy Flight Preparatory School, 

Colgate University, Hamilton, N. Y., April 18, 1943

It has been some time since you heard from me or since I have heard directly from you. A lot has happened. 

Before me I have approximately fifteen months of training. 

3 months at Colgate N.F.P.S., which is ground school and indoctrination. 

2 months at a CAA or WTS flight training center, probably Cornell - actual flying. 

3 months at North Carolina Pre-Flight at Chapel Hill which is extremely tough physical work and ground school. 

2 months at a primary training base. 

2 months at an intermediate training base. 

2 months at an advanced training base. 

1 month advanced operational training. 

15 months.

This is going to be very long and very hard. But it will be very good for my mind and body. 

Here at Colgate we are beginning. I am in a class of 51

cadets from the high schools of northern New Jersey. The class just ahead comes, for instance, from the 

Rochester section. All cadets at Colgate N.F.P.S. are from the Third Naval District. The surroundings are bleak and covered with snow and wind right now. The college campus itself may be more cheering when spring really gets here. Colgate is a small, well equipped college in a small upstate town. The faculty is good. The 

Naval staff is good also. The commanding officer is Lt. 

Comm. E. T. Thomas of Princeton c. 1922, who is a gent. Of course, I don't think I'll ever want to be bosom pals with most of my classmates but they are a cleancut lot on the whole - not up to pre-war Naval Aviation standards, however. A couple of Elis and Princetons are around. My best friend is a boy from Princeton, Dickie Frick, who comes from Pittsburgh and was in the class of '46. I room in a two bunk suite with a genial, intelligent Irishman from Seagirt, New Jersey. His name is William Leahy and he is 22. We room alphabetically. 

Naval Aviation is an education in itself and a good one. They are not fooling around here, as one soon realizes. I am not surprised by the toughness of the physical workout or the intensity of the ground school Math and Physics, but war has definitely deglamorized itself in my mind during the past few days. Perhaps the life of a naval man in London, Miami, or Cairo is all gold braid, champagne and girls, but in Naval Flight training it definitely isn't. My training period is going to be hard, uncongenial, and healthy. All I pray is that m52m

I lose neither my sense of humor nor my taste for the gentle and beautiful and fine. 

I think the Athletic Director, Lt. Ambrose, ex-of the

Brooklyn Dodgers, is deliberately trying to brutalize us. 

This I believe is unnecessary. His indoctrination talk yesterday consisted of threats, warnings, snarls, sarcasm, and chest-thumping. "We don't want no mama's boys or yeller-bellies or gripers, there are too many goddamn mama's boys in the world. Christ! There are some right here" - belligerent glare at the audience - "If I had my way I'd shoot every goddamn one." The worthy Lt. then proceeded to explain how he disliked manifestations of physical pain. "I don't give a damn if you have a broken leg and are on crutches. Come on down here and report in uniform or I'll see that you're busted." This concluding remark was a Brooklyn gem. "When you get out of here, I want to be able to look at you and say, "There goes the meanest bastard in the world.'

Well, I guess I'm just an effete intellectual, but I think fighting spirit can be instilled with fewer words and more restrained and sincere sentiments.

Well, this is war and the tough guys rise to the top, but it seems to me that the quiet courage of the average British officer has done pretty well. But America's youth, not being born with the ingrained noblesse oblige which makes Lord Peter Whimsey stand on the French parapet exposed to a hail of fire, must be bludgeoned along on "dog eat dog" talk, "kick 'em in the balls" technique and instilled with a Superman physical complex. 

Perhaps I am being unfair. Suffice it to say that the 53

Navy is making haters, killers, and excellent physical specimens out of us with amazing efficiency. Our teachers, our textbooks, our exercises, our drill, and our life are all excellently made to order. I can assure you that the Naval Air Force training is the toughest in the world. 

The results vindicate it. British pilots are, for the most part, gentlemen and scholars. This generation of American pilots, many without the resources of heart and mind which immortalized the Spitfire pilot of 1940, fight perhaps even better, but with a different driving power. 

We will fight as superb machines within a machine. "The enemy is the enemy. My plane is the best plane. My body is the stronger. Go in and kill."

Americans still do not have the incentive to kill the enemy that Poles, Englishmen, etc., do. 3. Dive bombing, torpedo bombing, and pursuit work at sea is incomparably dangerous and tough.

Lawson in the Field Artillery, and Potter in Anti-Aircraft. Crocker is joining Naval

After leaving the states in June I went to Pearl Harbor where I was very soon transferred to the subtender U.S.S. Fulton, one hell of a tub - overmanned, overcrowded, and G.I. as hell. She pulled out for Midway almost immediately. I spent about two months in

We ran down to Saipan. I went ashore at Saipan but you can read all about that in Time. Nothing much, but mud and ruins and heat and souvenirs. We were forced to return again to Saipan sooner than we expected, because the port screw developed a noise the Jap sound gear could pick up ten miles off. We were just on station at the time and that meant another week's run back to Saipan and a week there with divers over. Finally we shoved off somewhat depressed by consistent bad luck. 

Our area was a strip of the China Sea between Luzon and Hongkong. At first things were pointless. Every night we fruitlessly chased down contact reports radioed by other subs. Guys were speaking of a "dummy run," not uncommon out here now. 

A few days after the Philippines were invaded we got our break. We heard about a convoy of Jap reinforcements and ran it down. There were five cargo ships and two or three escorts. When we arrived on the scene three other subs of our wolf pack were already attacking, but they made submerged runs and were soon being depthcharged. We went in fast on the surface. The Jap radar is lousy and very scarce or we couldn't have done it. 

Our radar is wonderful. I stand battle stations on the 

APR, which is radar detection device. The Jap convoy had air support all night and their radar almost screamed the phones off my ears, but they didn't make out.

We attacked three times on the surface and fired 20 fish. We sank three ships and damaged one. Each time we caught the escorts at a disadvantage, because of our radar spotting, and each time we hauled ass at 19 knots, 103

easily outrunning them. At 3 A.M. we submerged. 

The wolf pack was depth charged all the next day.

We got off easy though - very easy. One of the other boats didn't.

We sank one more Jap in the course of the patrol and picked up an American naval pilot off Luzon. These were the only other high spots. I am not kidding, I could have used a lot more excitement. Perhaps I'm just green. 

We went straight to Midway and then Pearl. We were out 75 days - a very long patrol. Food and dispositions were damn poor when we got in.

I am now on two weeks rest cure at the Royal 

Hawaiian Hotel. Your buddy Quentin Reynolds is around. Should I stick him for a drink? I would just as soon be back at sea after four days here. No nothing except millions and millions of sailors and doggies.

Dear Father, U.S.S. Snook, Dec. 20, 1944

War in the Pacific has reached a leisurely pace so far as subs are concerned. They are obviously in no rush to get us back out there.... I'll probably be able to write you a couple more letters. 

At present I am blue as hell. Most of it derives from the morning news of the German counter-offensive, which probably means another six months on to the total of the war. It is sad and also maddening to see the indifference and everyday playfulness of shipmates, who all want to go home but haven't the slightest idea of what a thing like a 22-mile gain for the Nazi enemy does to their chances. All this set me thinking, Father, about you, and how much I should like to talk to you, and have you tell me a hundred things I want to know. I can't help but remember Kasserine Pass. The whole story never got out. It was just barely suggested that if it 106

hadn't been for the British First Army to the North, we would have been in a bit of a jam. You probably know all about that - about morale, about the general quality of our troops as compared with others. From some things 

I've seen and from the general attitude, I sometimes have grave doubts about the ultimate "unconditional surrender" victory.

There are an awful lot of guys who are just fed up with the war. Not usually Marines with a combat record or sub sailors, but the masses of mosquito-bitten, ill-fed, bored-stiff sailors and soldiers who garrison the Pacific. 

Errol Flynn characters of the up-and-at-'em type are rare as hens' teeth. The aw-you-can-be-a-hero kind of talk has become a fetish. You hear guys say: "I hand it to any guy who's smart enough to be 4-F." That's bad talk. 

The Home Front (what a name!) increasingly doesn't give a good damn, and you can't expect the servicemen to be free from the infection. But the Pacific War had the original fury of the Avenge Pearl Harbor motif to spur it along, and the Japs are physically and spiritually a much more unattractive enemy than the Germans. 

Albeit the "Pearl Harbor Avengers" are a rather winded, tired lot by now, and the flame of martial vengeance has petered to a flicker, but what must it be in Europe, where, for the Americans, there is no residue of race hatred and national pride to fall back on when morale is low? I could say a lot more, but it is purely opinion based on a minimum of experience. Some of the combat reporting in Yank and Time certainly is glib and not even apologetic about action which is patently running away. The 107

day of "last stands" (vis Custer) seems to be considered passé so far as our army is concerned. Or am I wrong? 

What happened in Belgium? Was it over-confidence? 

I can well understand that, with everybody saying “It's all over, we've won," it is harder for a soldier to risk his life than when it's back to the wall. Nobody wants to be an armistice day casualty.

I try like the devil to see the bright side, to think we've fought a hard, brave, skillful war, and that we'll make a good peace - but right now I don't know. I am inclined to think that we've fought this war more on our immense superiority in physical resources, money and guns, nylon pup-tents and radar, than on guts. The press is forever making excuses and couching bad things in a matter-of-fact frankness. But do they have war neurosis on a large scale in other armies? I don't imagine they have psychoanalysts attached to the Russian Army. In fact, I read a statement by the Surgeon General of the USSR saying that war neurosis was unheard of in the Red Army. Now, God knows, the average Russian can't be compared to the average Yank. His soul is different, but what about the British Army? Where do they draw the line between plain cowardice and "war-weariness," etc.? I think we are pretty charitable on those lines judging from a few guys I've talked to.

I have read all your letters, re British war effort and people, with interest. Comparisons are invidious. When 

I first read the paper this morning my first reaction was a smothered hope that the troops involved were British, or at least half and half, not because I want Englishmen 108

to die instead of us, but because my national pride makes me shrink from having to make our country's army bear the onus of another failure, a failure which cannot alter the final outcome, but which like Cassino and Kasserine is a black question mark - better not talked about. 

I do wish you'd write me about all this. Do you follow my train of thought?

As for myself: I have been out of the States for seven months. A lot of guys have been out 27 months. I have made one patrol. A lot of guys have made seven or eight or more. What action I've seen has been pretty one-sided. As yet I haven't been hurt or scared or lived in filth or gone more than 24 hours without sleep. So the natural assumption is that perhaps I am talking out of turn. Perhaps I am. Well, we shall see how I pan out. 

You said at the end of your letter that you admired my "patriotism" and contribution to the war effort. You said you were proud of me. Kind words, Pa, but undeserved. 

At the risk of being dramatic, I can say that I am not afraid to die and seldom give it a thought. But frankly you are the patriot. I am just a limelight artist. . . . I am in a damn good service, and I'd rather be a swab-jockey on a sub than a captain on a tanker or transport. 

My ardor for action is not dampened as yet, but my homesickness is running riot and tips the scales. After a few more runs my sensitivity will no doubt be dulled. 

After a few more runs I might even swallow my pride and take stateside duty. But presently I am still curious. 

I am curious as hell.

Dear Uncle Corliss, U.S.S. Snook, Dec. 29, 1944

Some subversive person slipped a copy of Soviet Russia Today into a shipment of magazines which lately came aboard. It was the August issue and included an article by you, which reminded me I owe you a letter on various subjects.

Grandpa sent me a clipping of my old friend, Benjamin 

De Casseres', recent attack on you. Your letter to him was awfully clever. It obviously left him speechless with rage, because his comments in reply were incoherent, to say the least. What that old dud needs is a good punch in the nose, and I am tempted to write telling him to have his boss William Randolph Hearst assign a few bodyguards his way. If I ever get stateside, I'd like to hold field-day on him.

But what kind of talk is this? The main intent of this letter is to express my extreme misgivings and disappointment at the general drift of Soviet foreign policy. 

I see that you have written a tract entitled Soviet Russia and the Post War World, of which I would greatly appreciate a copy, my entire fund of information on current events being the gleanings of pocket editions of such capitalist, right-wing publications as Time, Newsweek, and Life. Indeed for long periods I have no other news but the briefest of press releases via the ship's radio. 

Perhaps, therefore, you will excuse the factual sketchiness of my argument in some cases. As to your pamphlet, though, I expect you have some inside information on Russian purposes and policy. American and British 110

correspondents in Moscow, hampered by the NKVD, censorship, and secrecy, and lack of freedom of movement, must rely upon the rather rare statements of Stalin and Molotov on foreign policy in writing their articles. 

Without adequate information, you can't blame them for sometimes drawing conclusions with which you disagree. I noticed that even Soviet Russia Today was unable to produce the text of a recent important Soviet decree on marriage and divorce - "unavailable." 

The thing that bothers me most is Poland. It seems to me that in her dealing or lack of dealing with the Polish Government-in-Exile the Soviets have been unpardonably arrogant, intolerant, dictatorial, and blatantly unfair 

a very bad example of diplomacy in our "bright new world" and an ill omen for the future. The technicalities of boundary disputes are not the important issue, to my mind, because nobody has the moral right or physical might to deny Russia what she wants in Eastern Europe. 

Nobody will deny that Russia has played an overwhelming part in the German defeat to date, and at a ghastly and stupendous cost to herself. Granted, however, that the Soviets cannot be expected to gush with the political purity of Wilson or the magnanimity of Lincoln, it could reasonably be hoped that the Soviet Union would at least make a gesture toward the new order of international fair-play, frankness, tolerance, and forbearance, which the American, and also the British, governments are fighting this war to establish. But the Soviets have not made much of a gesture even. 

They used the patent pretense of insult over the lll

alleged murder at Minsk of Polish officers to break relations with the London Poles. They then turned their native press and foreign mouthpieces to a campaign of vilification, crying "Fascist, traitor, reactionary" at the

Sikorski and Mikolajczyk governments. It is obvious that the Soviet didn't want to waste time talking to the Poles and therefore resorted to the tongue-in-cheek farce - "You're a swine and I won't have a thing to do with you."

This was reminiscent of the days of 1939 when the Germans were top dog and could afford to use the same technique of the ultra-cynical; because they knew perfectly well they could get what they wanted and, by calling their opponents "assassins," could obviate the tedium of the conference table. As a matter of fact the reviled "emigré" government is composed largely of peasant Socialists. Even Sosnowski is hardly a Fascist. They are merely patriots, inconvenient indeed, but still patriots. 

But the Russians seem childishly (perhaps wickedly) incapable of granting an opponent to be sincere and arguing thence with candor. 

Well, at this writing, I gather a patched up settlement has been arrived at in Moscow, whence poor Mikolajczyk hurried, as a Hacha to Berlin, and the atmosphere was probably callously one-sided.

I am not going to argue about the Curzon Line or Russia's right to a buffer republic in Eastern Poland. 

Who shall say nay? Not I. But we have heard that the

Soviet leaders are super-intelligent, super-realistic gentlemen. They are neither if they hope to foist upon strongly Catholic Poland a clique of renegade leftists. 112

Puppet governments do not seem to be the monopoly of the Nazis and Japs. And, of course, if Russia takes Eastern Poland, the "strong and independent Polish state,” which Stalin reputedly seeks, will have to seek territorial compensation in the West. Once more there will be the ancient ranklings of German minorities and German cities, like Posen, under the heel of Slav "oppressors."

Indeed the very fact that the Soviets seem to think that their interests can best be served by the creation of buffer and satellite states in Eastern Europe, would indicate that they put little faith in the efficacy of a strong postwar international organization. The final solution of 

European peace, I feel, does not lie in an eternal defense complex of buffers and the like.

I had hoped that the Soviets would abstain from political and physical reorganization of Eastern Europe, until after complete victory-then at a conference table, seated with her allies, for appearances' sake alone, if needs be. 

But the Soviets are playing power politics already, and the British, not to be outdone, follow suit. There is talk of "spheres of influence" for the British in Italy and Greece and, for the Russians, elsewhere to the East. I have read that in the Victorian and Edwardian eras Africa and Asia were split into "spheres of influence" by the great powers. This policy worked for a while, but inevitably spheres clashed. There were incidents between the British and French in the Soudan and between 

British and Russian in Afghanistan. I don't like the idea of civilized and independent European nations being apportioned off to the big winners in the present conflict 113

- even in most circumspect fashion. A bloc of nations gathered together by mutual consent for defense or economy in common is another thing. A western European bloc under the aegis of Britain was suggested, but the Soviets saw anti-Russian designs in it, and squelched the idea with a barrage of stage whispers. This seems a bit dog-in-the-mangerish, for the Russians are protecting their western flank by somewhat similar methods, only the Red Army and the occupation commisars don't wear suede gloves, and Poles, Rumanians, and Bulgars being a diverse and unreliable lot, naturally the sunshine and mutual confidence angle doesn't bulk large.

Miner). The Big Four or Three must undertake morally and financially the responsibility for policing the world. 

You can't expect Paraguay or Switzerland to be particularly eager or decisive on the subject. An international police force to guard the pie after Russia has taken her slice seems to be the current Soviet attitude. The British outlook is somewhat too old-line and seems to be conditioned by their traditional fear, now proved by four years of fire, that they are weak without the safety of alliances, ententes, a balance of power, and European economic dependencies. The Penobscot River tumbles southward and seaward from the dark evergreen forests of the hinterland to where it meets the first salt breezes and salt water, and then, shorn of its fury, flows into Penobscot Bay. River currents join now with the ocean tides, racing southwestward down the broad reaches, past a hundred islands, until the last shade of shore recedes behind and the grey 

Atlantic swallows all.

Here where land-locked waters end and the sea begins are a dozen or more small granite islands. They are in fact the outposts of the continent. They are the last blue 157

line of Maine coast that an eastbound sailor sees until he makes his landfall in the Azores at Fayal. Of these lonely islands my White Island is one of the outer, and unbroken ocean swells roll in against its seaweed cliffs with a dull unceasing thunder. Nothing lives on the 

White Island but the wild, screaming sea birds and no one ever lands except those few who love the place. 

To me that spot is ever tangible, for I can see and hear and smell it now.

I think of the White Island with the wind blowingon a sou'west day when the little land clouds come drifting out from the hills of the mainland over the bay to these islands. The ocean is cold blue, flecked with a host of white caps, but by the brown ledges, where Eider duck squat on wet fronds of seaweed, the water turns to a translucent green. Foam topped, the swells pile on down Hurricane sound while we tack out to the White Island. The wind hums in the stays. The ship lays over, spanking each successive wave. On all quarters are green islands ringed with white rocks and whiter breakers.

Then it's always "hard a starboard - backstays — jibsheets," and we bring her up into the anchorage. One moment the lee rail is awash, she's tossing the biting spray across her bows - the next she rights herself and glides into the cove without a ripple. It's a snug and quiet cove with the White Island on our port as we reach in, and another little sister off to starboard. There is hardly fifty yards between the two islands, but the water is deep. We usually give her five fathoms of anchor chain. Splash - the anchor goes over stirring a 

158

There is a silence that doubly magnifies the dip of an oar or the rude tenor of a human voice. Great slabs of granite creep out from the moss at the wood's edge and lie white, gleaming in the sun. Booth Tarkington, Exeter, 1889

Two Pulitzer Prizes for literature have been awarded him, and today he stands among the foremost writers of this age, an expert at the barb of satire, a humorist unequalled. His name is Newton Booth Tarkington. He graduated from Exeter in the class of 1889, where, to quote a schoolmate, he was "renowned as an artist, not as a writer." 

Tarkington was born in Indianapolis, Ind., July 29, 1869. His father, a Civil War soldier, died at the ripe old age of 90, living to see his son grow famous. Young

The Decline of the Roman Republic 

There were three outstanding reasons for the decline of the Roman Republic from 201-78 B.C. First and fore most is the inadequacy of city-state rule for a teeming empire.

The second factor in republican downfall is a corol- 192

lary to the first, for constitutional anachronisms and an inefficient form of elective government led to the rise of a democratically inclined mob within Rome itself. 

Third- the rise of the Army, an army which owed all to its commanders and nothing to the state for which it fought.

By 133 B.C. Rome had about nine provinces, from 

Farther Spain on the west to newly acquired Asia on the east. This vast expanse of territory was governed in rather haphazard fashion by avaricious Roman magistrates. Central authority rested in Rome, where a senate of financiers and landowners, and an opposition of grasping equites and jealous riff-raff kept honest administration in the background. Romans who would bother to prosecute a governor in his home town, when after all the horse was out of the stable and the horse's owner was a barbarian. In Italy itself discontent was rife. dull type below the nice big black headlines will come across the uglier, more sinister aspects of Martin Dies....

He was still a rather obscure but noisesome southern congressman when in 1936 he began proposing investigations, one after another. His speeches became superpatriotic all of a sudden. Among the half dozen investigations which Dies proopsed was one to investigate Secretary Ickes for some irrelevant statement about "America's 60 Families," then a topic of debate. To all it was obvious that the young Texan wanted desperately something to investigate, preferably something along the alien-Communist-New Deal line. The international bankers, a stock in trade point of investigation some years before, were now all exposed. The obvious headline hunt was an investigation of subversive activities, i.e., the New 

Deal, labor unions, etc. And so Anti-Red Dies and Anti 

Nazi Dickstein collaborated in pushing over a $25,000 appropriation to start such an investigation in May, 1938.

At the last minute to sway the votes of skeptical New Dealers, Dies hit the headlines with the amazing news that "our beloved" President Franklin D. Roosevelt was threatened with assassination by Bolshevik agents. On 

May 26, 1938, the Dies Committee was born. The United

States was on the verge of the swellest Red hunt since the days of old leather-lunged State Senator Lusk of 

New York....

Speaker Bankhead of the House made Dies chairman of the Committee to Investigate Un-American Activities in June, 1938. Under him were put four Democrats and two Republicans. All but two were Dies mouthpieces, 204

but he did not need them. As the public soon found out, Dies does most of the talking for the Committee. Angry

Sam Dickstein, whose verbose anti-Nazism is political success on New York's lower east side, was left in the cold. • • • 

Trouble came right at the start when the Dept. of

Justice refused to lend the Committee any investigators. 

Dies was furious and made so great a stir that the President had to back up Thurman Arnold in refusing Dies Dept. of Justice investigators. Dies retorted that the New 

Deal was seeking to discredit his committee. The Roosevelt administration and Dies were at odds and have been ever since. Investigators from the painstaking LaFollette Committee on Civil Liberties were offered. Dies turned them down saying LaFollette was bent on sabotaging the committee's work.

As the summer wore on the Committee and its chairman swung into the work. Dies loved acting as sole interrogator of all witnesses. Many amused him greatly. 

The witnesses poured in renegade leftists, ex-union officers and members turned tale-bearer, avowed spies, thugs, Trotskyites, and patriotic citizens, all bursting to tell about the Red menace. Dies let go a weak volley at the Nazis and Fascists. Then he muzzled anti-Fascist editor Girolamo Valenti, who offered proof of Italian Fascist propaganda activities in the U. S. Once the name of Tom Girdler slipped into the testimony as a Fascist sympathizer, but Dies turned back to the Reds. He traveled the same road as Hamilton Fish. He sought to link 

Communism with the C.I.O., the C.I.O. with the New

Deal, the New Deal with the Spanish Loyalists, the Spanish Loyalists with left-wing "stooges," stooges with Reds, and finally Reds with trade unions. These exposures were timed to coincide with the Democratic primaries. 

Shirley Temple was damned as "a tool of the O.G.P.U." when Dies decided to expose Communism in Hollywood. 

Among other movie actors, called "stooges" by Dies, were Clark Gable and Bette Davis. This was an undeniable boner, for people started laughing at the Texan sleuth. Congressmen shrugged their shoulders. "A headline hunt? Anyway he only got enough to carry him through the summer." The New Deal was getting embarrassed. Then in October Dies told reporters his funds were exhausted. Chagrined New Deal congressmen, who had voted Dies $25,000 in June, started to breathe easy again. Their relief, however, was short lived, because the glib witnesses kept coming in from Minnesota, Michigan, and California to tell how the Reds infested this and sabotaged that. . . . 

Harper Knowles, Chairman of the California American

Culbert Olsen, Democratic candidate for Governor, and 

Sheridan Downey, Democratic candidate for Senator, were loyal subscribers to the Communist "Party line." 

He branded the Democratic candidate for Lieutenant

Then six Republicans arrived from Minnesota to testify that the Farmer Labor Party was Communistcontrolled and that Governor Benson was a Communist "tool."

Dies had done his work well. The Republicans won in 

Minnesota and Michigan.

New Dealers were wild, and anxious to do something about "that man Dies," but the President urged restraint. 

Dies was riding high. He demanded that Harry Bridges, west-coast labor leader, be deported because of evidence brought to light by the Committee's investigation. The 

Dept. of Labor refused, but recommended that Bridges be examined and a decision on deportation made by brilliant Harvard Law School Dean James Landis. Dies then accused Secretary of Labor Perkins of "unbelievable laxity" in handling alien agitators. He attacked Ickes for baiting the committee, and W.P.A. Administrator Hopkins for harboring Reds in the W.P.A. Flamboyant Harold Ickes was hopping mad and was, by the President's wish, narrowly prevented from broadcasting a speech entitled "Loaded Dies." 

Congress convened in January, 1940, and voted Martin 

Dies $75,000 for another year of investigating. The motion was whooped through 345-21 under terrific pressure from the American Legion lobby in the capital. 

Liberal opposition was utterly ineffectual. Liberals all over the United States, including Jerry Voorhis of Cali- 207

fornia, who is on the Committee, pleaded in vain to end the red hunt.

The Texan marches on in true inquisitorial fashion. 

Recently he has been after the National Labor Relations Board. The technique of Dies' prosecution is simple. A poor bedraggled little pink is led in. Dies shouts at him until the harmless witness is so scared that he will say anything. Dies then crams words down the man's throat.

The following courtroom technique is typical of what has been going on at the hearings of the House Committee to Investigate Un-American Activities. 

Dies - You believe in the principles of the Third International, don't you? Don't you! 

Witness - I really...

Dies - Come on, admit it. Your allegiance is to Russia, not the U. S.! 

Witness - I am a member of the Communist party and . . .

Dies - It's about time you admitted you are simply a paid agitator for Stalin.

The House Committee to Investigate Un-American Activities is a disgrace to a democratic nation like our own. The methods of securing testimony and witnesses are altogether unlawful. For instance, Dies Committee investigators recently raided the Communist Party headquarters in Philadelphia, carrying off all the files and records. The press on which the party paper is published was smashed, the offices ransacked. If such action is to be permitted in the name of democracy, then we might as well form a Gestapo or O.G.P.U. of trained thugs who 208

can do the work even more efficiently than Dies and his investigators. Perhaps the rottenest thing that Dies has done is the publication of the membership list of the American League for Peace and Democracy. Dies indiscriminately branded eight hundred people on this list as Reds. Many a poor school teacher lost her job simply because she had joined this group favoring sanctions on aggressors and aid to Loyalist Spain. Incidentally the lists were seized without any legal right. 

What can be done? Something must be done by all those who value the Bill of Rights. Dies has deliberately violated the Bill of Rights on numerous occasions, but the danger lies not in the unfairness of the Congressional 

Committee. Other Congressional Committees have been almost as unfair. The danger is the apathy of the American people toward Martin Dies. The means do not justify the end. An exposure of Un-American activities is fine, but let it be done impartially, legally, sanely with a definite end in view. If Dies has any purpose it is to drive anyone with a radical or progressive thought in his head outside the country. Reactionaries like Coughlin and the steel magnates are left alone to continue their form of social revolution in peace.

National Socialism came to Germany with the persecution of the Jews. Totalitarianism will sneak into this country under the guise of super-patriotism, 100% Americanism, and other oft heard catch-words which lull Americans to sleep. The Communists are the likely scapegoat at first. Then it may be Jews, Negroes, or even

Roman Catholics (Beware, Charles Coughlin). The 

209

forces of reaction have the money, the speakers, and the skill; they lack, however, the decency and common sense which most Americans respect.

The way of maintaining civil liberties and public esteem for labor leaders, progressive politicians, and even for mere adherents of the Bill of Rights is to defend the civil liberties of all, even Communists and Nazis themselves. To squelch Dies rather than to be intimidated by him would seem to be elementary prudence for the intelligent citizens of the United States of America.

Washington, 1940

The deep voice rolled on in slow southern tones. The words slid out smoothly and glibly - each syllable of every word pronounced with a detached emphasis - "Americanism; our duty towards our children; alien isms which threaten," and on and on while in the gallery a Georgia delegation of legionnaires shouted approval and clapped vigorously after each dramatic point. "Every year many hundreds of aliens from war-torn Europe enter this fair country. You know them as well as I do. They are penniless, good-for-nothings, exported from their own lands. I know for a fact that many of these here aliens are Communist agitators, and other such so-called liberals, and intellectuals, who come here from Russia and such places, bent on undermining our 210

great democratic form of government. Why must we continue to admit to these free shores the exiles of Europe, who are most of them foreign Jews and revolutionary riff-raff of one sort or another, when in this country there are now ten million true Americans, like you or me, who are unemployed? Therefore, the primary purpose of my bill is to stop this immigration of aliens from that hell-hole across the sea. Secondly, so that the 

U. S. may be restored to real Americans, my bill proposes to have all aliens, who have entered this country since 

1917, fingerprinted and placed under military protection.

We must, I sincerely believe, do this to crush these forces of sedition and unrest. By means of my bill we make safe for our children their rightful heritage, this great United States and the great democratic way of life - I thank you all."

The senator from South Carolina took his seat amid a roar of applause. The legionnaires were on their feet waving placards and flags while the gavel on the rostrum banged disconcertingly. An exuberant hush gradually settled down over the Senate, and when all was quiet another senator, with a wing collar and a droopy little black tie, rose "to second what my worthy colleague has so nobly said."

There was in the enthusiastic galleries one figure which did not cheer or clap. Beside him sat two men in pearl grey fedoras who talked excitedly, casting disagreeable glances from time to time at the man in worn clothes, sitting silently. "This here bill's gonna put an end to all these kikes 211

coming in each year from Germany," said one of the two men in pearl grey fedoras. "By Christ, we oughta lick hell outa anybody who don't back this bill," said the other. "Yeah, that's just what I was thinking at, too."

The silent man was listening, but he looked straight ahead with his lips tight shut. "You know," said one of the two men, "when I was back in Brooklyn last week they had a big party for Father Coughlin in the K. of C. Hall. He says it's time us Christians has got to take action against these here 

Goddamned sheenies and Bolsheviks."

The silent man bowed his head and closed his eyes to think. His hair was white and piled in loose waves which fell backward from a broad forehead. His face was elongated like a piece of rubber, but the features were firm and resolute. The rough cheeks were seamed with deep lines, channels of grief and age. His eyes were closed, but now and anon the heavy eyelids twitched. From the

American viewpoint he was poorly dressed. A threadbare coat, too small for his tall frame, was stretched over him like a gunny sack on a scarecrow. You could see the bumps of the vertebrae through the thin cloth, and people stared and raised their eyebrows. But what caught the eye of some were his hands, sticking out from the short sleeves, fine hands. The fingers were long and sensitive. When they trembled they made you think of a musical instrument, so sensitive it responds to the slightest touch; perhaps piano keys. The silent man's fingers were sinewy and lithe, products of delicate work; not 212

the chubby fingers of the idle rich, nor the coarse and muscular fingers of the laborer.

The senator from Georgia had just concluded his oration in favor of the bill, and the galleries cheered again. 

The silent man raised his head and heard the cheers.

Senior Class Oration 

It is the duty of the Class Orator, with accuracy, to interpret and give voice to the considered opinions of his classmates. Today this is no easy task. Our opinions are as the armies of the United Nations. They are flung from equator to pole, from flowered idealism to bitter cynicism. I like to think, though, we strike an intelligent medium, which is a difficult attainment for some of us in these times. But, in larger numbers than usual, we have our radicals. We have our dinner-table Fascists. 

We have our dreamers, our fighters, our cowards, and our boys who just don't care. Robert Frost thinks we will all mellow in time. Even a generation which has felt the awful shock of war on peace will simmer down to a contented plane. Perhaps we will - if we live long enough, and if we win.

Yet no one can easily synthesize the ideas of American youth in 1942 into concrete formulae. The war has knocked the supports from under that happy abstraction, the American way of life. It is easier for us to tell you what we do not believe in than what we do believe in. 

In the nineteen-twenties and thirties, when the Japanese 216

were learning baseball from visiting American teams, when we lived in security, in peace, and in relative plenty, it was not hard then for the orator of a graduating class to outline the pattern of thought. It was not profound thought. Why should it have been? Violent death was an unreal fate. The most you had to worry about was your best girl and whether you could make $5,000 the year you graduated from college. And the orators, the spokesmen of Exeter's youth and of America's youth, would pass on their sentiment, materialistic in an optimistic way, optimistic in a blind way, and politically conservative, if not Republican, in a traditional way. Relatively speaking, unanimity of opinion, unanimity and certainty of ambition and expectation among youth was at its heydey.

In June, 1942, we think our past materialism, wondering about $5,000 a year or a car for graduation, was rather bloated, almost amusing. Now the domestic political issues of yesterday are faded and trivial. Looking back, we realize that we never honestly tried to puzzle their validity then. Six years ago only the most intellectual Exonians debated the issue of the N.R.A. Youth felt that the world would go on about the same whether the Supreme Court was New Deal or not, and whether the National Recovery Administration stood or fell. We were partially right. No force ever compelled us to solve in our minds the problems of yesterday. Well - quite simply, we are marching off to war, and we are trying to take mental stock of ourselves, to summarize the intangible forces which govern our lives. We are trying to 217

solve, in our own minds, the riddle of history and of life's purpose, for the present and for the future, and we cannot.

For the boys of the twenties and thirties, there was an easy pattern of opinion. Political concept, national destiny, personal future: These all had answer cards. 

Today they are the most uncertain and intangible of terms. They are hardly realities. 

Why the confusion? you say. Forget the causes and effects. Look to immediate necessity. Get in and fight. 

Don't worry, we will. I should like to quote John Robbins, President of the Harvard Crimson, who says, "Those who have called us 'yellow' and 'afraid' were sadly mistaken. We were, it is true, very confused; but now most of us have emerged from the confusion of ideological conflict and have picked our path." We at Exeter have picked our path. I think most of us expect to fight, and we do not delude ourselves with hopes of a short war. 

We are fighting to save the country, and we are anxious to begin.

Yet, let me try and explain why some of us are in a state of mental flux and change, why of natural need our fight must bring some form of broader and more liberal, firmer and more lasting, reformation. 

American youth of my generation was brought up to regard war as a far-away monstrosity. It was morally hateful, yet, at a distance, somewhat physically exciting.

We were told that Socialism and Communism were evils which would deprive us and all men of initiative and the nicer fruits of labor. We were told that virtue, compas- 218

sion and Christian unity were turning the tide for eternal peace. Pacifism was in the air. We drank it in. We absorbed to a degree the peace-time canards: "America was dragged into the Great War"; "Youth will never fight again." And now every one of those precepts has been refuted and shattered, including the conception of a morally distasteful war. Most of us will fight and kill without conscience.

As to the evil of Socialism and Communism, Russia has vindicated herself in our eyes by unheard-of sacrifice of blood, by heroism which has saved Russia and saved us. It is difficult indeed to see how a country can resist as Russia has, if its people are enslaved in abject poverty by a blood-smeared bureaucracy of revolutionaries. The answer is that we have been deliberately deceived, and that is why most of my classmates who have thought about it, seem to feel that a modified Socialism is inevitable in America after the war. This war, this 

Peoples' War throughout the world, has, through economic necessity, hastened the leveling process. In America as well as Britain, the social and economic barriers between fellow-countrymen are crumbling. The spirit of democratic cooperation, the equality of privilege, of sacrifice, and the leveling of wealth engendered by this conflict is going to be permanent. Some of us already feel that Socialism, or a Social democracy, would be a good thing. Youth's prejudices are not deep-set. They are easy in coming and fast in going. At any rate, the swing is definitely towards the left. In 1940, this upperclass Academy was about 85% Republican. In my class, 219

at present, anti-Roosevelt feeling is hard to find. Of course, considerable of the switch-over to Roosevelt may be attributed to the pressure of war unity, but the appeal of the New Deal itself has grown in proportion to the popularity of Franklin Roosevelt. 

And what about Socialism, or a broader economic equality, taking away the luxuries in life? We cannot wax sentimental over that. The luxury in life is gone already. 

Virtue; compassion; Christian unity; pacifism. What about them? They too have been knocked from under us. For there is no religious creed strong enough to maintain any spiritual unity. There is no universal morality. Christian pacifism, the turn-the-other-cheek attitude, has failed for obvious reasons. The church has failed to outlaw war and now must needs support it. We feel, therefore, that in temporal, or shall we say, practical fields, the potency of the Church, at least in America, is a nonentity.

There is one attempted explanation for the manifest indifference of youth to organized religion. In general, those who have drifted away from religion for philosophical reasons, feel simply that it is absurdly hypocritical even to talk of applying the Golden Rule in a war of survival. They feel it is impossible to practice 

Christian ethics in a vacuum.

But youth is not without faith. American youth has turned to nationalism - not consciously, but through necessity - for psychologically it fills the breach left by the inability of religion, or faith in science, to provide a 220

driving, realistic credo, to satisfy our desires and aspirations. The cross, for most of us, has lost all dynamic meaning. The flag has taken its place. 

I think, however, that some of us, although we have yawned and carped with justification at every sermon in the Phillips Church for the past four years, may gladly ask for salvation in Christ when death stares us in the face.

Yet we have turned to nationalism first in this crisis. 

What is nationalism? It is an unpleasant term, connoting to Americans the theory of racial solidarity, purity and superiority, which is the foundation of the Nazi and Japanese states. That is racial nationalism. It is a crude and brutal form of a concept which is inherent in and common to all nations.

American nationalism is political. It expresses itself through the word democracy. Its symbol is the flag. To the American form of government we owe undivided allegiance. We fight for a flag; a form of government; a way of life, detached from theology. We realize that the economic administration of this nation will and must change, but we avow that the political structure of the United States is the fairest, most liberal and most equitable yet devised. For this we will fight under the flag with as much fervor as those young men who fought for many of our principles almost a thousand years ago under the cross.

But we are realists. We do not link the word Crusade with World War II. You have not heard us flaunt the old phrases - "A war to end wars"; "Make the world 221

safe for democracy." The gold trappings have been cut away. We do not say all we feel. But we are resolved to stamp Fascism from the face of the earth, and, rest assured, we will not leave the peace conference to the wabblings of obese old men.

Some years ago, boys from England and America marched off to war. It was almost a Holy War. They burned with idealism - men like Rupert Brooke - and they died in vain. Their sacrifice was blasphemed and wasted by the inferiors who ruled the peace after the conflict. Baldwin, Chamberlain and their ilk, too old to fight, and the blind but chosen representatives of America who betrayed the League of Nations. In America, our elders had the choice between the League of Nations and national isolation. They took the latter as a path without commitments or even moral obligation to the outer world, and even up to Pearl Harbor the people of America thought they had the choice between war and peace. They thought they could choose, but they could not. War has come. And yet a choice still remains. We can choose the peace. We can build a better world order, and now is the time, if ever, or we can revert to isolationism. It would seem that a few of America's more obtuse and Fascist-minded politicians would still favor that reversion. Less dangerous men scoff at international reform as at all reform. They are only bigoted or badlydisillusioned. Weak men shudder because the mere thought of a new and better world organization frightens them by its magnitude. Pessimism, cynicism, reaction, greed, and hate are all concentrated against those 222

who even try to build. But youth is forever optimistic.

Schoolboys have never been intimidated by life's terrors. 

We will not, in the writing of the peace to come, be disparaged or intimidated by past failures. We are cooler and more worldly than our contemporaries of the last war. If we fight as bravely as they did, we will do honor to ourselves and to our country. Yet we hope there will be a wider, finer result to our struggle. We will fight primarily to save the homes and soil of America, but also for the peace and freedom to create something really better, not just the same or a slight improvement on the old model. De Sales has said that as the war expands and evolves, ideas concerning the organization of the peace will change and change still more. The only denominator common to the people fighting the Axis is the will to save themselves from slavery. More constructive programs will have to wait. Wait they shall, but they must not be forgotten or laid aside in the hour of victory. 

It is a rotten thing to die in vain.

NAVAL RECORD OF THOMAS W. LAMONT II 

T OMMY entered Harvard as a freshman in June, 1942, and enrolled in the Naval R.O.T.C. In the autumn of 1942 he volunteered for service as a naval aviator; he was inducted into the United States 

Naval Reserve on October 30, 1942. However, he remained at Harvard on "inactive duty" till the spring of 1943 when he completed his freshman courses. 

Following receipt of orders to report for active duty, Tommy left Cambridge, and on April 15, 1943, became an aviation cadet at Colgate University, Hamilton, New

York, where the Navy conducted a school in pre-flight training.

Upon completion of his Colgate courses, he reported in 

August at Holbrook, Arizona, for flight training. Soon after he had begun solo flights his instructors decided that Tommy lacked flying aptitude, and in late September he was "washed out" along with a large group of other trainees. A recent change in Navy regulations made him ineligible for training as a bombardier, navigator or gunner, so he could not continue in Naval Aviation. He was given the opportunity, however, of enrolling as an officer candidate in the Navy's V-12 course. He refused this chance because he longed for immediate active duty as an enlisted man. He was transferred to the 

U. S. Fleet as Seaman Second Class, reporting to the 

U. S. Naval Training Station (boot camp) at San Diego on October 1st, 1943.

P245

At the end of eight weeks' training at boot camp, Tommy was again asked, on the basis of grades received in his tests, to become a V-12 officer candidate. Again he refused and applied instead for Quartermaster training, deeming this the quickest way to sea duty. His application was approved, and he entered the Quartermaster School at San Diego in early December, 1943. At the end of its sixteen week course, he was graded a Seaman

First Class. 

Tommy had applied for submarine training to follow his quartermaster course, and was accepted in April, 1944 at the Submarine School in San Diego. While at this school he took a two weeks' cruise on the U. S. Submarine S-34 in April and early May. This was his first real sea experience, and having been trained as a Quartermaster, his duties included the helm and look-out and the handling of bow and stern planes, which are the diving rudders controlling the submersion of a submarine. 

He stood night radar watches. Toward the end of May he again went on a two weeks' cruise, this time on a submarine tender. By the middle of June he was in San 

Francisco, at the Navy's submarine base on Mare Island, awaiting assignment to the Pacific. 

He did not have long to wait: on June 20th he left for

Pearl Harbor. In early July, he was assigned to the 

U.S.S. Fulton, a submarine tender and sailed for Midway 

Island. There, for a brief period as a relief seaman he engaged in overhaul work on submarines. 

Then on August 28, 1944, Tommy joined the crew of the U.S.S. Snook. This was a submarine with a fine rec- 246

ord, having already sunk or badly damaged some thirty Japanese ships. The Snook left Midway on or about September 5th and proceeded to Saipan. Tommy's first (the Snook's seventh) patrol began when they departed 

Saipan on September 15, 1944.

In a press release issued in October, 1945, the Navy told the story of this patrol: "On the second day out the 

Snook encountered heavy weather. Two days later, added to the mounting seas, the submarine developed a loud noise in the port propeller shaft. To continue the patrol under the existing conditions was impractical. The submarine's skipper, Commander George H. Browne,

USN, explained the situation to the crew. Due to the rough sea, the skipper would not order diving personnel over the side. They could either return to base, or they could, but with volunteers only, conduct diving operations on the spot. Four men, including the executive officer, immediately volunteered to go down and take a look at the propeller. "At night and without the aid of light, these men, led by the executive officer, made numerous dives while the submarine wallowed in the rolling seas off the coast of Formosa. During these operations a heaving line which had fouled the propeller was removed, but this failed to stop the noise. Having exhausted all efforts to locate the trouble, the Snook was forced to return to base at Saipan. "Repairs were made in record time, and the Snook got under way again for her assigned area deep into enemy waters. Operating north of the Philippines for the next two weeks proved uneventful. Concentrated carrier 247

strikes on Formosa and Luzon had kept enemy ships hugging the harbors. But once the fleet moved south to cover the Leyte operation, Jap ships, like scared rats, began scurrying from Luzon towards the homeland. "The Snook was operating northeast of the Philippines when she received the first word of a convoy moving north. Moving at full speed to join with other submarines, the Snook made her first contact with the enemy at 11:30 p.m., October 23, 1944. "Approaching from dead ahead and moving to a position on the convoy's starboard bow, the Snook closed the range fast. Off to one side a Jap plane dropped a flare, but the submarine held its course. The convoy was close now, and the Commanding Officer, having ordered a surface attack, chose the largest and closest ship as a target. 

Almost a half hour had elapsed since the first contact had been made. The ship's clock was only seconds from midnight. "At about 3,000 yards the skipper gave the order to fire, and a spread of four torpedoes went racing for the target. Then everyone waited - those seconds that seem like hours - for the explosions which would follow if the torpedoes found their mark. "The clock pointed to 12. It was October 24, 1944, in the far Pacific. "There was a thundering explosion as two torpedoes found their mark. Smoke and flame billowed into the air, and the Jap ship, a 7,500-ton cargo type, began settling. A smile of satisfaction passed from man to man. 

Someone yelled 'Happy Birthday, Snook,' and then it 248

dawned on everyone simultaneously. It was the Snook's second anniversary. up "But there were other things to think about. Coming fast on the Snook's port beam were two Jap escorts. 

Commander Browne called for right hard rudder, and the submarine turned away from the escorts. The two ships gave chase. For the next two hours it was a touchand-go affair, but the Snook, outmaneuvering the two escorts, found herself in position for a second attack on the convoy. "Approaching from the port bow this time, the Snook made contact with two ships in column. Here was a chance for a double kill.' Working with machine-like precision, the crew let go three 'fish' at the first ship. A quick shift to the second target and three more torpedoes were fired. Like the old 'one-two punch, two torpedoes caught the first ship flat, causing severe damage. A moment later one torpedo crashed into the side of the fireroom of the second ship, disintegrating and sinking it almost instantaneously. "But the Snook was now almost within 'broom-swinging' distance of the escorts, and the Jap ships opened fire. 

Again the chase was on. One of the lookouts spotted another escort closing fast from astern. The skipper ordered a spread of four torpedoes fired from the stern tubes. This was enough. Though the Jap escort succeeded in dodging the torpedoes, she gave up the chase, and the Snook was clear of the enemy. “Dawn was breaking as the Snook returned for a fourth attack. Only three major ships remained. Already the 249

Snook, along with other attacking subs, had either destroyed or crippled seven of the original ten ships. “Approaching again from the convoy's port side, and still surfaced despite the early morning light, the Snook closed on the three ships of the badly battered group, letting go five more torpedoes. Another hit! Two 'fish' caught the Jap ship full in the side. Like an earlier victim, the ship appeared to disintegrate. In the meantime, the escorts, still covering the two remaining stragglers, turned south towards the Philippines. "The Snook, her birthday already well celebrated, spent the day searching for other stragglers. Late in the evening she turned north towards Formosa. With only one spread of torpedoes left, but still eager for action, the Snook resumed her hunt off Formosa. She didn't hunt long. Less than 48 hours after reaching her new area, the Snook contacted three ships moving southall 2,000-tonners. Maneuvering ahead of the group the skipper brought the targets into a 'shooting duck' position. One of three torpedoes fired scored a hit on the closest ship. For a full seven minutes the entire area was completely covered with a huge pall of black smoke. 

When the crew of the Snook finally observed the damage, they found only two ships remaining. But they had to remain. With no torpedoes left, the Snook retired from the area. "For the next three weeks the Snook was assigned 'downed-pilot' search duty. With the carrier forces now back in the area, it was the Snook's job to pick up pilots forced down at sea. Her efforts in carrying out this 250

assignment were fully repaid when, on November 3, the ship rescued a pilot from a rubber life raft who had been stranded for over four days. "Returning to Pearl Harbor on November 18, the 

Snook received a triumphant welcome. To the skipper, Commander Browne, went the Navy Cross. All hands were awarded the United States Navy Submarine Combat Pin and Citation.” 

So the Snook was credited with three ships sunk and one damaged out of the convoy of ten ships, and later during the patrol she sank one additional ship. The Navy officially credited the Snook with 32,000 tons of Japanese shipping sunk or damaged on this seventh patrol.

Friends in the Navy have said that the official release omitted certain details. The Snook, they say, in the course of its action against the Japanese convoy was bombed by an enemy plane and crash-dived too late to avoid some slight damage to its superstructure. Whether as a result of the damage or from some other cause, Snook's diving machinery failed to function properly at this juncture. It appeared for a few seconds as though the Snook might be unable to level off from its very sharp diving angle, but skilful improvisation saved her. There followed many hours of depth-bombing; but the Snook escaped further damage and in due time rose and escaped. 

After seventy-five days at sea the Snook returned to Midway, low on supplies due to its long absence. After makeshift repairs and reprovisioning the ship proceeded to Pearl Harbor arriving there about November 20th.

After some weeks of rest for the crew and repairs to 251

the ship itself, the Snook departed Pearl Harbor on 

Christmas Day, 1944, for its eighth and last full patrol.

A new commanding officer, Commander John F. Walling, USN, had relieved Commander Browne. This eighth patrol lasted fifty-six days and though no suitable torpedo contacts were found the cruise was a difficult and dangerous one conducted under most trying weather conditions. The Navy's account says: "Enroute from Pearl Harbor the Snook, going by way of Midway, ran into heavy seas north of this tiny base. 

As the submarine continued north, winds increased, and the barometer, falling fast, predicted even more threatening weather. Freezing temperature and the spray breaking over the ship made the job of the officer of the deck, the top-side lookouts and the quartermaster almost unbearable, but it failed to cool their determination to carry out the mission. "Reaching their assigned area, conditions had improved little. Two additional hazards presented themselves in the form of ice floes and low visibility. That no enemy shipping would be encountered was pretty much of a certainty. Ships would not venture into these icefilled waters. But for almost two weeks, at times going as much as three days without obtaining a position, the 

Snook continued to carry out her patrol. "Having completed the first phase of her mission, the Snook moved on to the next area of her patrol. Even here conditions were little better. One contact on a small Jap patrol boat was made, but visibility made it impossible to press home an attack. 252

"Still hoping for better weather and some possible contacts, the Commanding Officer requested an extension of time in the area. The request was granted, but three days later the patrol was abandoned due to the increasing unfavorable conditions. "Although the Snook suffered no severe damage on the patrol, even in the ice-infested waters, the continuous heavy seas made a routine overhaul necessary. She returned to Midway the middle of February."

Other members of the crew have said that on this eighth patrol the shores of Japan were in sight as the Snook proceeded northward. The ship, they said, suffered considerable damage to its superstructure as a result of collision with a small iceberg one night in thick weather. They sighted many icebergs and it was very cold above deck where Tommy stood his watches. He was thankful for his heavy clothing.

The Snook remained at Midway until March 14th when its last patrol began, again under the command of Commander Walling. They proceeded westward, reaching Guam on March 24, 1945. During this run between 

Midway and Guam the Snook had a very close call during a routine dive. It has been stated that the ship accidentally submerged at much too steep an angle, possibly fifty degrees or more. Its descent was only retarded when one of the stern torpedomen turned an emergency lever which "blew" the tanks appropriate for leveling off. The

Snook departed from Guam on March 25th in company with two other United States submarines, the Burrfish and the Bang. These three boats formed a coordinated 253

attack group which was assigned to patrol Luzon Strait, the south coast of China, and the east coast of Hainan, and to perform lifeguard duties for Philippine based aircraft. The Snook, however, returned to Guam on the 

27th for emergency repairs, and left again on March 28th to rejoin her group. Because of the delay the Snook was diverted from her previous assignment and directed to join another group of American submarines for “attack" patrol in enemy waters.

Radio contact with the Snook was maintained by the flagship (the U.S.S. Tigrone) of her group until April 

8th, at which time the Snook reported that she was just east of Hainan in position latitude 18-40 north, longitude 

111-39 east at 7:40 Greenwich Civil Time. This was the last message known to have been received from the Snook. On the 9th the flagship which was not far from this position was unable to raise her by radio. However, this was not considered unduly strange considering the employment of the ship and her proximity to Japanese bases. On April 10th the Snook was ordered to move to the vicinity of Sakishima Gunto, a group of Japanese islands about 150 miles west of northern Formosa, in order to provide rescue service for aviators of a British Carrier Task Force which was then bombing those islands in support of the American attack on Okinawa. 

The Snook was not required to acknowledge receipt of these radio instructions and no acknowledgment was received. 

On April 20th, the Commander of the British Carrier

Task Force reported that he had a plane down in the 254

vicinity and was unable to contact the Snook which should by then have been in position to rescue the crew of the plane. The Snook was ordered to search this vicinity and to acknowledge receipt of these orders. Upon the failure of the Snook to acknowledge her orders, another submarine was directed to proceed to the vicinity and to rendezvous with the Snook. This relief submarine was unable to raise the Snook by radio and saw no trace of her although it did succeed in rescuing the three downed aviators. Subsequent information received from other air commands which had been striking Sakishima Gunto indicated that none of their planes had been able to contact the Snook in that vicinity. 

On April 29th, the Snook was ordered to depart her station and proceed to Saipan. No acknowledgment was received from this message. After a suitable interval of time permitting the Snook to reach Saipan after allowance for all possible delaying contingencies, telegrams were sent to next of kin, of each individual member of the crew, advising "missing after action." According to the Navy "the Snook must be presumed to have been lost while on war patrol in enemy waters sometime subsequent to April 8, 1945.”

On the Snook, Tommy performed the duties of Quartermaster "striker." A striker is a seaman, lacking a 

Quartermaster rating, but performing the latter's duties, duties similar to those he had performed on the U. S. Submarine S-34. He mentioned in one letter that on submerging and rising he was last man down and first man 255

up. His immediate superior on his last two patrols was 

Lt. (jg) Andrew W. Welch, Jr., Communications Officer of the Snook and a recent Harvard graduate. Tommy was his assistant "on the plot" (at battle station position); so presumably these two young Harvard men faced and met death together.

Tommy's ability as a submariner was attested by his commanding officer. Just after the Snook returned from its eighth patrol Tommy finally applied for admission to the Navy V-12 program. His application, dated February 

21, 1945, was later found in the office of the Chief of Naval Personnel, Washington, where it had been forwarded with an endorsement from Commander Walling. 

He wrote that the candidate was "fully qualified in all respects for the Program and considered outstanding for training as an officer candidate"; further, that he considered the candidate "would be a definite asset to the service as a commissioned officer" and that "except for his professional training he was completely qualified now to assume the duties of an officer of the line." 

Some one has said that regular naval officers are conservative in their written reports on the fitness of reserve officer candidates from their commands and that they are seldom inclined to generosity in the marks they give. In this case, on the basis of the Navy's maximum of 4.0, his commander graded Tommy from 3.8 to 3.9 in the various qualities upon which a candidate's fitness is judged. His high marks of 3.9 were for "intelligence," "loyalty" and "reaction in emergencies."

LETTERS ABOUT TOMMY TO HIS PARENTS 

Dear Tommy, Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, Dec. 24, 1944

I spent a very interesting and pleasant half hour yesterday afternoon with young Tommy. I was very much impressed by him. It has been several years since I have seen Tommy, and even then I saw him only casually. I don't suppose it is news to you and Ellie when I tell you that he is a great kid. He seemed well and strong, full of pep, keen about his work, extremely modest about what I happen to know they have been doing, and full of courage about the future. He really gave me a great thrill. 

I got in touch with him via the Red Cross yesterday afternoon. As he said he wouldn't be around here much longer, I suggested that he come over to see me in the late afternoon, which he said he could do. About five minutes past six he poked his head in the office and I called him in. I wish you could have seen him. He was in blue dungarees. They had just been laundered but there wasn't a square inch on them that wasn't torn or ripped. At least one side of every pocket was ripped.

Across his back in big black letters was written "Tommy Lamont." He sat down, and as I say, we talked for about half an hour. The first part of the talk he told me about his job, how he was, and where he had been. He seemed very keen about his work and his experiences, 259

which have been something. He then told me a little bit about what he thought his next tour of duty would be like. He says he eats well, lives well, and likes the crew. 

We then switched to the U.S.A.. He was particularly interested in my having seen you recently and in the fact that you had been to Paris. He asked me several times what you had said about Paris, and as I couldn't remember too well, I probably misquoted you considerably. He said he thought you were entirely right in coming home.

He finally had to go to catch a boat to the mainland. 

Before he left I asked him if there was anything I could do for him and he assured me there wasn't. He hadn't been out the door two minutes before he bounced back in again and said he needed a chit from me authorizing him to wear dungarees. I couldn't help but smile as they looked more like blue lace curtains than dungarees. 

However, I gave it to him and he went out on the double. I watched him out the window on the way to the boat. Just after he got out the door Colors sounded. 

You never saw anybody snap to attention and salute more smartly, just like an old-timer. I wish I could have seen more of him, but it wasn't possible. You and Ellie should be very proud of him. . . . 

As ever,

HARRY (Harry P. Davison)

CORNING BENTON

Mother wrote me several weeks ago of the terrible news of Tommy's submarine. I tried to write you then but it was hard to do. This is another attempt. You know already everything I could want to say so will understand what I write, however badly expressed. Please forgive the pencil, but it's all that's available, as we're on our way up to North Haven in our new boat, which incidentally is a real beauty.

By now the idea that Tom is gone has taken hold. 

I am just one of many who have lost a close friend, the most precious thing a guy can have. We knew each other since we were knee high to a grasshopper, and to me he was the kind of friend you take for granted and in the best sense of that word. Every year we renewed the friendship at North Haven, and seeing him with all 261

any of you was part of the place. I never forget all the wonderful summers there, many of which I was continually over with you, hunting frogs, chasing sheep, playing tag in the garden behind the big house, playing tennis while bloated with blackberries, picnics, sailing on the "Rose,' or going over on the "Reynard" to climb the Camden

Hills or explore Seal Island. Those are a few of the nostalgic and wonderful times of past summers, all closely associated with Tom and all of kids we Like you. used to scrap at times. He knew me so well that he could "needle" me and get me sore any time he wanted to. He also had interests as a kid along much more mature lines than I, and in both respects was very much of an educating force in my life. Our differences complemented each other's and I know we did each other a lot of good. 

At college, in spite of a few wranglings at odd times, I got to know and respect him more than ever. Talking to him was real fun because he was always so quick, and it was instinct more than anything else that saved me in many arguments where he was far ahead of me. I can honestly say that conversations with Tom prompted more thinking in me than those with any other guy and it was really stimulating and helpful. What I'm trying to say is that losing Tom is to me the loss of a close companion, friend, and, at college even more than that as we talked. Dear Tommy and Ellie, Burcote Brook, Abingdon, England, June, 1945

The news of your appalling loss just breaks our hearts in two with sorrow for you.

JOHN MASEFIELD 

Brooklyn, N. Y., Aug. 7, 1945

It was with very real regret that I read in this morning's Herald Tribune that your son Thomas had given that "last full measure of devotion" to his country. 

Ever since the commencement exercises at Exeter in 1942, when my son Norman was graduated, and I had 264

My husband and I were so impressed with him on that day, his ability to express so beautifully such serious and inspiring words, at so tender an age. Both our hearts and eyes were full when he had finished, and we felt America's destiny was quite safe, if boys brought up like yours could express sentiments so common to the heart of man. Dear Mr. Lamont, United States Army Air Forces, Carlsbad, N. M., Aug. 15, 1944

There are a great many of us, I feel sure, who were in 265

school with your son, Tom, for whom this last issue of

Time carried news that makes us wish to express deepest sympathy to you and your family, even though we may not have been his intimate friends.

At Exeter, I knew your son only slightly, being two classes behind him. Although we were on the Exonian and Review at the same time, I doubt if he would more than just remember me. But to me he always signified a type of leadership which has meaning beyond school days alone.

In particular, I remember an Exonian editorial of his during the winter following Pearl Harbor, attacking the apathy and feeling of disinterest in the war which seemed already to have settled on the student body. Possibly it was not his best editorial writing, but it showed an awareness and an enlightened concern about what was happening in the rest of the world that practically none of the rest of us seemed to possess. 

When a boy, then, can editorialize in that fashion, it almost goes without saying that he will grow into a man of understanding, a man with belief in the aims of the conflict he's engaged in. And therefore it seems, even to one who knew him, personally, hardly at all, that although nothing can dull the sorrow you must feel at this time, Tom's life, and death, even so near the end, are given a meaning and purpose that do not belong to the sacrifice of every man dying in his country's service. 

With deepest sympathy,

ALAN WILLIAMS (P.E.A., '44)

WHY I AM PROUD OF MY BROTHER 

When Tommy volunteered for submarine duty as his part in this war, it should not have come as a great surprise to us, his family and friends, for it was typical of him to pick this job - one of the toughest, most dangerous, unglamorous, and vital. Because prior to that time he had always been what I would call a "fighter,” a fellow that was fazed by nothing. Not only did he overcome physical obstacles (he was slight of build), but more important than that, Tommy always stuck to, his ideas and principles regardless of the opinion of the majority, whether in debating at Exeter or during a discussion over the family dinner table. 

He took the bad breaks with a smile, and he had his share of them, too. His failure to make the grade as a 

Naval Aviator was a tough and discouraging blow for Tommy, but he took it in his stride. And then, although he had the chance for officers' training, Tommy turned it down and picked the submarine service. Hard as his particular assignment was, with most of his friends and other fellows of his background becoming, or already, officers, and with more than a year of active duty in the Pacific, without seeing the States or his family, he never once complained or griped about his lot. And, believe me, that is something mighty rare among our soldiers and sailors. On the contrary, he said he wished that he could have the opportunity to do as much as some of his other compatriots were doing: the Marines in the Pacific, the 

Infantry in Europe. 270

He made the most of his job, enjoyed it, did his work well, and he and his buddies on the Snook achieved a fine record for their ship.

My brother served his country well and courageously that's why I'm proud of Tommy.

EDWARD M. LAMONT, S 2/c USNR
