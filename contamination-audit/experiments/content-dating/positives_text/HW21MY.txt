FOREWORD 

TH HIS article on War Prevention is a clear statement of what is necessary to prevent war. 

When the League of Nations was proposed by President Wilson, it was at once apparent to the author that such a League would always remain powerless to prevent war, and at that time this article was written, in an endeavor to formulate a plan that would not fail in operation.

There is no use in considering makeshifts. The idea of establishing a governing body which is impersonal in relation to all countries, is the basic principle here set forth. The Encyclopedia Brittanica states part of the problem thus: "Any attempt to organize the concert of powers must always fail, as it failed in the early part of the nineteenth century, so long as the spirit of national and racial rivalry is stronger than the consciousness of common interests.' ??

If an impersonal organization with power had existed in 1913, there could have been no war. 

The burning question is: Do you, or do you not desire to prevent war?

INTRODUCTION 

It is purely Utopian to consider the present plan of the League of Nations, or any other, which cannot reach the goal-namely, abolition of war. 

Treaties and International Laws without means of enforcing them would parallel the case of having criminal laws but no means for apprehending and punishing the criminals. We learned that in the 

United States before 1917.

A pseudo effort for war prevention diverts the attention of the public from the real aim, and those who believe in the principle of saving human lives are unfaithful to that principle if they do not insist upon a fully operative plan. A consistent and determined effort, in the course of a few years, will achieve the desired result.

POSITIVE WAR PREVENTION 

Much has been said about the elimination of war, but in the last analysis mankind has taken no effectual steps in that direction. On the contrary, scientists and militarists of every nation, in the name of “defensive research" have exerted their efforts and talents to making war more inhuman and destructive to property and lives.

We know that racial prejudices, religious bigotry, political ambition, machinations of adventurers, national animosities, international jealousies, psychopathic rulers and commercial competition are among the principal causes of war. 

We are aware of the fact that there are in the world millions of people who would risk their lives in a war solely for adventure's sake, entirely irrespective of its merits. Many young men have run away from home and enlisted in the army with a total disregard to any possible dangers; furthermore it is about a century ago that duels to the death were fought. 

A prominent instance was the duel fought between 

Alexander Hamilton and Aaron Burr; in England,

Disraeli, later Prime Minister, challenged Daniel 

O'Connell to a duel.

During the French Revolution plunder was a potent motive for war. The French Republic needed 3

It is most remarkable how a man possessing a strong and captivating personality can lead a nation into excesses which upon calm reflection must be judged as marauding and murderous expeditions. 

While walking with a French gentleman in Paris about forty years ago, the writer mentioned that the Tomb of Napoleon was the most impressive monument that he had ever seen, whereupon the French gentleman remarked that in his belief Napoleon was a wholesale murderer, adding words to the effect that he was likewise a glorified gangster. These statements came as a surprise and gave rise to serious thought. 

The late Dr. Allan McLean Hamilton, an eminent and highly esteemed alienist, and the grandson of the distinguished American, Alexander Hamilton, wrote an article in 1904 in which he diagnosed the mentality of a contemporary ruler and maintained that he was a menace. In 1915 he wrote a second article for the same magazine showing that his prediction had been fulfilled. Nevertheless today many citizens of this ruler's country are oblivious to the fact that their erstwhile ruler was a pathological subject.

One must think of the world as an old tinder-box structure in which the manufacture of highly inflammable articles is carried on. There is always the likelihood of a smouldering fire existing in one spot or another, which, when blown upon, bursts into flames, and which, as it spreads, carries with it disaster and ruin.

In the last sixty-five years, there have been no less than twelve major international conflicts, not to mention numerous civil wars and revolutions in all quarters of the world. Beginning with the Franco-Prussian War of 1870, the list includes: Russian-Turkish war in 1877; Chinese-Japanese war in 1894; Turkish- 

Greek war in 1897; Spanish-American war in 1898; the South-African war in 1899; the Boxer Insurrection in China in 1900; Russian-Japanese war in 1904; 

Italian-Turkish war in 1911; Balkan war in 1912; 

World War in 1914; Chinese-Japanese war in 1931. 

Comment might be made upon the fact that the Peace Palace at Hague was dedicated in 1913 and the next year almost the entire world was plunged into the greatest holocaust that has ever been known in history. Mention might also be made of the fact that in 

1919 the Peace Conference met at Paris, yet neither the Peace Conference nor, subsequently, the League of Nations proved efficacious in deterring Japan from its invasion of Manchuria in 1931, and when China ap-

pealed to the League for its intervention, Japan promptly withdrew from the League. Germany, likewise, withdrew for its own reasons.

Today no nation can rest secure against the possibility of war. Yet we fondly hold on to our illusions. 

Nothing is being done except the effort to reduce armaments, thus avoiding making provisions for an emergency. In the Fall of 1915 we had little thought of entering the World War; in 1917 we were in it, and up to the present time, with all the money spent and suffering endured, we have not paid the full toll. The slogan "He kept us out of war", was no security against our being drawn into the conflict. 

Twenty-five years ago it was said that a war could not last more than a year because of the great improvements in weapons and means of warfare. We found, however, that the Great War continued for more than four years. As time went on there would have been increasing disregard for the code of what might ironically be termed "civilized warfare." One infraction would have brought about others in retaliation and there is no telling to what barbarous extremities the conflict would have proceded.

It is too early to forget the World War with its torpedoing of passenger ships, the terrorizing of London by the bombing of Zeppelins and the permanent

disabling of soldiers through gassing. Yet the nations which committed these atrocities did not consider themselves uncivilized. 

War prevention can be attained through eradicating belligerent movements in their incipiency, or by warding them off altogether. The attack must be directed at those who can be identified as inciters of international enmity. As far back as we can look in history, every community has its leaders. Today in all societies, associations, clubs, or organizations of any kind, there is invariably a president, captain or predominant figure and in the case of wars there are just a few leaders who, so to speak, inflame the populace and then plunge into action. Keeping that in view, war prevention must be aimed directly at the persons in control. This can only be done through having all the nations unite in an association of an entirely impersonal character, so that there can be no justified feeling of resentment entertained by a single nation against the Association.

It is necessary to create a suitable Intermediary Body with subdivisions to control in an effective manner the various factors which lead to war. The Intermediary Body must command the fullest confidence of all peoples because of its integrity and the sound judgment of its members if it is to be invested with author-

The various countries must collaborate and agree upon what are points essential to preserve their interests and likewise to protect the interests of citizens of other countries. A constitution should be drawn up covering these points specifically. Nations that have not been engaged in war during the last seventy-five years might well be entitled to double representation in the Association.

National patriotism must be completely suppressed in the deliberations of the Intermediary Body; espousal of the interest of any one country should not be tolerated. Only the greatest good for the international interests should dominate. 

The procedure for the selection of members of the Intermediary Body is of paramount importance. From each nation a group of ten or more reputable individuals not all of one religious or political affiliation should nominate candidates for membership. Each member will be appointed to the respective division in accordance with his individual ability and characteristics.

The members of these groups must present a statement setting forth the place of birth, religious denomination, political affiliation, occupation, resi-

dence and age of their candidate, and the period of time they have known him.

The nomination should be in the form of a signed paper stating also the qualifications of the nominee recommended, to wit: that he is known to be amicably disposed towards people of other nations, religious or political affiliations; that his nobility of character and high principles would enable him to hold himself aloof from bias that might be the result of patriotism, religious training, political experiences or other prejudice-producing influences; that he is intelligent and that he has a record of accomplishment indicating his ability and efficiency.

Almost every observant man with broad, worldly experience has at some time met the exceptional type which measures up to this standard. Thus we know such men are to be found.

The nominees in turn shall nominate from among themselves and elect double the number apportioned to each nation. This last selected group from the various nations upon careful investigation shall choose from among themselves the actual number required, together with a few to serve as alternates. 

The choice should include persons of various religious beliefs, whether by birth or observance, but non-believers are to be considered equally eligible. 

The finally selected members of the Intermediary

Body must secure from their native countries a certificate absolving each from allegiance to his country. 

They must take oath or pledge themselves that they will serve international interests faithfully; that they will fulfill the duties attached to membership in the Intermediary Body conscientiously and to the utmost of their ability; that they are above being influenced by any religious bias or teaching and that should they be approached by persons in a manner indicating a desire to exercise any improper control, they will immediately communicate the facts to the Association.

Although having certain separate functions to perform, the activities of all these divisions are to be coordinated so that the fullest possible advantage will be derived therefrom, with the result that each division, besides attending to matters within its own province, will keep other divisions informed of any facts or situations that should be considered. 10

The prime object of having many divisions is to keep in better touch with all the conditions and developments in the various countries. Through exchange of information, the Intermediary Body will be better able to accomplish beneficial results. 

Division A. Mediatorial: Upon learning of any differences existing between two countries, this division should offer its services as mediator, its duty being to eliminate at the earliest possible moment the chance of these differences becoming serious. All steps taken by this division, and all its proceedings, should be a matter of record and open to the inspection of other divisions. In the event that the 

Mediatorial Division is unable to effect an amicable settlement, it should present the case to the Judicial 

Division for determination.

Division B. Judicial: Either upon its own initiative or in the event that the Mediatorial Division should fail to accomplish results promptly, it would devolve upon the Judicial Division to proffer its services for a judicial determination.

The Judicial Division should insist upon prompt action and should render prompt decisions. Nonconformity with the terms of a decision made by the Judicial Division would subject recalcitrant leaders to the punitive action of Division C. Division B should 11

take the initiative in summoning witnesses, establishing its own laws of procedure and conducting investigations.

Refusal on the part of a country to submit its case for determination would at once subject the leaders / of such opposition to the action of the Disciplinary and Punitive Division. 

Division C. Disciplinary and Punitive: It should be clearly understood that the function of Division C is to exert a powerful influence over the leaders of any movement which is calculated to cause war. 

The prompters or leaders of any movement for wrongful action should be held personally responsible for the wrong-doing.

It should be incumbent upon the members of the leaders' families, or their friends, who have any knowledge of acts of propaganda inimical to the purposes for which this Intermediary Body is organized, duly to notify the Disciplinary Division. Failing to do so they would expose themselves to fine or imprisonment, or both, unless they could prove conclusively that they had used every effort to discourage and dissuade the leader.

It would be the imperative duty of Division C not only to punish those who take the initiative in any agitation tending toward war, but also those who aid or encourage such leaders. 12

Let us suppose that someone with an obsession succeeds in gathering together a number of supporters, commits acts of injustice which are likely to cause war, and persists in so doing after due warning from the Intermediary Body. If they disregard the warning to stop such activities, Division C, with the concurrence of Divisions A and B, may issue an edict of outlawry against those individuals and take steps for their apprehension or execution, it being reasonable that no one should be permitted to lead a nation into committing aggressive acts. The fuller the power given to this Division, the less occasion there will be for the exercise of such power.

The Disciplinary and Punitive Section should have at its disposal several able psychiatrists who could pass upon the mentality of rulers or wouldbe rulers. This is necessary because of the fact that there are many instances on record where people, possessed of a fixed idea, through their personality or earnestness have influenced others and gained support, even when their ideas are untenable, unreasonable and irrational.

There are many different types of insanity which could be diagnosed by a psychiatrist who for years has made a study of those so afflicted. Public movements that might arise under these conditions could be 13

of so insidious a nature as not to be recognized by the ordinary individual. There are many historical cases of this kind, where zealous and disordered minds have caused great sacrifice of life. 

It would be very easy to cite many hundreds of well-known instances of the subjection of the multitude to the influence of unbalanced minds such as Cola 

Rienzi's, those of the false mahdis of the desert, and of the pseudo-messiahs like Madame Blavatsky and

Johanna Southcott. 

It is an accepted fact among those men who have made a study of the human mind that the mind of the group or crowd is different from the individual mind. 

Once won over to a certain opinion the multitude often-times refuses to relinquish that opinion in the face of common sense evidence to the contrary. 

When Mahomet commanded the mountain to come to him and it did not obey his command, Mahomet said "If the mountain will not come to Mahomet, Mahomet will go to the mountain." And such was the loyalty of his followers that they accompanied Mahomet to the mountain, more impressed, probably, by his power of command over themselves, than if the mountain had actually obeyed their leader's command. 14

Again, the subjects of King Canute maintained that their ruler could do anything or command anything, even when King Canute, to dissipate this idea, had his chair carried to the bank of the Thames at Westminster and commanded the tide to recede. The tide, as King Canute had told his subjects it would, continued to rise. But many of his subjects, instead of learning the lesson he had sought to teach them, persisted in the belief that their monarch might have stopped the tide if he had really wanted to do so. Once converted to a certain faith the multitude will cling to that faith, even in some instances if the object of that belief himself attempts to disillusion them. 

When a decision of the Judicial Division is not obeyed, it will then be necessary for the Disciplinary and Punitive Division to take prompt action. A flagrant, overt violation on the part of a faction or nation shall be met with drastic action on the part of the Disciplinary Division which shall be fully authorized to inflict punishment. This Division might be paralleled in some respects to the Police and Detective forces of a national government, when they are properly administered. It shall have a secret service, a number of aeroplanes, dirigibles and war vessels, a carefully selected volunteer army provided with the most modern means for warfare and a research department where 15

the production of poisonous gases and the means for neutralizing the effect of destructive gases may be studied. The research deparment may also conduct. experiments for the purpose of keeping the Division' well ahead of the nations in respect to the ways of destroying or saving human life. 

The contributions of the various nations to the general armaments, etc., as well as personnel, may be in proportion to the armaments and drilled personnel sustained by the government, some allowance being made to governments who are forced to keep up military organizations in their colonies or in such lands where they have a mandate.

Division C. Shall keep in close touch with the other divisions and with all national movements. 

Division D. Legal: This Division shall occupy itself with studying the laws and their results in the various countries.

Advantage should be taken of any opportunity to collaborate with any other division in the perfecting of existing laws and the formulating of new laws, as suggestions to the various nations.

When inquiring into the laws of the various countries, this division may be brought into intimate relationship with those who have to do with the making and execution of laws. They will thereby gain valua- 16

ble insight into national viewpoints, which would be of service to other divisions in clearing up misunderstandings that might develop. 

It shall also be within the province of the Legal Division to perfect fundamental laws for the ratification of the nations, which would serve as a basis of action for all the divisions. 

All laws shall be calculated to improve the indi vidual, and the interpretation of those laws by the Courts should also conform to that end. 

Division E. Enlightenment: This Division should occupy itself with public propaganda to undermine popular superstitions and as a substitute therefor inculcate the doctrine that all matters should receive logical consideration.

In cases where there is a very strong prejudice to overcome, it should impress upon those harboring such bias the necessity for examining their own ideas with the same critical spirit which they would apply to the ideas of others.

It shall be the aim of this division of the Intermediary Body to cause all schools, secular or sectarian, to teach that a fine character can exist under any of the principal religious faiths or among agnostics in any nation or race and that prejudice because of religious faith, race or nationality is harmful, sinful and unintelligent. 17

When any wave of wrong-doing manifests itself, the Enlightenment Body should present a logical criticism of the same so as to assist the people in arriving at a proper understanding.

It should study the best points of contact in order to influence people to abandon inconsistent and unsound ideas. Then by logical, lucid illustrations, it would be able either to win them over to proper views or at least to render their unsound opinions harmless.

Very few persons, if any, can claim that their minds always work rationally. A brilliant leader with a streak of insanity may create a somewhat delirious state of mind in a large community and cause a movement which a little later is regarded as an act of folly. 

Cotton Mather, who died only about two centuries ago, was a striking example of just such a person. He influenced the people of his community to condemn and hang many people for witchcraft. the right counteracting influence had been exerted among these people at the time, such atrocities would not have been committed.

The Enlightenment Division shall perform the function of explaining to the citizens of the various countries that nobility of character and uprightness are fundamental to national and individual happiness. 18

It should also show that efficiency, reasonable thrift and progress are of great help toward benefitting the world, and that the reverse of these three would mean retrogression and degeneration. 

Few people prefer to live like the Fuegian Indians, depending upon the mollusks that they can gather on the beach to sustain their lives, living under shelters made of brushes and under conditions that permit only the strongest to survive. The hobo and vagrant are also examples of ambitionless people. Among civilized nations the great majority prefer the comforts and pleasures of the better life, and laws should encourage this betterment.

In the pursuit of this work Division E will obtain much valuable data which should be communicated to the other divisions.

Division F. Efficiency: The Efficiency Division shall practically take the place of an executive officer. 

It shall follow up the work of all the divisions, stimulating them and suggesting improvements either in their organization or in the planning and handling of their work.

When it finds individual members who lack the required ability, the attention of the entire Intermediary Body should be called to their failings. There should be a common responsibility of the entire body for the 11 19

acts of its members, and it must, in an impartial way, institute proceedings for ridding the body of any members who are not a credit to it, because of their lack of ability, efficiency or honor. Any clannishness or working "hand-in-glove" for the promotion of individual interests should be promptly eradicated. 

Each member, individually, should contribute prestige to the Intermediary Body while enjoying the honor of membership.

This Intermediary Body should be supported by appropriations from the various nations. Needless to say the total cost would be only a fraction of what is expended yearly to keep up the armies and navies.

The various countries may reserve to themselves such rights as they have enjoyed up to the present time in relation to immigration, emigration, tariffs and such other understandings with nations as have been acquiesced in generally. It would be difficult to state here what the governments would wish to exempt from the jurisdiction of the Intermediary Body. 

Certainly no nation would wish a foreign country to send into its land a very large number of unassimilable people, or a class of people that would so lower the standard of living as to make life almost unendurable to those who had been accustomed to better conditions.

The Intermediary Body should possess the same right to extradite people for crimes committed against its laws as the United States would have should it wish to extradite a criminal from England; but with this distinction, however, that crimes committed against laws which the Intermediary Body is supposed to sustain throughout the civilized world should be considered as if committed on its territory and the perpetrators should be tried and punished according to its laws. The laws punishing such crimes however should first be acquiesced in by the nations subscribing to this Association.

It is obvious that members should not take part in any proceedings in which their country is directly involved.

Some nations may wish the privilege of withdrawing which might be permissable upon their giving three years' notice; but all their contributions, whether war material or money, must remain with the Association. No additional restrictions shall be placed upon any agents or representatives of the Intermediary Body established in a country before its withdrawal. 21

Any one with a knowledge of history, who grasps the spirit and meaning of this paper, must admit that if such an Intermediary Body had existed during the last century it would not only have prevented wars between nations but would have blotted out the memory of war, except as history of the barbarous actions of irrational peoples. 

This plan should be presented to all persons who could possibly be interested in war prevention, and especially to the fathers, mothers and friends of children. It should place upon all of this generation the responsibility for the discontinuance of wars. 

All well-known methods of informing the public and securing their support may be used. The final result would be that the votes of the people in all civilized countries would influence their respective governments to adopt the plan. 

The slogan would be "No More Wars", or a similar phrase which might prove more effective, but the slogan must be backed by an absolutely preventive plan.

This program is a reversal of the old order of international relationships. Whereas in the past the multitude has suffered and the war schemers have usually escaped suffering, it is proposed that the warschemers be punished and the multitude be spared. 11 22
