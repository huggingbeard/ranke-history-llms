CHAPTER ONE 

S she paused by the old clump of lilacs Alison felt a tremor of nervous excitement, a sensation between homesickness and stage-fright. "I'm running away from home," she thought, as if the fact had impressed her for the first time. Her young, slight figure concealed by the great bush, she stood looking at the lighted windows of the old house. She broke off some of the lilac plumes; they were firm and full and moist from a twilight shower. Alison had often crouched, hidden, in the very center of the stout branches and she had lain in its shade on days of sunshine, and sometimes cried herself to sleep when moonlight dappled the fragrant shelter. But there was little else she minded leaving. 

The bay window of the dining-room was uncurtained and without blinds, and it was partly open so that the babble of voices could be heard by any passer-by. 

The Pray family cheerfully lived its rampant life unshuttered; it would have been bored and fretted by privacy.

If its small world, the village of Jeremiah, Illinois, had not discussed every intimate detail of Pray activity the Prays would have been completely miserable. Like

youngsters skipping stones against placid waters, they eagerly watched every widening ripple. To-morrow they would run all over town, happy in excited, garbled announcements: "Alison has run away!" "Alison has joined up with a vaudeville team!" They would even contemplate her suicide-anything to make her absence dramatic. "What a family!" she thought. Yet honestly she knew that she was leaving like this, mysteriously and impulsively, because she was a Pray and could not bring herself to make sensible plans. They were incapable, anyhow, of discussing the matter sensibly. The clamor of questions and absurd arguments would have exhausted her, so that she might even, wearily, have remained.

They would have ripped up the house and their own wardrobes to make her weird, inappropriate clothes; they would have remembered outlandish relatives who would "look out for her" in Chicago; they would have given noisy parties and brazenly hinted for farewell gifts (later criticizing their monetary value). Her father, the Reverend Frederick Pray, would surely have referred in his morning sermon to "the sad heart of 

Ruth who stood in tears amid the alien corn." And all the Prays, sitting prominently in forward pews or choir benches, would flourish the dry handkerchiefs of feigned, noteworthy grief. Worse, they would have quarreled about her taking the puppy, Josephus, who with quiet faith was sitting in the cardboard carrier at her feet.

She had nursed him and reared him and named him after her father's latest favorite commentator on the Bible. She looked at her father now, feeling a detached interest in his mild, ascetic face. Yes, the thick volume of Josephus was open at his elbow. While he balanced a forkful of indifferently fried potatoes, he studied the paragraph marked by the sugar shell. He was unaware of the barbaric noise; having forgotten quietude, he could isolate himself in the midst of utmost confusion. 

He delivered long, heretic sermons, but his opinions were clothed in such studious language that scarcely anyone in Jeremiah suspected his views to be unorthodox. For fifteen years he had confided his religious waverings publicly. He had, in turn, been a transcendentalist, a Berkleyian idealist, a pragmatist, a Darwinian, a pagan, and even come dangerously near fancying himself a high priest, which would have ended his calling in the Methodist church. Fortunately, his voice was sweet and persuasive, his life saintly, and his sermons rhetorically involved. When, with a sincere throb in his tones he beseeched his followers to embrace the gospel of pragmatism, they crowded to the altar. He was especially noted for his evangelical work. He could sing like an angel, and he made the driest philosophy a golden creed. He converted everybody to everything that happened to come into his head and they were unaware of the theories to which they subscribed. "Preacher Pray," from being first a humorous label which tickled a pun-minded parish, became a sober

designation for the cloth. They were very proud of him in Jeremiah, and felt vastly benefited by his long words. 

It was rumored that big city churches wanted him, or that he could make millions as an evangelist. 

Once, Alison remembered, her father had come close to disaster. A new doctor, Vanderpool, came to practise in the village. He listened to the sermons with a quizzical smile increasing on his face. Later he called and said to the minister: "The study of biology has naturally prejudiced me in favor of the Darwin Theory." 

Frederick Pray's face lighted up; here was somebody with whom he could converse. But the new doctor touched him significantly on the arm and said as if they were conspirators: "Sh-sh! I understand. I won't give you away!" When he was gone, the minister was still puzzled and hurt.

Alison admired her father and he loved her deeply, perhaps, but they had never been close humanly. She remembered that once he had looked at her as if for the first time and said: "H'm! I do believe my little Alison has grown up. Yesterday you were an infant." Another time he observed: "That's a pretty string of beads you are wearing. You have excellent taste." He had given them to her for a birthday present. 

To-morrow he would probably say, on finding that she was gone, "Dear me, if one's cognizance of psychological motives equaled one's comprehension of physiological motion we would know where little Alison is this minute."

Her frivolous, improvident, absurd, unpredictable mother, Adella Montague Pray, was sitting at the foot of the table. It was she who had recently snatched the voile curtains from the bay window and now she was trying to fashion them into a costume for Frederika. 

Frederika, two years younger than Alison, was consciously beautiful. That was accepted. "Isn't Frederika beautiful?" Mrs. Pray started to demand when Fredi was two years old with curls like golden bells all over her fair head. "Isn't she marvelous! Isn't she graceful! 

Dance for the company, Fredi. Dance! Dance! Très bien! Oh, in my youth I was never happier than in the rhythm of the dance. You know I am French. The 

French are fond of dancing. Dear Frederika is exactly like me do you see the resemblance?"

While Mrs. Pray sewed, she managed to eat in a hasty, greedily slipshod manner. The fading ash-blonde hair flew out from her thin nervous face in disordered, curling-iron curls. Once she had been very pretty and she had retained the manner of a spoiled, pretty women who had been too busy to study her mirror since the age of twenty. It hadn't occurred to her, possibly, that in the busy neurotic years, she had changed from the fascinating Adella Montague, a slim Montreal girl who danced in floor shows.

Jeremiah, Illinois, had wavered at first about accepting her as the minister's wife. But she was so sentimentally devout and she made such dramatically heartfelt testimonials at Wednesday prayer meetings and sewed

so furiously for the Ladies' Aid and Missionary Societies, that gradually she had attained a secure, a powerful, position as arbiter of the desiccated little town. 

She was indefatigable. She taught all her children to dance, and she made dancing seem devout, unworldly. 

She arranged theatricals and staged them, directed them, herself. Her girls were, of course, thrust into spotlights on every occasion, and there was a bit of grumbling now and then, but the lethargic townspeople were helpless against electric personality.

She was not satisfied with Alison who was quick and brown and graceful but had the thin, strange, sad face of a gipsy child. Frederika, the blonde, conventional beauty, was the obvious heroine of the Pray's drama.

With both round elbows on the table she was leaning toward her mother and protesting loudly, "I won't have a back in that dress! If you put a back in it I'll tear it out." "Don't be ridiculous," said Mrs. Pray, stitching very fast. "Don't be outrageous," remarked Carter. "Give me a corn fritter," said Sammy-he was the youngest. Rather a nice, sensitive boy, Alison sometimes thought, if only he were away from the rest. Perhaps she should have taken him along with the puppy. Frederika absently tossed him a plump fritter which he deftly caught in his mouth. Mrs. Pray laughed and clapped her hands. "What a thing to do! What a thing to do! 

I do wish Amanda Tone had been here to see it."

"I can just see her face," mused Sammy, very pleased with himself. "Silly old parrot!"

Reverend Pray shook his head and remarked as a matter of course, "Goodness, what language." "It describes her exactly!" said his wife. "Throw me another," cried Sammy. "Another, Fredi! 

Another!" The syrup jug was upset and when Myrtle, the fat hired girl came in from the kitchen with a towel, she was slapped and poked and tickled. “I never in my born days," she said mournfully as always. Wisely she seldom left the kitchen. "Don't we have fun?" sighed Sammy, patting his stomach with satisfaction. 

Frederika looked all at once gloomy. "I wonder if we do. I wonder if we do." All of them had the habit of repeating their remarks, for the first was seldom heard. "Sometimes I wish we could have a decent meal like decent people-flowers and candles—" "And lobster and champagne," suggested Carter. "And finger bowls and soft music," Sammy added. "Dear me," said Mrs. Pray, dreamily, "how well I remember in Montreal at the old Bon Vivant restauranta gentleman called Louis de Gallé (or was it Gailli?) anyhow he took six of we young ladies there to a most luxurious supper and we had-" "You've told that a thousand times," Fredi interrupted. "Now look at that dress! Look at it! I ask you, can I dance in it?"

"Can you dance in anything?" asked Carter. "I can dance ten times better than Alison," said Frederika, which was not true. 

Outside, clenching a branch of lilac furiously, Alison heard herself muttering, "Shut up! Beastly little liar!"

Then apprehensively she crammed a small fist against her lips. But nobody had heard. "And a hundred times better than Rosalie," Fredi continued, her mouth full of bread pudding. "Where is Alison, anyhow?" asked Mrs. Pray. "Up in our room, fixing her costume." "She thinks she's so smart," said Sammy. "She wouldn't let anybody else make it." 

Alison's eyes stung with quick tears. She was fond of the fifteen-year-old, and always loyal when he got into scrapes. But he wasn't loyal-none of them was; her anger completely routed the former sense of homesickness. "She always tries to steal my stuff in the shows," said Frederika. "She always tries to. And she makes herself better costumes and hides them till the last minute. And 

I have to wear some horrid old voile curtains." That, at least, was true. Alison had a flair for design and she was vain of her slim, graceful body-complacent because she knew it would never become soft and plump and white like Frederika's. What if Fredi did have a face like apple blossoms? A body is a symbol of a self, far more than a face. It has more functions, more meanings, more adaptations. "A face," she thought, "is just the artist's

signature on his work. . . . And suppose I were in there," she reflected bitterly, "and made a remark like that. Nobody would answer sensibly. No real conversation has ever taken place at that table. I understand what 

Fredi means when she longs for a decent meal like you read about and see pictures of crystal goblets and roses and damask. . . . But she never tried to understand me, she's too damned mean."

But Frederika and her mother would be furious this evening when they got to the town hall for the dress rehearsal of the latest musical comedy which Mrs. Pray was directing. Then, and only then, would they miss

Alison. It was the largest production Jeremiah had ever attempted-one hundred from the locality in the cast. 

Such an insipid thing, called "Pearls and the Princess"!

Enchanting, really, to be free of such idiocy! Going away, going away, going away chanted against her ears like revolving train wheels. "Where's Rosalie?" she heard somebody ask. "We had a fight," Carter was explaining cheerfully. "She wanted me to take that job selling fire extinguishers, and I wouldn't. Can you tie it? Wanted me to take a job selling fire extinguishers!"

His dark, handsome face challenged the table for comment. He smoothed his black, curly hair and straightened his tie. "Why don't you take that million-dollar contract in 

Hollywood instead?" Sammy asked lazily. "Oh, let him be Mayor of New York," said Frederika.

"Don't discourage him. They wired him yesterday. Or, of course, there's that chance to be president of a steel corporation." 

Carter flushed, but managed to laugh airily.

Adella Pray rallied in defense of her favorite son. She could not help preferring the handsomest of her children, Frederika and Carter. Sammy was red-faced and tow-headed and bony. And he had been such an ugly baby that she didn't mind when her husband asked timidly to christen him in honor of old Samuel Pray, his aged father, a vicar in Staffordshire, England. Nor had she ever forgiven her oldest daughter, Corinne, for being drab and marrying a garage man called Gus.

Now she stamped her foot in its worn, high-heeled satin mule. "Nasty children! Nasty! I say it's outrageous to torment Carter when his wife is to have a baby shortly, and he doesn't want to sell fire extinguishers and he has a lovely chance of becoming an actor!" Carter and Rosalie had married when they were in high school and had lived under the parental roof for two years. Shy and unassuming as a chickadee, Rosalie had been naturally intimidated by the violent Prays. Now they talked her over as impersonally as if she were a chair in Carter's bedroom. "I think," Frederika said, "that she's neurotic. If she would get about and do things and quit worrying and take some exercise-I mean, get about. I shall always get about even if I have hundreds of babies." "I always got about," said her mother. "Didn't I,

Shouting derision everybody rushed in different directions; only the Reverend Pray sat for a moment still

reading. Then the lack of noise disturbed him. Startled he glanced up at the disordered table, closed his book with a replete sigh and walked slowly out of the room. 

Alison took up her small black suitcase and the container-so heavy with the puppy's 'fat trustful body-and hurried down the alley back of the old house. "Goodby!" she said aloud to the lilac bush, but only as a matter of politeness. The nostalgia was gone. She was free. 

Alison was alone in the dingy outer office when the large blonde girl came in. She was the same girl who had been at the employment agency yesterday, and now she had appeared, Alison knew, looking for the same job. Both had chosen an unusual time of day, for it was after five o'clock but the managing editor, Chester Kearney, was still at work within that closed inner office. 

After trying for four different places that afternoon, Alison had retained only frail, darting hopes. She looked into the mirror of her purse, then closed it quickly. She did not want to believe that her face really looked so tired, nor that her home-made hat was so eccentric. She tried to assume a radiant expression by widening her eyes and turning a false smile off and on. Then she noticed that the blonde girl was gazing at her with an expression at once supercilious and sulky.

The blonde wore a suit of green and brown plaid taffeta and she had three artificial gardenias pinned to her shoulder. Her strident perfume dominated the room like a calliope. But her new, brown straw hat Alison coveted; and it revealed extravagantly golden curls waved with authority and precision as if by an order of the Supreme Court. Her hard stare derided Alison's skimpy blue flannel suit, her thin gipsy face, and straight black hair lacking the benison of beauty parlors. 

Alison took a slip of paper from her purse and began reading it with eager, triumphant eyes as if it were a message of private good fortune. It was an old memorandum in her mother's dashing scrawl:

The blonde girl sniffed and stared coldly at the closed door. When it was suddenly flung open, both started nervously. 

A handsome man probably still in his thirties glanced from one to the other and said equivocally, “Come in, Miss Uh-"

The two rose and hurried toward the door. Just as they reached it, Alison felt the blonde's sharp elbow deliberately stabbing against her ribs. She had the wild delicious urge to battle, but she was so used to physical combat that she had no delusions about her strength. 

The other woman seemed gigantic and if she ever got in first-what a shameful defeat for Alison! "Come on, help me, God," she entreated wordlessly, involuntarily. 

Then, coöperating with divine aid, Alison darted beneath the blonde's strong, upraised arm and, in swift transit, reached as high as she could. Her fingers clutched the new brown straw hat and hurled it across the room. 

The blonde with a cry of rage saw the door slam. Alison was inside. She stood meekly, breathing hard, waiting for the handsome man to speak.

But he couldn't, at once, say anything. He leaned over the desk, holding his sides, laughing uncontrollably. "Well-" he began, once or twice, in the incoherence of mirth. "Well!... Lovely fight! . Referee, am I? ... Wish the job was worth it. . . ." Then governing his sense of humor, as a young executive must learn to govern it, he took the telephone and spoke to his secretary in an important voice: "Tell the young lady in the outer office not to wait." 

He turned to Alison. "How are your ribs?" "Fine," she said. "Except for one or two." She hated herself for trembling all at once from nervous excitement. His eyes were blue, worldly and appraising. She

wished she could look like a fascinating woman but knew her hat was not up to it. 

She continued in a crisp voice, her dark eyes fierce and challenging—almost as if she would fight him also, almost as if she were backed against the drab wall and were attacking him like a young, wild, but amusing, little animal. “You wanted somebody to file pictures. I can file pictures. The Universal Employment 

Agency sent me. I can start to-morrow." "How long have you been in Chicago?" he asked, all at once starting to make meaningless scribbles on a deskpad.

She flushed at his sure assumption that she was from out of town. "A week. I came from Jeremiah, Illinois. 

My father's a minister there. I haven't any references— 

I ran away. But I've had high school and one year in a business college. I'm twenty-one. I've got a room on 

North Dearborn about eight blocks above here. I can-"

She stopped, appalled at the blurted speech, so different from the dignified self-recommendation she had practised all day. Was it because she was tired and hungry? 

Or because she was so worried that her money wouldn't last much longer? "I am," she continued in a suddenly deep and impressive tone, "a competent business woman." She pressed her soft lips tightly together and folded her arms, trying to overpower him with the hypnotic dazzle of stern dark eyes. "Don't!" he said, waving one hand feebly, yielding

again to ungovernable laughter. “I begin to think you're a bandit. You frighten me, you terrify me. Take the job, take it, take it. Only don't scare me out of my wits." 

She answered with uncompromising severity. "Please be serious. What time do I start Monday? What salary?"

Wiping his eyes with a crisp monogrammed handkerchief he managed to reply: "Monday at nine. Fifteen a week." "Sixteen-fifty," said Alison. She did not realize that she scowled and looked as desperate and ridiculous as a baby pirate. She only knew, by rapid calculation, that she required the extra dollar and a half for feeding Josephus. 

Chester Kearney pretended to wheedle: "Let us off for fifteen seventy-five." "Sixteen-fifty," Alison repeated in a lower, grimmer voice. "Sixteen." "Sixteen-fifty."

He carefully lit a cigar, while she stood unflinching beside the desk. At last he said, with a charming pretense of vanquishment, "Oh, all right. But you'll have to work that much harder." 

She bowed stiffly. Her knees felt weak. "You look funny," he said. "Do you feel funny? 

You'd better sit down." 

Alison shook her head. "I'm going. I have an appointment. I'll be here Monday at nine. By the way, what is the name of your publication?"

He was genuinely wounded. "Do you mean to say you don't know? Captain Twinifort's Magazine for 

Boys? Two million circulation-! Captain Caleb Twinifort-?" "Sorry," said Alison, reluctant, but adamant in her honesty. "I really never heard of it. . . . Monday at nine, then. Thank you very much. Good-by."

She went out, trying to swagger like a corporal, although she longed to burst into tears. His final glance had held amusement and pity-and that silent charity she could scarcely bear.

But out in the street, looking back at the old, musty publishing house, her spirits lifted to the rhythm of an exciting refrain: "I've got a job! I've got a job! Monday at nine! Sixteen-fifty!" And it was all very mysterious.

Captain Twinifort's Magazine for Boys. She was to file pictures-but what pictures, and how would she do it, and why? But she had no fear of not doing it well. She had efficiently filed her father's extraordinary collection of letters and sermons and notes. True, he never was sure just how to find any of them, but he had been extremely pleased to know they were safely organizedas he remarked dreamily, "Beyond my poor power to add or detract." And he started a new, dusty hodgepodge to grow upon his desk.

As Alison unlocked the door, Mrs. Betelstone met her in the dim reception hall. Mrs. Evy Betelstone had a strange, even mad face, and she spoke in a voice which always beseeched the utmost secrecy. "Hello, my dear,” she whispered hoarsely, her pale eyes widening. "The strangest thing's happened. She come in, and there she is in your room. The big hall was the most elegant Alison had ever seen and she took infinite pleasure in it. 

The fireplace was of marble and there were many marble topped tables bearing great lamps and china dogs and gilt vases. The carpet was of deep crimson velvet like the stately portières which concealed the vast parlor that nobody had been allowed to use since Mr. 

Betelstone was "taken away." She said he wouldn't like it, for that was where he had lain in state. His hat still

Mrs. Betelstone. "You'd best go up and see to your friend. If she can't pay the money she'll have to go, bag and baggage.' "I'm girding up my loins," said Alison. She ran up the stairs and opened the heavy sliding doors which had no lock, except from the inside. Frederika was lying on the bed. She had already made a shambles of the ascetically neat hall-bedroom.
