THE TENURE OF THE PRESIDENCY To the People of the United States and Their Representatives in Congress:

I. 

Government is the instrument of the people, contrived for the good of the people. To understand and to achieve their own good, the people must be sufficiently educated. They must not be guided by their ignorance. "The happiest society is that in which the supreme power resides in the whole body of a well-informed people."

In the United States the supreme power happily resides in the whole body of the people, but a people even today not well informed. Illiteracy as to that which promotes the public good bulks large, and votes heavy. The illiterate can be, and are, beguiled. Under such circumstance much can be said for the steadiness, the firmness, and the prudent self-control of the limited monarchy. Under such circumstance, however, unless every reasonable safeguard be provided, there inevitably arises the spectre of unlimited monarchy, which is only the back side of rampant, likewise of decadent, democracy. 

After one hundred and fifty years of progress under the noblest Constitution of record, it is doubtful if the people of the United States would change from a republic to a monarchy-limited or unlimited. Doubtful, but well within the bounds of possibility. History records many occasions when peoples, at a time of socio-political upheaval, despairing of their ability to rule themselves, have called one man to rule them.

After a season of misgovernment, the Israelites of old demanded of their chief justice, Samuel, "Now make us a king to judge us like all the nations." And Samuel named them a king. At another time to commander Gideon they ap-

pealed, "Rule thou over us, both thou and thy son and thy son's son also." To his credit be it ever remembered, Gideon firmly refused. Caesar after being elected consul of the Roman republic, and later chosen by the people dictator for life, became the first of a span of emperors which continued five hundred years. Cromwell, the Protector, a man-if there ever was one "conspicuously born for sovereignty," looked on all legal defects in his title as wholly cured by the consent of the nation. "I called not myself to this place," he urged; "God and the people of these Kingdoms have borne testimony to it." 

His people elected Napoleon a consul, then within three years consul for life, and then, within two years, Emperor, by a vote of 32 million to 2½ thousand.

In our own time have occurred the one-man usurpations of absolute power over a hundred million people, by Leaders Hitler and Mussolini, who with full warrant could claim rulership by consent of their respective peoples. Were their peoples ill-informed, misled? They were. Could Americans possibly resemble, even imitate, such peoples? In substantial proportion we are their sons, daughters, cousins, grand-, and great-grandchildren, many of us raised under, used to, and still respecting dictatorships. The hazard is far too lively not to be feared. Even now "Letters to the Editor" express such sentiment as this: "The coming presidential election is not a matter that concerns America alone. The entire world waits its outcome. For we are no longer provincials. Fourth term or twenty-fourth, the head of our ship of state is a captain unequalled and must be reelected." 

In his "American Commonwealth," James Bryce writes, "The larger a community becomes the less does it seem to respect an assembly, the more is it attracted by an individual man. A bold President who knew himself to be supported by a majority in the country, might be tempted to over-ride the law, and deprive the minority of the protection which the law affords it. He might be a tyrant, not against the masses, but with the masses." Every chapter of history proves that a tyrant is a tyrant, whether called by the people or by himself. 2

II. 

Utterances of our statesmen confirm the possibility of usurpations in this country, in spite of our reverence for the 

Constitution. Washington in his farewell address, warning against the dangers of party and faction, said, "This leads at length to a more formal and permanent despotism. The disorders and miseries which result gradually incline the minds of men to seek security and repose in the absolute power of an individual, and sooner or later the chief of some prevailing faction * turns this disposition to the purposes of his own elevation on the ruins of public liberty. Without looking forward to an extremity of this kind (which nevertheless ought not to be entirely out of sight), the common and continual mischiefs of the spirit of party are sufficient to make it the interest and duty of a wise people to discourage and restrain it.”—How France must regret not having heeded such counsel during the past quarter-century! America, take warning!

Again, cautioning against the use of its powers by one branch of the government to encroach upon the sphere of another, Washington said, "The spirit of encroachment tends to consolidate the powers of all the departments in one, and thus to create, whatever the form of government, a real despotism. A just estimate of that love of power and proneness to abuse it which predominates in the human heart is sufficient to satisfy us of the truth of this position. * If in the opinion of the people the distribution * of the constitutional powers be in any particular wrong, let it be corrected by an amendment in the way which the Constitution designates. 

But let there be no change by usurpation; for though this in one instance may be the instrument of good, it is the customary weapon by which free governments are destroyed. The precedent must always greatly overbalance in permanent evil any partial or transient benefit.'

The possibility of usurpation was contemplated by Andrew Jackson when he said, in his second message to Congress, 765464 3

"It was a leading object with the framers of the Constitution to keep as separate as possible the action of the legislative and executive branches of the Government. To secure this object nothing is more essential than to preserve the former from all temptations of private interest, and therefore so to direct the patronage of the latter as not to permit such temptations to be offered. Experience abundantly demonstrates that every precaution in this respect is a valuable safeguard of liberty." 

William Henry Harrison feared usurpation. In his inaugural address he said, As long as the love of power is a dominant passion of the human bosom, and as long as the understandings of men can be warped and their affections changed by operations upon their passions and prejudices, so long will the liberties of a people depend on their own constant attention to its preservation. The danger to all well-established free governments arises from the unwillingness of the people to believe in its existence or from the influence of designing men diverting their attention from the quarter whence it approaches to a source from which it can This is the old trick of those who would usurp the government of their country. In the name of democracy they speak, warning the people against the influence of wealth and the danger of aristocracy. History, ancient and modern, is full of such examples. * There is, on the contrary, no instance on record of an extensive and well-established republic being changed into an aristocracy. The tendencies of all such governments in their decline is to monarchy, and the antagonist principle to liberty there is the spirit of faction * in times of great excitement imposes itself upon the people as the genuine spirit of freedom. never come. * which It is in periods like this that it behooves the people to be most watchful of those to whom they have entrusted power. 

The reign of an intolerant spirit of party amongst a free people seldom fails to result in a dangerous accession to the executive power introduced and established amidst unusual professions of devotion to democracy."

That danger to our republican form of government is ever present was also the conviction of Andrew Johnson, who in his first annual message quoted Washington as above, and in his third said, "The Union and the Constitution are inseparable. * ** If one is destroyed, both must perish together.

The destruction of the Constitution will be followed by other and still greater calamities."

Battles are won against despotism, but the campaign must go on forever. Lurking over a nation in time of misgovernment, oppression, distress, war, is always the danger of its people's embracing a protector, a dictator, a "savior"-with power to abolish the old and establish "new and juster" laws. 

Only a man may qualify for 'savior'. Most people understand, will trust, some one man before any council, committee or parliament of men. One name influences more people than ten reasons. What most individuals will do at some time or other, a whole people-even the American people-may do under incitement. And it need not be the whole people. Morley in his Cromwell truthfully observes, "Nothing in all the known world of politics is so intractable as a band of zealots, conscious that they are in a minority, yet armed by accident with the powers of a majority." A group of determined men, who see their course with the terrible simplicity of the fanatic, can be more than a match for millions of muddled Americans. 

In consequence of the absolute power so recently conferred upon two men by their respective peoples, millions have suffered and died, and for a generation millions will suffer and prematurely die. Against even a shadow of the 'savior' delusion, let the people of this nation neglect no safeguard which in years to come may spare them such obloquy and guilt as down through history must be borne by the peoples of 

Hitler and Mussolini.

III. 

"A good government, like a good coat, fits the body for which it is designed." The Constitution of the United

States was designed for a people suited to the republican form of government. For other peoples, other designs-but for this people a republic now and always! A coat upon occasion may need alteration, a body may need discipline. Observable today is a tendency in our people to sag in their respect for the Constitution, to prefer personal gain before public welfare, security before liberty. That tendency must be disciplined. 

And to preclude its ever being exploited, to diminish any lingering possibility of one man's usurping absolute power over this nation, to forestall the remotest likelihood that the people temporarily deluded would elect a dictator, in short, to prolong the life of this republic through wars and depressions to come, a strengthening of the Constitution is advisable. 

Duration in office, wrote Hamilton, a monarchist at heart, is one of the four essential "ingredients which constitute energy in the Executive." To bolster his statement that "energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government," he cites but one, and that a damning, example. "Every man the least conversant in Roman story, knows how often that republic was obliged to take refuge in the absolute power of a single man, under the formidable title of Dictator, as well against the intrigues of ambitious individuals who aspired to the tyranny, and the seditions of whole classes of the community whose conduct threatened the existence of all government, as against the invasions of external enemies who menaced * Rome."

While Hamilton stressed energy as a qualification of the 

Executive, three others are more important. The Executive must be upright, able, and, above all, responsive to the will of the people as expressed in their Constitution and their laws. 

The citizen may not elect which laws he will obey, nor the Executive which he will enforce. To secure an Executive who embodies those attributes, and to discourage in an elected Executive their deterioration, a definite limitation upon his tenure of office would be conspicuously helpful. 

Hamilton devoted two issues of the Federalist to advocating the presidential term of four years with no limit on re-

eligibility as the Constitution now provides. He, however, contemplated election of the Executive not directly, but by representatives of the people-election "by the wise and the good," not by the bad, the indifferent and the good. Such likewise was originally the intent of the Constitution. Had he possessed the foresight of Jefferson, Hamilton still would have supported the Constitution as offered by the Convention. 

But he might likely have tempered certain of his arguments with reference to duration in the office of President. 

IV. 

In the light of a century-and-a-half's experience, a simple statement of Hamilton's argument for unlimited reeligibility is sufficient in the main to refute it. Non-reeligibility, he said, would diminish the inducement to good behavior. There are few men, knowing that the advantages of their office must terminate at a definite time, who would not feel much less zeal in the discharge of duty than when they might hope, by meriting the office, to continue in it. "Even the love of fame, the ruling passion of the noblest minds," would deter a man from undertaking extensive and arduous enterprises for the public benefit unless he could finish the work before he quitted the scene. The most to be expected of most men in such case is the negative merit of not doing harm, instead of the positive of doing good.

Another ill effect of non-reeligibility would be the temptation to sordid views, to peculation, and, in some instances, to usurpation. An avaricious man, looking toward the time of his replacement, would feel a propensity to make the best of his opportunity while it lasted and might not scruple "to make the harvest as abundant as it was transitory; though the same man, probably, with a different prospect before him, might content himself with the regular perquisites of his situation, and be unwilling to risk the consequences of an abuse of his opportunities. His avarice might be a guard upon his avarice." In simpler language, Hamilton is saying, if you have a thief in your service, why exchange

him for another and perhaps a hungrier one? As to usurpation, he continues, an ambitious man, "seated on the summit of his country's honors," would be much more violently tempted at every personal hazard to embrace a favorable opportunity to prolong his power, "than if he had the probability of answering the same end by doing his duty.' 

Hamilton seriously gives as a reason for restricting the production of Presidents, the inadvisability of having the country all cluttered up with Ex-Presidents. He asks, "Would it promote the peace of the community, or the stability of the government to have half a dozen men who had had credit enough to be raised to the seat of the supreme magistracy, wandering among the people like discontented ghosts, and sighing for a place which they were destined never more to possess?" V.

A third ill effect of exclusion from reelection would be to deprive the country of the advantages of experience, and a fourth, to exclude from the office men who, in certain emergencies, might be of the greatest service to the State. "There is no nation which has not, at one period or another, experienced an absolute necessity of the services of particular men in particular situations; perhaps it would not be too strong to say, to the preservation of its political existence. ***

At the breaking out of a war, or at any similar crisis," the exchange of the chief magistrate for another of equal merit, would be detrimental to the community, would substitute inexperience for experience and tend to disturb "the already settled train of the administration." But milk and meat cannot be had from the same cow. To advance that argument, Hamilton must have forgotten that a crisis, real or pretended, always affords the ambitious executive his very best opportunity to exercise that ever-to-be-feared predilection toward usurpation. Here the argument must be joined.

Hamilton's statement that every nation upon some occasion or other has found certain men absolutely indispensable

in certain situations may contain a measure of truth. But it does not say and cannot mean that every nation, or any nation, has ever found a single man indispensable to its welfare. 

Caesar, Gustavus Adolphus, Hampden, Lincoln, were killed, but their states lived on. Has any nation which deserved to live ever died for want of a man? The truly great Executive recognizes it as a paramount obligation to his people to train and permit to be trained other men to administer as well as himself. No possibility of life-time incumbency should be allowed to encourage an executive to neglect such duty, or to perform it by grooming a son or a favorite for the succession. 

When a people finds one man indispensable to its government, it has lost the liberty to think, speak and act. "There are, perhaps," said Andrew Jackson, in his First

Annual Message to Congress, "few men who can for any great length of time enjoy office and power without being more or less under the influence of feelings unfavorable to the faithful discharge of their public duties. Their integrity may be proof against improper considerations immediately addressed to themselves, but they are apt to acquire a habit of looking with indifference upon the public interests and of tolerating conduct from which an unpracticed man would revolt. Office is considered as a species of property, and government rather as a means of promoting individual interests than as an instrument created solely for the service of the people. Corruption in some and in others a perversion of correct feelings and principles divert government from its legitimate ends and make it an engine for the support of the few at the expense of the many. ** I cannot but believe that more is lost by the long continuance of men in office than is generally to be gained by their experience." 

Franklin D. Roosevelt before he became President said, 66# * * the fate of America cannot depend on any one man.

The greatness of America is grounded in principles and not on any single personality." Again, "Were it possible to find 'master minds' so unselfish, so willing to decide unhesitatingly against their own personal interests or private prejudices, men

almost godlike in their ability to hold the scales of Justice with an even hand, such a government might be to the interest of the country, but there are none such on our political horizon, and we cannot expect a complete reversal of all the teachings of history."

Hamilton's final argument against non-reeligibility was that it would operate to interdict stability of administration. 

The new broom would have a mandate for a clean sweep. 

There would be a change of measures, of policies, of experienced subordinates. "These causes together could not fail to occasion a disgraceful and ruinous mutability in the administration of the government." Were Hamilton alive today, he could look back upon few new administrations which suffered appreciably by the replacement of experienced men, and he could observe one which, in spite of the record incumbency of its Chief Magistrate, has replaced important subordinate officers with an unprecedented frequency. An effective civil service system, which Hamilton did not contemplate, now assists in stabilizing the sub-machinery of government.

In its first draft of the Constitution, the Convention provided a single seven-year term for the Executive. Hamilton advocated a life-term. Therefore, his argument in the Federalist for the four-year term with unlimited eligibility supported a compromise which he hoped would provide all he desired. Fortunately for the republic, the early precedents of 

Washington, Jefferson, Madison and Monroe have helped to postpone a fulfillment of that desire.

This reply to his argument must not detract one iota from the full credit due Hamilton for his magnificent work in promoting adoption of the Constitution. Perhaps it is merely that common hindsight is better than brilliant foresight. 

VI. 

On the subject of eligibility for reelection to the Presidency, the thought expressed by our statesmen who have 10

lived under the Constitution is in order. Washington, without disapproving of the practice, refused a third term and set the precedent which the nation followed for a century and a half. Jefferson, the second President to serve two terms, was petitioned by the legislatures of eight states to seek a third. His reply to Vermont's (the first) invitation is classic. "That I should lay down my charge at a proper period is as much a duty as to have borne it faithfully. If some termination to the services of the Chief Magistrate be not fixed by the Constitution or supplied by practice, his office, normally for four years, will in fact become for life; and history shows how easily that degenerates into an inheritance. 

Believing that a representative government, responsible at short periods of election, is that which produces the greatest sum of happiness to mankind, I feel it a duty to do no act which shall essentially impair that principle; and I should unwillingly be the first person who, disregarding the sound precedent set by an illustrious predecessor, should furnish the first example of prolongation beyond the second term of office." 

Andrew Jackson in his first annual message to Congress suggested an amendment to the Constitution affecting the manner of election of the President (never passed but accomplished under the present party system), and he added, "In connection with such an amendment it would seem advisable to limit the service of the Chief Magistrate to a single term of either four or six years. 

His second message said, "I renew the recommendation with an increased confidence that its adoption will strengthen those checks by which the Constitution designed to secure the independence of each department of the Government and promote the healthful and equitable administration of all the trusts which it has created. 

The agent most likely to contravene this design of the Constitution is the Chief Magistrate. * In order that he may approach the solemn responsibilities of the highest office in the gift of a free people uncommitted to any other 11

course than the strict line of constitutional duty, and that the securities for this independence may be rendered as strong as the nature of power and the weakness of its possessor will admit, I cannot too earnestly invite your attention to the propriety of promoting such an amendment of the Constitution as will render him ineligible after one term of service.' 

In his third, fourth and fifth messages he repeated the recommendation and in the sixth Jackson said, "Could * the terms of those officers (President and Vice President) be limited to a single period of either four or six years, I think our liberties would possess an additional safeguard." 

He stood by this conviction through his eight years of service. 

William Henry Harrison in his inaugural address had much to say about the Chief Magistracy and its tenure. To permit eligibility of the same individual to a second term he said was a defect in the Constitution, which error of the sages may be the source of bitter fruits we are still to gather, if it continues to disfigure our system. 

Republics can commit no greater error than to adopt or continue any feature in their systems of government which may be calculated to create or increase the love of power in the bosoms of those to whom necessity obliges them to commit the management of their affairs; and surely nothing is more likely to produce such a state of mind than the long continuance of an office of high trust. Nothing can be more corrupting, nothing more destructive of all those noble feelings which belong to the character of a devoted republican patriot. When this corrupting passion once takes possession of the human mind, like the love of gold it becomes insatiable. It grows with his growth and strengthens with the declining years of its victim.

It is the part of wisdom for a republic to limit the service of that officer to whom she has entrusted the management of her foreign relations, the execution of her laws, and the command of her armies and navies to a period so short as to prevent his forgetting that he is the accountable agent, not the principal; the servant, not the master." * * * 1 12

James Buchanan said, "The example of Washington, followed by Jefferson, Madison and Monroe, has forever determined that no President shall be more than once reelected. This principle is now become as sacred as if it were written in the Constitution." we

In a special message to Congress, Andrew Johnson proposed an amendment which included fixing the term of President and Vice President at six years and forbidding reelection. In his inaugural address Rutherford B. Hayes made the same recommendation. Grover Cleveland said, “When we consider the patronage of this great office recognize in the eligibility of the President for reelection the most serious danger to that calm, deliberate and intelligent action which must characterize a government by the people." 

After leaving office, Benjamin Harrison wrote, "Ineligibility to a second term will give to the Executive action greater independence." John Sherman said that to disregard the two-term rule "would be criminal". William McKinley merely stated he would not accept nomination for a third term if it were offered. The first Roosevelt said, "Wise * * * limits the President to two terms. custom * It is not well that a strong Executive should be a perpetual Executive.' After he had served a term, William Howard 

Taft wrote that he was strongly inclined to think it wiser to provide for the Executive a six- or a seven-year term without reelection. George W. Norris opposed the third term until late in life. At one time he wrote, "When this influence (patronage) is combined and used for the perpetuation in office of the head of our government, it is a danger to free institutions and strikes at the very root of democracy. Such a danger takes away from the people themselves the right to select their own Chief Magistrate. (The danger) exists now to a greater extent than it did because federal offices have been multiplied many fold and partisan political machines are now enthroned in power greater than ** * ever in our history.' 13

VII. 

To this incomplete review of the opinions of our statesmen it should be added that the constitution of the Confederate States of America contained a provision limiting executive tenure to six years. At least 150 resolutions have been introduced in Congress to limit executive tenure in one way or another and in several instances these have been adopted in one branch of Congress, three times by large majorities. The Democratic Platform of 1896 contained this plank: "We declare it to be the unwritten law of this Republic, established by custom and usage of one hundred years, and sanctioned by the examples of the greatest and wisest of those who founded and have maintained our Government, that no man should be eligible for a third term of the Presidential office." The 1912 platform of the Democratic Party, which elected its candidate, contained the following plank (later honored in the breach): "We favor a single presidential term and to that end urge the adoption of an amendment to the Constitution making the President of the United States ineligible to reelection, and we pledge the candidates of this convention to this principle."

It must be admitted that the pronouncements of many of our statesmen on this century-old subject have been belied by their actions. Jackson, in spite of his constant effort for a one-term limit, accepted a second term. Cleveland was favorably disposed to a third-term invitation. The first 

Roosevelt accepted such an invitation. Wilson repudiated the pledge of his party. George W. Norris supported a thirdterm candidate. "Many of our statesmen most distinguished for talent and patriotism," said William Henry Harrison, "have been at one time or other of their political career on both sides of each of the most warmly disputed questions." 

This trait is still common to many. When a man's personal interest or position changes, his ideas, if not his avowal of them, are apt to change. What stronger argument can be made for a limit upon the incumbency of one man in the most powerful office of the Republic? 14

VIII. 

The tenure of the Presidency of the United States should be (1) for four years, (2) with eligibility to reelection (3) limited to one additional term. That this was the established tradition of our nation until recently broken by the people and a President is not proof of its soundness. Precedents have been known to outlive their usefulness. Here, however, was a tradition, expressly endorsed by many statesmen and openly opposed by few, the fundamental wisdom of which a further examination will substantiate. 

The honor and perquisites associated with the Presidency are sufficient to attract an abundance of candidates were the term limited to one year, but probably not men best fitted in character and ability for the high honor and the arduous office. The public good requires a reasonable stability in government in view of the time necessary to plan, undertake and accomplish many enterprises of public benefit. It requires stability for the successful prosecution of private enterprise. Time is required for the people to determine their own sentiment as to political questions and to judge the worth of public plans, their execution and the ability of their executive. The most upright, able and energetic statesmen might not be attracted to the Chief Magistracy for a short term. In fact such men would hardly flourish in à permanent turmoil of frequent election. Neither would the people. The four-year term is a reasonable minimum for obtaining ability and stability in the government, and a reasonable maximum to be allowed an administration before seeking anew the instructions of the people. The governors of more than half the states are elected for four-year terms and no state provides a longer term. 

The Executive should be eligible for a second term solely that his talents and virtues may be prolonged in the service of the people, if they approve, in order further to promote that stability so essential to good government. Eligibility for a second term may encourage the newly-elected Execu- ? but not the people 15

tive to give of his best in the hope of added honor and honorarium. However, if eligible for a second term, he may be tempted during his first to compromise with conscience and temper his independence with an eye to reelection. If ineligible, he might yield to the very same temptation with an eye to later preferment in private life by interests he might favor during his incumbency. Since it can hardly be determined whether his independence would be more promoted or impaired by either of these arrangements, little weight attaches to the argument that the prospect of a second term encourages an incumbent to worthier deeds. 

While the public good requires a reasonable stability in government, it likewise requires a reasonable animation, versatility, mobility-a periodic infusion of new blood. 

Government must not become "too set in its ways.' Above all, however, the public good and the public safety require that no Executive be granted that essential element of time in which to become an ambitious idol of the people, upset the republic, and build a throne upon its ruins. Napoleon did just that in five years. Recent repetitions of this feat begrim the memory of millions. Who now will say, "It cannot happen here"? By constitutional amendment eligibility for reelection to the Presidency must be limited to one additional term. IX.

Before enumerating the possible and probable-means of attempting usurpation, against which limited eligibility of the Chief Executive would act as a safeguard, two other quite sufficient reasons for the proposed amendment render its adoption advisable. No form of government is so subject to internal agitation, to civil wars, as the democratic or popular, because none tends so strongly and so constantly to change. 

None, therefore, demands greater vigilance and courage on the part of its citizens if it is to avoid a change in form. None demands a more plentiful supply of serious and devoted leaders. Sad the circumstance of a nation which can name 16

but one, two or perhaps three men capable of administering its government acceptably. A score of citizens should be commonly recognized as possessing the ability to lead. 

Where the possibility of achievement is relatively slight, able men will not be encouraged to an undertaking, public or private. Where the reward is great in honor and fortune, and the chances of achievement reasonably good, intelligent men of character will always be attracted. That competent leaders and statesmen in abundance may be discovered, encouraged and trained in the ways of administration, it is important that rotation in high office be reasonably frequent.

A reasonable rotation in Executive office likewise promotes a responsiveness to the will of the people, the reasoned will of the people, as expressed in their constitution, by their laws and in their political platforms. The people "commonly intend the public good," but they do not always reason rightly about the means of promoting it. They know they sometimes err. Upon such occasion it is the duty of the Executive courageously, and enduring their temporary displeasure, to guard the people's interest while time is given them for cool and careful reflection. An Executive whose tenure is limited can better afford to base his claim to lasting esteem upon sound but temporarily unpopular action, than can one whose tenure is indefinite and who therefore, by avoiding any displeasure of the people, might expect their continued suffrage. 

Give an Executive time, and the inclination; let him be surrounded, as inevitably he will be, by subordinates whose jobs and courtiers whose day-in-the-light depend upon the persistence of his power; then may he be reluctant, if it conflicts with his personal interests, to obey the reasoned will of the people. Then may he be loyal to party, rather than to people. Then may the people rest easily if a known limit upon his incumbency has been thoughtfully and formally established beforehand. A reasonable rotation in office affords us more and better Presidents. 17

X. 

That it will provide an additional safeguard against usurpation of the supreme power is the strongest reason why a limit upon eligibility to reelection of the Executive is urgently needed. When the founding fathers framed the 

Constitution, when Hamilton praised it in the Federalist, when Washington in his Farewell Address warned that the possibility of usurpation by the Executive "ought not to be entirely out of sight," when Jefferson advocated a two-term and Jackson a single-term limit as "an additional safeguard of our liberty," none of them contemplated the kind of popular election or the organized party system which today prevail in the United States. Were they alive today, think you, would they be less, or more, fearful of a Cromwell, a 

George the Third, a Napoleon? 

"Despotism, which is at all times dangerous, is more particularly to be feared in democratic ages. 

It is now entirely possible for a skillful intriguer in the Executive office, by adroit cooperation with local political organizations and the shrewd use of an unparalleled power of patronage, to control the nomination for and the election to the Presidency of himself or his chosen successor. It is entirely possible, while giving lip-service to the visible democratic precepts of our forefathers, to change this nation into a single-party state under the domination of an all-powerful leader, protector, commander. Such a change was apprehended by Woodrow Wilson when he wrote, "It is intolerable that any President should be permitted to determine who shall succeed him-himself or another-by patronage or coercion, or by any sort of control of the machinery by which delegates to the nominating convention are chosen." Such a change is as remote from the contemplation of our citizenry today as was the popular election of Presidents from the mind of our first President. It is no less a probability, however. 

The likelihood of its occurrence is enhanced by reason of the fact that education in the fundamentals of democracy and willingness to fight for liberty are not as prevalent as prior 18

to the War between the States and the years of our great immigration.

The multitude will never be philosophers. An extensive enlightenment and education of the electorate, however, is clearly a possibility and an objective devoutly desired. 

Science, by inventing the radio, the motion picture and the airplane, has quickened the day of its achievement. But what a tool, inconceivable to the framers of the Constitution, has science forged for the hand of an eloquent Executive whose inclination lies toward rulership! In a day, with his own voice he can talk to every man, woman and child in the nation. 

He can by-pass the admirals and the generals and speak to every private in the armed forces anywhere in the world. 

Within a week, by motion picture and airplane he can appear before them in person.

But it is through the use of these and other tools for purposes of indoctrination that the first danger lies. Dominating his party, one man educated the youth of Germany, then drove his nation to destruction. Education of the people of this nation must never be committed to the hands of one man, one party or one government. "Ghosts" and propagandists are not good educators. The people will be soundly educated only if that matter be left in their own hands.

Hamilton truly observed, "The history of human conduct does not warrant that exalted opinion of human virtue which would make it wise in a nation to commit interests of so delicate and momentous a kind, as those which concern its intercourse with the rest of the world, to the sole disposal of a magistrate created and circumstanced as would be a President of the United States." States armed cruisers four years ago was by Executive agreement with no confirmation by the Senate. In the same way an important oil compact affecting a great area of the Orient was recently made between Great Britain and the

Under modern domestic and world conditions it is difficult to comprehend how the multiplicity of foreign relations could be handled except by the Executive or some officer with power to act. But this power is one abuse of which is easy and tempting. It may seem absurd, even scurrilous, for a writer who looks back upon a succession of Executives of unimpeachable integrity to suggest that a President might employ this power to promote his personal fortunes. Let 

Hamilton, who could look only toward future Presidents, state the argument. "A man raised from the station of a private citizen to the rank of Chief Magistrate, possessed of a moderate or slender fortune, and looking forward to a period not very remote when he may return to the * * 

An station from which he was taken, might sometimes be under temptations to sacrifice his duty to his interest, which it would require superlative virtue to withstand. ambitious man might make his own aggrandisement, by the aid of a foreign power, the price of his treachery to his constituents."

A greater danger arising from the power of the Executive alone to deal with foreign nations lies in the facility for creating a crisis, even a war with or without the declaration of the Congress,-events of superlative advantage to an Executive bent upon usurpation. This danger becomes the more acute as we abandon the long-accepted principle that a strict construction of the powers granted by the Constitution is the only sound and safe theory of the Constitution. XII. 

Under the Constitution "The President shall be Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States, and of the Militia of the several States, when called into the actual Service of the United States." This places a helpful tool in the hands of a monarchy-minded Executive. Cromwell seized supreme power by employing the army. But the army is not a necessary tool. Hitler did not control the army. 

Using the schools, the radio, the press, he built his own military force and "Gestapo" outside the army, and then seized the army along with the dictatorship. An Executive of the United States would labor under no such handicap. In 

1940, testifying before the Senate Judiciary Committee, Jacob Gould Schurman, former Ambassador to Germany, said, suppose our country at war and the Chief Executive then in supreme command of a great army. "Let us assume also that, whether in his first or second Presidential term, he still had behind him a victorious political party which he continued to lead with ability, skill and sagacity. The question is, whether it would be possible for a Chief Executive under such auspices, in the midst of war, to subvert by means of force if necessary our constitutional republic and to set up in Washington a dictatorship analogous to that of 

Caesar's in Rome.

I think the possibility of such a consummation is not excluded. * In any event our republic should never be exposed even to the slightest possibility of such a peril and disaster." 

XIII. 

The Congress of the United States is the branch of government most closely identified with the liberties of the people, most responsive to their will, and most to be relied upon for 21

their protection against usurpation of the supreme, power.

Yet our statesmen from the beginning have warned the Congress against encroachments on their power by the Executive. 

Such encroachments have occurred. A type of law wholly new to the nation, a type not contemplated by the foundersnot statute but Administrative law-pronunciamento by the Executive, is binding upon us today. Congress, a constantly changing body of men, has not over the years had the singleness of purpose or the consistent leadership to develop its function efficiently in line with multiplying problems, or to resist encroachment. It remains enmeshed in rules and procedures of a generation past. Profiting by the lack of a constitutional definition of "legislation" and the inability of Congress to cope with the legislative problems of today, the easy and tempting road for the Executive is to enact his own legislation as far as he can, rather than to assist in the reorganization of Congress, and to insist on its performing its whole duty. This encroachment perhaps cannot aid an Executive greatly in seizure of supreme power, but it does invade the field of Congress, it does weaken the faith of the people in Congress, and it does disturb that balance of power contemplated by the Constitution and admitted on all hands to be essential to the preservation of liberty. It is the urgent duty of Congress to revise its rules and procedures, adopt modern methods, and regain the full confidence of the people. 

The Federal judicial bench numbers about 305 seats. The current incumbent of the Presidency, now rounding out his twelfth year, has appointed 296 judges who fill or have filled about 200 seats. He has made appointments to about 68% of the total number of seats (78% of the Supreme Court). It easily lies within the power of a long-time President, through his appointments, to impair the independence of the Federal Judiciary, to disturb the balance of power contemplated by the Constitution, and to strengthen the power of the Executive over both the Legislative and the Judicial departments. 

The Executive dispenses the honors and holds the sword of the community. The Legislature commands the purse and 22

prescribes the duties and rights of citizens. The Judiciary influences neither the sword nor the purse. It cannot make or execute laws. It can merely judge. It is the weakest of the three departments of power but it is vitally necessary to liberty. Still further to weaken the Judiciary, to subjugate it to and finally to merge it with the Executive Department would mean the end of liberty, the beginning of despotism. 

All possible steps should be taken to assure the Judiciary's remaining wholly independent of the Legislative and especially of the Executive departments. The limitation upon eligibility for reelection to the Presidency will contribute appreciably toward that assurance.

XIV. 

Emperors and kings are obsolete. But unless history has changed, peoples will still hanker for a man, style him what you will, to rule over them. To have the answer decreed is so easy as compared with solving a problem. During great tribulation the danger of dictatorship mounts. Out of great tribulation, leaders, true and false, arise. Then,-THEN is the time to be constant on guard. The man most dangerous to free institutions has ever been the most popular of men. 

In a recent letter to a New York editor, I read, "Our American election is not just a domestic event. It involves our relations with the whole world. To the peoples of Europe and 

Asia, Roosevelt means the United States. He is more than a man and a President; he is to the outside world the symbol of this nation. To remove this symbol and provide them with an unknown name is to destroy their faith in this country and what it stands for."

Only by arduous effort is it possible to write with moderation concerning such a sentiment which is today widespread. 

This Republic, founded by Washington and preserved by Lincoln, has one of the oldest governments in the world and a glorious history of accomplishment to its credit. If it will 23

lose the confidence and the understanding of the rest of the world unless one and only one mortal man be its symbol, the intelligence of our people and of foreign peoples has been vastly over-rated, and our history but a lucky turn of fortune. "A people may prefer free government, wrote John 

Stuart Mill, "but if from indolence, or carelessness, or cowardice, or want of public spirit, they are unequal to the exertions necessary for preserving it; if they will not fight for it when it is directly attacked; if they can be deluded by the artifices used to cheat them out of it; if by momentary discouragement or temporary panic, or a fit of enthusiasm for an individual, they can be induced to lay their liberties at the feet of even a great man, or trust him with powers which enable him to subvert their institutions-in all these cases they are more or less unfit for liberty; and though it may be good for them to have had it even for a short time, they are unlikely long to enjoy it." The United States was and is dedicated to the repudiation of absolute power in government or in one man. It is devoted to the belief that a dangerous freedom outrivals a servile security. It is still one spot where government of the people, by the people and for the people has not yet perished from the earth. These are traditions we shall fight to keep unbroken.

While the unique circumstance with regard to the possible reelection of a President for a fourth four-year term is admittedly the inspiration of these writings, I accuse no man of contemplating usurpation of the government. I do sound a grave warning for the future. Unless its repetition be prohibited by constitutional amendment, the precedent established in 1940 will menace the liberty of this republic as long as it stands. And if, upon foundations laid by Executives past, present or future, some later President builds himself an empire, the names of such contributors to our lost liberty must be joined with that of the dictator for the final appraisal of history. 24
