CHAPTER I 

Wall Street

ALL STREET is barely more than a third of a mile in length, but its influence extends from one end of the country to the other, and into the furthermost corners of the world as well. 

Forty feet wide at its widest point, this canyon could be paved and walled with solid gold and yet use only a fraction of the wealth in which it has dealt during the century and a half of its financial history.

Wall Street derives its name from the fact that it was the site of a wall that was built in 1652 by Peter Stuyvesant, following the line of a fence put up by Governor Kieft, Stuyvesant's predecessor, to keep cattle from straying. The wall is gone, and Wall Street now often wishes it had something along that line to restrain some of its obstreperous bulls and bears.

The first prominent resident of the street was 

Captain Kidd, the pirate. He lived too soon, and

was hanged. Had he been alive in more recent times, he doubtless would have graced the chairs of many a Board of Directors. One imagines that the ghost of the daring captain must frequently blush with shame in comparing his pikerish exploits with those of his successors.

Wall Street very early in its history had dealings in stocks, but they were not of the kind dealt in these days. The early stocks of Wall Street stood beside a cage, a pillory and a whipping post, and were for the benefit, among others, of those found guilty of "shady" transactions. Some people think that a return of the original stocks to Wall Street would be a salutory warning to modern offenders.

The first public trading in Wall Street took place in the market house, built in 1709. It became the official public market for the sale of corn, grain and meal. On Wall Street too, slaves were bought, traded and sold. It seemed fated that this narrow little street should become, first, the mart of the city, and later the market place of the country and of the world. 

At one time, it did seem as though Wall Street's destiny as a street of trade was nearing its end. 

That was during George Washington's administration, when the street took on an aura of social eminence. From the doorsteps of favored homes, 2

The centering of the banks on Wall Street naturally attracted the commercial and speculative interests to the Street. Land values increased rapidly, and the many pretentious private houses on Wall Street were razed to make room for commercial buildings. The great fire of 1835 put the finishing touches on the transformation from a residential street to a business thoroughfare. In the rebuilding process, practically every new building was erected for business purposes.

An interesting commentary on the connection between stocks and Wall Street that existed in the minds of people even a century ago is had from the following article that appeared in the New 

York Mirror in the early 1800's. "It is as difficult to wend one's way through Wall Street as it ever was. Physically as well as financially there is peril in perambulating the street. Stocks may rise but stones are falling prodigiously in all directions.' 

Now that Wall Street had definitely become the business and financial center of New York City, it began to be linked up with the political and economic forces that were shaping the course of the nation. Despite the fact that the seat of government was transferred from New York to Philadelphia and later to the newly created Wash- 4

Wall Street today looks every inch the important financial center it is. Overshadowing the subtreasury, before which stands the statue of George Washington, are lofty buildings, containing thousands of business and brokerage offices. Everything about the Street reminds you of the mighty forces that are at work shaping the financial destinies of the country. One feels that he is listening at the heart of the nation, engaged in its task of receiving and transmitting the vital flow of money through the financial arteries of the world. CHAPTER II The Growth of the New York Stock Exchange NEW 

EW YORK was established by the Dutch in 

1623 as a trading post. The Indians, so the story goes, were "bearish" on the place, and readily sold the island of Manhattan to the Dutch for about $24 worth of beads. It is even hinted that the Indians sold "short" and later tried to "scalp" the Dutch market in an endeavor to cover in on their short sale.

The Dutch, however, were possessed of both courage and vision. They "ran in" the Indians, and proceeded to build up values. They built wharves at the foot of Wall Street and established a market place where the manufactured goods of 

Europe could be auctioned off on their arrival. At the foot of Broad Street they established an "Exchange" for dealings in meal and water-borne produce from the interior of the new continent. 

Prior to and during the Revolutionary War, there was no security market in the country, for the excellent reason that practically no securities existed in Colonial America. With the creation of the first Congress, which assembled in Federal 14

Hall (where the historic New York sub-treasury building now stands) the government authorized the issue of $80,000,000 in 6% bonds, or "stock" as it was then called.

The authorization of this government issue, which was for the purpose of consolidating and refunding the Revolutionary War debt, developed the need for the creation of a new financial machinery. In 1791, the United States Bank was established at the insistence of Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury.

The refunding plan of Hamilton's was responsible for the first wave of security speculation in the country. It was confined, however, to only a comparatively few people. These knew that Hamilton was determined to put the nation's money on a sound foundation by redeeming the outstanding obligations of the government, which were in circulation on the basis of a few cents on the dollar. 

The individuals "in the know" formed the first "pools" or speculative syndicates of Wall Street.

In 1789, when these syndicates were formed, there were no telegraph lines or any means of speedy communication, particularly in the backwoods part of the nation. Agents were sent out with cash and instructions to buy up all the available Continental paper at depreciated prices. 15

Soldiers, farmers and small merchants, not knowing of the redemption plan, parted with their paper at a substantial loss. They found later that they lost, coming and going. The taxation necessary to take care of redemption imposed a double burden on them, for it virtually fell on their backs. Members of the original "pools" had reason to congratulate each other, for they cleaned up handsomely on their operations. 

Issuance of "public stock," the forerunner of modern government bonds, as well as the sale of the scrip stock of the new government bank, soon became the source of a great deal of trading. 

There were dealings also in the shares of the Bank of North America (incorporated in Philadelphia) and in the shares of the Bank of New York, the first two banks of the United States.

The result of this trading caused a flurry in the business of the auctioneers who were centered around the eastern end of Wall Street. Enterpris ing individuals began also to set up small establishments for dealing in securities. On February 6, 1792, the following advertisement appeared in the New York newspapers, representing the first instance of co-operation on the part of brokers and of active solicitation of public participation in stocks: 16

STOCK EXCHANGE OFFICE 

This day will be opened at No. 22 Wall

Street, a large convenient room for the accommodation of dealers in stock, and in which public sales will be held at noon, as usual, in rotation, by the public's much obliged and humble servants.

A. L. BLEECHER & SONS JOHN PINTARD MCEVERS AND BARCLAY CORTLAND AND FERRERS JAY AND SUTTON 

This organization comprised some of the more prominent auctioneers of Wall Street. They had formed the public "stock exchange" in an attempt to monopolize the trading in securities. This they soon found they were unable to do, as the independent agents continued in their competition for the business and grew more aggressive than ever. 

Some of these independents decided to have regular meetings for the purpose of exchanging quotations and for the transaction of their business. They met daily under a buttonwood tree in front of Nos. 68-70 Wall Street. These meetings proved to be the direct forerunner of the present 

New York Stock Exchange. 

Business under the buttonwood tree prospered, and it was not long before the independent trad- 17

ers combined in an effort to drive the auctioneering establishments from the field. They resolved to have no dealings with the auctioneers and drew up an agreement covering the terms under which trading was to be done. The agreement was simple. It called for a uniform commission of onequarter of one per cent, and it stipulated that members should confine their brokerage business among themselves.

There were twenty-four signatures to this interesting document, which was the first stock exchange agreement of any kind in the country. It marked the inauguration of what has since come to be the world's foremost security market. It is a far cry back from the present stately structure at the corner of Wall and Broad Streets to the tiny curb market of 1792. The trading that began so humbly in the rain and dust of a village street was destined to reach out to the furthermost corners of the earth.

The exclusive nature of the trading, the compactness and aggressiveness of the small group, soon resulted in the members doing a large proportion of the business, and eventually routing the auctioneers from the field. The twenty-four individuals or firms that met each day under the shade tree in the first year of the organization 18

soon dominated the brokerage business of New 

York's thirty-five thousand inhabitants.

The following year, these pioneering brokers moved indoors to the then pretentious new business structure, the Tontine Coffee House. The building became the commercial center of New York, and the associated brokers housed there found themselves enjoying a virtual monopoly of the stock-trading business. The New York Stock 

Exchange was definitely launched.

The new nation developed rapidly, and with it grew the new stock exchange. After playing an important part in the development of our earliest banks, it presently enabled the creation of fire and marine insurance companies by obtaining the necessary capital through the sale of the new securities. Just prior to the War of 1812 trading was carried on in the stocks of ten banks, thirteen insurance companies, and a number of government issues.

The War of 1812 added many millions to the government debt and resulted in the issuance of more government bonds. Furthermore, it brought about a heavy increase in the purchase and sale of securities in the New York securities market.

Industrial corporations began to be formed, and the public demand for participation in the new enterprises through the ownership of shares soon 19

taxed the facilities of the growing stock exchange. 

The simple agreement of 1792 was no longer adequate for the new responsibilities of the Exchange. 

In 1817, the need for a more closely knit organization becoming imperative, an association was formed, and a constitution and by-laws adopted. Quarters were rented at 40 Wall Street for about $100 a year, and the name of the "New 

York Stock and Exchange Board" was inaugurated. The new association was formed by only seven firms and thirteen individual brokers. Trading was permitted in only about thirty stocks. 

In 1820 the constitution of the Exchange was revised. Wash sales were prohibited. It was also found necessary to set specific rates of commission for the different types of bonds and stocks traded on the market. As evidence of the leisurely transactions on the "Board" of that period, the following article in the constitution is quoted: "It shall be the duty of the President to call the stocks at the hour that may be fixed upon by the Board, from time to time, as the season may require. · · 99 The next two decades saw a great development in canal building and railroad construction. In 

1821 the Morris Canal stock was sold to the public. In 1825, the completion of the Erie Canal definitely transferred the commercial center of the 20

United States from Philadelphia to New York

City. In 1829 the first experiment of a steam locomotive in this country occurred. The following year the stock of the Mohawk and Hudson Railroad was listed for trading. The first "corner" recorded in the annals of the Stock Exchange occurred in the Morris Canal stock in 1835. 

On a December night in 1835 fire destroyed the building in which the Stock Exchange was housed, together with seventeen other buildings in the neighborhood. Many a Wall Street man, "who had retired to his pillow in affluence, was a bankrupt on awaking." In the general distress, the

Board of Brokers was compelled to install its stock board in a room that once served as a hayloft for a stable in the rear of what had been the stylish home of a Mr. Jauncey. This room was used until the Tontine Coffee House was rebuilt. 

Trading was largely occupied with government, bank, canal, insurance, and public utility securities until about 1835, when railroad stocks took the lead a lead which they held until 1913. On May 

13, 1835, the total number of shares traded was 

3,161, of which 3,160 were railroad stocks. In 

1837, the year of the first serious panic, sales reached approximately 8,000 shares a day, a figure commented upon by the press at that time as being exceedingly large. 21

As the agriculture, business and finances of the country fared, so fared the stock market. Its growth measured the progress of the nation. The invention of the telegraph in 1842 made possible close contacts between the market and every part of the country. The discoveries of oil in Pennsylvania and of precious metals in the West were soon reflected in ever-growing activity on the Exchange. In 1857, the volume of trading reached the unprecedented level of 70,000 shares in a single day.

The ever occurring problem of over-crowded space developed again. In 1863, the New York 

Stock Exchange Building Company was created for the purpose of providing a permanent and commodious home for the Exchange. In the same year, the present title of "The New York Stock 

Exchange" was adopted. Two years later, the market moved into its own building on Broad Street, the site of the present much larger quarters of the organization. In 1869, the Stock Exchange took into membership the open Board of Brokers, an independent association formed in 1864.

The after-math of the Civil War was a period of intensive railroad building and development of new enterprises. The Atlantic Cable, laid in 1866, created important arbitrage between New York and London. Stock speculation flourished and 22

overflowed into three additional and newly organized minor exchanges, including an open-air exchange, the forerunner of the present New York Curb Exchange, which, in some respects, was a prototype of the original market of 1792 under the buttonwood tree. In 1867, the electric stock ticker was adopted.

When the New York Stock Exchange and the 

"open Board of Brokers" were consolidated in 

1869, the 533 members of the Exchange and the 

354 members of the "Board," together with 173 members of the "Government Bond Department" (an organization devoted to dealing in United States government issues), constituted a total membership of 1,060. Membership in the new organization was temporarily limited to that number, but in 1879 it was increased to 1,100 by the sale of forty new "seats" to defray expenses in expanding the Stock Exchange Building southward along Broad Street.

The panic of 1873 really marks the beginning of the modern period of the New York Stock

Exchange. It had now become a prominent and permanent institution in the life of the nation. Its purpose was to furnish an open free market to its members and to guard against anything that might be construed against its interests or that of its members. High standards of business morality 23

had been developed, and practices once winked at were now severely dealt with. 

In 1878, telephones were first installed on the Exchange floor to facilitate communication between it and the offices of its members. The telegraph was also adopted to connect members' offices in Wall Street with their branches or correspondents in offices distant from New York.

Stock tickers were being gradually introduced throughout the country and in Canada. A farreaching and practically instantaneous quotation service system was steadily built up. 

The building originally occupied by the Exchange (built in 1863) extended through the block from Broad to New Streets, but did not reach Wall Street. By 1901, it had outserved its purpose, and a greater trading house was needed. 

Space was acquired on either side of the original headquarters, and in 1903 a new building was erected on the expanded site. While it was being built, the New York Produce Exchange courteously permitted a portion of its floor to be used by the Stock Exchange. It was in these surroundings that one of the most dramatic events in the annals of Wall Street occurred-the Northern 

Pacific corner. 

In 1922, a new addition to the Stock Exchange was erected, in the form of a twenty-three story 24

skyscraper. This brought the building over on to 

Wall Street. Further additions have since been made which have extended the quarters to New Street. No stock exchange in the world is so adequately housed or so efficiently operated as is the present New York Stock Exchange. Many pages could be filled with but a bare description of the physical and mechanical equipment of this enormous trading institution. 

The Stock Exchange is a voluntary association, and is limited as to membership. Admission is possible only by purchase of a "seat" from some member or by admission to a vacancy. In 1929, presumably influenced by modern corporate practise, the Stock Exchange gave its members what amounted to a 25% stock dividend, by distributing an additional quarter-membership to each member; thus creating 275 new memberships and bringing the total up to 1,375. 

The term "seat" had its origin years ago, when members had desks at which they sat and transacted their business. In modern times, under normal trading conditions, an actual seat would be a hindrance to a floor member, since the volume of business and the number of securities traded in are so great that he not only has to be on his feet, but literally on his "toes" as well. The term 25

"seat," as denoting membership, however, is still used. 

No better commentary is to be had of the growth of the New York Stock Exchange than from the consideration of how prices of "seats" advanced from $500 in 1860 to $625,000 in 1929, an increase of 125,000% in almost seventy years. 26

CHAPTER III 

Panics of the Past

ANICS have been a periodic phenomena of mankind as far back as human history records. 

Since they are largely the product of human mistakes, it is likely that they will continue to make their appearance every so often. That they are distressing, no one can deny. But, they are not without value. They can be compared to the fallow periods the farmer allots to his soil, so that subsequent harvests may be more bountiful. It is a fact that practically every depression has been followed by a period of greater prosperity than the one that came before.

Just now, the world is working out of a dire depression-the worst it has ever known, so it seems. That feeling has been characteristic of virtually every past depression. It has always seemed as though the world couldn't possibly get out of its mire of adversity-yet, it always has. It is, therefore, interesting to review the highlights and deep shadows of some of the big panics of the past to see how they compare with the present one, and to see how they eventually ended, i.e., to be followed by better times than ever. 27

THE PANIC OF 1837 

The panic of 1837 followed a period of great prosperity, and, as is usually the case, was in turn followed by a period of greater prosperity. But, until the turn for the better came, the country suffered keenly the pinch of hard times. Companies failed by the hundreds, and banks followed one another in their fall like a row of nine pins. 

The same paradox of privation amidst plenty that perplexes us today existed in those days. A bumper wheat crop impoverished the farmers and a record cotton crop crucified the cotton growers. Practically nothing could be sold at a profit. General world conditions were bad, and there did not seem to be any prospect for improvement for a long time to come.

Yet, within a decade, the country was rolling along to a period of prosperity unparalleled in the nation's existence. Railroads began to spread throughout the land. Telegraph lines sprung up. 

Gold was discovered in California. Banks blossomed. A wave of pioneering, prospecting and projecting of all sorts of industrial and financial enterprises swept the country. The problem was to find the people with which to fill the jobs created by the new prosperity. Commodity and security prices soared again, and the American pub- 28

lic was possessed of a plentiful supply of cash, courage and confidence.

THE PANIC OF 1857 

Again, the pace became too rapid. The industrial and financial progress of the period was not seasoned. It collapsed in 1857 with the failure of the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company, one of the most substantial banks of New York City. 

Banks immediately began to call loans. Money tightened up. Runs on banks developed, and one bank after another closed its doors. Specie payments were suspended. The stock and bond markets crashed to record low levels. Again, generous crops of wheat and cotton had to be disposed of at ruinous prices. Hard times prevailed in Europe, and the future seemed dark and forbidding to practically all the world.

In two years, credit conditions in the country improved and business swung back to normal. Security and commodity prices rose, and the tide of prosperity set in. The industrial progress of the nation was accelerated by the feverish extension of our railroad systems. Despite the set-back caused by the Civil War, the advance proceeded at a rapid rate until well into the early '70's. Then the stage became set for one of the worst panics in the history of the country. Untrammeled specula- 29

tion in railroad stocks and in wild land ventures gave the people an unwarranted psychology of optimism and a belief in a never-ending prosperity. It was the old, but ever-new, story of the boom before the crash.

THE PANIC OF 1873 

The panic of 1873 burst upon the people with the unexpectedness that generally characterizes such holocausts. It began with the suspension of 

Jay Cooke & Company, the government's financial agents during the Civil War. It brought the financial structure of the country down in ruins. 

Land booms exploded. Business men threw up their hands as banks failed and receivership, bankruptcy and reorganization abounded on all sides. 

The Stock Exchange closed, and brokerage houses toppled by the score. Europe was sorely beset, and conditions all over the world seemed to be going from bad to worse as time went on.

The country emerged from the depression of 1873 slowly and painfully, but it did emerge. 

Business came back, the railroads were put on their feet, real estate again began to boom, and foreign trade revived and flourished. Before the public began to realize it, the country was again riding the crest wave of prosperity. A great boom in mining stocks swept the country. Big market 30

operators appeared on the scene. The use of telephones began to be wide-spread. Transatlantic cables were laid. Crops were good and were disposed of profitably. New railway building was resumed, credit was easy, and stocks advanced steadily for a period of about five years. The stage seemed set for a permanent era of prosperity. 

THE PANIC OF 1884 

The panic of 1884 was more peculiarly a financial disturbance than an industrial depression. It arose primarily out of the vast money-manipulations of the "young Napoleon of finance," Ferdinand Ward, who built a house of cards on the foundation of General Grant's confidence and credulity. The crash of Grant & Ward brought down banks and brokerage houses, but did not seriously affect industrial conditions. Hetty Green contributed her bit to the financial debacle by suddenly calling for $25,000,000 in securities and $475,000 in cash from the prominent firm of J. J. Cisco & 

Company, which forced the bankers to close their doors. The shock to the security markets was severe, but its effect was over comparatively quickly. The saviour of the situation was J. Pierpont Morgan, who had come into prominence at about this time as a rising power of finance.

The next period of prosperity was to see an 31

entirely new development in the history of the United States. It was the age of the "trusts." The nation became industry conscious and gigantic enterprises were formed, largely through consolidation and centralization of a number of small units. 

This era produced many of the "Captains of Industry" who were to play such important parts in the upbuilding of the country. It brought to the fore such great figures as Rockefeller, Rogers and 

Flagler in oil; Carnegie, Frick and Schwab in steel; Hill and Harriman in railroads; Morgan and Schiff in banking, and a host of lesser industrial barons and financial magnates. The country was geared to "Big Business" and the consequent advances to prosperity were stupendous. 

THE PANIC OF 1893 

"Big Business," however, was no guarantee of permanent prosperity. Abuses of power and excesses in corporate financing crept in and finally brought a top-heavy industrial structure down in ruins. The collapse started with the failure of the 

Cordage "trust" in 1893, and gained momentum until business houses by the thousands and banks by the hundreds plunged on the rocks. Brokerage firms by the score went to the wall, and about 

30,000 miles of railroads went into bankruptcy.

The stock and bond markets hit abysmal depths, 32

and for nearly three years the country wallowed in a mire of despondency. In addition to the 

United States, no less than fifteen countries were in serious trouble.

In 1896, an unexpected upturn in wheat prices led to the election of McKinley, and business and the stock market picked up smartly. Two years later, the successful termination of the Spanish- American War stimulated the industrial activity of the country, and industry rose to levels unheard of in previous times. The United States was hailed throughout the world as a leading power and a land of unexampled prosperity. What a commentary on changing conditions and psychology in this country is to be had from the fact that in a few years the nation rose from the depths of despair to the peaks of optimism. "Big Business" grew bigger than ever. The gigantic United States Steel 

Corporation was formed by J. P. Morgan, who became recognized as the ruling power in American finance. It was in challenge to that dominion that the forces that caused the stock market panic of 1901 were created. 

THE PANIC OF 1901 

The 1901 stock market panic came without warning. A fight for control of the Northern 33

Pacific Railroad between the Morgan-Hill inter ests and the Schiff-Harriman contingent created an artificial corner in the stock and forced the price up in a single day from $170 to $1,000, principally on short covering. The bottom fell out of the rest of the market, with leading shares dropping 50 to 75 points during that hectic session. Call money went from 3 per cent to 70 per cent. The next day the contending groups patched up their differences and allowed what shorts that were left to cover in at $150 a share. Many largescale market operators were ruined as a result of the Northern Pacific battle and countless margin traders were wiped out, but the damage done to the banking and industrial structures of the country was very slight.

Except for a comparatively brief set-back in 1903, the country experienced an almost uninterrupted interval of six years of prosperity. The stock market, after the Northern Pacific crisis, kept working upward. In 1907 it ran into the collapsed boom of the Amalgamated Copper Syndicate, and prices took a nose-dive. The ill-fated copper combine, launched by the National City 

Bank and financed by Morgan, had cornered copper, but it could find no buyers for the metal at the artificial prices fixed for it. 34

THE PANIC OF 1907 

Like the previous panic, the effects of the 1907 crash were confined principally to the stock market, and to a few banks directly sponsoring highly speculative issues. The man then credited with stopping the debacle was J. Pierpont Morgan, but recent historical revaluations give the honor to Banker James Stillman. Morgan, however, got the spoils of the panic and came out of it with valuable properties, chief of which was the Tennessee Coal and Iron Company which he handed over to the United States Steel Corporation. 

The years following the panic of 1907 saw

American industry expand still further. Giant corporations came into existence and an era of "intercorporate relationships" developed. The automobile industry came out of its "swaddling clothes."

Public utility enterprises grew rapidly as the electrification of industry and the home spread. Stocks continued to move upward for the greater part of the time. Panics were forgotten and an extended period of peace and prosperity appeared assured. 

THE PANIC OF 1913 

Disturbing rumors of the possibility of war began to develop in 1913. Although not taken seriously by the American public, business conditions in this country began to get bad, and then went 35

from bad to worse. Commodity prices broke. Unemployment and failures spread. The stock market crashed. When war was declared in August, 1914, business was paralyzed, and the stock market went completely into chaos. The New York 

Stock Exchange and other exchanges, as well, closed from August to November.

Again, the United States gave a dramatic exhibition of a sharp "come-back" from the depths of depression to the heights of activity. War orders poured into the country and revitalized industry and finance. The stock market opened, and operations for the rise were resumed. The swift advances of the "war babies" stirred the speculative impulses of the nation. Our entrance into the war proved to be the beginning of an unparalleled period of intense business and speculative activity. 

The government Liberty Bond campaigns edu cated a vast public into the intricacies of investing and trading.

THE PANIC OF 1921 

The end of the war immediately stalled the mighty machine of production that this country had reared up. Manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers were caught with huge inventories which could not be moved as commodity prices began to move sharply downward. A wave of contract cancellations swept the country. Banks were forced 36

into many businesses through the inability of concerns to meet their obligations. Many people were thrown out of employment. 

Again the country rose-this time to peaks of prosperity that dazzled the world. Under the impetus of the demands of millions of men released from war-time pursuits to civilian life, industry and finance expanded beyond America's wildest dreams. A gigantic building boom developed. The automobile business flourished. New industries, such as radio, moving pictures and airplanes blossomed into activity. Employment, incomes and standards of living rose to heights never before witnessed in the history of the United States. The stock market rose steadily, with but intermittent set-backs, until prices reached fantastic figures, an advance which was unwarranted and carried much too far.

THE PANIC OF 1929 

When the first blow of the Great Deflation fell upon Wall Street in October, 1929, few believed that it marked the beginning of what has since proved to be one of the worst depressions this country has ever experienced. The "new era" that many believed had definitely arrived was too strongly imbedded in the consciousness of the American public. It seemed impossible that the 37

lofty structure of production and profits reared by our hands and brains could be shaken down. But, as in the case of previous big depressions, world forces were too strong to overcome. Rapidly declining commodity prices took their inevitable toll and business and finance were dragged down unsparingly. Unemployment mounted tragically while bank and business failures presented ghastly totals. The stock market experienced a prolonged bear movement and prices came down to the lowest levels in many years.

There is no reason to believe that this depression will not be followed by greater prosperity than ever before. This country has a population of nearly 130,000,000-the largest in its history -with inculcated standards of living unprecedented in human history. The world has been wracked and strained by a mighty war and its aftermath. What has been taking place constitutes the readjustments necessary to bringing world conditions back to sound economic order. 

The very intensity and prolongation of this depression furnishes grounds for the belief that the next period of recovery and subsequent prosperity will be longer than any we have had before, and that the heights this country will achieve in the future will out-distance all previous tops. 38

HOW THE PRESENT DEPRESSION COMPARES WITH SOME OF THE WORST DEPRESSIONS OF THE PAST 

This depression has unquestionably been one of the severest with which this country has ever been afflicted. Widespread losses and intense suffering have been encountered on all sides, commercial and bank failures have been heavy and unemployment has been exceptionally great. Yet, in many respects, this depression has lacked the ferocity and the frightful aspects of some of the past major depressions.

The panic of 1837 saw bread riots, with soldiers patrolling the streets of New York. It saw the suspension of specie payments. It witnessed mobs of famished poor, driven desperate by the high cost of food. A reign of fear and terror gripped the country with a paralyzing intensity. 39

The panic of 1873 saw the New York Stock

Exchange closed for ten days. The country, as in 1933, went off the gold standard, and banks resorted to the issuance of Clearing House certificates. Eighty-nine railroads went on the rocks. 

Starvation and riots were rampant.

The panic of 1893 saw a financial upheaval in this country and in Europe. It saw Coxey's Army of unemployed in its march on Washington. It witnessed bloodshed and property destroyed. The great Pullman strike paralyzed the railroads.

Federal troops had to be called out to stop rioting and looting.

These were the three panics that in intensity and duration fairly well matched the panic through which this country has lately been passing. We have passed through nearly four years of hardships and despondency, but we have the consolation of having been spared the spectacle of bayonets flashing in our streets, the sounds and sights of rioting, looting and shooting, such as have characterized some of the serious depressions of the past. 40

CHAPTER IV Bulls and Bears of 100 Years 

HERE always has been a pale of mystery surrounding the operations of the most successful speculators in the stock market. Why this should be so is difficult to appreciate, for there is actually nothing so very mysterious about these men of affairs, nor is there anything supernatural or enigmatic in their activities or success in making fortunes through their speculations. 

Apparently the mystery is entirely the result of general awe at the knowledge that there are men successful, and permanently so, in the art of speculation and manipulation.

There is undoubtedly some justification for a mystery of that kind, for the formula is really so simple that it probably escapes completely from the grasp of most humans who are always and forever looking for the hard way of doing anything. There is no great secret for such market success. It is well known and apparent, yet how few there have been in the past thirty years, or even a century, who have followed the rules of the game with sufficient ingenuity to take a place 41

among the immortals who have come to be known as the "Men of Mystery"!

Less than fifty men of all the millions who have participated in stock market speculation are entitled to have their names ensconced on the roll of this select group; and yet careful perusal of the operations of these mystery men show them to be little different than Mr. Brown, two doors down the street, or Henry Lowder, that man we chat with at our broker's each morning at ten. 

Lowder's a successful business man, you'd say. 

At least he's the head of a prosperous company.

And Mrs. Lowder belongs to the same afternoon bridge as your wife. A fine woman, your wife reports; and you know that both Mr. and Mrs. 

Lowder are quite religious, besides being active on the Town's Welfare Board. You have heard that Lowder is a pretty sharp business man, gaining a large part of his present financial success through "pull" among the Selectmen, in addition to his friends in the Legislature; and you even heard it reported that he was responsible for the bankruptcy of a friend of yours whose small company was beginning to cut into Lowder's profits. "But, then," you say, "I suppose that's business. 

No sentiment."

Some of these mystery men are just about like 

Lowder. They're sharp in business. If they weren't 42

they probably would not have been financially successful. They seldom wince at the ruination of even their best friends during a market battle. 

They give no quarter, nor do they ask it. And on 

Sunday morning you're just as apt to see these tyros of Wall Street gracing a front pew, or hear of them lending a friend several thousand dollars to begin over again after a serious reversal. These mystery men, after all, are just like the rest of us. 

Not much better; not much worse.

Although he profited in no way himself, it might be said that Alexander Hamilton, the first 

Secretary of the Treasury of the United States, was the first stock market operator. Hamilton's genius in solving the troubled financial affairs of the then youthful nation were directly responsible for the foundation of several of the great fortunes of the country, as well as for the beginning of speculation in securities. 

Hamilton's Redemption and Assumption plan which he succeeded in passing through Congress after exchanging in trade one of his party's major measures, greatly enriched his confidants who had previously bought up for a few cents on the dollar much of the scrip with which this impoverished country had paid off its Revolutionary War soldiers. With the passage of Hamilton's bill, this scrip was redeemed at par, much to the profit of 43

Hamilton's friends. This government paper, made valuable by Hamilton's genius became the first security to be "traded" in Wall Street.

Bank shares were later added, as Hamilton evolved the National Bank plan; and what may be considered of even greater interest in view of what has happened since 1914, is the fact that the securities issued by the government, or indirectly sponsored by the government following the Revolution, were the media which brought about "wild speculation" and "general public participation" in the security exchange of that day. 

How like our own day was this occurrence. 

Authorities are practically unanimous in their opinion that it was the education of the American public during the Liberty Loan drives which gave them the knowledge and desire to speculate in securities. The consequence, of course, was the wild bull markets of 1920-1929, and the equally startling panic and depression of 1929-1932. 

In Hamilton's day a similar boom and crash developed. The speculation was so excessive and general that the government actually became alarmed to the point of using its influence with newspapers to issue veiled warnings against the excess. Thomas Jefferson wrote in a letter to a friend: "Ships are lying idle at the wharfs, buildings are stopped, capital withdrawn from com- 44

merce, manufactures, arts and agriculture to be employed in gambling, and the tide of public prosperity is arrested in its course."

These government warnings in 1791, not unlike those of later years, were turned to advantage by those "in the know." Before the warnings were issued, the "insiders" of 1791, and they included many members of Congress, carefully arranged to sell out their security holdings. While their liquidation was going on, the banks continued to discount paper for independent speculators and for the public, as they had been doing for months, and continued to loan on securities. But as soon as the "insiders" had sold out, the banks suddenly ceased to loan a single penny to anyone, with the natural result, just as in 1929 when the banks virtually refused to grant any security loans, that a severe decline in the market developed. This was America's first panic. National Bank stock, that had been selling at 195 in August, dropped to 108 by 

September, and other securities followed a similar course.

The "insiders" had sold out at the top. The independents and the public were left "holding the bag."

This remarkable parallel between the happenings of 1791 and those of 1929 furnishes much food for thought. But the turn of the century 45

brought a big change in many conditions and marked the beginning of an era of machinery and invention. The vast expansion of opportunity brought with it greater fields for investment, speculation and manipulation. And with the opportunities came the men who successfully grasped them and made themselves either wealthy, well-known, or both, as a result. Many of the fortunes that are intact today had their foundation in the first half of the Nineteenth Century; the fortunes of such families as the Abbots, Lawrences, Vanderbilts, Astors and many others. 

These were the years when Stephenson developed his locomotive, following Watt's invention of the steam engine; Fulton enlarged the scope of navigation by adapting this engine to ships. 

The Erie Canal of 1825 and the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad of 1828 were milestones of the history which was to feature speculation and trading for many years, and out of which were to arise many of Wall Street's most famous Mystery Men -the first of whom was Jacob Little.

Little first attracted attention in 1835, and the fact that he was much disliked by others in Wall Street can easily be understood by investors who still own stock bought before the 1929 crash, when it is revealed that Little was the first Big 

Bear of the stock market. He gloried in his de- 46

structive efforts during the panic of 1837, and was the originator of that much discussed and 

Congressionally damned operation known as a "short sale." Little was the first real speculator in the market, the exact counterpart of the professional traders of the past ten years whose complete interest and life was tied up in speculation in securities.

Jacob Little also introduced another innovation into Wall Street-an innovation that has been worn threadbare by those who followed him. It concerned his short sales of Erie Railroad stock. 

Practically every other member of the exchange at that time was antagonistic to Little and they conspired to squeeze him hard and ruin him, if possible. They waited until his short contracts were about to expire and then started their squeeze. They bought every available share of 

Erie and ran the price way up. They figured that

Little would have to come to them to get the stock to cover his short contracts and they would make him pay his entire fortune. 

But they had figured without one of Jacob

Little's plans, for he completely outsmarted them. 

For some time Little had known of the fact that a large Erie bond issue had been sold in England some few years before, and he also learned that the bonds were convertible into Erie stock. So just 47

before his short contracts expired, and finding that he could not buy a single share of Erie in the market, even at the high prices that had been reached, he purchased a large block of the English bonds, and converting them into stock, made good on his deliveries, much to his antagonists' chagrin and financial loss.

Even Little's exploits seem picayunish, however, in contrast to those which marked the final fifty years of the Nineteenth Century.

Just before the Civil War, in the 50's and early 

60's, Commodore Cornelius Vanderbilt and tricky 

Daniel Drew began a series of railroad speculations and manipulations that dwarfed all previous records, and which seem prodigious, indeed, even today. Jacob Gould, too, was drawing into the picture, along with Jim Fisk, and the prize politician of them all, Boss Tweed.

Vanderbilt, at the beginning, was the leading steamship operator on the Hudson River. Drew began to compete in a small way, and gradually commenced to be a real competitor of the Commodore's line, with the result that Vanderbilt suggested an alliance. The consolidation took placeundoubtedly one of the first big mergers of the country.

These two men represented excellent foil for each other, for although similar in many respects, 48

in other particulars they were as different as night and day. Both Drew and Vanderbilt were given to bribery of a sort, and political intrigue was a part of many of their market operations. 

But Vanderbilt was forever the constructive optimist, always a bull; while Drew, the bear, was happiest when prices were breaking sharply. 

There was, for instance, the time that Vanderbilt had secured control of the Harlem Railroad, which then extended only to 26th Street. Vanderbilt saw the advantage that would be gained by the road and by himself marketwise if the Harlem were extended down to the Battery. Accordingly, by greasing Boss Tweed's palm, he secured a grant from the Common Council permitting such extension.

Tweed and some of his friends bought a large amount of Harlem stock at low prices on Vanderbilt's advice; and Drew, who was also "in" on the Commodore's plans, was another who bought heavily during the advance of Harlem from $8 to over $50 while Vanderbilt was getting control of the road.

When the road's right of way was extended Vanderbilt set right to work laying the tracks. 

Drew and Tweed were not so much interested in real work, however. The Harlem stock, upon news of the extension of the road, had advanced 49

to 100, and the bearish instincts of Daniel Drew came to the fore. He induced Boss Tweed to join him, and they proceeded to sell out all their Harlem stock. Next they went short of the stock very heavily, after which Tweed used his influence to force through the Council an order rescinding 

Vanderbilt's extension of Harlem's right of way.

The Harlem was dealt a heavy blow by this betrayal, and with Drew, Tweed and some of their political friends still selling short, the market for the stock turned downward sharply. 

Vanderbilt let them sell for a while. In fact, he let them sell so much stock that before they were aware of it they had sold 27,000 shares more than the total 110,000 shares outstanding. The price had dropped to $72 by this time, with every indication that it would break below $50, but Vanderbilt stepped in at that point and supported the market. Quickly he advanced the price. It went to $100, then to 150, 170 and finally 179. At that price the bears were glad enough to settle with the Commodore. While Drew was squeezed hard in that deal, he had made so much more on his previous purchase of the stock, that he had a net profit despite his treachery.

Still intent on extending the Harlem to the Battery, Vanderbilt approached the Legislature 50

at Albany for a new grant similar to that rescinded by the Council.

Drew was still on his trail, however, and saw a chance to make another turn in the stock on Vanderbilt's latest move. But here again his avarice proved his undoing, for Drew and his friends repeated almost exactly their mistake of selling more stock than was actually in existence. The stock dropped precipitously from 150 to 100; but as quickly it jumped to 110, 140, 150, 185, and in a few days to 285. Vanderbilt was again squeezing them, and enraged at this double trickery, he wrathily ordered the price of the stock marked up to $1000. His cooler associates prevailed upon him to be less harsh, however, so he agreed to settle at 285. Drew even balked at paying this price, and finally Vanderbilt let him off at a lesser figure, although taking a total of half a million dollars away from him.

Drew learned a valuable lesson from his illfated manoeuvers in Harlem. He fully appreciated the leading speculative position of the railroads, and he learned by his experiences that the most money was made by being "on the inside." With this knowledge, and with dogged determination he finally succeeded in obtaining admission to the directorate of the Erie Railroad, which was to 51

play an important part in later developments among the Mystery Men. 

Commodore Vanderbilt's next moves included a series of mergers, in which he sought to unite his Harlem road with the New York & Hudson

River Railroad, control of which he had bought for $25 per share. And while he was battling his political adversaries for legislation permitting the merger, a matter which consumed five years, he also bought control of New York Central Railroad, at a cost of $18,000,000. 

When he finally secured the necessary legislation in 1869, Vanderbilt merged all three roads into a single system, and immediately rewarded himself for sponsoring the merger. Out of the treasury of the combined roads he voted himself a generous bonus of $6,000,000 in cash and $20,- 

000,000 in new stock.

Meanwhile, Wall Street was noting the rise of another colorful character, a young man of but 25 who made his way to the financial district after an unsuccessful career as a surveyor, an author and a leather tanner. 

This man was Jay Gould, penniless, but with a burning ambition to enter the railroad business. 

Strangely, his desires became an accomplishment through the assistance of Philip Miller, the wellto-do father of the girl Gould married. 52

Miller was heavily interested in the Rutland & 

Washington Railroad, and loaned Gould the funds to purchase the road's control. Within a few months Gould had made the railroad into a valuable property which was sufficiently attractive to another line so that Gould sold his interest in the Rutland road for $130,000, most of which was clear profit.

Enthused by his immediate success in a business which was much to his liking, Gould bought control of another small road which was in financial difficulties, and repeated his first success by selling this line, the Cleveland & Pittsburgh Railroad, to the Pennsylvania Railroad for $120 a share against a cost of only $40 a share. 

Gould's profits of $100,000 bought him a partnership in a Wall Street brokerage firm, where his ability soon carried him to a prominent position among the active leaders on the Street. Soon he became associated with Daniel Drew in many of the spectacular operations that made the group famous.

The third member of the Erie triumvirate was James Fisk, Jr., who had proven to be a master drygoods salesman when working for Jordan, Marsh & Company of Boston, and who proved even a better security salesman. Drew first employed Fisk to market some stocks for him, and 53

the unbelievably high prices which Fisk could induce people to pay for the securities brought immediate recognition from Drew.

The latter had firmly intrenched himself on the Board of Directors of the Erie Railroad by this time, and he had found that, while the road itself was having considerable difficulty in making both ends meet, nevertheless, the "inside" information which he got was making him many a profit in the market.

So successful had Drew become in his combined position as director and virtual "specialist" in Erie stock, that the Street soon began to remark that if Drew said "up," Erie would go up; when he said "down," Erie went down, and if Drew said "wiggle-waggle," it would bob both ways.

Eventually the Erie began operating profitably and was paying dividends. Drew took Gould and 

Fisk into partnership with him in the management of the road, and with this line-up, Drew was able to make more money in his manipulation of Erie stock than the entire road was making for itself from its operations.

Drew's success in his speculations was so great as to attract considerable attention from numerous other brokers and financiers, and among these was 

Cornelius Vanderbilt, the bull who had long since become the Wall Street enemy of Drew, the bear. 54

Vanderbilt had just concluded the merger of his three railroads into the New York Central & 

Hudson River Railroad, and he was anxious to add to it the 770-odd mile Erie system. The Erie was not particularly necessary to Vanderbilt's plans, although it might possibly have become a competitor, but it held considerable appeal for him for two reasons which he held to be quite sufficient. First, was the possibility of another conquest, and second, was the fact that he considered Drew and his crowd as nothing but common gamblers. 

Commodore Vanderbilt therefore announced quite publicly that he intended to get control of Erie and throw Drew's crowd out. It was this impending battle between Drew and Vanderbilt that brought Gould in closer touch with Drew.

The latter needed all the assistance he could marshall, and he secured Gould's support by taking Jay in as a member of the board of directors. 

Gould had been a broker specializing in Erie, and thus his customers controlled many shares of the stock. 

Vanderbilt started his campaign. "Buy Erie as low as possible," he ordered his brokers, "but buy 

Erie." The excitement waxed warmer. Soon it reached fever heat. Every conceivable tactic was employed by the opposing groups. Judges friendly 55

to Vanderbilt issued injunction after injunction against Drew forbidding all sorts of things. Other judges, friendly to Drew also issued many injunctions, these, however, ordering Drew to do many of the things expressly forbidden by the orders of the first judge.

All the time Vanderbilt was pouring millions of dollars into the market buying Erie shares, while

Drew and his associates were selling and pocketing the Commodore's money. With memories quickly calling to mind the Harlem corners of a few years earlier, there were many wagging heads in Wall Street. It began to look as though Drew was in for another squeeze. But it was not to be so, for Drew had prepared well for this opportunity. It was later to be discovered that the Erie directors had quietly authorized the issuance of a $10,000,000 convertible bond issue, with the price set at 722. After Drew had sold all the stock of the road which he owned, his broker had purchased first half of the Erie convertible bond issue, and later the other half.

Just as Vanderbilt was about to work his squeeze on Mr. Drew, Vanderbilt's brokers were stunned to find that 50,000 shares of Erie stock were offered for sale on the market, which was then 83. Another 50,000 shares followed, and the stock broke sharply to 71. It closed the day at 78, 56

but meantime Drew had taken $7,000,000 from the Commodore. With the printing press working for Daniel Drew there was seemingly no end to the number of shares of stock which he could throw at Vanderbilt, so the Commodore ceased his buying operations, and laid plans for recovering his money.

Drew, with Gould and Fisk were together that night tying Vanderbilt's money in bundles when word reached them that the Commodore was planning to send them to jail before the day was over. Acting quickly, the three escaped to New

Jersey, to which point they transferred the head office of the Erie Railroad. They secured fifteen

Jersey City police as an armed guard around their hotel, and additional railroad detectives were placed on watch in the street and wharves in the vicinity. Drew was not going to let Vanderbilt kidnap him or steal back the $7,000,000. Even cannons were mounted on the piers, and Fisk became "Admiral" over some 50 armed men who patrolled the Jersey side of the River in life boats. 

Meantime, Vanderbilt was smarting severely under the defeat, and he could find no one who would buy or lend him money on the Erie shares which were the recent product of the printing press. The Commodore's cash resources had been 57

sorely taxed, and he did not find the experience exactly to his liking. 

Finally in desperation, he sent a note to Drew explaining that he was "sick of the whole damned business. Come and see me." 

Drew did not show the note to Gould and Fisk, thinking he could reach terms with Vanderbilt which would be most satisfactory without counting the other two in on the spoils, but Jay and Jim were becoming exceedingly smart, too, and they followed Drew to the rendezvous. These negotiations were to mark the close of Drew's successes in Wall Street, for while Gould and 

Fisk let Drew keep his ill-gotten gains, and agreed to have the Erie treasury buy back the 100,000 shares of newly printed stock that had been saddled on to Vanderbilt, the condition upon which they agreed to these terms called for 

Drew's withdrawal completely from the Erie picture.

As a result, Drew kept his profits, Vanderbilt got his $7,000,000 back, and Gould and Fisk obtained the Erie.

Vanderbilt learned an expensive lesson from the Erie affair, and in future operations he was exceedingly conservative. He lost but $2,000,000 in the final adjustment of the Erie deal, and when he died in 1877, his estate aggregated over $90,- 58

000,000, indicating that on the whole he was quite successful in preserving his fortune. 

Drew tried several times to make a comeback in Wall Street, failing once in particular through following Gould's advice too closely. They planned to sell Erie short and then depress the entire market by withdrawing large amounts of money which they had previously deposited in several banks. Their pool operation worked most successfully, for when they withdrew their money from the banks the latter were forced to call many of their largest loans to provide the funds. The result was an advance in the call money rate to 

160%. Important business men of New York succeeded, however, in getting the Secretary of the Treasury to release reserve currency to relieve the stringency, and Gould heard of the move in time to go long of the market. He failed to inform 

Drew, however, and Daniel was caught in the ensuing rise in share prices. 

Once again Gould "took" Drew. During a period when the entire market was falling, Drew felt the urge of Wall Street again and sold Erie short in sizable blocks. Gould and Fisk, in close touch with the situation, were aware of Drew's sales and when they felt the time was ripe they ordered a "squeeze." Erie accordingly jumped from 35 to 47 in a single day, and four days later 59

reached 62. Drew went to Gould, trying to effect a compromise, but Jay would have nothing of it, so Daniel finally bought in his shorts at a loss of about $2,000,000.

As a final blow, the panic of 1873 carried with it the brokerage house of Kenyon, Cox & Company, in which Drew was heavily interested, and when at 80 years he went into bankruptcy, the one-time "Big Bear" listed assets of scarcely $500. 

Gould and Fisk participated in one of the most remarkable attempts ever made to corner a market. It was the gold conspiracy, in which Gould hoped to corner the entire floating supply of gold in the country.

He worked months in planning the coup, and at the height of his operations had purchased over $100,000,000 in gold. His unprecedented scheme came to dismal failure on October 4, 1869, when the United States Government began selling its huge gold reserves in order to break the corner.

Ever since then, that date has been known as Black Friday. It was to this occasion that the 

Senate Investigating Committee referred recently when it summoned President Richard Whitney of the New York Stock Exchange to Washington on a memorable Saturday, on rumors that a bear 60

clique in Wall Street was planning another "Black Friday."

The events of that day in 1869 will always be remembered. When Gould started his buying, gold was selling at 130. It rose to 137 within a short time, under the stimulus of his purchase orders. Gould also bought $1,500,000 of gold for the account of Abel Corbin, President Ulysses 

Grant's brother-in-law, who was reputed to have considerable influence over the President, and through whom Gould planned to prevent the Federal Government from breaking his corner in the gold market, which it might do by selling part of its gold holdings that amounted to nearly $100,- 

000,000. Gould's purchases for his own account also eventually reached the stupendous sum of $100,000,000.

When Gould had reached this point, he made several serious mistakes in tactics. He became alarmed lest the government might begin selling gold despite his efforts to forestall it. And with this in mind, he wrote to the Secretary of the Treasury setting forth the advantages to the country of the premium on gold. Through some channel, President Grant heard of Gould's operations, and he also learned of Corbin's activities in the matter. Through Mrs. Grant, the President re- 61

quested Corbin to cease his connections with 

Gould, and Corbin reluctantly complied.

This latest turn of affairs further upset Gould, for with Gould having purchased $100,000,000 in gold, at a time when he knew that the floating supply on the Gold Exchange was only $20,000,ooo, he realized that his squeeze of the shorts who had sold him the additional $80,000,000 of the metal might be rendered completely ineffective if the Government sold its holdings and allowed the shorts to buy gold at a low figure with which to cover their short sales. 

Gould learned from convincing quarters that the government planned to do just what he feared, and it became necessary therefore for him to get rid of his gold before the price broke if he was to avoid bankruptcy. $100,000,000 in gold was not an easy thing to sell in a day or two, however, and he knew that he would break his own market were he to attempt to unload that huge quantity of the metal in so short a time. Yet the circumstances left no time for delay. He set out immediately to achieve his goal. 

On Thursday, October 3, 1869, Gould called in Jim Fisk and instructed him to go on to the floor and mark gold up to 160 no matter how much he had to buy. Gould also instructed Fisk 62

to give his orders to as many different brokers as he could, and to give them verbal orders only. 

So the Exchange opened that morning. Fisk and his principal broker, Albert Speyer, began buying, along with Belden and other members of Gould's pool. The offerings were heavy, but still 

Gould's crowd bought and bought. Brokers on the floor could see that Gould's men were doing almost all of the buying. No one knew who was selling, that is, no one but Jay Gould. In the first two hours of the session he sold $50,000,000 of his gold, and before the day was over he had sold the entire $100,000,000. He had obtained top prices, too, all because Fisk and his various brokers had made the market for him. And of course, they had also been the buyers of most of 

Gould's gold.

The pool members met that evening to discuss their next move. They decided that they had enough gold to complete their squeeze and planned to take control of the market the next day. Gould attended the meeting, but he sat silently, giving no inkling of the fact that the gold they had bought was what he had sold. So Fisk, Speyer and the others completed their plans for the big squeeze. 

The next day, October 4, 1869, turned into the notorious Black Friday. Excitement on the Ex- 63

change was of panic proportions when trading opened. Gould was there but remaining significantly silent. Not a word did he utter, merely sitting in a corner tearing paper into bits. Trading commenced. Fisk stalked around the floor offering to bet $50,000 that gold would go to $200 before the day was ended. Fisk and his brokers bought gold at 145, then the price jumped to 150. Still they bought; 155, then 160. Wall Street and most of New York was in feverish excitement. Fisk bid 

161 for $5,000,000 in gold. There was not a single seller. Then he bid 162 for five million. 

Again he yelled "162 for $5,000,000," and this time a canny Scottish banker, James Brown, who had led the opposition to Gould's pool, quietly answered "sold at 162." 

Immediately word spread like wildfire that the government was selling its gold. Pandemonium broke loose. Gould still sat silent. He knew that this day would shake the country to its very foundation, but it had been necessary for him to get rid of that $100,000,000 in gold. Like a plummet the quotation dropped to 140. The telegram from

Washington had arrived. The government really was selling. Fisk and his brokers continued their crazy bidding. They bid 160 while gold was selling at 140. Albert Speyer went crazy as he marched 64

around the floor yelling, "Shoot me, shoot me," all the time bidding 160, 170, 180.

At the close of Black Friday, gold was back to 

135. Gould's corner had failed, but he had not lost a cent. Hundreds of brokerage firms failed as a result, however, and Jim Fisk quite naturally was tremendously involved. He had bought so much gold on Gould's orders that he could never hope to pay for it, and yet Gould had known that this would happen and had planned for just this contingency. That was his reason for instructing 

Fisk to give all his buying orders verbally, and to put none of them in writing.

The final plan called for Fisk's repudiation of all the purchases he had made through Speyer and Belden, his principal brokers. With no written orders, and with Fisk's word unchallengeable on the Street, this would leave Belden and Speyer with the entire burden. They were to be declared bankrupt, as indeed they very much were, and as compensation Gould would give Belden and 

Speyer an income for life. Boss Tweed again entered the picture here, for his word was practically law in New York City, and through his influence the courts restrained anyone from enforcing contracts against Fisk or Gould, making them accept Belden and Speyer as the actual purchasers. Although the courts were crowded for 65

years with legal actions arising from the Gold Conspiracy, so effective was Tweed's control that nothing ever came of them.

Gould held on to his entire gains, despite efforts to disgorge them. But very shortly he was to lose his able friend and assistant, Jim Fisk, by death, and thereafter Gould's affairs were less tranquil. 

The entire nation was not only shocked at the developments, but an English group who were large holders of Erie securities succeeded in ousting Gould from the railroad, and secured a return from him of a substantial sum of money which it was charged he had illegally received from the road. 

Gould's day was over.

AFTER THE CIVIL WAR 

The period following the Civil War was one of furious activity in the construction of transcontinental railroads and of manipulation in their securities. Among the largest of these enterprises was the Northern Pacific Railway, a $100,000,ooo venture, and it was to play a vital part in the history of a gentleman by the name of Jay Cooke, who attempted to finance the road alone when financial conditions did not allow sale of securities to the public. It was the failure of Jay Cooke & 

Company that precipitated the panic of 1873. The 66

failure of Cooke's firm at that time was as serious as that of J. P. Morgan & Company would be today. 

As a result of this firm's failure, over 35 other brokerage houses and banks closed their doors that day. So severe was the situation that the Stock 

Exchange closed for a period of ten days, one of the few times in its history that such action had been taken. The depression had set in, however, and after the Exchange reopened there were many more failures. Security prices fell steadily and sharply. Unemployment followed and general business distress was acute. Altogether the situation then was quite similar to that which has prevailed in the past three years.

One of the most colorful of the plungers in Wall Street during the early 70's was James R. Keene, who had left his native England for the sunny shores of California when he was some seventeen age. years of Keene had a varied career, including a position as a newspaperman for a while, after which he studied law. In this latter field he specialized in mining cases, of which there were many in the West in those days. Keene quite naturally took many gambles in mining stocks, in fact there were very few Westerners of the 70's who did not.

Jim Keene was remarkably successful in his flyers and in one purchase particularly he profited very 67

handsomely. Keene commenced a vacation in

Europe where he intended to remain about a year. 

Before departing he bought several hundred shares of stock at a few dollars a share. In his absence a great boom in the shares developed and he returned to this country to find his few hundred shares worth a total of $200,000. 

Keene's natural gambling instinct served him well at this point, for he figured immediately that the stock had risen altogether too rapidly and was riding to a fall. He consequently sold out his holdings and went short of the market in heavy amount. His judgment was excellent, and the ensuing decline increased his fortune to around the $3,000,000 mark.

Having amassed this sum through speculative operations, Keene journeyed to Wall Street where bigger game could be stalked. In New York his operations were attended by even greater success than he had known on the Pacific Coast. Within a short time his profits had risen to $13,000,000, and his activities broadened into commodity markets and the race tracks. Undoubtedly Keene's phenomenal luck went to his head. He was idolized on all sides, with his "native" California being especially loud in its acclamation; and so the path was paved for the undoing of Jim Keene. 

Jay Gould, who was a power in the market at 68

the time, was instrumental in forcing Keene's hand, based on information relayed to him by Addison Cammack, one of the less known traders on the Street, that Keene was attempting a corner in the grain market, at the same time that he held large blocks of his favorite stocks. Cammack informed Gould of his opinion that Keene had overextended himself, and Gould accordingly backed Cammack in an attack on Keene's pet stocks. The selling commenced and when Keene was called for margin by his brokers, his castle began to tumble. 

Jim sold out his enormous holdings of wheat at a tremendous loss to provide funds with which to stem the tide of selling against his stocks. This was not enough, however, and he was forced to let some of his stocks go too. This only aggravated the condition, of course, just the same as when any margin trader sells out some of his holdings in order to protect other shares which are also held on margin. Keene, just like any margin trader, was thereby breaking his own market, and was soon wiped out entirely.

Cammack and Gould made a two-sided operation of the matter, for with Keene out of the picture they stopped applying the pressure and bulled the stocks they had previously been selling. The advance was as sharp as the previous decline had been. While Jim Keene was of course unable to 69

participate in the rally, it is a fact that had he been able to hold on to his stocks throughout the decline and during the subsequent rise, he not only would have recovered all of his capital but more than $10,000,000 besides.

A few years more and we were to see a radical change in the types of men who became leaders of Wall Street circles. Practically all the bigger lights of the railroad construction era had been out and out speculators, men who gambled in stocks both on the bull and the bear side of the market.

The next era was to be one of vast railroad consolidations and the beginning of an industrial era which has not concluded to this day. E. H. 

Harriman, James J. Hill, John D. Rockefeller, Andrew Carnegie, Charles M. Schwab and J. Pierpont Morgan, the Elder, were the names that figured high in the late '80's, and in the concluding years of the Nineteenth Century, as well as up to the present time in some cases. 

Cornelius Vanderbilt's son, William H. Vanderbilt, served a distinct purpose in this period. 

He had succeeded to the control of his father's vast properties, and became famous-or infamous -for his alleged remark, "The public be damned!" He was also to make another statement somewhat at variance with his more greatly popularized saying, at the time that he disposed of a 70

large part of his holdings in New York Central 

Railroad. William H. Vanderbilt informed a

Wall Street syndicate that "There is a certain feeling among the public about one man having so much-I won't say it's wrong or that it's right, but there is such a feeling. I am a man who understands the public sentiment and I am always ready to meet it." And he thereupon sold his holdings in 

Central to the syndicate at a price of $120 a share, which was about 10 points below the market for the stock.

The importance of this transaction lies in the fact that it brought into the limelight a man who was to be a tremendous force in the financial world from that day forward, namely, J. Pierpont Morgan, who was then the junior partner in the banking firm of Drexel, Morgan & Company. Morgan was the principal figure in the negotiations with young

Vanderbilt for the sale of his Central stock, and he was extremely successful in distributing the syndicate's purchase at substantial profits. 

As a result of this success, Mr. Morgan assumed a very important place among the financial leaders of the day. Late in the panic of 1884-1885, which was precipitated largely through the failure of the brokerage firm of Grant & Ward, of which former President Ulysses S. Grant was a partner, young J. Pierpont Morgan took the initiative in 71

stemming the tide of depression which had assumed serious proportions. Through masterful negotiations he was instrumental in inducing the 

Pennsylvania Railroad, West Shore Railroad and

New York Central to stop their rate-cutting war and co-operate more freely. This masterful stroke brought an immediate reversal in the trend of security prices and eventually led to a general bull movement. The days of the trusts and super-consolidations were dawning.

In the railroad field the various mergers followed one upon the other in such rapidity that by the beginning of the present century the major systems were controlled by only six groups, known by names that still stick today, in most cases. 

These groups were the Vanderbilt, Morgan, Harriman and Pennsylvania, each of which controlled about 20,000 miles of road; the Gould group with 16,000 miles, and the Hill roads of 5,000 miles. And the dominant industrial position that had been gained by the railroads served to install their bankers - Morgan, Rockefeller and

Kuhn, Loeb-in the rôles of the nation's outstanding banking leaders-a rôle they have largely maintained to this very day. 

Morgan, the greatest banker of them all, soon came to be regarded as both a genius and a saviour in times of depression. He again came to the res- 72

cue of the country in the panic of 1893, which was really precipitated by passage of the Sherman Silver Purchase Act in 1890. The Act had resulted in a severe drain on the gold reserves of the nation, and the stringency became so great that 642 banks, many brokers, and over 15,000 business firms failed in the depression that followed. Even the great Union Pacific and Northern Pacific railroads went into bankruptcy.

The situation was desperate, so far as the United States was concerned, for the Government needed gold badly. 

One night found J. P. Morgan on a train bound from New York to Washington, accompanied by 

August Belmont, who represented the Rothschilds in this country. These two men visited President 

Cleveland early the next morning and arranged the $62,000,000 gold loan that brought both praise and condemnation to the House of Morgan. Despite the long profit that the Morgan 

Belmont syndicate made on the loan, their ability to supply gold to the country undoubtedly averted a serious situation, the effects of which it would be difficult to imagine. And Morgan, after all, was a banker and business man, and not a philanthropist.

While Morgan was thus becoming one of the greatest money powers of the world, of which more will be said later, another financial giant was 73

expanding his field of influence by leaps and bounds.

A young Cleveland produce commission merchant by the name of John D. Rockefeller, was at 

22 years of age what would be considered even today very successful. This was in 1861, and in this year Rockefeller and his partner showed their first interest in the oil industry. Seeing the profitable possibilities of this new field they began modestly to operate an oil refinery, the branch of the industry possessing the best prospects in their judgment.

In the first four years of oil refining, Rockefeller and his partner netted over $100,000, and with this excellent progress Rockefeller's imagination was fired to the extent that he decided to make the oil industry his sole interest. His ideas were considerably larger than his pocketbook, so he sought out friends who would assist him. After considerable persuasion he succeeded in interesting Henry M. Flagler, who in turn secured the active cooperation of Cleveland's wealthiest citizen, Stephen Harkness.

Immediately they set out upon a series of mer gers. The Standard Oil Company of Cleveland was organized with original capital of $1,000,ooo, and small refineries here and there were purchased or acquired through exchange of stock. 74

Other shrewd men came into the fold as the Standard Oil expanded, including such as William 

H. Vanderbilt, Henry H. Rogers, Charles Pratt and John D. Archbold. Smaller units were continually added to the rapidly growing Standard 

Oil, added at an ever-rising scale.

Much has been said and written of the methods used by the Rockefeller group during this period of rapid expansion, and it is entirely true that they did enter into many secret agreements designed to benefit the Standard Oil and to crush their competitors, but at the same time Rockefeller justified these unethical practices through his willingness and even eagerness to welcome into the Standard Oil practically any competing company that expressed a desire to merge. Not only was this the fact of the situation, but those competitors who did take advantage of Rockefeller's offers realized enormous profits from the Standard Oil stock that they received in payment for their properties. 

Rockefeller in this way became the dean of the oil barons. His properties, securities and cash holdings became so great that it was but natural that he should also have become a power in the banking field-through the Equitable Trust Company of New York, for the most part, and through the present Chase National Bank of New

York, since that bank absorbed the Equitable insti- 75

tution. While Rockefeller, Sr., nor his son, John 

D., Jr., are neither of them a director of the

Chase National, they are very capably represented by five or six members of the Board of Chase 

National Bank, America's largest.

During the same years that Rockefeller's ascension to the peak of petroleum leadership occurred, the vast expansion of the railroads of the western part of the country took place. Those were the days of Jim Hill and Edward H. Harriman.

The first part of their story is one of the romance of great constructions. Hill had early settled in the Middle West, in that territory where the broad prairies began. After very indifferent success in several mercantile ventures and in the shipping business, Hill found his great opportunity in the panic of 1873. 

The badly managed, financially weak St. Paul &

Pacific Railroad went into receivership early in that depression. The road was in miserable physical condition, as well, and was quite aptly characterized as "two streaks of rust and a right of way."

Hill and his associates, Donald Smith and Norman Kittson, determined to buy this railroad, despite their complete lack of cash. After lengthy, disheartening negotiations, principally with Dutch investors who held the bonds of the defunct rail- 76

road, they succeeded in acquiring the St. Paul & 

Pacific in exchange for stock and bonds of a new company that they organized. 

Their great experiment then began. Instead of endeavoring to drum up patronage for their road in the sparsely settled sections of the Northwest, they began to advertise the advantages of the Northwest, to press-agent the entire eastern section of the United States, and even in far-off 

Europe. Hill's railroad offered free transportation to anyone who would settle in the St. Paul & 

Pacific territory, and so successful was this unique scheme that settlers flocked westward by the thousands. Practically one-third of the entire population of Denmark was attracted. Jim Hill created a vast patronage for his road where none had existed before. His success brought also the financial interest and assistance of the House of Morgan, and with it came the domination of the most prosperous railroads of the Northwest, the Northern Pacific and the Great Northern.

Meanwhile, E. H. Harriman had acquired a prominent position among the railroad pioneers of the country and was casting covetous eyes on 

Hill's profitable properties. 

Harriman, in 1869, was a clerk on the Stock 

Exchange, then being only 21. The next year he bought himself a seat on the Exchange with 77

$3,he borrowed from a wealthy uncle. He enjoyed a very profitable commission business as a broker for a number of years, and in one speculative venture for his own account he was reasonably successful. Later, however, another operation which he attempted to engineer on the Exchange ran afoul of the opposing activities of John Jacob 

Astor, and Harriman found his earlier profits gone, together with part of his capital. 

Somewhat sobered by his experience, he thereafter confined his market operations solely to commission work, and satisfied his speculative desires through several business projects. He first bought a Hudson River steamer, and later purchased a 

34-mile railroad. The latter he successfully resold to the Pennsylvania at a very fine profit, which led him to buy and resell other roads in similar fashion-always aiming at higher stakes. 

One of these roads was a small line in the Middle West, the Illinois Central. He was anxious to merge this road with one of the larger systems, and he saw his opportunity during the depression of 1893. 

At that time the powerful banking House of

Kuhn, Loeb & Company was engaged in reorganizing the Union Pacific Railroad. Jacob H. Schiff, the senior partner of the banking firm, was handling the negotiations. He had not been at it long, 78

however, before he found that his plans were meeting with concerted opposition from some indeterminable quarter. Schiff thought that Morgan was behind the movement, but upon inquiring of J. P. and offering the suggestion that Kuhn, Loeb would gladly accept Morgan's co-operation in the reorganization, Schiff was informed by Morgan that the latter had no knowledge of the opposition, and also had no interest in the future of the 

Union Pacific. Morgan was, however, quite willing to assist Schiff in finding out the source of the opposition. Through the facilities of his vast organization he soon discovered that Harriman was the man to be reckoned with. 

Schiff immediately sent for Harriman and asked his reason for opposing Kuhn, Loeb's plans, to which E. H. replied, "I want the Union Pacific for myself."

Schiff's smile ended the conversation, but it was not many days before he again sent for Harriman. 

He had discovered that Harriman's campaign was so well organized that reorganization of the Union Pacific was impossible without some compromise. 

Harriman's demands were not great. He wished a substantial stock interest in Union Pacific on the same terms granted to Kuhn, Loeb, and he 79

also wished to be made a director and Chairman of the Executive Committee.

All his desires but the last were allowed, and Schiff, in withholding the Chairmanship which he had already promised to another man, assured Harriman that if he eventually proved to be the right man for the job it would be given to him. 

Harriman was not established in the social register, nor was he particularly noted for great wealth, to the end that his colleagues on the Union Pacific board were for some time cold and distant to him. He soon demonstrated his ability, however. By 1900 he had proved his worth and gained the coveted position as Chairman of the Executive 

Committee. Not only had Harriman been dominating the policies and operations of the road during these years, but he had also been buying up stock of the road until he was the controlling interest when he became Chairman. Kuhn, Loeb had found co-operation with Harriman very profitable.

In the meantime, Jim Hill, backed by J. P. 

Morgan, had been extending his control of Northern Pacific, Great Northern and other northwestern roads. Hill had his desires built up to securing control of Chicago, Burlington & Quincy, which would give the Northern Pacific a terminus at Chicago. 80

Kuhn, Loeb and Harriman also saw the value of the Burlington road, at about the same time. 

Not only did they wish to defeat Hill's plans, and thus limit the dominance of his roads, but the Burlington paralleled much of the Union Pacific and was a potential competitor. 

Kuhn, Loeb made the first attempt to secure control of Burlington. Schiff and Harriman formed a pool with George Gould, Jay's son, and Stillman, at that time president of National City Bank, and they began buying the stock in the open market.

They desired 200,000 shares or enough for control. From May, 1900, into July they succeeded in buying 80,300 shares at an average price of $124.

The final 10,000 shares of this Burlington stock was most difficult to secure, requiring six weeks. 

The price continued to advance to $140 in December, but they had not bought any more stock, having figured that it was impossible to gain control in the open market.

Having reached that decision the syndicate began to liquidate its holdings which then showed a profit. They sold 60,300 shares at an average price of $135, and then distributed the remaining stock, 5,000 shares each, to the four members of the pool.

All during this time Morgan and Hill had been negotiating with the Burlington directors, and in 81

March, 1901, succeeded in buying control from the majority stockholders for $200 a share. The purchase was made for the account of Northern Pacific.

Kuhn, Loeb was not inclined to drop the challenge even here. They made a demand on Morgan for a share in the Burlington road, which, of course, was refused. The real battle thereupon commenced, with Kuhn, Loeb determined to buy Northern Pacific itself, if necessary, to gain control of Burlington.

Northern Pacific at the time had $80,000,000 in common stock outstanding, and $75,000,000 preferred. Both classes of stock enjoyed equal voting rights. Morgan and Hill owned but $35,000,ooo of the stock, but with the scattered holdings this amount was sufficient for controlling purposes under ordinary circumstances. Schiff and Harriman, however, launched a buying campaign in March, 1901, to buy up control. The campaign lasted six weeks, and so quietly was it conducted that their opponents were entirely unaware of it. 

By May, 1901, Kuhn, Loeb's group had bought $37,000,000 in common and $41,000,000 in preferred stock of Northern Pacific, and Schiff thereupon informed J. P. Morgan & Company that his group had control. 

Morgan himself was in Europe, where he had 82

just completed the purchase of England's second largest shipping company, but one of his partners, Robert Bacon, who had been left in charge in New York, handled the matter. The Morgan group had a trump card up their sleeve which must have been totally unknown to Kuhn, Loeb, for in the by-laws of the Northern Pacific was a clause allowing directors of the road to call the preferred stock for redemption on January 1 of any year. If Morgan could gain a clear majority of the common stock, and then redeem the preferred, Kuhn, Loeb's erstwhile control would be removed. 

Bacon thereupon cabled Morgan for permission to buy up 150,000 shares of Northern Pacific in the open market. Permission was immediately received, and on the next Monday morning Bacon issued orders to buy the 150,000 shares of Northern Pacific.

The orders were executed in a single day, sending the price of the stock from 112 to 14934, and immediately the directors authorized redemption of the preferred stock on the next January 1, before the next stockholders' meeting. Morgan had retained control of Northern Pacific, but in the process, serious affairs had taken place on the 

Stock Exchange. 

The sharp uprush in the price of Northern Pacific had attracted a considerable number of 83

shorts, who, of course, knew nothing of the undercover operations, or of their meaning. They recognized quite rightly that the rise in Northern Pacific was abnormal and that it was bound to drop again, and quite sharply. They sold short in heavy amounts. Suddenly they found that there was no stock to borrow. The two rival factions, Morgan and Kuhn, Loeb, had taken practically every share of stock that was in the open market, and had even bought from the shorts some stock that was not actually in existence. 

As these shorts frantically tried to buy in their shares, the price continued to go up, and a serious squeeze developed. The Northern Pacific corner, about which much has been written, developed as a result of this situation, on May 9, 1901. 

The stock opened at 170, up ten points from the previous close. The shorts became desperate. 

Not only were they forcing the price of Northern Pacific up on themselves in trying to cover, but they forced the remainder of the market off many points as they dumped stocks right and left in order to protect themselves while they endeavored to get a fair settlement in their Northern Pacific. 

Twenty minutes after the market opened, the stock was up to 205. Every sale was for cash-at 

225, 230, then 280. Call money jumped to 70%, and a premium of 85% was demanded for the loan 84

of Northern Pacific stock, with only a very few shares available for borrowing even at that figure. 

The next sale of the stock was at 300. Then 650, and 700, and one desperate bear bought in at $1,000 a share.

In the meantime the rest of the market had dropped like a plummet, declining from 50 to 75 points. Union Pacific was off 57 points, Atchison 

47 points and Delaware & Hudson 74 points.

Hill and Harriman had not intended to produce such a corner, nor were they desirous of aiding in its continuance, particularly when other stocks were being made to suffer so greatly, so at 

2:30 that afternoon, they announced that they would lend their stock to the shorts, pending negotiations for a fair settlement figure. The relief was immediate. Call money dropped to 40%, immediately to 6% and at the close was down to 3%. Stocks rallied as sharply as they had broken, and the next morning the Morgan and Kuhn, Loeb groups announced that they would settle with the shorts at $150 a share. The total transactions on the exchange that memorable day were 3,200,000, the record up to that time, and even an active session compared with the dull days of 1932. 

The unintentional Northern Pacific corner had been broken, but there was still the matter of control of Northern Pacific for the two groups to 85

battle over. If the preferred were retired, Kuhn, Loeb would not have control of the road. But if 

Morgan did retire the preferred he was smart enough to realize that the fine line governing the rights of the opposing party would lead to a bitter and long-drawn-out legal battle. The natural result was a compromise. The two groups went into the situation on even terms. A new concern, the

Whereas the 1800's had seen Wall Street talking and working with millions of dollars, the Twentieth Century was still young when this figure rose to billions. The World War produced many millionaires, and in the Coolidge bull market of 1924-1929, pool operations were known to produce profits of from $4,000,000 to $5,000,000 in a single week's time, something that even a Vanderbilt or Gould was fortunate to accomplish in many months.

WILLIAM C. DURANT 

Not one whit of exaggeration is required to state that William Crapo Durant dominated the greatest bull market this country has ever known.

No man ever wielded so much financial power before or since as that credited to Durant during the years from 1924 to 1929, and yet he was noted as being the "most successful failure" on the Street.

Durant was a rich man in 1905, broke in 1910, owner of the wealthy General Motors Company in 1915, and lost his $90,000,000 fortune again in 1920. Yet by 1924 he had again amassed $20,- 000,000. 

Born in Boston, Massachusetts, on December 8, 1861, Durant and his parents moved shortly afterward to Flint, Michigan, where the young man was educated in the public schools. 

On entering the business world with no money, 96

but an indomitable will, never-ending courage and complete confidence in his ability, he borrowed $1,000 and with a friend founded the Durant- 

Dort Carriage Company in 1886. This venture prospered greatly, becoming the largest carriage manufacturing concern in the world, with an output aggregating 150,000 units a year.

Durant was quick to grasp the tremendous financial possibilities of the new automobile industry, and in 1903 he organized the Buick Motor Company. In 1908, immediately following the 

1907 panic, he formed the General Motors Company and in the next two years purchased the 

Cadillac, Oakland, Oldsmobile and several other companies.

He capitalized General Motors at $10,000,000, an almost unheard of figure for a new company in those days. His friends wondered at his folly, believing earnings of the company would never justify such a heavy capitalization.

Their surprise was to be even greater, however, when the very next year the gross sales of General Motors totaled over $34,000,000, with net profits exceeding $10,000,000.

Profits rolled in so rapidly that Durant was freely offered large credits for expansion of his concern, and he used those facilities copiously in 97

adding body plants, accessory factories and other adjuncts to his company.

In 1910 the bankers of the country faced a condition of panic, and as suddenly as they had granted huge credits to Durant, so did their fear immediately cause them to as rapidly withdraw them. As a result control of General Motors passed from Durant to the bankers, and Durant had to start all over again.

And begin he did. He immediately organized the Chevrolet Motor Company and when it began to return sizable profits early in 1915, he did an amazing thing. He offered General Motors stockholders the opportunity to exchange five shares of Chevrolet for one share of General Motors. He also went into the market and bought stock, advancing it from $24 a share to $558. 

When the directors' meeting in October, 1915, arrived, he had secured sufficient stock so that with 

3,000 shares owned by John J. Raskob and Pierre

S. duPont, he had full control of General Motors once again.

Durant continued his affiliation with Raskob and duPont, and by 1920 the duPont Powder 

Company had invested $50,000,000 of its wartime profits in General Motors.

The enterprise had been remarkably successful. 

Durant had accumulated a fortune of $90,000,000 98

by 1920. General Motors had launched a $300,- 

000,000 expansion program, even though Durant had bitterly opposed it, and in the midst of the expansion, with the company $200,000,000 in debt, the panic and depression of 1920 hit the 

United States.

General Motors was in serious need of additional money. An offering of $64,000,000 in stock was attempted. Part of it was underwritten by an English group at $20 a share, despite the fact that the stock was then selling in the market for $38½. No underwriter for the remainder could be found. Meanwhile the stock was declining sharply.

Durant gathered together a group of friends to support the stock. They bought heavily at 32. 

They bought more at 30, with Durant guaranteeing his friends against loss. The stock dropped to

On December 1, 1920, 59 years old and gray haired, he walked into the offices of General Motors Corporation, said good-bye, tendered his resignation and left the shattered hulk of his great enterprise to the piloting of duPont and Raskob.

Durant would not accept defeat, however. A month later he organized Durant Motors, Inc., which started him back on the road to wealth once more. This company never was a great success, however, and in 1929 Durant pledged himself to pay back to original investors any money that they had lost in their Durant stock. 100

Within two years Durant was a powerful factor in the market, with a following running into the hundred thousands. He created somewhat of a sensation through his spectacular operations in

His leadership in the great Coolidge bull market of 1924-1929 was almost fabulous. He threw his own millions into bullish operations, and many millions more furnished him by the greatest pool ever organized, composed of some 25 multimillionaires. Under his personal direction he bought for the pool in 1928 alone more than $1,250,000,ooo in stocks, while the partners in the pool invested at least two or three billions in addition on his advice. In that year Durant handled over

There had never been anything like Durant's pool before. Millionaires walked quietly into his office, left checks for millions of dollars, and as quietly departed. Although Durant said in 1928 that "No one man or group can or does control," this Croesus came as near to being THE market himself in 1928 as anyone probably ever will be.

Despite the sensational character of Durant's market operations and the remarkable success that attended his work from 1920 forward, one incident that took place early in 1929 serves to illustrate the sagacity of the man and his great courage.

Mr. Hoover listened carefully, questioned occasionally, but was non-committal. 

The Federal Reserve, as everyone knows, did not change its policies, and the next month, May 1929, Durant who had meanwhile sailed to Europe, began to unload the huge holdings of his pool. Within a month prices had dropped twenty to thirty points. But Durant had sold out his entire line of stocks, had withdrawn nearly four billion dollars from the market. Some losses were taken in the decline, of course, but the profits that had 104

The panic of late October and November, 1929, made Durant's prophecy into fact, and the world has been struggling to recover from it ever since.

Durant retired with many millions. He had broken the five-year cycle of earlier years which had seen him rich then poor again every five years, and he had taken his profits before the crash. 

As the years 1930-1933 pass by with signs of awakening bullishness, it is interesting to recall a remark made in December, 1929, by William 

Crapo Durant, who now is over 72 years old.

He said then: "Eventually we will take control again.' 

Perhaps he will.

ARTHUR W. CUTTEN 

It took Arthur Cutten five years to save his first 105

$1,000, from 1890 to 1895. In the same length of time thirty years later his Wall Street operations brought him profits of more than $150,- 000,000.

Next to Durant, Cutten was probably the most spectacular operator in stocks in the Coolidge market. So great did his fame become that practically every move in the market was attributed to his buying. He was a member of the Durant group, but his personal operations were nearly as great and were remarkably successful.

In 1890, when Cutten was 20 years of age, he came to Chicago from his birthplace in Guelph, Canada, going to work for A. S. White & Company, grain brokers. His salary was only $7.50 a week, but by 1897 he had saved sufficient money to enable him to purchase a seat on the Chicago Board of Trade, where the knowledge he had gained from his apprenticeship with A. S. White proved invaluable.

Within ten years Cutten was a millionaire and soon became an international figure in the grain markets of the world. He has probably dealt in more grain at one time than any other man that ever lived.

Cutten had but one major setback in his grain operations, when J. Ogden Armour outfoxed him 106

in 1922. Cutten held from 2,000,000 to 3,000,000 bushels of wheat for May delivery, yet there was only about half that amount available in the Chicago grain elevators. Armour was back of the elevators in this operation, and faced a heavy loss if he could not deliver the wheat to Cutten when the May contracts expired. Armour succeeded, however, in inducing the Board of Trade to declare wheat on freight cars and private elevators as good delivery, and though Cutten fought the ruling bitterly, Armour prevailed, whereupon he rushed wheat into Chicago by rail and made good on his deliveries to Cutten. As a result, wheat broke from $1.46 per bushel to $1.16, with Cutten a heavy loser. 

Cutten was to profit indirectly later, however, from the operations of Mr. Armour, in taking advantage of opportunities arising out of the postwar depression of 1920. Armour had become heavily involved as a result of that 1920 crash, and when he died he owed Armour & Company some $18,000,000. In 1928, Cutten bought 600,- 

000 shares of Armour B stock which Armour had pledged against his debt, and was elected to the directorate of the company. Cutten bought the stock for about $3,900,000, which was 25% under the market price at the time. 107

The greatest coup that Cutten scored in the grain market came immediately after he had cleaned up $1,500,000 in corn. It was in 1924, and Cutten methodically began to buy wheat. His purchases were heavy, but he used every known tactic to conceal his activity. Wheat was down near $1 a bushel when he began his operations, and he was to be instrumental in sending the price up beyond $2 before he stopped. 

During all his buying, however, Cutten was aware that some stranger was riding hard behind him. When Cutten bought, the stranger bought. 

When Cutten sold, the stranger's selling orders echoed immediately. It was a new experience for

JESSE L. LIVERMORE 

The "Boy Plunger" was a title that clung to Jesse Livermore for many years after he emerged from the 1907 panic with profits of $3,000,000 from his bear operations. Later another title was added, this time, the "Big Bear." And for the past twenty-five years whenever stocks suddenly took a downward surge, those who always try to blame such things on some one person could be heard remarking "Livermore selling." More often than not, Livermore had no connection at all with the stock in question, but such reports about various market operators have become a commonplace in recent years.

Those who know Livermore appreciate, too, that the title "Big Bear" is something of a misnomer, despite the fact that Livermore has played every decline since 1906 for all it was worth. 

Notwithstanding his bearishness at such times,

Jesse himself is responsible for the statement that he has made more money as a bull than as a bear. 

And his various operations conclusively show this to be true. 

Fifty-five now, Livermore looks ten years younger, to that extent still meriting the "Boy 114

Plunger" title of 1907, and he prefers it to the other.

As said before, Livermore has been blamed for almost every decline that has occurred in Wall Street since his first bear coup of 1907. He was blamed for the dark days of 1920 and 1921, and they blamed him at first for the crash of 1929.

In 1940 or 1950, when another set-back may visit us, most likely we will again hear the words whispered from boardroom to boardroom "Livermore raiding the market."

With very few exceptions, Livermore has always played a lone hand. Unlike operators such as Cutten, he never holds a position in any stock for very long. His uncanny trading ability was developed when he was a mere youth employed as a clerk in a Boston brokerage house.

His success in Boston led him to larger fields, and he went to Chicago with the few thousand dollars he had accumulated. He was practically unknown in New York when he emerged with his $3,000,000 profits from the 1907 panic, but his prestige since has grown to proportions that led Presidents of the United States and leading bankers of Wall Street to seek his advice and assistance at times when constructive market operations were planned to help the promotion of prosperity. 

President Harding is said to have summoned him 115

to Washington in 1922, and before President

Coolidge was elected, Livermore made a startling prediction—that is, it was startling for a man who was held to be forever a bear. As he was leaving for Florida on December 29, 1922, eleven months prior to the election, he said: "I expect the prospect of Coolidge's victory to furnish the basis for higher prices. So far the advance has only been in an investment phase in which quiet absorption of standard issues by influential interests has been in progress. But later on 

I think the movement will be characterized by all the speculative excitement of big bull markets of former years."

He had made a similar bullish statement in 1921 at the bottom of the bear market of that year, saying that the deflation was at an end and rising prices could be expected. 

His prediction in 1921 proved remarkably correct, partly because he threw the full force of the millions he had made in the bear market into a personal bull operation of considerable magnitude. 

He bought heavily in Baldwin Locomotive, Studebaker, American Locomotive, General Electric and United States Steel. His friend, George 

Whelan, who then controlled the extensive United 

Cigar Store properties, agreed to run Mexican 

Petroleum and Pan-American Petroleum for him, 116

and so in a few days the bull market that Livermore had predicted began in earnest. 

All of his stocks moved up sharply. Mexican 

Petroleum had jumped 75 points by June of 1922.

The advance in the other stocks was but slightly less extensive. And not only did Livermore make several millions in profits during that movement, but even today it furnishes him with a hearty laugh when he recalls the rumors that were circulated during the rise to the effect that he was caught heavily short and had lost several million dollars. 

He has lost very much money at times, however. He was flat broke in 1913, but starting on the veritable shoestring he began a comeback in 1914. 

The World War ended this attempt, however, and the crash of prices that came with the closing of the Stock Exchange, forced him into bankruptcy in 1915, owing some $4,000,000. 

By 1916 he was again on the way back, with borrowed capital, and by bull operations in Bethlehem and United States Steel he was able to pay off his creditors in full and buy $1,000,000 of 

Liberty bonds as well.

He continued his bullishness on the steel stocks for two more years, but in 1919 his uncanny intuition told him that a crash was near. He abruptly changed to a bear, going short of Atlantic Gulf & West Indies, American Sumatra and many other 117

active stocks. He even sold short 40,000 shares of

Mexican Petroleum. The post-war crash of 1920, which carried with it the great General Motors Corporation, naturally brought large gains to such a foresighted speculator. 

When Livermore deserted Wall Street for the 

Chicago grain pits in 1924, his friends declare he took $10,000,000 of stock profits with him. He made as much more in the grain pits, especially in the move in which he followed Arthur Cutten so closely. 

At that time he began buying wheat at $1.04 a bushel and held it until the price was close to $2. 

His ability to change from bull to bear in a flash served him well in that operation for the rise in wheat culminated on January 28, 1925 at $2.04 a bushel, and by April 3, 1925 had sunk to $1.36.

Livermore had sold out practically at the top, and the moment the price began to slip he became a bear, selling short something between 10,000,000 and 15,000,000 bushels of wheat. He made millions of dollars of profit on the crash, and judged it so accurately that he caught it on the rebound. 

Livermore was no beginner in the grain pits in 1924, for as early as 1908 he had been active in commodity operations. He made a $500,000 killing in cotton that year. He lost $5,000,000 in cotton in six weeks in 1923, and in 1927 played the 118

cotton market on its advance from 13 to 19 cents a pound. During the World War markets he dealt in 5,000,000 bushel lots of wheat, and since 1929 Livermore has been one of the principal traders in the grain pit.

The recent inauguration of a heavy government tax on short sales seems likely to work somewhat of a hardship on an operator whose trading tactics are like those of Jesse Livermore. For even when he is bulling the market he always has some short stock out just as a matter of protection. He may be long of 80,000 shares, but at the same time makes certain to be short of perhaps 20,000 shares.

During the long period of rising prices of the 

Coolidge bull market, Livermore was less a factor in the market than for many years. He was in and out in a small way many times, but was neither bulling nor bearing to any great extent. Throughout the years he occasionally tested the market by selling a few thousand shares of stock to see if the time had arrived to begin bear tactics. But his nimbleness enabled him to do this testing without great loss.

Many may be surprised to learn that the "Big Bear" was actually a bull right up to the time that prices began to break in 1929. Through August and September of that year he maintained a 119

long line of such stocks as Radio, General Motors, American & Foreign Power, North American and 

Anaconda. With the very beginning of the severe crash, however, he sent his holdings overboard and went short of stocks throughout the list. His entire fortune went into his bear operations, and he emerged with new millions. But he operated only for himself. He did not head any bear raiding pool, as the public was wont to believe, nor had he begun his selling before the decline commenced. 

It was natural, though, for the public to blame Livermore for the break, for no other man had a reputation big enough to fill such an order. And so the public's eyes turned toward his offices on Fifth Avenue and whispered of his big raids on the market.

Behind the unmarked doors of that secret, hidden office, Livermore was as busy getting out of stocks as the average investor, while his staff of twenty or more statisticians bustled around with feverish activity.

Much glamour has attached itself to Livermore's office. The telephone company will report that they have no phones in his name or at that address. But anyone fortunate enough to gain admittance to the outer office would find several unlisted telephones for general use, and in Jesse's private office, the more than thirty phones, many 120

direct connections with brokers' offices, would quickly brand the phone company's denials as being most humorous.

There in his suite of offices, Livermore employs more than twenty men, enough to run a large brokerage house. His statistical and research staff would do honor to a large corporation. A standard-sized quotation board, a private telegraph operator and other specially trained men toil in his personal employ.

When he is in New York, Livermore, too, often works late into the night in that 18th story office, studying and checking the reports and figures that his expert statisticians have compiled for him from the stacks of clippings, books and other data that have come in daily from all corners of the country. 

There are financial statements of corporations, reports of trade and commerce, figures on crops and commodities. Not a single happening in the economic world but that his staff weighs it, and if found valuable, transmits it to their employer. 

Livermore knows the worth of a statistical and research organization, and he knows that the successful investor or speculator must keep fully informed on all important matters at all times. 

One of his characteristic utterances, and advice which his success indicates he follows himself is "Don't put all your hope and money on today. 121

Look into the future and if you are able to discern what the future holds you will be successful."

THE FISHER BROTHERS 

Wall Street received one of its greatest thrills as well as biggest surprises when it woke up one morning in 1926 to find the Fisher brothers buying their way first into one company and then into another. 

These seven brothers, Fred J., the oldest, with 

Charles, William, Lawrence, Alfred, Edward and 

Howard, came to New York with $300,000,000 that they had received from General Motors for their Fisher Body Company.

Their rise has been phenomenal, and their operations in Wall Street were to be but slightly less spectacular. 

Their grandfather, Andrew Fisher, and their father, Lawrence, had both been blacksmiths. All but Howard, the youngest, had been apprenticed as blacksmiths, too, going to work in their father's shop when they were little more than fourteen or fifteen years old, only barely through the grade schools.

Their experience had widened considerably when their father and uncle opened a shop in 

Norwalk, Ohio, where they made wagons and carriages, and it was with this background that 122

they had formed the Fisher Body Company in 1908, about the same time that Durant was organizing General Motors just after the panic of 1907. 

Fisher Body Company grew with astonishing rapidity. It was first capitalized at $50,000, but it grew much faster than many of the larger automobile companies which it supplied. Increase in output of the Fisher plant of as great as 200% to 

500% in a single year was not unknown in that early period. In 1909, the company employed 100 men. By 1923 its 44 plants in 13 cities were manned by more than 50,000 employees. They were turning out 575,000 automobile bodies in 1923 when General Motors acquired the company. 

This big automobile concern had previously purchased a 60% interest in Fisher Body, as a result of William Durant's determination to obtain control of the company when he was its head in 

1918. Durant had visited the Fishers at that time, offering to buy them out, but the seven brothers would not listen to the proposition. The only way that he was able to get the 60% was by agreeing to leave the Fishers in operating control of the company.

After the two companies were consolidated in 1923, the Fishers assumed prominent positions in the General Motors company, two of them becom- 123

ing directors, and others taking their places at the head of subsidiaries of the motor giant. The $200,000,000 to $300,000,000 which they received for their body company was partly in cash and the remainder in General Motors stock, and the bull market that came with the election of Calvin Coolidge to the Presidency added about $200,000,000 more to their wealth. 

In 1924 the seven brothers organized a family investing company, Fisher & Company, with original capital of $25,000,000. It was with this fund that they first entered Wall Street, and their first and most spectacular market operation centered around the stock of Baldwin Locomotive Company. 

Fred J. Fisher, the chief of the clan, went to

New York in 1926 to attend a General Motors directors meeting. While there, one of a group of 

Wall Street operators who was heavily loaded up with Baldwin Locomotive, passed Fisher a redhot tip to buy the stock. It sounded good to Fisher, who had never done much in stocks before. So after a conference with his brothers, they began to buy the stock.

Much to their surprise, the price refused to go up. They bought more, but still it stayed down. 

They became suspicious of the tip at that point, 124

but decided to investigate the company before retreating.

They discovered that the stock they had been buying was sold by the very men who had given them the tip; but they also discovered that those operators had been so certain of the timidity of these blacksmiths from Detroit that they had sold thousands of shares of Baldwin short waiting for the Fishers to throw their holdings overboard and break the market.

The Fisher brothers evidently didn't know the rules of the game as the Wall Street operators played it, for they didn't throw their stock overboard. Instead they bought more. The price began to rise, and with new courage they continued buying. Arthur Cutten, among others, got wind of the affair, and he stepped aboard also. The Fishers had advanced the quotation from 92 to 150 when 

Cutten joined them in the bull move. By June, 1927, Baldwin was 233, but on the last day of that month it broke 15 points in one day. 

Samuel M. Vauclain, 71-year-old president of

Baldwin, had issued a statement that the company was operating at only 20% of capacity and that the stock wasn't worth 130, much less 230. 

The Fishers were undismayed, however. They had bought their stock outright and had no fears of margin calls or weakened bank loans. And they 125

determined to study the situation even closer before making any drastic move. 

They sent engineers to Philadelphia, not one engineer, but several. And their reports fully justified the strategy. The engineers found that Baldwin owned, among other assets, a piece of property in Philadelphia which they estimated to be worth at least $30,000,000. It was carried on the company's books at only $17,330,000. On the basis of this estimate they advised that Baldwin was worth $350 a share.

The Fishers thereupon started buying again. 

The price recovered to a new high for the year at 265. Many of those who were short of the market lost millions on the advance. The Fishers had won, and in buying over half the total outstanding common stock and a large block of the voting preferred shares they earned themselves two places on the company's board of directors. Cutten, too, joined the board and became a close ally of the 

Fishers in future market operations. 

By 1927, the Fishers, according to Fred J. himself, had made profits of $125,000,000 in their security dealings. They had bought Texas Corporation around 50 and saw it rise to 74. Richfield 

Oil advanced to 56 from their purchase price around 25. International Telephone & Telegraph stock, which they accumulated at 150, was split up 126

and rose to the equivalent of 445. Heavy investments that they made in Westinghouse Electric, Westinghouse Air Brake, Radio, United States 

Steel, Anaconda, Mexican Seaboard Oil, Ameri can & Foreign Power, Standard Steel Car, Gen eral Electric and American Rolling Mills brought them additional millions.

Their holdings in many cases gained them positions on directorates of the companies. Fred

Fisher and his brothers became directors of National City Bank; Postal Telegraph, the principal subsidiary of International Telephone; of the Big

Four, one of the major constituents of New York 

Central, and of General Motors, of course. 

They have become industrial as well as financial giants. They were not market operators in the strict sense of the word. They sought to invest their funds in companies which they could aid in a managerial capacity, and when they had decided upon a certain company they went out and bought its stock regardless of the price they had to pay. 

They were investing upwards of $500,000,000 in permanent holdings, and they looked upon the machinery industry which grew from inventions and patents as the greatest opportunities for profitable investment, rather than such "service" industries as chain stores and merchandising. 127

JOHN J. RASKOB 

John J. Raskob is one of those brilliant men who has made a fortune for himself through aiding others to achieve great wealth. Twenty-odd years ago he was just an ordinary secretary at a salary of $3,000 a year. Through his confidential work for Pierre S. duPont, he is today a millionaire many times over, and during the prosperity of 1928 and 1929 his wealth could probably have been counted at well over $100,000,000. 

His ability while in the employ of E. I. duPont de Nemours Company brought early recognition from Pierre, the head of that great company, and it was not long before Raskob became Assistant Treasurer of the company, later to be Treasurer, and now vice-president and a member of the finance committee.

He it was who was assigned to confer with 

William Durant at the time that duPont first became interested in General Motors. His advices were instrumental in leading duPont to affiliate with Durant in the motor company, and Raskob, with the duPonts, went onto the board of directors of General Motors. Raskob later became chairman of the finance committee, a post which he relinquished in 1928 when he became chairman of the Democratic National Committee and con- 128

ducted the Presidential campaign for his friend 

Alfred E. Smith. 

Raskob is still a director of General Motors, as are several of the duPont family, but it is understood that opposition of the Fisher brothers, who are Republicans, has been sufficient to keep him from regaining his position as chairman of the finance committee.

This brilliant man holds several other important posts beside those with duPont and Motors.

He is a director of the Bankers Trust Company and of several other banks, and also on the board of American Surety Company and Missouri Pacific Railroad.

His affiliation with General Motors, of course, was his greatest achievement. During the five years from 1915, when Durant regained control of General Motors from the bankers, Raskob succeeded in getting the duPont company to invest an additional $50,000,000 in the automobile company. When the crash of 1920 saw the stock of the motor giant tumbling, to leave Durant a ruined man, Raskob was less certain of the wisdom of the advice he had given the duPonts. But when, in conjunction with J. P. Morgan, the duPonts continued their interest in Motors, even investing $25,000,000 more, he was to see his foresight 129

fully vindicated. DuPont's investment a few years later was worth more than $600,000,000. 

Raskob's operations in the market have been heavy and sensational, and the profits he made in the Coolidge bull market enabled him to pile up cash resources great enough to carry him through the depression with hardly a single care. All his securities have always been owned outright. He is one of the greatest opponents of margin trading, and while his paper losses in the 1929 debacle were enormous, he had relatively few worries. 

When Meehan, Smith, Bragg and numerous other leading operators were active in the Radio pool of 1928, Raskob, too, was participating in the pool. He also bought heavily of Radio stock for his personal account, acquiring thousands of shares below $100. He, like Cutten, held most of these through the panic, however, believing that the future of Radio Corporation would be so great that the stock merited being classed as a permanent investment. 

John Raskob's friends, William F. Kenny, Nicholas F. Brady, J. J. Riordan, and others who fraternized with Raskob and Al Smith, made hundreds of thousands of dollars profit in that Radio pool. And many of them rode to big profits through following Raskob's lead in buying

Anaconda, Warner Brothers Pictures, duPont, 130

Loew's Inc., United States Steel and American 

Can. And while Raskob traded in and out of General Motors stock practically every month from 1927 to 1931, he always maintained a large holding in his greatest interest. His General Motors stock is held in several different accounts, including two privately owned companies, the Regent Cor poration and the Archmere Corporation. 

MICHAEL J. Meehan 

When Wall Street brokers and bankers journeyed theatreward during the World War, most of them, including partners of J. P. Morgan &

Company, usually bought their tickets from a redhaired Irish lad in his middle twenties, who worked in a ticket agency close to Wall Street. 

In 1918, this same Irishman became a part of Wall Street as a member of the New York Curb, which then was still operating out of doors. And in two years, "Mike" Meehan had made enough money from his Curb commission business so that he paid $90,000 for a New York Stock Exchange seat. 

That was in 1920, when America was experiencing the peak of the post-war depression which brought suffering to many and great opportunities to Meehan. His rise to stock market fame was to be almost sensational, particularly because of his 131

vision in recognizing the extraordinary future that lay ahead of the radio industry and its largest concern, Radio Corporation of America. 

Meehan was the first bull in Radio. When that stock was listed on the Big Board in 1924, he immediately began accumulation of the issue. In his wisdom he became the "specialist" in the stock, and as his brokerage profits increased through the substantial business that came to him from operators who early recognized his ability, he increased his holdings in Radio, and urged his friends to buy and hold.

Durant favored Mike with many orders. The leader of the biggest pool in Wall Street's history found in Meehan a broker who could execute difficult orders right under other brokers' noses without them knowing that anything had happened. 

And in the heavy trading that Durant and other bulls directed toward Meehan's firm, Mike was virtually swept along into fame and fortune. His commissions in one day alone often amounted to $10,000 to $15,000 as the market advanced toward its peak, and the Radio shares that he had bought around $40 swelled his profits to more than $25,000,000. 

With Meehan ardently bulling Radio, it was natural that Durant and his group, and other keen operators like Cutten, should also buy heavily of 132

the stock, and all this activity in Radio meant more profits to Meehan, for as "specialist" he received a commission on every share of the stock that was bought or sold through him. 

As the Coolidge market wore on into 1928, numerous pools began operating in Meehan's "pet" stock. This meant more commissions for

Mike, for most of these pools placed heavy orders through M. J. Meehan & Company; and Mike's enthusiasm for the stock was so great that his wife participated in at least one of the large pools that made big profits in the issue. 

All the time the quotation for Radio was rising.

It went to 420 in 1928, and to the equivalent of 

570 in 1929. Meehan's profits on his $40 stock were well into the millions. 

But all this did not "spoil" Mike, in the least.

He still was Mike to everyone. His seat on the 

Exchange had grown to eight in number. His nine offices employed over 400 persons, and they all knew him as "Mike."

But his increased wealth had brought with it greater responsibilities and much more hard work. 

In the hectic days when Radio was churning around during sessions when 300,000 to 500,000 shares of the stock were traded in, Meehan bore the brunt of the entire affair. He was often the center of a yelling, jostling crowd of from 50 to 133

brokers all trying to execute orders in Radio, and for many days Mike was by far the busiest broker on the whole floor. 

His duties as specialist required that he hear every possible bid or offer of the stock. He must execute his orders before others had the chance, and above all else, he must keep constantly in mind the thousands of orders that were on his "specialist's book," and see that every one was taken care of at the right moment. 

One particular session, during the four days when Radio rose 61 points on a technical corner, Meehan was focal point of hundreds of brokers and orders, and so frenzied was the crowd around him that he practically had his clothes torn from his back. At the close of the session he was nearly exhausted, but his orders had been executed in masterly fashion. And what is one of the most interesting sidelights of his spectacular career is the fact that seldom did he enter any trade on his book until the session was over. Then, in the quiet of his office he would jot down every transaction, the number of shares, price and the broker with whom he dealt. And never a single forgotten trade or serious error.

Meehan's success is probably sufficient evidence of his shrewdness and ability, but one example in particular is vividly illustrative of the way in 134

which he could operate without even the cleverest of the floor traders knowing just where he stood. 

In one of the wildest sessions in Radio, Meehan was reported as having bought over 200,000 shares of Radio either for his customers or for himself. And indeed he had. But it was not even suspected that he had also that same day sold the entire 200,000 shares. For many days even some of the floor traders believed he still held the stock. 

The details of some of the more interesting transactions in Radio will be told a little later, and some of the enlightening information about 

Meehan's activity brought out in hearings before the Senate Finance Committee which is investigating Wall Street's operations, but anyone who has seen Mike working at that Radio post in 1928 and 1929, and days and nights without sleep during the 1929 panic, would tell you that a man with that much vitality and stick-to-itiveness was bound to be a success. 135

CHAPTER VI 1931 Bear Operators 

T may be a surprise to many persons to learn that many of the "Big Bears" of 1930-1932, were among the "Big Bulls" of 1928 and 1929.

When the Senate Finance Committee made public a list of short sellers early in 1932, and summoned some of the more important to Washington, there was confirmation of what many investors had heard and a few knew, that Tom Bragg, Ben 

Smith, Matt Brush, Percy Rockefeller and half a dozen other equally prominent market operators had been the “Big Bears" who "cleaned up" on the steady decline in prices since 1929. 

But that these same men were among the biggest bulls of the preceding period is something that was not published in every newspaper. 

Bragg, for instance, has stood out head and shoulders for more than a year because of his bearish stand on American Telephone & Telegraph shares. He is understood to have sold the stock short well above 180 and to have maintained his position as an investment. By July, 1932, A. T. & T. had sold off to 704, lower than it had ever 136

CHAPTER VII The Kreuger & Toll Swindle SERVANT, entering the Paris apartment of Ivar Kreuger the morning of Saturday, March 12th, 1932, found the greatest organizer of the Twentieth Century, the Match King of the 

World, stretched out on the floor-dead by his own hand.

Several hours later an unsuspecting world heard the news with a mixed feeling of sadness, apprehension and pity. This man who was the idol of the Swedish nation, the saviour of a score of nearbankrupt Central European nations, and the founder of an industrial empire that stretched from one quarter of the globe to all other quarters, had broken under the strain of business depression, and in a moment of insanity, it seemed, had taken his own life. 

The Kreuger & Toll securities, traded on every important Stock Exchange in the world, the International Match securities, representing its principal subsidiary, hesitated in their market courses as trading opened the next Monday, then slowly turned downward as if their national flag was 154

Then with a crash that reverberated throughout the world came the astounding news on April 5th, that Ivar Kreuger's castles were crumbling, his monument to great organizing genius had been built on the quicksands of fraud, deceit and falsification.

With this news the decline in the Kreuger securities became a slaughter. The Kreuger & Toll

More astounding than anything that the present generation had ever witnessed was the disclosure of the gigantic fraud that had been perpetrated upon the public of the world. England's

Available records seem to indicate that Kreuger's early business years were spent in the United States, where he attempted to sell real estate in the city of Chicago, which had not at that time come to be known as the "Bad Lands."

CHAPTER VIII Operating in the Market 

HERE are many ways of operating in the market, from an individual standpoint. Let us define them.

INVESTING. Buying securities primarily for income and protection of principal is the basis of investing. The investor is generally satisfied with a reasonable yield, but has clearly in mind the safety of his capital. He buys high-grade bonds and sound preferred stocks, and, in some instances, the better grade of common stocks, which have good records of earnings and dividends.

SPECULATING. Recognizing that specula tion plays an important part in business and industry, many individuals buy securities on a speculative basis. In this case, income and safety of principal are not the motivating influences, although, of course, they are not entirely ignored. The main consideration is the possibility of substantial profits through market price appreciation.

This 

TRADING FOR LONG SWINGS. 

of course, is speculating, but it is speculating with a plan. It purposes to benefit from the long-term swings of the market, buying securities generally 189

after they have had a protracted decline, and selling them after a long, sustained advance has been experienced, and repeating the process. 

TRADING FOR SHORT SWINGS. This is essentially the practice of the expert trader-the individual who spends the greater part of his time watching the action of the market. It is a highly specialized pursuit, calling for intimate knowledge of market mechanics and of technical and fundamental factors, and, generally speaking, requires years of experience in learning to interpret the meaning of price changes as they occur on the ticker tape. 

Trading for long swings constitutes the most general course of action in the market. The average individual hasn't the time, patience or experience to attempt, with any appreciable chance for success, to take advantage of the short swings.

Knowing that the normal trend of the country and of corporate earnings is upwards, he finds it more satisfactory and, in the long run, more profitable, to buy securities at attractive levels and to hold them for the long pull. 

That does not mean, of course, that the security buyer, whether he has purchased for investment, speculation, long or short term trading, needs simply to forget about his holdings, and "let nature take its course." Eternal vigilance is the 190

price one has to pay in security matters as well as in every other pursuit.

Financial Services 

Every security owner should subscribe to some dependable financial service. While no one can claim infallibility in the financial field any more than in any other line of endeavor, the fact remains that the personnel of a financial service is constantly studying market and business conditions, and is in position to furnish at all times a fairly accurate picture of the situation. 

Such a service also is in position to uncover at various times attractive issues selling at prices that should enable long-term traders to realize substantial profits through their purchase. The function of a good financial service is to furnish such service to its subscribers that losses may be minimized and profits may be multiplied. 

Owners of securities should periodically-say every three months-check up on the standing of the companies in which representation is had. This can be done effectively if the individual is a subscriber to a service such as we have described. 

Up-to-date information, as well as expressions of opinion regarding the merit of the securities, based on such information, is thus readily procurable. 191

Diversification 

Diversification of holdings is a commendable feature in owning securities, but, like most good things, it can easily be overdone. It is far better to own a few good stocks and bonds than to own a large number of indifferent ones. Securities have to be watched carefully for evidences of impairment of values, and it is easier to watch a few issues than to keep track of a large list of holdings. 

In limiting one's holdings to a few industries, the security owner is apt to be more careful in making his selections. There is always the danger of carelessness where wide diversification is employed, as the security owner in such cases is inclined to rely too much on the law of averages and not enough on the individual merits of the securities he acquires.

Fashions in Securities 

There are fashions in securities, just as there are fashions in clothes, furniture, amusements, customs, etc. Years ago railroad bonds and insurance stocks were in favor. Then traction stocks came into popularity. Industrial and mining securities later came into vogue. Utilities, motors, moving pictures, radio and aviation stocks in turn caught the popular appeal.

No investor or trader can afford to ignore these 192

periodic trends in securities. They mark the prevailing public demand, based on the ever-changing developments of our national existence. To change with them in the matter of security holdings usually means avoiding losses and acquiring profits. 

For example, the individual who confined his purchases years ago principally to railroad and traction stocks, and held them to the exclusion of other classes of securities, certainly would not have fared as well as those who sensed the newer trends and profited by the advances that took place in the stocks of the later industries-industries which, in some cases, have virtually revolutionized our habits and customs.

Just now, there does not appear to be any clearcut evidences of a fashion in securities. All classes of stocks have suffered by the intensive liquidation that has taken place during the past two years, due to disturbed economic conditions. When the turn for the better takes place, it is very likely that the shares of some new industry will come into popularity and provide the opportunities for large profits, particularly in the early phases of the industry. Every forward-looking investor and trader should be on the alert for such a development. 193

Judging the Market

An ability to judge the market is a pre-requisite of every security owner. He needs that ability to tell him when to buy and when to sell. Most people are inclined to believe that such ability comes without study or much experience. Nearly everyone thinks that he can judge the market correctly. The taking for granted of that premise is largely the reason why so many people fail to make the most of their security opportunities. 

Judging the market is a fine art. It is not merely a matter of knowing that prices went up or down today. It is determining what the trend is, whether higher or lower prices are indicated for the short or long terms. It means, in short, knowing what to do in the market by reason of knowing what the market is going to do.

That isn't easy, nor is it particularly hard. It seems easy to a lot of people who accept the obvious as the correct answer. For instance, most people are willing to assume, without attempting to verify it, that rising prices indicate a continuation of the advance, and that declining prices mean a continuation of the decline. 

To others, the price changes in the market seem aimless and decipherless. They find it difficult to anticipate or to analyze market movements. With the idea of clarifying misconceptions and to fur- 194

nish a working basis for judging the market, the following suggestions are offered.

Market Movements 

The market is always in a state of activity. 

Sometimes, some stocks are practically motionless, but the market itself is constantly moving upward and downward in reflection of various forces. 

Aside from the movements of a particular day, there are three distinct movements or trends.

One is the long-term movement-the bull or bear market trend-which generally runs from one to three years. While difficult to determine at its beginning or ending, the trend is easy to define once it has been established.

Another is the short-term trend-the minor movement in a major market. Usually, it represents a recoil from the main trend—a reaction in a bull market, or a rally in a bear market. The periods of such movements are usually a matter of a few weeks or months.

The third phase is the day-to-day trend, occupying generally a three- or four-day turn. Such movements, in themselves, are rather meaningless, but as they check and re-check the short-term trend, they serve as an indicator of the long-term movement. 195

Forecasting the Market 

There are a number of methods employed to determine the future course of the market. They are: 

1. Forecasting by evidences of fundamental factors; i.e., industrial activity, car loadings, steel tonnage, electricity consumption, etc. 

2. Forecasting by evidences of technical factors; i.e., brokers' loans, call money rates, market activity, extent of short interest, etc. 

3. Forecasting by charts of active stocks. 

4. Forecasting by tape reading. 

5. Forecasting by the action of the market "averages." 99 The ideal theoretical method of forecasting market movements would, of course, be a system combining all five methods. Such a system, of necessity, would entail a great amount of work, and could only be carried out properly by a large organization of financial experts.

The five methods described have their various devotees, but the most practical method for the average individual is the one that has to do with the action of the market "averages." This method calls for the gauging of the market movements through the composite price changes of a group, or groups of selected stocks. 196

The Use of "Averages" 

Several sets of "averages" are compiled and their price changes charted by different agencies. 

The most commonly accepted are the Dow, Jones "averages." Separate sets of Dow, Jones "averages" are maintained for industrial, railroad and utility stocks, but the action of the industrial and railroad "averages" is considered as the most significant in relation to market movements. 

The Dow, Jones "averages" are made up of thirty leading industrial stocks, twenty rails and twenty utilities. The basic idea is that the industrial and railroad "averages" must confirm one another in any definite change of trend. 

For example, if either the industrial or the rail "averages" break through a previous low or a previous high, the other set of "averages" must do the same thing in order to confirm the trend. Failing of confirmation, the trend of the market has not been clearly defined, and cannot be considered to have changed.

It is well to note at this time that the use of "averages" does not imply an infallible method of determining the course of the market. Infallibility is something that can not be claimed for any method. However, over a period of many years, the operation of the Dow, Jones "averages" has 197

furnished a fairly accurate gauge of the market movements. 

How the "Averages" 

Gauge the Long Swings

So long as the "averages" of both the industrial and railroad group continue to make new highs or new lows, the market is regarded in a bull or bear movement. Once, the "averages" stop making new highs or new lows, the major trend of the market is considered to have changed.

While the "averages" have fairly clearly defined the long term, or major movements, of the market, they should never be followed blindly. Consideration of fundamental and technical factors must always enter into an appraisal of the market situation and of its possible future course. Obviously, the most dangerous times to follow the "averages" are during the heights of a bull market movement and during the depths of a bear market.

For at such times, the trend of the market and of the "averages" is apt to change without notice.

Confirmation of the change, however, is soon made, but usually it is not soon enough to avoid losses if one has bought at the top, or sold "short" at the bottom. 198

How the "Averages" Gauge the Short Swings 

The progress of the "averages" plays a useful part in gauging the short-term swings of the market. They aid in determining the "tops" of rallies and the "bottoms" of declines. Assume for example that the market has staged a rally of about five or ten points in the "averages" from a definite low point. The rally stops and a reaction sets in. If the reaction does not retrace more than half of the distance gained on the rally, and starts up again, the "averages" are watched carefully to see whether stocks can continue on up through the point at which the rally was stopped. If the market goes through, the trend is still up. If it stops again, and begins to turn down, the market has made a "double-top," and a resumption of the down-trend is indicated.

The same principle applies when the market is going down. If the "averages" show that the decline has stopped a second time at or near the previous low level, a "double-bottom" has been established, and an up-turn in the market can be anticipated.

Double and Triple 

"Bottoms" and "Tops"

Sometimes, the "averages" indicate more than 199

two "tops" and more than two "bottoms." It never pays to take anything for granted in the market. No matter how accurate a method or a principle worked out in the past, it must never be followed blindly. There is always the exception to the general rule. 

One has to be prepared for "triple-tops" and "triple-bottoms," and even "quadruple-tops" and "quadruple-bottoms," keeping one's decisions as to the ultimate trend in abeyance until the action of the market definitely confirms the deductions arrived at with the aid of the "averages." 

Trading in Stocks For the Short Swing 

If one desires to trade in stocks for the short swings, he must be prepared to spend most of his time at a brokerage office and watch the market carefully. Certain fundamental principles of action need to be observed. Some of them are as follows:

Buying and selling should be governed by method and not by emotion. A stock should not be bought or sold on a basis of impulse, snap judgment, hearsay, opinion, market activity, or a combination of these elements. There must be a real reason for the transaction.

The best plan is to confine one's trading activities to one or two stocks, and to keep on trading in 200

them as long as activity is maintained in them. 

Trading in one stock is better than trading in two, if the stock selected shows sufficient activity to permit making a satisfactory number of trading turns in it.

The individual who attempts to trade in more than one or two stocks at a time has his work cut out for him. If the trend is definitely with him, he may be able to achieve temporary success, but should the market turn before he does, he soon will find himself in plenty of "hot water." 

One of the principal requisites for successful trading is an ability to make up one's mind quickly and to act on it promptly. That is quite possible when it comes to deciding what to do about one stock. It is a little harder to do when there are two stocks, and proportionately harder when there are more stocks to decide about. 

Furthermore, if the market moves against the holder of a number of trading stocks his paper and possible losses are proportionately greater than in the case of the trader with one or two issues to contend with.

DON'T OVERTRADE. Don't try to make a killing on one transaction. Small profits are better than large losses and large losses invariably come with overtrading. Always buy LESS shares than your capital permits, particularly on the initial 201

transaction. Endeavor to keep part of in reserve at all times. your funds

Select a medium-priced stock that is reasonably active, but not too much so. A stock with too much of a swing in price is difficult to follow and difficult to cash in on. A stock with a normal market range of one to three points a day is a better issue for trading purposes than one that generally has a five or more point swing in it in one session. 

The stock with the one to three point swing generally moves in eighths and quarters, while the stock with the wider changes is apt to jump one or more points up and down between sales, making it extremely difficult to gauge and still harder to operate in. Obviously, a stock with a price range of not much more than a point a day is of little value for trading purposes.

The chief advantage derived from trading in one or two stocks exclusively is that one has the opportunity to study the characteristics of those issues. It is a fact that stocks appear to possess individual characteristics, due, of course, to different methods employed by the pools operating in them.

One gradually becomes able to discern the accumulating and distributing stages in a stock; is able to see evidences of support and supply in it, and to govern one's buying and selling operations 202

to conform with the recognized actions of the issue.

That does not mean, of course, that the action of an individual stock ever becomes a matter of positive knowledge to a trader-that its movements become an open-book whose recorded messages can be relied upon without question or risk. 

Even the pool operators, with the best of plans and intentions, often find it impossible to do what they would like to do, and are frequently forced to change their operations suddenly, because of a turn in either fundamental or technical conditions. 

The trader must always be alert for those changes. 

In general, the experienced day-to-day trader tries to buy his stock when it appears to be getting support. Support is indicated when the reaction in an active issue is stopped at or above the low of the preceding day. To really be in an up-trend a stock should receive support at least a fraction above the previous day's low.

The trader either plans to sell with a fair profit the same day, or if the trend is unquestionably upward, he may decide to hold his position until evidences of difficulty in meeting supply become pronounced. This is seen generally in the inability of the stock to make much progress through a high point established a short time before. 203

Stop Loss Orders 

Many traders use stop-loss orders to protect their positions. For instance, a purchase might be made on the evidences of support, and a stop-loss order placed just under the low of that day or the low of the previous day, since the drop below would indicate that support was being withdrawn. 

Stop-loss orders are important adjuncts to successful trading, but their proper use constitutes a fine art, founded on the possession of experience and judgment.

Short Selling 

The experienced trader usually is just as ready to sell short as to go long of a stock. To him, it is generally a matter of going with the trend, and to all intents and purposes, he doesn't care whether a stock goes up or down, so long as he can gauge the trend and be able to play along with it. It is not possible for us in this short volume to enter fully into a description of the mechanics of a short-sale, or into a discussion calculated to either justify or denounce the practice of short selling, but from a trader's standpoint, short selling appears to be logically, legally and morally permissible. 

The trader contends that if it is all right for him to buy a stock for the chance of a profit because he thinks it is low in price and should go up, 204

it ought to be just as correct to sell a stock short that he thinks is high and should go down. Besides, if he buys on margin, he actually goes short on money advanced by the broker, for which the trader pays interest charges, whereas if he sells on margin, he goes long of money loaned by the broker to the owner of the borrowed stock, on which the broker receives interest, so he argues that it is a 50-50 proposition.

Margin Trading 

Most traders buy and sell on margin. But no successful trader ever expects to win by attempting to trade on a small margin. You will find that the really successful trader generally figures on a 50 per cent margin and plans to have in reserve another 50 per cent to protect his trades, if necessary. A "shoe-string" margin trading account usually lasts as long as the first snow of a winter stays on the ground. The odds are against it right from the start. A small amount of cash will have a better chance to grow through the outright purchase of some good low-priced stock, than in attempting to beat the margin game. Averaging

Sometimes a trader is fairly well convinced that the trend of the market is definitely established. 

In that case, he decides not to use stop-loss orders, 205

but to maintain a long or short position, as the case may be, and average his holdings. In other words, if he is long, he buys additional stock on the set-backs of the market. If he is short, he sells on the upward "bulges." If he is right on the trend, he is eventually able to close out a number of transactions with a profit. Pyramiding

Once in a while, a trader believes his stock is in for a "line play," and decides to try to take advantage of it. In other words, he looks for a definite, sustained up or down swing. In that case, he places orders to buy or sell every two, three, four or five points away, hoping that his closing transaction will enable him to make a "clean-up." 

Pyramiding is an extremely hazardous process, and is only rarely successful, and then in a major trend movement, with practically no interruption for an extended period. Often one sharp change in the trend will smash a well-planned pyramid and cause a severe loss to be sustained as the pains of an arduous pyramiding process.

From the foregoing, it will be obvious that margin trading, as a profession, is no child's-play. It calls for the possession of a substantial amount of capital, considerable experience in tape reading, a rare combination of caution and courage, decision 206

and the ability to act on it quickly, and a temperament which can stand taking losses without much perturbation and the making of profits without much elation. It requires a keen "nose" for news, a keen eye for trends, and almost a "sixth sense" for impending changes. 207

CHAPTER IX How Present Conditions Shape the Future THE PAST 

ROM 1922 to 1929, this country reared a In creative achievement, volume of production, diffusion of wealth, and general well-being, all periods of the past paled into comparative insignificance. 

Colossal structure of prosperity. 

A "new era" appeared to have dawned. A new technology seemed to have been created. Wizard dreams of laboratory and factory transformed the work of men and machines into a growing arc of production and profits. As the structure rose, the tempo of trade, industry and finance ascended.

Such a pace as was witnessed in the last two years of the boom could not conceivably last. It was exhausting the consumptive needs of the nation. It was drawing on the accumulated capital of the country. Prices, profits, wages and stocks were soaring. It had to end. 

In a little less than three years, this country went from the heights of unparalleled prosperity to the depths of unprecedented depression. In that 208

time, practically the whole structure of progress, as measured by security and commodity prices, was torn down. 

THE PRESENT 

It is well at this time to consider that despite the damage that was done to industry and finance, the real wealth of the nation has not been destroyed. We still have a large population- 

130,000,000-of vigorous, healthy people, possessing high standards of living and a splendid level of education.

We still have more than 500,000,000 acres of good farm lands, capable of producing sufficient wheat, corn, cotton and other crops to take care of the needs of all the people. We have nearly half a billion acres of pasture lands, capable of feeding livestock to provide all the meat and dairy products we require. 

We have forests and lumber mills with which to produce all the lumber we can use. There are sufficient coal, oil, copper and iron ore reserves in the United States to fill our requirements for hundreds of years. We have unlimited water resources for developing all the power we can ever use. 

We have vast systems of railroad transportation, great highways for motor vehicle traffic, comprehensive communication systems, steel mills, 209

factories, stores and mercantile establishments of all kinds. Great public utility enterprises furnish us with electric light and power.

We have all our facilities intact to provide our growing population with the food, shelter, clothing and other necessities, as well as the luxuries we need. It is true that conditions have arisen that have so dislocated the purchasing power of the people that millions are unable just now to secure many of the things they need. Eventually, those conditions are going to be corrected.

If this country were poor in natural resources, backward in its industrial development, laid waste by war or pestilence, we might be justified in despairing of our future. But this is not the case. 

The United States is still the richest nation the world has ever known, and our wealth is still intact and our resources practically undiminished. 

Consider the facts and figures of our national wealth. It is conservatively estimated as being between $350,000,000,000 and $400,000,000,000. 

The total income of the country is placed at between $60,000,000,000 and $65,000,000,000. 

Our per capita wealth is about $3000 and our per capita income is $500, counting every man, woman and child in the United States.

The savings banks deposits total nearly $30,- 000,000,000, and life insurance policies exceed 210

$100,000,000,000. The nation's money in circulation of nearly $5,000,000,000 is backed by a stronger gold reserve than that of any country on earth, even though gold is no longer a medium of circulation.

It is inconceivable that a nation so rich in resources, in possession of such great material wealth, in development of such high standards of living, with its past record of progress and achievement, can for long stay on a low plane of employment and industrial activity. 

Let us briefly review the factors that brought about the present situation. We can dismiss with a word, the excessive speculation that terminated with the crash in 1929. That was more of an effect than a cause. Had the rampant market activity not occurred, we might have had a less precipitous decline and perhaps a less severe depression, but we would not have escaped the deluge.

The forces that brought about deflation were locked up in the structure of prosperity itself. The seeds of dissolution had been planted when the foundation was built. Intensive production for war time needs made necessary a gigantic machine for large-scale output. When that machine was put to work for post-war requirements, it quickly outstripped the limits of peace time consumption. 211

Unbalanced demands for commodities of all kinds disturbed the equilibrium between production and needs and, at the same time, raised commodity prices way out of proportion. The improper balance between supply and demand could not support the unwieldy commodity price structure that had been reared. It had to come down. 

Every one realizes that, whatever other contributing causes may have entered the situation, the drastic drop in prices of goods has been the chief cause of paralyzing business and of prolonging the depression. Manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers stop buying in declining markets and, in addition, have to take heavy inventory losses. 

It is true that the shrinkage in security prices was considerably greater than the decline in commodity prices. This is partly due to the fact that commodity buyers in 1929 were already beginning to be wary of prices and were curtailing purchases, whereas security buyers in that year were paying no heed to the warnings that stock prices were too high and were buying to the full extent of their cash and credit. 

Every period following a great war has been one of violent readjustment of the commodity price level. The severe depression of the '70's witnessed the crash of prices from an inflated level which had reached its peak in 1865, the year the 212

civil war ended. Not until 1879, fourteen years afterwards, was the process of deflation completed.

It is interesting to note that the depression-low year, 1932, was fourteen years from the termination of the world war. Prices of many commodities came down to levels which were low by comparison with those in existence ten years before the world war started. World prices declined, in terms of percentage, at least, as much as or more than after any past period of war in recorded history. 

Not in the memory of man had such basic commodities as cotton, rubber and copper been so cheap. Sugar, wheat, silk, oil, silver and most of the other commodities came down to prices much below the cost of their production. Recovery in commodity prices is inevitable, and already in these few months evidences of an upturn have occurred in oil, sugar, wheat, cotton, and live-stock and most other principal commodities.

As for the stock market, prices decreased, in terms of averages, to the lowest levels in a quarter of a century. In terms of percentage, as well as in the number of points of decline, the downward movement was the severest in the history of the 

Stock Exchange. 

Liquidation took its drastic toll in every field. 

Bank loans were reduced to a negligible amount. 213

Brokers' loans dropped more than 95%. Farm lands, real estate and properties of all kinds were deflated to the limit.

The process of deflation unquestionably went far beyond reason. Ever noted for its excesses in any one direction, this country has gone to the extremes in putting its financial house in order. 

What an opportunity the present holds for the future! The chances of a lifetime are at hand.

Properties of all sorts are still available at so many cents on the dollar. Stocks of seasoned, well managed, financially strong companies can be purchased, in many cases, for the proverbial song. 

The depths to which the country went in this last depression left a wide space for a recovery in values. Even a rise to levels where commodity prices would represent actual cost, and a rise in security prices to where they would reflect net tangible assets, would, in many cases, constitute a sharp rebound. 

That recovery is inevitable every thinking person admits. That it is already on the way is almost apparent. The first indication was a cessation of liquidation. Except for isolated dribbles, liquidation practically ceased. To all intents and purposes, complete liquidation has been achieved.

The next step is the restoration of confidence, and the expansion of credit. 214

Statistics indicate that in normal times the United States requires about $50,000,000,000 of cash and credit to carry on business at a satisfactory level. Under the old gold standard we had something over $4,000,000,000 in currency circulating backed about 100% by gold. Under the present gold bullion basis, with the dollar devalued to 59.06 cents, our gold is worth nearly $8,000,000,000. Currency in circulation may be expanded in a short while, due to Government expenditures, to close to this latter figure. 

In the past, it was banking practice to allow each dollar of currency to support $10 in credit. 

When this limit is reached, or exceeded-as it usually is in boom times-we reach a crisis. A panic follows, and depression ensues. This was the case in 1929. We had all extended our credit far beyond our ability to pay, and far beyond the point where currency and gold holdings would support it.

In the future our credit support practices may be somewhat restricted. This would be in accord with the Government's trend of thought in financial matters. Even if currency were allowed to support only $5 of credit, the $8,000,000,000 added to the $40,000,000,000 it would support would adequately care for our credit needs. A $5 215

ratio would be extremely conservative, to say the least. 

There is no question, therefore, of our ability to expand credit to normal proportions. The question arises in our willingness to go forward and use the available credit, and in our willingness to use it sanely and within our ability to support it without default.

The matter consequently simmers down to our confidence in the future prosperity of the United States and to our strength of character and will which has been gained, we hope, from the rigors and hardships of the years of depression and suffering arising from abuses of the past. 

Before we look into the future, it is fitting we review the immediate past and note the many things that have been accomplished to rebuild confidence in the future of our country and to prevent recurrence of the many evils of the past that brought excesses and resultant depressive reaction.

When President Roosevelt assumed executive leadership of our country on March 4, 1933, conditions throughout the nation were in a state bordering on complete collapse. Starting in February, with closing of all banks in the State of Michigan, there had been a successive wave of bank closings in other parts of the country. The first act of the 216

new President was to order all banks in every State of the country closed indefinitely. 

We all remember that occasion! We all, no doubt, recall the message sent to every corner of the nation over the radio-a message which minced no words, but breathed confidence and reassurance in the future.

The days of the bank moratorium were filled with humor and pathos, with contrary feelings of bewilderment mixed with relief and wonder. But they were days marking the first cycle of what the 

Administration has labeled "The New Deal." 

All payments in gold were likewise suspended, as were foreign exchange dealings. The country at that time went off the gold standard, although it was not called that for some weeks. 

Federal Reserve Banks, Clearing Houses and even individual communities made plans for issuance of millions of dollars worth of scrip. Some small amount of these "tokens" were actually issued in many places. 

Trading in security markets was, of course, suspended, and action of foreign markets, both security and commodity, was eagerly watched.

Congress was called into extraordinary session by the President. It made history in those weeks. 

Emergency legislation of stupendous proportions 217

was enacted almost without knowledge on the part of Congress of the content of many of the bills. 

The Emergency Banking Law was passed, placing an embargo on gold exports, forbidding hoarding and calling for return of nearly $1,400,- 000,000 of gold which had been taken out of banks by frightened depositors.

As suddenly as they had been closed, the banks were reopened on March 13th, after a colossal amount of research, study and planning on the part of a dozen or two Administration leaders in

Washington, leaders who had scarcely slept during those harrowing days and nights.

Banks were re-opened only under license from 

Federal and State authorities, and only after examination disclosed their individual solvency.

Most large and important banks readily qualified. Others required closer scrutiny; but none were allowed to open their doors unless their strength was proven. About 12,000 of the 18,000 banks of the country were declared sound and reopened in the first month. 

Within ten days the nation's confidence in its banking facilities was regained and reassured. 

From that day to this nothing remotely like the former fear and worry over bank solvency has reared its head in the United States. 218

THE FUTURE 

Conditions similar to those of the past four years were in evidence in the previous depression periods of 1873, 1885, 1896, 1907 and 1921. In 229

each case, they were followed by periods of good business, once the beginning of revived credit and restored confidence set in.

There are some who doubt the ability of the country to make any kind of a satisfactory recovery, because they argue that we have never been confronted with such an adverse set of world-wide conditions before. Similar contentions have been declared during every major depression. 

It is true that no two depressions are exactly alike in their relation to world economic conditions. But, from a fundamental viewpoint, the basic factors that make for an important depression are substantially the same.

The nations of the world are closely related economically. That is why deep depressions are generally world-wide. Recovery, by the same token, becomes a universal condition, once the trend toward better times becomes established. 

The United States, being the largest single community of advanced civilization, and the richest nation on the globe, will bring the rest of the world with it out of the depths of depression to the heights of prosperity. It is not so much a matter of world conditions affecting us, as of our affecting world conditions.

A busily engaged America automatically opens up diamond mines in Africa, revives the silk indus- 230

try of Japan, stimulates the perfume industry of France, uses up surplus of commodities like sugar, coffee and chemicals which are produced in such countries as Cuba, South America and Germany. 

In return, the rest of the world draws upon us for machinery and manufactured articles of all sorts. An exchange of credit and goods is developed, which helps to restore economic equilibrium and to stabilize commodity prices at more normal levels.

The great war was principally responsible for the conditions that led up to the depression. It dislocated production and credit throughout the greater part of the world; destroyed billions in property and untold wealth in human lives. As we pointed out in a preceding page, its aftermath brought on a period of inordinate over-production and an orgy of speculation. The world has paid dearly for its mad spree.

There are some who admit the premises that most of the economic ills of the world have been largely cured that the disease of deflation has run its course. Economic forces are so shaping themselves that a return of prosperous conditions is virtually inevitable.

We now have a clear idea of the road ahead which will take us out of the depression, avoid the 231

dangers of the past, and bring us once again into the regions of prosperity. 

We are on the road to recovery.

Even now well informed bankers and experienced investors are buying stocks and bonds, building now for their future income and profits. They are doing just what other shrewd investors did at the end of previous depressions. 

Strange as it may seem, they are unfortunately few in number.

The great mass of American investors who are not buying now, will be rushing to buy stocks 50 to 100 points higher. Believe it or not! 232
