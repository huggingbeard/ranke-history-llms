AM NEW HAMPSHIRE! I speak in the silence of mountain

IAN tops and the thunder of waterfalls. I am present in the fragrance of purple lilacs, the weathered roughness of granite rocks, and the green sanctuary of winding valleys. 

I am calling you. From the high solitudes of my Mount

Washington to the enclosed hush of my notches, I am calling you. From the most Northern Connecticut Lake to the departure of the Connecticut and Merrimack Rivers across my southern border, I am calling you. From the winding intervales of my Connecticut valley to the haunting rhythm of my ocean tides, I am calling you.

I have a gift for you. This gift is your rightful heritage. It is the product of Nature's miracles through untold centuries and

PREFACE 

yours of man's progress through many generations. If you have ears that understand and eyes that appreciate, this gift can be forever whether you remain with me or travel far away. For when my gift is taken into your heart it is never forgotten. It becomes a part of you. It is the gift of myself — New Hampshire!

I am as old as the Creation of the World. My short coastline was ancient neighbor of the pounding Atlantic, and my mountain peaks were early companions of the long-journeying winds. 

In the mysterious Beginning, all the earth was covered with ocean. 

When the water receded my White Mountains were among the first high peaks to behold the light of the newly-created sun and moon and stars. Then glaciers spread a heavy blanket of ice over me. Through long centuries the glaciers wore down my high mountain peaks. They broke off cliffs and carried them to distant places, carved bowls for lakes and valleys for rivers, chiselled odd rock formations, and crushed boulders to stony soil. Records of all these things are still visible. They are found in strange fossils on my hillsides, in the rocks of rivers and stonewalls, and in the granite profile of the Great Stone Face. 

I am rich in natural advantages that make life balanced and happy. I have the woodlands and waterfalls and farm intervales of a land refreshed by many rivers. When the glaciers melted away, I heard the music of rushing streams. From the slopes of my mountains in the north I saw brooks cascading down forest-clad notches to join other brooks escaping from the crystal silence of many lakes. Together these waters grew into widening streams in search of the long-remembered ocean. Some of the streams flashed down the sides of my rolling central plateau that sloped

south to Mount Kearsarge and Mount Monadnock. Other streams hurried east to ocean beaches patterned with salt marshes and rocky headlands. I became a mother of many rivers. The long valleys of my Connecticut and Merrimack, the broad waters of my Piscataqua, and the winding corridors of my Saco and Androscoggin, were like strong arteries pulsing from my heart. 

Someday these rivers and their tributaries would become natural highways leading inland from the ocean to my ancient mountains. 

Someday I would speak, and people would hear my voice. They would come, and I would give them farm lands and forests and water power and great beauty.

The first people who came were the color of oak leaves in 

Autumn. My lakes and rivers reflected their campfires and canoes and homes of animal skins. These first people had no knowledge of books, but they soon learned wisdom from my woods and streams, and their hearts were reverent toward the Creator. For many generations I was contented. Then I came to realize that the few and simple needs of these people were not enough. I had rich resources which they could never imagine or use. I felt a great longing for people who would challenge and develop my land and forests and rivers. 

So I began to dream. I dreamed of farms and towns, factories and highways, homes and schools and churches. I dreamed of a busy life balanced by the good things of my four seasons and inspired by the ancient grandeur of my scenery. I dreamed of people coming to work and play and worship. Many skilled artisans would pursue a variety of crafts at my waterfalls, transform my trees into homes and ships, and translate my granite into

great buildings. And many seekers after peace and beauty would linger by my lakes and seashore and climb my mountains. I dreamed of young folks, like you, who would learn to love me and who would inherit the glory of my past and the promise of my future. 

And while I dreamed, a white sail appeared where the Atlantic and Sunrise meet. Fair-skinned strangers explored my shore and went away. They must have heard my voice in the pine trees and repeated what they heard, for other ships soon arrived. More and more people came. Their settlements grew into towns, paths became roads, and mill wheels increased at my waterfalls. My ancient dream was coming true!

More than three centuries have gone by since that sail appeared out of the East. Today, millions of people know and love me, and the products of my land and trees and waterfalls are beyond my counting. Yet this is only the beginning of my dream.

I shall accomplish even greater things in the years ahead. I need searching minds and creative hands. I need young folks, like you. I am calling you. I am New Hampshire!

CHAPTER I Settlements by the Sea 

HE recorded history of New Hampshire began in 1614 when England's renowned adventurer, Captain John Smith, wrote a report of a trip he had just taken along the New England coast. Since then, during more than three centuries, people from all over the world have added their lines and paragraphs to New Hampshire's story. And still the story grows. 

Smith's interest in the New World dated back to the accounts of other early explorers and to the claims of King James I of England who in 1606 announced that England owned American territory from the Cape Fear River in North Carolina to Halifax, Nova Scotia. The king divided the territory into northern and southern halves which he granted to two groups of leading Englishmen known as the North Virginia Company and the

South Virginia Company. In 1607 the South Virginia Company established a colony at Jamestown, Virginia, and Smith came over to the new settlement. After several interesting experiences, he returned to England full of enthusiasm for the New World. 

He was ambitious to see English colonies started all along the American coast, and he grew impatient when the North Virginia Company did nothing about colonizing its grant. He finally decided to visit the neglected northern region and study its geography and natural resources in relation to colonies and trade. 

So in 1614 Smith sailed again for America. He explored the coasts of Maine, New Hampshire, and Massachusetts, discovered the Piscataqua River, and visited the Isles of Shoals which he named Smith's Isles in his own honor. When he again returned to England he published a map and a description of his trip. This report contained the first recorded facts about New 

Hampshire's coastline and natural resources. Smith gave his report to King James' son, Prince Charles, who named the new region New England. The report attracted wide attention and awakened a new interest in this northern territory. As a result, a colony was established at Plymouth, Massachusetts by the Pilgrims in the autumn of 1620. Less than three years later New 

Hampshire's first settlement was started at Pannaway, near the mouth of the Piscataqua River mentioned in Smith's report.

Meanwhile, other settlements were established along the Atlantic coast, until finally New Hampshire was one of thirteen colonies which became the foundation of the United States.

He finally centered his attention on a pleasant region lying south and west of the Piscataqua River. 

At first he called the region Mariana, but in 1629 he changed the name to New Hampshire in honor of his home county of Hampshire. Smith's report had convinced Mason that a colony at the mouth of the Piscataqua River would yield fish, lumber, and furs for a profitable trade with England, and he devoted a large part of his fortune to getting such a colony started. His faith in the venture and his willingness to spend his money freely to make it a success rightfully entitle him to be called the father of New Hampshire. Pannaway

IN 1622, Mason and his associates selected David Thomson to start New Hampshire's first settlement. Thomson, a big, blue-eyed Scotchman, was well educated for those days and had a personal knowledge of the country to which he was being sent. 

His father-in-law, a ship builder, had made it possible for him to travel more than most people could, and he had only recently returned from a trip to New England. Mason and his company granted Thomson 6,000 acres of land along the coast south of the Piscataqua River, helped to equip him and ten other men for the voyage to New Hampshire, and secured a ship for them 2

called the Jonathan. As the new colonists sailed away toward the West, Mason hoped for early and large profits from Amerifish, lumber, and furs. can

In May, 1623, after a journey of several weeks, the Jonathan anchored in a sheltered cove by a high point of land. Just north of the cove, the broad Piscataqua River emptied into the sea. 

Great Island, now Newcastle, lay green at the river's mouth, forests of pine and oak covered the mainland, and the dark ledge of Great Boar's Head jutted sharply against the sky in the south. 

The point of land which Thomson chose for New Hampshire's first settlement is now called Odiorne's Point. At that time it was known by the Indians as Pannaway or Little Harbor. It had a fresh-water spring, a safe harbor, and fertile soil. Wild game was plentiful, and there were forests for lumber, and friendly Indians for trading valuable furs. Cod and mackerel were abundant in the sea, and the streams had seasonal runs of salmon, shad, and eel. Along the shore were salt marshes where salt could be collected for curing fish for distant markets. All these natural resources were sure to attract more fishermen, lumbermen, and fur traders as time went on, as well as ship builders, merchants, and farmers. From the beginning, New Hampshire offered a variety of ways for getting a living.

Thomson's first job was to get some sort of a shelter built. 

It was very crude, with a stone fireplace and walls made of rubble stone and logs chinked with swale grass and clay. The furniture was probably made of logs and the bare earth served as a floor. 

It is supposed that all the men in Thomson's group lived in the one house for several years, together with some of their families

who came over later. The building itself disappeared long ago but historians found the spot where they believed it stood and a monument marks the place today. 

None of Thomson's men kept diaries and there were no newspapers in the New World then, so very little is known about what this small group of colonists did for the first few years. This is what the first settlement on New Hampshire soil at Odiorne's Point might have looked like

Probably the next thing after building a shelter was to clear a piece of land and plant a few vegetables to eat with the native bear meat, venison, and fish. No doubt the ships which kept coming from England brought a few cows and pigs and maybe some oxen, and these useful animals required care. The men surely must have built boats soon after they arrived because they

were supposed to catch fish and salt them and send them back to England. They probably made friends with the Indians and persuaded them to accept knives, beads, and cloth in exchange for furs which were also sent to England. But doubtless the men spent most of their time just getting enough food for themselves, cutting wood for the fireplace especially during the long winter months, and keeping their clothes and guns in good condition. 

In the meantime, more and more people were coming over from England, but few of them stayed at Pannaway. 

They went further up the Piscataqua River and some of its tributaries and built their cabins and fish wharves wherever they could find good sites. It was more sheltered along the rivers and easier to get around by boat, which was the common way of travelling until roads were built. The Pannaway settlers began to realize that the spot they had picked out wasn't so good after all and they started leaving. Most of them found new places a little further inland, while Thomson, according to some historians, moved to an island near Boston. Finally Pannaway was abandoned altogether, but even though Thomson and his little settlement disappeared, they deserve credit for getting New 

Hampshire started.

New Hampshire in 1650

ALTHOUGH new settlers arriving in New Hampshire in 1650 didn't find many people, probably less than 1,000 all together, they did find an amazing variety of activities. Everybody worked from dawn to dark six days a week, and even on Sunday there were many chores that had to be done. As there were no stores in New Hampshire at that time, families had to get or make for themselves nearly everything they needed or wanted, such as food and clothing, dishes and furniture, light and heat, medicines, and playthings and ornaments. In addition to providing all these items, the men had to engage in fishing, fur trading, and wood working in order to have products which they could exchange with England or the West Indies for things they couldn't produce themselves, such as sugar, cooking salt, tea and spices, articles made of iron, and sometimes a fine piece of cloth for a lady's dress. On top of all these things, there were always community

jobs to be done, such as building grist mills and saw mills, constructing boats to get up and down the rivers, clearing rough trails and building carts for carrying heavy loads, making clay bricks for chimneys, clearing land for buildings, crops, or pastures, Groups of little cabins grew up at several spots around the seacoast area in the early days of New Hampshire organizing town governments, attending to matters of religion and education, and protecting themselves from Indians and wild animals. A man's work, or a woman's, was never done in those days, and if a family had an hour or two on Sunday afternoon to sit around and do nothing, they thought they were wasting

time. But in spite of the long hours of hard work nobody complained. These people were building for themselves a new home in a country they had chosen and they were content. But because there were so many things to do and so few people to do them, progress in New Hampshire was painfully slow. 

ONE of the earliest activities in New Hampshire was fishing, all kinds and varieties of fishing. One reason for this was that the people needed fish for food. Another reason was that dried and salted fish was a salable product in England. Mason and the other men who financed these early settlements expected to get something back from them; in fact they expected to get rich and they were urging the early settlers all the time to send back fish, furs, and lumber which could be turned into money. So New 

Hampshire in 1650 had all kinds of fishing enterprises. Fishing wharves were built soon after the first cabins were put up, and young folks of those early days, like Susanna and Samuel Hilton of Dover, probably spent more time than they should watching fishermen returning from the Isles of Shoals with a day's catch of two or three hundred cod or mackerel. The fish were tossed onto the wharves then carried to open sheds where they were split, salted, and dried on wooden platforms called flakes. The salt used on the fish was gathered from the salt marshes by leaving marsh water standing in clay-lined hollows until the water dried up and left coarse, strong salt. When cured, the small fish were kept at home for winter eating, and the best ones were shipped to England, the West Indies, and ports around the Mediterranean Sea in exchange for sugar, cotton, cooking salt, and other

products. In the spring and early summer, fishermen caught salmon, shad, and eel in nets stretched across the rivers below the falls. Other salt water foods included oysters from Fore River, lobsters from Little Bay and Great Bay, and so many clams from the Back River region that the Dover settlers fed quantities of them to their pigs.

NEW HAMPSHIRE people were drawing heavily on the forests for all kinds of products in this early period. Here again, English merchants were urging the production of pine masts, clapboards, and barrels which could be sold for hard cash in England or the West Indies. Some of the best mast trees were thirty-five inches through, over one hundred feet high, and worth five hundred dollars which was an enormous price in those days. 

Clapboards and barrels were made in cooper shops and were commonly used in place of money. The clapboards, split and shaved to measure four feet long and six or eight inches wide, were used locally as frame houses gradually replaced the early log cabins, and many were sent with shipments of barrels to the

West Indies. The barrels were needed by West Indies traders, especially on English-owned Barbados Island, for shipping cane sugar and molasses to England and America. As early as 1642, the demand for clapboards and barrels was great enough so the four towns had to pass laws to prevent wasteful cutting of trees. 

For instance, Dover ordered that no man should cut down more than ten trees at a time and he must use them before cutting any more or pay a fine of ten shillings or about two dollars and a half for every extra tree. In 1657 the number was limited to five. Joseph Austin was appointed to fine anyone breaking this law, and he was allowed half of every fine he collected. rye, up Although New Hampshire men tried to produce all the things that the English merchants wanted, a large part of their time and effort was devoted to improving their villages and manner of living. The first thing every town wanted as soon as possible was a grist mill. These people raised their own corn and but grinding it by hand was slow work and carrying it by foot or cart several miles to a mill in the next village was hard and slow, too. So, since a grist mill was of benefit to every body, all the men turned out to help when one was built. Exeter was lucky enough to have Thomas Wilson build a grist mill its first year. Like other early grist mills, this one had a large wooden wheel with buckets around the rim, installed so that the falling water filled the buckets and their weight turned the wheel. The turning wheel caused two flat stones to revolve against each other and grind the corn or rye dropped between them. As money was scarce, Wilson took furs as pay or kept part of the meal he ground. These villages were also anxious to have saw mills.

Without a saw mill, lumber had to be sawed in a saw pit. This was hard work. A log was laid across a deep pit, and a twohandled saw was pushed up and down by two men, one standing in the pit beneath the log, and one above it. 

Exeter's first saw mill, built by Edward Gilman, was considered a great improvement over the saw pit. It had a sash saw which was pulled down 

A gundalow, a kind of clumsy boat used on the bays and rivers in the early days of New Hampshire by a waterwheel crank and lifted up by the spring-like action of a limber pole. The saw cut only on the down stroke and probably required twenty minutes to slice one board off a log. Crude as those early grist mills and saw mills seem now, they were a great help to early New Hampshire families.

SHIP and boat building engaged the time of many New Hampshire men in 1650. They made boats for fishing, gundalows and ferry boats for river travel; and ships for trading along the coast and with England and other distant points. All these boats could be built right beside the towns that needed them because nearby forests and saw mills at the several waterfalls made lumber easy to get, and there were sheltered bays for ship yards and ready markets when the ships were finished. At Dover's 

Cocheco Falls, Richard Waldron's mill made timbers and boards that went into many sloops and ketches built in the coves along

Many early New Hampshire people spent a good deal of time fishing Another job that required a lot of time in 1650 was the clearing and development of land. Clearing the land was hard, slow work, so at first there were no farms as farms are known today. Among the tree stumps the men planted patches of corn and rye and uneven plots of beans, turnips, pumpkins, and squashes, but the limited crops hardly fed their large families, even along Back River where the soil was unusually fertile. As 

Ambrose Gibbons of Portsmouth wrote to Mason in 1634, he and his wife and child, together with four men, had lived for

three months on one-half barrel of corn and a small amount of meat, and things weren't much better by 1650. Pasture land was as scarce as tillable land. Mason had sent cattle from Denmark to Portsmouth in 1632, and as the stock increased, the problem of pasturage also increased. Until each family was able to clear a pasture lot of its own, they turned their cattle into one common plot and that is why in some New Hampshire towns the village green is still called the "common." Hampton fenced off part of its Meeting House Green for a cow common, and Exeter paid Gowen Wilson in corn and butter to drive his neighbors' cattle to the common pasture every morning and return them at night to their owners. As for hay for winter feed, Exeter was importing part of its annual supply from coast areas as late as 1692. Added to the shortage of cleared lands were the problems connected with land ownership. The settlers wanted to feel that they owned the land they were trying to develop, but when John Mason died in 1635 his heirs made trouble over what New 

Hampshire land belonged to them. Then in the hundred years beginning in 1641, New Hampshire and Massachusetts united and separated several times and land grants were made by each government which often conflicted with previous claims.

Home Life in 1650

Meanwhile, New Hampshire's government had undergone

several changes. In 1679, after thirty-eight years of union with 

Massachusetts, New Hampshire was made a royal province by the king. In 1686 New Hampshire was made part of the Dominion of New England which lasted for three years. 

Then for a time New Hampshire was left without any outside government, due to political confusion in England. But starting in 1692 political matters settled down again and from then until New Hampshire became a free and independent state during the Revolution it managed most of its own affairs. Connecticut River Settlements 

IN THE meantime people were moving into New Hampshire's other main river valley, that of the Connecticut, although at a slower rate. The Connecticut, or "long tidal river" as the Indians called it, was quite different from the Merrimack, with few waterfalls, and extensive quiet stretches in between which loitered around great curves known as ox bows. There was a great deal of flat, fertile meadow land along the river and hundreds of attractive home sites. Since everybody depended on the land and its products for life, the Connecticut valley would have probably been more popular with the pioneers than the Merrimack had it not been for the Indians. But the quiet river, which provided an easy waterway for incoming settlers in the spring and a broad highway of ice in the winter, was equally convenient for raiding Indians from the North, who roamed up and down for many years looking for white men to capture.

Progress of Fifty Years

As we look back now at this period of New Hampshire's story we might think that nothing much had been accomplished. 

It is true that nothing very exciting happened. But in a period covering roughly half a century most of New Hampshire's area had been laid out for settlement and nearly every section had people in it. This was the first step in taming a wilderness and it was an important job. Some of the towns established early in this period looked something like a town today. The newer ones were simply collections of cabins in the woods connected by footpaths. Each town or settlement governed itself; they had little communication with each other; and they paid no attention to what was going on in Portsmouth or England. They were building themselves homes and clearing the land, and that was important.

The End of An Era 

Gov. WENTWORTH left New Hampshire suddenly in 1775.

Beginning with 1770 feeling in the coastal towns of the thirteen colonies had risen so sharply against England that open war at last seemed certain. Wentworth decided to throw in his lot on the side of England. His leaving marked the end of the colonial period in New Hampshire, a period of a century and a half during which the present area of the state had been pretty well filled up with people. New Hampshire was soon to form its own independent government and later to join with the other colonies in forming the new republic. But New Hampshire made a good deal of progress under Gov. Wentworth, as we have just seen, and because of his efforts it entered the war more united and more powerful than most of the other colonies. New Hampshire might have considered in 1774 that it had at last reached manhood, after a long growing process, and that it was finally ready to go out on its own and do a man's job.

For instance, if you could have taken a stagecoach tour through New Hampshire in the early 1770's, a year or two before the Revolutionary War began, you wouldn't have found any preparations for a war going on, or even any serious thought of war. Around Portsmouth you would have heard a good deal of angry talk against the English government, but it wasn't very different from the kind of talk about government that you can hear almost any time. Some of the men in Portsmouth who made their living from the shipping business weren't making so much

Growth of Population 

NO MATTER what else happened in New Hampshire, its population always went on growing, and that was especially important in these years after the war. In 1774, when a count of the population was made, it was found that there were 82,000 people in the state. But in 1790, when the first federal census was taken, the state had grown to 142,000, a tremendous increase. By 1800, the population approached the 200,000 mark. Some of the increase was from the big families everybody had in those days, but people were also coming in from Massachusetts and eastern New York where good land was getting scarce, and some came from England. But wherever they came from, most of them went on the land and by 1800 most of the desirable land had been taken up. 

Not all went on the land however. And one of the interesting developments of this period was the growth of the small mills

and home industries, especially in the older towns. In the beginning, a town felt itself lucky to have one gristmill. If it had a sawmill as well, it felt itself very well off. Things were different now. In 1795 a traveller in New Hampshire stopped at Exeter and found that it had not one gristmill but ten, several sawmills, a fulling mill for finishing homespun cloth, a mill for making shingles and clapboards, a snuff mill (snuff is a form of tobacco used by some people in those days) and some mills for making chocolate. The other older towns were similarly well supplied. These mills are not to be confused with the factories and factory system which came later, but they mark the beginning of division of labor which had not been noticeable before. In the earlier days of New Hampshire, as we have seen, every family had to do everything for itself, and did nothing very well. But now, the various kinds of work were beginning to be divided up. 

A miller ground all the corn for a group of families and made his living that way; people in Londonderry made cloth for everybody; of cobblers, as in Northwood, for example, made shoes not only for themselves but for whole communities. CHAPTER VI Coming of the Factories 

IF YOU could have visited a New Hampshire family on the [last day of December 1800, you would probably have found quite a little excitement. For the next day was not only the beginning of a new year, it was also the beginning of a new century, and that's always an occasion. We can imagine that there were many New Year parties held in the state's larger towns that night and that there was a good deal of speculation about what the new century would bring forth in the way of progress. 

It is very doubtful, however, if anybody foresaw what was going to happen in New Hampshire in the next 50 or 60 years.

What happened was a real change in people's way of life, some-

thing that had not happened before. Up to 1800 the main activity in the state, as we have seen, was clearing and settling, with more and more people taking up land. And the average family in 1800 lived very much as their great grandfathers and great grandmothers had in 1700. Each family still raised most of its own food, provided its own light and heat, made its own clothes, and people still worked most of the time just to get those bare necessities.

But by 1860, when the Civil War came, most people were living quite differently. Factories had grown up in the meantime which were making a number of different kinds of goods and merchandise, and the railroads had come so that these goods could be moved around easily. All the larger towns and many of the smaller ones had stores, and instead of having to make everythings they wanted, families could now buy many of those things; and two of the most important - we take them for granted today - - were stoves for heating and cooking, and oil lamps to replace the old fashioned candles. Many of the men, instead of working on farms as nearly all of them had in the older days, now earned their living by working in shops and factories and mills. And under this new arrangement everybody had more of everything than they ever had before and they didn't have to work quite so hard to get it. 

This change in the way of living was taking place at the same time in other parts of the United States, of course, as well as in New Hampshire, and it is generally called the Industrial Revolution. It included several different developments. One of them was pointed out in the last chapter, the beginning of a

division of work, that is, one man a miller, another a cobbler, and so on. Another new development was power machinery. 

Using first the power from waterwheels and later from steam engines, men in both England and America were inventing machines to do jobs faster and easier than they could be done by hand. Machinery of this kind couldn't be set up in a home, so shops and factories had to be built to house them. One man didn't have enough money to build one of these mills, so men clubbed together, each one putting in a little, the beginning of the modern stock companies. With machinery and goods to be moved from one place to another, there was a new demand for easy transportation, and roads, canals, and later railroads were built. All these things fitted together to produce the changed way of living.

CHAPTER VII 

Canals, Turnpikes, and Railroads

T WAS pointed out in the previous chapter that one of the important parts of the change in New Hampshire's way of life between 1800 and 1860 was the development of transportation. You can understand what a really great change took place when you remember that in 1800 the only way to go anywhere or to move anything was by horse or ox-cart over very primitive roads. But in the following 60 years a whole system of canals and turnpike roads were built and used, and then they, in turn, were replaced by railroads which could carry freight and passengers at the rate of 20 or 25 miles an hour, considered a very dizzy

speed in those days. And along with the improvement in the means of transportation came an improvement in communications. 

In 1800, the only way to send a letter was by post rider, a man on horseback, and it might take two or three weeks for the letter to arrive. By 1860, the postal system was well organized and in addition the telegraph had been invented so that messages could be sent instantaneously for hundreds of miles. All these things, added to the coming of the factories, made a great difference in the way people lived.

The Canal and River Waterways 

CANALS were being talked about in the last years of the 1700's, and some New Hampshire people were very much interested in the idea of using canals and rivers to carry freight of various kinds. This was natural because New Hampshire had two big rivers, the Connecticut and the Merrimack, and many of the early settlers had used the rivers as roads to get into the wilderness. It was easy for them, with small boats or canoes, because every time they came to a falls or a rocky stretch they could take their boats out and carry them around. But you couldn't do that with the bigger freight boats and there wasn't much use in running them just between the falls. 

But men were dreaming about what a fine thing it would be if you could build canals with locks in them around these falls, so that boats with heavy loads could go up and down the river. 

Nothing much was done about it, however, until the Middlesex canal was built in Massachusetts. This canal started at Boston and came up to Lowell. But Lowell was on the Merrimack river

But Samuel Blodget was sure a canal, with locks, could be built around the Amoskeag Falls. He explained that really a very simple affair, just a section of the canal with gates at each end. You ran the boat in, closed the gates at each end; then, if you wanted to raise the boat, you let the water in, or if you wanted to lower the boat, you let the water out. Blodget had made quite a lot of money as a merchant and lumber dealer and he decided to spend it on a canal. The interesting thing is that he was 70 years old when he started his canal in 1793, saying it would take only five years. The idea was all right, but when it came to carrying it out, it was quite a job. The canal had to be a mile long to get to smooth water above the falls, and there was a difference of water level of 50 feet, which meant not one set of locks, but several. Blodget spent all his own money, and got his friends to put in their money. People became convinced it couldn't be done. Instead of five years, the task took 14. But one day in 1807 Blodget stood proudly on the deck of a small boat which was taken into the canal below the falls and finally floated out onto the river above.

When the success of the Manchester canal was firmly estab lished, there was a great rush to take advantage of this new way of transportation. Similar canals and locks were built around bad spots in the river at Bow and Hooksett so that by 1810 or 1812 boats could load at Boston and go all the way to Concord

without unloading. Companies were organized at once to operate boats on the river. The boats were not large because the channel of the river was not very deep, but they were 25 to 30 feet long and could carry as much as 15 tons even in the upper reaches of the river. Down below they sometimes carried as much as 25 tons. These were not true canal boats drawn by horses or oxen, but river boats which were rowed or poled up the river and which often used sails. The boats were fitted with sails to take advantage of the long stretches of quiet water. Going down, they took advantage of the current, going up they had to be poled or rowed. Since the poles and oars were generally made of white ash wood, the river crews used to say that their boats ran by "white ash breeze."

Getting the boats up the river was hard and arduous work and only the biggest men could stand it. They were generally called "River Gods" and they were admired by young and old all along the river. People came down to the wharves to see the boats come in, and they listened for the songs of the boat crews as they passed. Many thousands of tons of farm products and lumber went down the river on these vessels, and thousands of tons of other things, such as hardware and glass and paint, went up.

This Connecticut River traffic started, as far as New Hampshire was concerned, just about 1800, when a series of locks were built around Turner's Falls on the river in Massachusetts. That made it possible for freight boats to get up as far as Bellows Falls and Charlestown. A few years later locks were built around Bellows Falls, then around Sumner's Falls still further up the river and finally around rough water opposite Hanover. Meantime another canal had been built on the lower river at Enfield, Conn. 

That made it possible for boats to traverse almost the whole length of the river from Hartford, Conn., to the vicinity of 

Woodsville, N. H. The river traffic which grew up then flour-

ished for nearly 40 years, until the railroads began taking away freight after 1840. It took 20 days to make the trip up-river from 

Hartford to Woodsville, and ten to go back. We cover the same distance today in less than a day by automobile or train, in an hour or two by plane. But in the early 1800's the river boats were a great improvement over any other means of transportation and most people regarded them as very fast. 

And at one time, around 1830, there were a few steamboats operating on the upper Connecticut River. Morey, you recall, had built a little one before 1800. They were rapidly improved and developed and the "Blanchard," the "Vermont," and the "Ledyard,” steam side-wheelers, were seen occasionally as far up as Bellows Falls. But it was hard to get them through the small locks and the only established steamboat business on the Connecticut was below Hartford, Conn. Altogether the traffic on the 

Connecticut and Merrimack Rivers was an interesting and picim- turesque chapter in New Hampshire's history, and a very portant one economically.

Development of Turnpike Roads 

IT WAS Young Governor Wentworth, you remember, who first dreamed of long distance roads for New Hampshire and who managed to get three built with general funds of the province. 

Nothing more was done for several years. But just before 1800 the state became interested again in the road problem. It didn't feel wealthy enough to build roads itself, but it gave permission to groups of men who built roads with their own money and then were allowed to charge tolls for their use, just as you have to pay

tolls to cross some bridges today. Such roads were called turnpikes because of the gates at the toll houses.

People in the northern part of the state also wanted roads, and turnpikes were built all through the principal passes of the

White Mountains in these early years of the century. The Crawford Notch road was one of the first and quite a freighting business grew up on it. Most of the loads were hauled in winter, however, when horses and oxen with sleds could get along more easily over the snow. A few families living along the road turned their houses into taverns for the convenience of the drivers and these early tavern keepers such as the Rosebrooks, the Willeys and the

Crawfords, were quite famous in New Hampshire. 

The Franconia Notch road was built in 1805, from Woodstock to Franconia. The crew camped out along the route as the work progressed. While they were camped at Profile Lake, one of the men happened to look up and noticed the rock formation that thousands of people all over the country know today as the Old Man of the Mountains. This road builder is supposed to have been the first white man to see it, but we can't be sure. 

Within a few years, a road was built into the Kinsman Notch wilderness, then a route was laid out with great difficulty through the rocky and steep ravine of Dixville Notch. Finally, a road was built through Pinkham Notch, connecting Jackson and Randolph.

This last passed the lonely cottage of Dolly and Hayes Copp which became a popular stopping place for the teamsters. People travelling the road could always be sure of a warm welcome there and Dolly's apple butter was famous all over the state.

These roads were very poor and primitive, no better than woods roads hastily built today, but an immense amount of freight was moved over them. People in the interior sent down their surplus farm products such as butter and cheese, or pork and lard to be sold in the bigger towns, and in return they got cloth and

hardware and salt and other things they needed. These things were transported by wagon or cart or by sled and the amount of toll you paid for the use of the road depended on what kind of conveyance you used. A two-wheeled cart drawn by a horse or ox was taxed a cent and a half a mile, a coach and four horses paid three cents a mile, and a sled and a horse two cents. A man on horseback paid a cent a mile. You could also drive animals over the roads in groups; the rate for ten cattle, for instance, was two cents a mile.

Some of these roads were made mail routes and were called post roads. The mailman rode horseback and delivered and collected letters all along his route. This postal service was conducted by the national government and every effort was made to get the mail through in the shortest time. Ferrymen at the rivers could be fined $20 if they held up a post rider unnecessarily. If a post rider's horse failed him, the nearest farmer was expected to provide another on a rental basis. The riders got special rates at the inns and taverns, and they were paid six cents a mile for carrying the mail. That made postage high and people didn't write many letters. For instance, it cost 30 cents in the first days of the postal system to send a letter from New Hampshire to Philadelphia.

When the roads were straightened out a bit, stage-coaches started running over them to carry passengers, only a few at first, then more and more. At the height of the stagecoach period before the Civil War, there were 30 different stage lines running in and out of Concord. Haverhill, up on the Connecticut River, was another coaching center, like a railroad junction, with many lines running in and out. Taverns grew up along these roads

where travellers could get food and lodging. As time went on, the coaches took over the carrying of mail and small packages. 

The coach lines were organized very much like the railroads, running on a schedule, changing horses and drivers every so often, and making the best time they could. Because of the poor roads, Second class passengers had to walk, third class had to push when the coach came to a hill however, it is doubtful if they averaged more than five miles an hour over long trips. A great improvement in coaching came about toward the end when men learned how to build bridges over the larger streams, which before that had been forded. These bridges were built of wooden beams and girders and they were covered

to protect the beams from the weather. Some of these old covered bridges still remain in New Hampshire. 

An idea of what a trip in one of these coaches was like is provided by the story of a New Hampshire coach driver who had three rates of fare on his run. First class passengers paid $7, second class, $3, and third class, $1.50. They all rode in the same coach and were treated alike until the coach came to a hill or a bad spot. Then the first class passengers remained in the coach, the second class got out and walked, but the third class got out and pushed!

The Concord Coaches NEW HAMPSHIRE made an interesting and important con tribution to the general business of stagecoaching through the manufacture of the famous Concord coaches, a flourishing business in Concord for more than half a century. Lewis Downing realized early in the century that with new roads being built carriages and wagons and coaches would be in demand, and he started making wagons by hand in 1813, starting with $125 of capital. 

In 1826, he went into partnership with J. Stephens Abbot and soon after began making the coaches which were to carry the name of Concord all over the world. The business grew rapidly, employing several hundred men and producing several hundred coaches, wagons, buggies, hacks and the like every year.

The coaches were the most famous product, however. They were in use all over the United States at one time, especially in the West before the railroads developed. They were in demand above all others because they were toughly built of New Hampshire

hardwoods and stood up remarkably well under the wracking of the terribly rough roads of the time. Some of the coaches were finished with red plush seats and elaborate paint designs and sold to kings and queens and noblemen in Europe, and in many of the world. parts other

One of the features of the coaches was the system by which the body was hung over the wheels and axles on two heavy leather straps. These were not as effective as the steel springs which most wheeled vehicles have today, but they softened the shock of some of the lurching caused by rocks and holes in the roads. The coaches were heavy, equipped with good brakes, would carry several passengers, and required four to six horses to pull them. 

Even with six horses, their top speed was not over ten miles an hour.

At least two of these coaches are still on public display in New Hampshire and all New Hampshire boys and girls should see them. One is at the Flume in Franconia Notch and the other in the Concord railroad station. They are one of the many things of which New Hampshire may be proud.

The boat people might have wondered if the new plaything would eventually take away their business, but nobody thought so in 1838. 

They were wrong. Four years later the railroad had been extended to Concord, and the little trains were proving that they could haul more freight and haul it faster and more cheaply than either the river boats or the wagons and ox-carts. It was because most of New Hampshire's growing factories were located in the Merrimack valley that the first railroad was built in that valley; the railroad, in turn, helped the factories to grow at a faster rate.

Other railroads were built in New Hampshire very quickly. 

In 1841 a line from Boston into Maine was completed, running through Portsmouth. The Northern Railroad Co. built a line in 1846 from Concord to Franklin, and two years later this line had been extended to reach Lebanon and the Connecticut River. 

Keene had a railroad coming in from the south in 1849, and Exeter was linked to Haverhill, Mass., by rail that same year. A year later the Exeter line had been pushed north to Dover. Meanwhile, the Boston, Concord, and Montreal railroad was pushing north, reaching Tilton in 1848, Meredith in 1849, Plymouth the next year, Warren in 1851 and Woodsville in 1853.

The railroads killed the picturesque river traffic almost at

once, and some of the stage routes. However, the railroads were built only through the larger towns and cities, leaving many areas of the state just as they were, with only wagons and coaches for transportation. Stagecoaches, therefore, continued to be used for many years in the state, although in reduced numbers. In fact, the last stages didn't disappear until they were replaced by automobiles and busses in the present century. 

The new railroads not only carried freight, but they also carried passengers and soon took over the job of carrying mail and express. And with improved mail service the publication of newspapers and magazines began to spread. By the middle of the century, a few years before the Civil War, most of the larger cities in New Hampshire had their own newspapers, and several national magazines had been established, and copies were to be found in New Hampshire homes. People no longer felt cut off from the next town and the rest of the world; they knew what was going on. 

Then in 1844 Morse invented the telegraph. Like the railroads, this was considered a toy at first, but pretty soon wires began appearing along the railroad tracks, there was a funny clacking noise in the railroad stations, and people in Concord could know what was going on in New York five minutes after it happened.

Other things were happening in New Hampshire. More academies were being built, and hundreds of little district schools, and public libraries were established. Many of the fine old churches the state is proud of today were built in the first half of this century. Quite a few people started going to college; many homes had running water in them, piped in from a spring on a hillside. Iron stoves were installed for heating and cooking.

Roads were constantly being improved, better houses were being built in the smaller towns and villages. Altogether this was a period of rapid progress. As pointed out at the beginning of the chapter, New Hampshire people were living quite a different way of life in 1860 than they were in 1800.

CHAPTER VIII 

Webster, Pierce, and the Civil War

HILE New Hampshire had been busy building factories and a transportation system, the country as a whole had been edging toward civil war. There were were many hidden and complex causes for the war and historians are not sure yet that they have the whole matter straight. But the principal cause was slavery. The Northern states had been developing a factory system and a way of life in general that had no place or need for slave labor. The Southern states believed that unless they could retain slave labor, their whole plantation system would collapse. 

In order to maintain a balance of power in Congress, Southern leaders made every possible effort to see that as many of the new states being formed in the Middle West were slave as were free.

This race to keep up with the North started about 1830, when New Hampshire's factories were just beginning to get well established, and it continued until the war started in 1860. By that time, the national government had acquired by treaty or conquest or settlement all the land as far west as the Pacific Ocean, and the present boundaries of the United States had been fixed. 

There would probably have been no war over slavery alone, but when the South realized that it was not going to be able to get what it wanted out of Congress, several of the Southern states threatened to leave the Union and set up a separate republic of their own. Some of the national leaders thought this might be all right, let them go if they wanted to. But others were certain that if the United States were to be broken up into several smaller nations, these countries would be at war most of the time just as they were in Europe. 

Daniel Webster 

Daniel Webster, one of New Hampshire's most famous sons, was one of those who believed that above all the states of the Union must remain together, that no state or group of states had a right to leave the Union. Webster was a member of the 

National Congress for many years and earned a reputation as a great orator. You might think that a man who did nothing but talk could not be a very important figure. But ideas and words are powerful things and historians now agree that Webster may have played almost as important a part in preserving the Union as did Abraham Lincoln. For years, every time he had an opportunity, Webster preached the gospel of unity, pointing out

again and again that the only way for the states to get along without trouble was to remain together under one government as one great country. We all know today that he was completely right, but not everybody thought so before the Civil War. And because Webster was a gifted speaker, he was able to convert thousands of Americans to his point of view. When the war finally came, men in the North who had no particular convictions about slavery, were willing to fight and to die in order to keep the union of states together. Webster should get a good deal of the credit for that. He made many speeches on the subject of unity, and phrases from one of them, "Liberty and union, now and forever, one and inseparable" have become part of the nation's tradition. Every school child knows them. 

If you had seen Webster growing up in New Hampshire as a boy you would never have chosen him for a future great man.

He was a country boy, shy and bashful and seemed to have no gifts. He was born January 18, 1782, in Franklin, in a little two room house which you can still see. At that time there were few settlers in the area, no near neighbors. Daniel's father, Ebenezer, ran a little sawmill on Punch brook and did a little farming. 

When Daniel was only three years old, the family moved down into the Merrimack River valley, onto a farm, and the Webster home became a tavern. There was a good deal of travel up and down the valley, as you will remember from previous chapters, and many interesting people, some from outside the state, stopped at the Webster tavern. Apparently young Webster listened to what these men had to say and became interested in what was going on in the rest of the world. At any rate, he began borrow-

ing every book he could lay his hands on and reading them all, and his father decided the boy should have a good education.

It meant putting a mortgage on the farm but Daniel was sent to 

Phillips Exeter academy and then to Dartmouth College. He trained for law and his first office was at Boscawen. His father had the satisfaction of hearing him plead his first criminal case in the little courthouse that is now Plymouth's town library. But 

Webster was learning the power of his tongue and he moved to Portsmouth where he would have wider opportunities. From there he went to Boston. While he was in Portsmouth, Webster was elected to Congress, which started him on his long political career. He was elected to Congress from Massachusetts and then elected to the Senate from that state. He was twice Secretary of 

State and many times mentioned for the Presidency. As Secretary of State he helped to write the Webster-Ashburton treaty, which, among other things straightened out the boundary line between New Hampshire and Canada. That had been in dispute since the Revolutionary War and the people who lived in that corner of the state, disgusted because they couldn't find out whether they belonged to Canada or the United States, finally formed a tiny nation of their own called the Indian Stream republic. Webster also took part in the famous Dartmouth College case which was of interest to New Hampshire, and in many other famous debates. He was always on the side of a strong United States and helped in many ways to build up the young and growing country. 

After he left Portsmouth, New Hampshire didn't see very much of him. But he loved the state and often visited at Boscawen.

Franklin Pierce man to serve 

ANOTHER famous New Hampshire man, Franklin Pierce, was also involved in the quarrelsome and troublesome times that preceded the Civil War. He is the only New Hampshire as President of the United States and his term ran from 1853 to 

1857. Pierce was the opposite of Webster in many ways. Pierce was mild and charming and got along with people, where Webster was fiery and blunt and had strong opinions and made people angry if they didn't agree with him. Webster came of a poor family; 

Pierce's family was well-to-do as you can tell from looking at the mansion in Hillsborough where he was born November 23, 1804. His father was Benjamin Pierce, prominent during the 

Revolutionary War and a two term governor of New Hampshire.

Young Pierce must have heard much about government and politics while he was growing up, both from his father and from the guests who came to the house. He attended Hancock and Francestown academies and graduated from Bowdoin college in Maine in the same class with Nathaniel Hawthorne, the famous American author. They were lifelong friends and when Pierce ran for the Presidency, Hawthorne wrote his campaign biography.

When he finished school, Pierce trained as a lawyer and began his career in his home town of Hillsborough. He was then 

23. A few years later he married and moved to Concord where

to go he built up an important law practice. He liked law and wanted devote his life to it, but his friends were determined that he into politics and the Democratic party elected him to the state General Assembly when he was 29. He was elected to the national Congress a few years later and then to the Senate. He served as a brigadier general in the Mexican War of 1845-46, then came back to Concord. He became active in state affairs once more and about this time acquired ownership of The Concord 

Monitor, which had been founded in 1809.

Pierce was still in Concord when the national Democratic convention met to name a candidate for President in 1852. Since the party included both Northerners and Southerners, the delegates couldn't agree on anybody that would be acceptable to both sides; they knew the South wouldn't vote for a Northerner, the 

North wouldn't vote for a Southerner. Then somebody thought of Pierce. He had been well liked in the Senate, had always held moderate opinions, and hadn't made any enemies. He was one 

Northerner the South might accept. The convention nominated him for the Presidency and presented him to the voters as a "Northern man with Southern principles." He was elected. However, although Pierce had been named as a compromise candidate and was elected because he didn't seem to have very strong convictions, he was in some ways as much a champion of the Union as Webster had been. In his inaugural address, he said: "With the Union my best and dearest earthly hopes are entwined.”

The four years Pierce spent in the White House as President were full of quarrelling and unrest over slavery. It was no place for a mild man and Pierce found himself powerless to prevent

the ever-widening rift between North and South. He did what he could to keep tempers down, but it was no use. A of group Northern people had organized a very strong free-the-slaves movement, and Southerners were quite as determined to keep their slaves. The argument got hotter and hotter. Then Congress passed the Kansas-Nebraska bill which opened up a great new piece of Western land for settlement. Immediately a race began between North and South to occupy this land. The middle part of it, now the state of Kansas, became a battleground between slave-owners and free-state men and there was considerable bloodshed for several years before the national government was able to restore order. At the end of his term Pierce was glad to return to the peaceful life of Concord and he remained there until he died in 1869. New Hampshire has honored his memory with a statue near the entrance to the state house grounds in Concord. 

The War Between the States 

THE CIVIL WAR, or the War Between the States as it is now called, started in 1861 and dragged on for four weary years. 

New Hampshire did its part along with the other northern states in fighting this war to preserve the Union. It was different from the Revolutionary war, however, because the nearest battle to New Hampshire was in southern Pennsylvania and the state was never threatened by invasion or damage. New Hampshire had had a strong group of "abolitionists" before the war, people who wanted to free the slaves, and when the fighting started most of them threw their efforts into winning the war. Some of them had seen the new President, Lincoln, when he came to visit his

son at 

Phillips Exeter academy in 1860 and they liked his looks. 

His first call for soldiers was greeted with enthusiasm in New

Hampshire. That enthusiasm cooled, of course, as the war dragged on, but through the four years of the conflict New Hampshire sent nearly 40,000 men into the armed forces. The first man killed in the war was a New Hampshire man, Luther C. Ladd of Alexandria.

Among the group of 40,000 soldiers were several outstanding officers. The list includes Benjamin F. Butler, who commanded the Union army at New Orleans, and Commodore George H.

Perkins who led a division of gunboats in the surrender of New Orleans; Edward E. Cross, who organized and commanded the 

Fighting Fifth; George E. Belknap, who later became a rear admiral in the Navy; Brigadier General Benjamin F. Kelley who helped to keep West Virginia in the Union; and General John A. 

Dix, for whom Dixville Notch is named, who cried at the climax of a battle: "If anyone attempts to haul down the American flag, shoot him on the spot."

New Hampshire Feels the War

ALTHOUGH no battles of the War between the States were fought anywhere near New Hampshire, the state felt the effects of the war heavily. For one thing it lost several thousand men killed and wounded in battle. Taxes were high, to pay for the war; there was a shortage of labor. Altogether the progress which the state had been making up to the time the war started was slowed down considerably. And this war period was the only time in New 

Hampshire's history when the population didn't gain; instead, it showed a decrease between 1860 and 1870. Most of that decrease was due to Westward migration. Many young New Hampshire men had a chance to see the rest of the country as soldiers in the Union army and some of them liked the broad farmlands of the Middle West. As soon as the war was over they moved their families to these new lands. Urged on by their glowing accounts of cheap and fertile land, others followed them. A good many New Hampshire people left the state. But New Hamp-

shire didn't feel that it was defeated, or that it had lost anything vital. The moment the war was over, the state began going right ahead once more with all kinds of improvements and progress.

The Growth of Factories 

ONE of the very important changes in this period was the growth of factories. Factories were started soon after the beginning of the century but up to the time of the Civil War they were still mostly small and most of them dependent almost entirely on water for power. However, men all over the world had been experimenting with and improving the steam engine, especially in connection with the railroads, and by the time the Civil War was over, the steam engine had been developed to a point where it was practical for industrial use. This meant that bigger factories could be built-the Amoskeag mills in Manchester, for example, could never have gotten enough power from the river to grow to the size they did—and it also meant that factories could be built in places where there was no water power. 

So factories began to be built in sections of the state which had

never had them before, and the well established factories began to grow bigger. Another thing which aided in the growth of factories was the increasing use of metal for machinery. New 

Hampshire's first factories were largely equipped with wooden machinery. As men learned how to use iron, and then steel and later all kinds of other metals, much better factory machinery could be built.

This growth and development of factories went ahead steadily all through the period between the wars, from 1865 to 1917. 

One way to measure it would be to count the increasing number of buildings. But that isn't the best way, because some of the factories were big and some were little. A more accurate way is to see how many people were employed in these factories. So if you were to dig through the United States census reports and a lot of dry-as-dust pamphlets, you would find some interesting things. In 1870, for instance, you would discover that about 

42,000 New Hampshire people were working in shops or factories, quite a jump from before the Civil War. In this same year there were about 47,000 people working on farms, in the woods, or in the fishing industries. But 10 years later, in 1880, there were 

57,000 people working in shops and factories and only 45,000 people working outdoors. That tendency kept right on. In 1890, there were 67,500 working in factories, about 70,000 in 1900, 94,000 in 1910, and in 1920, which was the peak year, there were about 100,000. Of course the state's population was growing all the time, so the proportion of people working in factories compared with those on the land didn't change quite so much as those figures might indicate. But an interesting thing about that

1920 figure is that the 100,000 people in the factories were more than half of the whole number of people working in the state. In other words, there were more people working in factories than there were in all other occupations combined. This may all seem pretty dry, but it is very important and New Hampshire young folks ought to understand it. What it meant was that the state changed from a farming and agricultural state to a manufacturing state. That brought a different way of life, different and vexing problems, and new ways of doing things. 

WITH that general picture in mind, let's look now at the various kinds of factories which were growing up in New Hampshire. You will recall that the state's first little shops and mills were built to produce the simple, elemental things that everybody wanted, such as cloth, shoes and articles of wood. Interestingly enough, those products are still the most important New 

Hampshire industries.

The textile industry, that is, the making of all kinds of cloth and cloth articles, led all the others during the period we are talking about in this chapter. While the Civil War was going on, New Hampshire mills couldn't get cotton from the South, but they shifted over to woolen products for a time and kept busy on war work. After the war, cotton became available again, and for half a century the cotton factories were bigger and more numerous than any other kind.

The most famous cotton concern was, of course, the Amoskeag Manufacturing Company, in Manchester, which grew to be the biggest in the world. At the end of the Civil War the

company had several large brick mills beside the Merrimack River and was employing several thousand people. Finding a continually growing demand for gingham and other kinds of cotton cloth, the company continued to grow, constantly building new mills of its own and buying out its competitors. It also erected rows of solid brick houses for the mill workers to live in until much of the center of Manchester was occupied by Amoskeag property. The expansion went on and on until Amoskeag became the greatest cotton weaving system in the world; industrialists and business men and national leaders from all over the globe came to gaze at it and exclaim in wonder.

It should not be forgotten, however, that there were other cotton mills in the state-at Dover, Nashua and many other towns and cities. Nor should it be forgotten that woolen cloth of all kinds was produced, and knitted products, and some clothing. 

In 1900 there were about 100 textile factories of all kinds within the state, employing 27,000 people. By 1920 there were 35,000 people working in textile mills. Probably few people in that year realized it, but that was the peak of the textile industry. From that point it started to drop off, at first gradually, and then quite rapidly, as we shall see in the last chapter.

Newcomers in New Hampshire

Education, Literature, and the Arts

IN THE last few chapters you have seen how factories and transportation and electric power and other improvements developed in New Hampshire through the 19th century. Not everybody was engaged in these enterprises, however. Other men and women were building up schools and libraries and newspapers. 

A few of them were writing books and poetry and stimulating interest in reading. Still others had come to the realization that the state's natural resources must be taken care of and preserved so that New Hampshire people would always have what they needed. All these activities are interesting and important and 

New Hampshire young folks ought to know about them.

How The Schools Grew Up 

Boys and girls going to school today in New Hampshire are likely to take it for granted that the state has always had nice schools and plenty of them. But you remember from the first chapter that there were almost no schools in colonial times. A man who could write a short letter was considered well educated; many people couldn't read or write at all. Of course this was true of all the colonies as well as New Hampshire.

After the Revolutionary War, however, New Hampshire began to pay some attention to schools. A system of district schools was set up, little one room buildings all over the state where a dozen or so farm boys and girls could get a few years of education. In the towns there were bigger schools. These were what we call grade schools now. And one or two academies were started after the Revolutionary war. These academies taught what high schools teach today but they were privately owned and you had to pay to go to them. Not many did at first. But there was a growing demand for more education than the grade schools could provide, and after 1800 more and more academies were established. By the time of the Civil War there were quite a number of them. But in the meantime, there were people who thought that this higher education ought to be free, and Portsmouth built the first free public high school in 1830. And just as the first little railroad trains had sounded the death knell for the river freight traffic, so the first high school gave warning that the academies would finally be forced to discontinue. Many of them flourished through the Civil War and later, but by that time there were enough public schools to drive most of the academies

ones continued, out of existence. The older and better established c however, drawing students from outside the state. And some private schools were founded, including St. Paul's in Concord, which began holding classes just before the Civil War. In new addition to the academies, there are now nearly 100 public high schools in the state, enough so that any New Hampshire boy or girl can attend one.

Another step forward in the progress of education was the establishment of the Normal School in Plymouth in the 1870's to provide training for teachers. Up to that time, many teachers had but little more education than their pupils, and no instruction at all in methods of teaching. The school was popular and a second one was established at Keene in the early 1900's. Within

CHAPTER XI New Hampshire Today 

F YOU could go back and look around New Hampshire as it was in 1920, you would find it very much as it is today, with the same cities, very much the same kind of life, people dressed as they are today for the most part, automobiles, electric lights, running water, central heating plants in homes, movie theatres, airplanes. The only things you would look for in vain would be radios and electric refrigerators.

And because things would look to you very much as they do today you might come to the conclusion that nothing much had happened in the past 25 years and that as a result probably

nothing more would happen, that the life of the state had "jelled,” and was going to remain the way it is now indefinitely. That is a natural attitude to take but it is quite wrong. This chapter will try to point out changes that have taken place in the last two or three decades, and suggest changes that may be expected in the future. 

Booms and Depressions ups

ONE field in which there have been a number of changes in the past quarter century is that of business and industry. Along with the rest of the country New Hampshire has, in that period, experienced three business booms and two depressions, and there is hardly a family in the state that has not been affected by those and downs. The first boom was during the first World War, in which the United States took part in 1917 and 1918. Like the Civil War, the fighting was far away and this country was never in danger itself. New Hampshire's main contribution, as in the case of most other states, was in providing materials of war, in addition, of course, to furnishing men for the army and navy. 

Because New Hampshire had many factories and some shipyards, the production of war materials brought in a good deal of money. 

Labor was scarce, wages rose, many people made a good deal more money than usual; in short, the war was a period of considerable business prosperity.

But when the war stopped, the war orders stopped, too, and after awhile a period of business depression began. With smaller orders, factories began laying off some of their help. Every time a man lost his job, he had less money with which to buy the prod-

ucts of somebody else's factory, so other industries had to let some of their help go. Times were "hard," people said, and they remembered other periods of hard times in the country's history. 

But this depression wasn't serious. The automobile, radio, and movie industries began to grow, along with many others, and the business of the nation picked up again until in the late 1920's the United States was enjoying its greatest period of prosperity. 

That came to an end starting in 1929, when the New York stock market broke sharply. Most people called it simply a "money panic," but they didn't notice that even before that, the production of all kinds of goods had started to fall off. People were let out of factories, and every time a group lost their buying power that way, it meant that other people in other factories would be laid off. It got worse, with more and more people without employment. Then in the early part of 1933, there began to be signs of a serious panic. People began drawing their money out of banks, some banks were forced to close, and it was feared that the whole structure of American business might collapse. The federal government stepped in then and closed all the banks in the nation for a week in March, 1933. After people got calmed down a little, the banks were reopened, the federal government took steps to aid business, and after a period of some uncertainty things began to go back to normal. The years 1937 and 1938 were considered good business years. Then the war started in 

Europe, this country began to arm in self defense, and the third business boom was on. After the United States got into the second World War, the huge amounts of war material needed furnished more work than ever before, and the national income

rose to astronomical heights. Many people wondered if this boom would be followed by another depression, but even while the war was at its height, a small army of men in Washington were given the job of making plans for the period after the war so that a depression would be avoided.

That's a general picture. The same things were happening in 

New Hampshire, of course, except that the periods of prosperity were not quite so high and the depressions not so serious as in some other parts of the country. 

One reason for this was that the state's factories, with one exception, were mostly small. They were not able to make the huge amounts of money in boom times as the giant corporations did, but in depressions they were able to keep going better because they depended on smaller orders. Another reason was that although the state had many factories, it also had other means of income, such as farming and the tourist business, which offset the drop in income from manufacturing. 

Along with these general ups and downs in business, there were changes taking place in New Hampshire's manufacturing system which are interesting and important and ought to be well understood. As noted in previous chapters, the making of various kinds of cloth and yarn and clothing was the most important manufacturing business in New Hampshire all through the 19th century and into the present century. That reached a peak during the first World War. A few years later, in 1921, there were still 

23,000 people working in various kinds of textile mills. But after that the textile business began to slow down, not only in New Hampshire but all over New England. There were several reasons for this. One was that cotton spinning was being moved to the

South where labor was cheaper and taxes were lower. Another was that some New England firms allowed their machinery and methods to get out of date. The most important, however, was the new artificial silk,-rayon-, which the women adopted as enthusiastically as their husbands took to automobiles. Along with rayon came new women's styles. You don't have to be an economic expert to see that the lady of days gone by with long skirts and billowing petticoats required a good deal more cloth to be dressed than the lady of 1940. So the demand for cotton cloth fell off and the mills which made the cloth began to suffer. 

In New Hampshire the most dramatic illustration of what was happening was the case of the Amoskeag mills at Manchester. 

Once the biggest cotton spinning system in the world, and the state's pride and joy, the Amoskeag mills were caught in the general decline of cotton goods. Unable to change over to other products fast enough, the mills entered the depression of the 

1930's in poor condition and in Christmas week of 1935 they closed altogether. The company was bankrupt and the thousands of people who depended on the mills for their living were without jobs.

This was a serious blow to Manchester and if nothing had been done about it the city might have suffered permanent injury. 

But a group of Manchester citizens, exhibiting the traditional pioneer spirit of their great grandfathers, decided something could be done about it. They worked out a plan to buy the entire mill property, then sell or rent different buildings to a group of smaller industries. They found it would cost five million dollars to buy the mills, which seemed impossible for a group of ordinary people.

But a stock company, Amoskeag Industries, Inc. was set up stock offered to the people of Manchester. To make a long story short, Manchester people and firms bought the stock, the mill property was acquired and new manufacturers were brought in. 

The first new company to take over a mill building put 2,500 people back to work. By the time the second World War boom started, Manchester was well on its feet again. And it showed other New England towns and cities how to solve the same problem; the Manchester plan has been widely copied. 

While the cotton and textile business in New Hampshire was declining, the manufacture of shoes was increasing. In 1937, which was considered a normal business year, there were 18,440 people working on textiles, but 22,530 were engaged in leather industries, mostly shoes. Shoes, one of the state's earliest manufactures, had finally gone ahead of spinning and weaving cloth.

Electric Power 

ANOTHER important change in New Hampshire in the years since 1920 is the development of electricity. Starting with a few small generating plants at the beginning of the century, the state has seen more and more plants built, bigger and better as the years passed, until today every part of New Hampshire, including many rural regions, has electricity. One of the great problems of electricity is distribution, that is, the high cost of building power lines in country areas where houses are far apart. That has been partially solved in New Hampshire by cooperation all around and with the help of the federal government which financed some of the rural lines. Today New Hampshire leads the nation in the proportion of farm homes having electricity, and lines are still being extended.

An interesting fact about New Hampshire electricity is that most of it, 80 percent, is produced by water power. This should provide cheap electric power, since once the cost of the dam is written off, the water itself costs nothing. In many parts of the country electricity must be generated from coal or oil. 

But water power may become exhausted, just as coal and oil may be, if it is not conserved. And the principal method of guarding water power is to protect the forests which grow along the heads of streams so that water is held in the ground and released slowly during the year. A second method of conserving water is through a flood control system. This consists of reservoirs on streams which feed the larger rivers. These reservoirs can be closed in periods of high water and the water kept in storage until dry periods when it is needed. Of course, the first object of

flood control is to prevent floods by holding back water, but the storage angle is almost as important. 

The Franklin Falls flood control reservoir in New Hampshire is one of the most famous in the country because of the wide notice it received when the village of Hill moved to a new, location. In all other similar cases, when a town was to be drowned out by a reservoir, people in such towns simply went to other places to live. But 30 Hill families decided they didn't want to be separated that way, and they built a new village on a higher spot in the township. The story of this model village has been told and retold as an example of New Hampshire courage and resourcefulness.

The biggest and most famous electric generating plant in New England is also located partly in New Hampshire at Fifteen Mile Falls on the Connecticut River. This stretch of wild water was the despair of river boatmen in the old days who were not able to get through it, and canal builders shook their heads at the job of building locks around it. But one canal man is reported to have said that while the rapids made an obstacle for river traffic, they would someday be a boon to mankind because of their power. A huge dam and electric power plant was completed at the falls in 1930, known as the Comerford plant, and the electricity generated there is used in several states. 

Changes in Agriculture 

IN SPITE of the importance of industry in modern New Hampshire, it should not be forgotten that many families still live on the land. And just as the modern electric power system

is a long way ahead of the guttering candles of colonial days, so are the present day farms far ahead of the patches of beans and corn planted among the tree stumps at Pannaway. Farming today is a business or an occupation, not simply a way to live as it was in the early days. Instead of trying to raise a little bit of everything merely to supply their own wants, farmers now specialize in their efforts, some producing milk and dairy products, others fruit, still others poultry or eggs or livestock, or vegetables for city markets. Successful farmers hire help, keep books, watch markets, do their work scientifically and expect to "make money" just as a manufacturer or business man does. A well conducted farm establishment can have electricity, central heating, running water, and all the other things city homes have. The movement to protect forest lands has grown up along with the development of agriculture and is really a part of the same program since trees are a crop just as corn is. The Challenge of the Future 

AND now New Hampshire's story of more than three centuries is up to date. It is a long look back to Pannaway, and the glow of its fireplaces and candles is dimmed by the modern miracles of lighting; the creaking of the wooden mill wheels of a century and a half ago are lost in the thunder of modern machinery. The changes that have taken place are many and great. 

But reminders of an older day are to be found on every hand and they should not be overlooked. All the things which were done in the past have helped a little toward making the state what it is in modern times.

Today, New Hampshire people face a future as full of challenging problems and opportunities as at any period in the past. 

There are new inventions to be created for industry, better methods to be worked out for use of the land, greater developments of the recreational field. There are books and music to be written, pictures to be painted and statues to be modelled; there is work for statesmen, teachers, ministers, mechanics, and builders. The 

Granite state can always use pioneers in every field. And a motto for these pioneers might well be "Resourcefulness." That quality has played an important part in New Hampshire's development up to now and will be even more important in the years ahead. 

New Hampshire does not have a state song. But everyone who knows New Hampshire sings its praises. Perhaps the most appropriate way to close this book is to sing such a song of praise.

I'M HUNGRY FOR NEW HAMPSHIRE 

I'm hungry for New Hampshire. I must go 

Where mountains rise and purple lilacs grow. 

From highest hill to intervale and sea 

New Hampshire holds all treasures dear to me. 

Beneath a flag of blue my heart would stay, And find a peace that cannot pass away. 

Dear memories of home within me burn. 

New Hampshire waits to welcome my return. 

I'm hungry for New Hampshire. Near or far 

My love will guide me homeward like a star. 

Back home to mountain trails and waterfalls,

A covered bridge, red barns, and old stonewalls. 

I dream of hills at dawn and sunset glow, Of friends who'll greet me joyfully, I know. 

I hear glad voices calling me to come. 

I'm hungry for New Hampshire, home, sweet home!
