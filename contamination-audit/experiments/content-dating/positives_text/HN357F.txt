FOREWORD 

The purpose of this study is to trace with some detail the origin and development of the Vocational Guidance Movement in Boston. The author has not intentionally stressed the importance or value of the work of one person or organization over that of another, but has attempted to outline the general trend of the work as it was carried on by various agencies at or about the same time. The writer desires to give due credit to all those public-spirited persons who, with vision of the needs and a belief in the possibilities of guidance, initiated the movement in the interests of counseling, placing, and following up the careers of the young men and women in the industries or professions and of boys and girls in the schools.

The writer is indebted to Dr. John M. Brewer of the 

Graduate School of Education of Harvard University for the privilege of examining historical material in the files of the Bureau of Vocational Guidance and for helpful criticism during the preparation of the study; to Miss Dorothy Buck for assistance in locating some of the material; and to Miss Bertha 

Shepard of the Department of Vocational Guidance in the Boston Public Schools, for information and material relative to the work in the schools which she graciously gave me. 

Mary M. Young.

INTRODUCTION 

The idea of vocational guidance is not new, neither is the study of occupations as recent as the uninformed person supposes. As early as the days of Benjamin Franklin's boyhood parents were giving thought to directing their children's choice of a vocation. Franklin tells in his Autobiography of his father's taking him on a tour of the various trades in the town of Boston. We read as follows: 

He therefore sometimes took me to walk with him and see joiners, bricklayers, turners, braziers, etc., at their work, that he might observe my inclination and endeavor to fix it on some trade or other on land. 

In 1836 Edward Hazen of Philadelphia wrote a book entitled, 

"A Panorama of the Professions and Trades, or Everyman's Book." 

It was published by Uriah Hunt in Philadelphia, Pa., and is probably one of the earliest of its kind. It is just what the name suggests--a panoramic picture or survey of some sixty-five trades and professions, ranging from Agriculturist to Veterinary Surgeon. It includes such trades as tanner, shoemaker, cabinetmaker, upholsterer, carver and gilder, wheel-wright, gun-smith, black-smith, gold, silver, and copper smiths, iron-founder, glassblower. The professions cover author, teacher, minister, attorney-at-law, pharmacist, dentist, physician.

This book furnishes fascinating reading even today. The preface is particularly enlightening and arresting. It makes the definite suggestion that information about the trades and pro-

fessions should be taught in the schools. The following extract from the preface shows very clearly the viewpoint of the author on this subject:

The following work has been written for the use of Schools and Families, as well as for miscellaneous readers. It embraces a class of subjects in which every individual is deeply interested, and with which, as a mere philosophical inspector of the affairs of men, he should become acquainted. 

In the present age a great proportion of mankind pursues some kind of business as a means of subsistence or distinction; and in this country especially, such pursuit is deemed honourable, and, in fact, indispensable to a reputable position in the community. Nevertheless, it is a fact that cannot have escaped the attention of persons of observation, that many individuals mistake their appropriate calling, and engage in employment for which they have neither mental nor physical adaptation. Some learn a trade who should have studied a profession; others study a profession who should have learned a trade. Hence arise, in great measure, the ill success and discontents which so frequently attend the pursuits of men. 

The author speaks of the necessity of the parents' making wise choices of occupations for their children and regarding special capacities rather than the favor in which the occupation is held. "For," he says, "successful prosecution of an humble business is far more honourable than inferiority or failure in one which may be greatly esteemed."

He suggests that parents give their children the opportunity of getting at least a superficial acquaintance with several trades or professions in order that he (the parent) may get an idea of the genius of the children. This should be done in the home, the schools, and in places where practical exhibitions of the several employments may be seen. The author recommends a systematic course of instruction or information concerning the

common things of life around them and the processes of their manufacture. 

A set of questions on each occupation, for the use of the pupil or teacher, is included as a supplement at the end of the book. 

Thus we perceive that the idea of vocational or occupational guidance and information did not originate in the United States within the last twenty-five years. What is new is the attitude towards the child's choosing his vocation after a careful study of various fields by the child, rather than the parents choosing for the child.

Professor Frank Parsons

Civic, Service House in the North End of Boston was organby Meijer Bloomfield ized in 1901 as a civic center in the real sense. 

Work with children was omitted and the social side of the settlement, contrary to the usual custom, was incidental. A group of active, self-governing clubs was organized, emphasizing constantly the civic interests, rights, and duties of the members. During the first decade of service to the community, sixteen active house clubs, whose membership consisted of both young men and young women, were organized. These young people, many of them immigrants, were eager to prepare themselves for civic efficiency. 

To meet the needs of the new Americans a school for citizenship, called the Bread Winners' Institute, was started 1905:

The moving spirit behind the institute was Professor Frank Parsons of Boston University Law School. Professor Parsons was primarily interested in the adjustment of young people to American life and customs, to their learning our language, and fitting themselves for a vocation. This service Professor Parsons hoped to render through the new department of Civic Service House. 

It is from this beginning that we may say that vocational guidance had its start in Boston, for out of this grew the Vocation Bureau, one of the first organized movements in vocational counseling. (Other agencies were working in the interests of

Professor Parsons gave untiring service to the work of counseling those who sought his advice. He kept careful records of the "cases" and used the term "counselor" in respect to himself. 

He kept office hours in the Y.M.C.A., Women's Educational and 

Industrial Union, Economic Club, Civic Service House, for the convenience of those who wished to consult him in regard to their choice of occupation.

News of Professor Parsons' work soon spread abroad. He was asked to speak before various clubs throughout the city. In 1907 he repeated before the Economic Club the essence of an earlier paper, "The Ideal City." Soon after this Mr. Meyer Bloomfield, director of Civic Service House, invited him to speak to the graduating class of an evening high school on the subject of "Choosing a Vocation." After the exercises several young men asked him for an interview.

The results of these interviews were so successful that he was soon asked to draw up plans for a permanent organization.

Professor Parsons' plan was based upon the following statements or truths:** 

1. It is better to choose a vocation than merely to hunt a job. 

2. No one should choose a vocation without careful self-analysis, thorough, honest, and under guidance. 

3. The youth should have a large survey of the field of vocations, and not simply drop into convenient or accidental positions. 

4. Expert advice, or the advice of men who have made a careful study of men and vocations, and of the conditions of success, must be better and safer for a young man than the absence of it. 

5. Putting down on paper a self-analysis is of supreme importance.

The following extract from Professor Parsons' first report,

May, 1908, gives a vivid picture of the work of the bureau under the direction of its founder, and his convictions of the importance of the task of counseling young people in the choice of a vocation. 

Eighty young men and women from 15 to 39 have applied for help and have all expressed themselves as having received assistance. 

There have been two classes of applicants: 

1. Those having well developed aptitudes and interests and a practical basis for a reasonable conclusion in respect to a choice of vocation. 

2. Boys and girls with so little experience.or manifestation of special aptitudes or interests that there is no basis yet for a wise decision.

They are set to investigating different industries and practical testing of themselves to broaden their knowledge and bring to light and develop any special capacities, aptitudes, interests, abilities that may lie dormant in them or may readily be acquired by them. 

No attempt is made to decide for the applicant what his calling should be; but the Bureau tries to help him to arrive at a wise, well founded conclusion for himself. Its mottoes are "Light, Information, Inspiration, Cooperation." 

It helps the boy: 1st, To study and understand himself, his aptitudes, abilities, interests, resources, limitations, and their causes; 2nd, To get a knowledge of the conditions of success, advantages and disadvantages, opportunities, etc., in different lines of industry; and 3rd,

To reason correctly about the relation of these two groups of facts. 

If a man

The fundamental principle is adaptation. is doing work for which he has a natural fitness and an adequate preparation, if his abilities and enthusiasms are unified with his daily work and find full scope therein, he has the foundation for a useful and happy life. But if his abilities and enthusiasms are separated from his living, and the work he loves to do is side-tracked into the evening hours or pushed out of his life altogether, he is likely to be only the fraction of the man he ought to be. 

Dr. Parsons was not permitted to see the full fruition of the work he so enthusiastically and efficiently began, for he passed away before the end of the first year of the Bureau's existence, in September, 1908. The closing paragraph of the report quoted above seems almost prophetic. 

The work is in its infancy as yet but it is constantly growing in volume and importance. The director and those associated with him are enthusiastic over the results that have been achieved even in the few weeks since the Bureau was established, but they believe that in order to cover the field in the most complete and adequate manner the work should become a part of the public school system in every community, with experts trained as carefully in the art of vocational guidance as men are trained today for medicine or the law, and supplied with every facility that science can devise for testing the senses and capacities and the whole physical, intellectual, and emotional make-up of the child. 

Frank Parsons, Director and Counselor.

Professor Parsons was the first person to use the term "vocational guidance" as he uses it in the above report. The term has grown into common use from this beginning, as has the title of "vocational counselor," also first used by Professor Parsons in reference to himself. He can truthfully be called the founder of vocational guidance in America, at least in so far as published records are available.

The work of the Vocation Bureau of Boston steadily grew and broadened its scope of activities until they embraced four main divisions. Mr. Frederick J. Allen has stated these four-fold activities in his book, "Principles and Problems in Vocational Guidance" in these words:* 

1. The maintenance of an office, centrally located, for the collection and study of information concerning the various occupations of the community. When secured, this information is classified and made public in such a way as to help young people, teachers, and parents to understand what occupations hold out, their advantages and disadvantages, and the conditions for efficiency and success in each. 

2. To make clear the need of training and educational equipment for the desirable occupations, and by advice and cooperation to prolong the school period of young people whether by day, evening, or part-time courses, and also to secure other educational opportunities when needed. 

3. To organize personal vocational counseling both for those in school and for those already at work, in order to enable them to plan intelligently for their educational and vocational progress. * Allen, Frederick J. "Principles and Problems in Vocational

Guidance." Chapter I. McGraw-Hill Book Company, 1927.

4. To furnish opportunities for consultation to people of all ages who have personal problems connected with the trades, the professions, and academic or industrial pursuits. 

Mr. Bloomfield remained as director of the Vocation Bureau of Boston until 1917, when he resigned to accept a position with the United States Shipping Board as director of labor problems incident to ship-building. His experience in organizing the 

Boston Employment Managers' Association, the first of its kind in the United States to meet for a systematic study of the hiring and discharging of employees and similar questions concerning the personnel of industrial concerns, made him of great value to the government at this time.

The Vocation Bureau was transferred to Harvard (1917) and became known as the Bureau of Vocational Guidance.*

Other Agencies 

While Professor Parsons and Meyer Bloomfield were working out their plans for guidance in the Bread Winner's Institute and the Vocation Bureau of Boston, other organizations were working along similar lines independently of the Vocation Bureau. 

One of these, the Women's Municipal League of Boston, did noteworthy service in the field of guidance and placement. 

As early as 1902 this League opened Evening Centers in Boston for children leaving school at fourteen. The Department of 

Education of the Women's Municipal League organized in 1910 two active committees. The first committee investigated opportunities for vocational training in Boston and published a series of charts on various types of training; such as, Industrial 

Training, Commercial Training, Professional Training. These charts were gathered in a "Handbook for Vocational Training in Boston," published on May 1, 1913, by the League. This handbook was designed to aid parents, teachers, employers, and vocational counselors. It contained much information of value not found on the charts.

The second committee studied and reported upon "Extended Use of School Buildings." Under the able chairmanship of Miss Mary 

P. Follett this committee devised, in 1911, a plan of vocational guidance based upon advice, placement, and follow-up.

III BOSTON SCHOOL SYSTEM'S COOPERATION WITH THE VOCATION BUREAU The Training Course for Vocational Counselors 

The Vocation Bureau recognized that its work could not function to the fullest extent without close connection and cooperation with the schools of Boston. The minutes of the School Committee in 1909* contained the following references to vocational guidance: 

April 26, 1909.

The School Committee passed measures relative to the High School of Commerce and summer employment.

Ordered: The Superintendent is hereby authorized to expend an amount not exceeding $100 of the appropriation of $750 heretofore made for special lectures to the pupils of the High School of Commerce for the employment of one of the special lecturers during the current year to conduct the summer employment feature of the vocational work of said school.

Definition of Terms 

The terms vocational guidance, vocational instructor, vocational assistant, vocational counselor, may require explanation since there are various interpretations of their meaning. As used in the Boston Department of Vocational Guidance, they are defined as follows: 

1. Vocational guidance includes both educational and vocational direction to assist the pupils of Boston schools both while they are in school and after they leave. 

2. Vocational instructor is applied to men members of the staff of the central office who devote all their time to the duties of the office. 

3. Vocational assistant is applied to women members of the staff of the central office, who devote all their time to the duties of the office. 

4. Vocational counselor is a teacher (man or woman) in the elementary, intermediate, or high schools who is appointed to do "counseling" in addition to his regular work, without additional

salary. (He is allowed promotional credit, however.)

The Vocational Guidance Bulletin* defines Guidance as it was used in the Vocation Bureau of Boston:

Vocational guidance is organized common sense used to help each individual make the most of his abilities and opportunities. Vocational guidance is not a scheme of finding jobs; of forcing vocational decisions upon children; of merely adjusting human "pegs" to "holes"; or of narrowing the range of service open to the fit. 

It has been the aim of the leaders in guidance not to put mere "placement" as the prime factor in the guidance work. 

Phases of Guidance** Guidance, Educational and Vocational 

The Department of Vocational Guidance is one of the organized supervisory departments in the school system for the care of the child. Its work begins in the elementary school, and carries through the secondary school and beyond into the higher educational field of the commercial and industrial world.

Its reason for being is due to the many complexities in the social and economic life of the times. When one realizes that the home occupations and apprenticeship systems no longer predominate, and that the kinds of occupations today number in the hundreds, if not the thousands, organized vocational guidance of some sort is absolutely necessary. The enrichment of our school curricula, in keeping pace with the above changes in our national life demands educational guidance.

It may be interesting to trace briefly the evolution of the Harvard Graduate School of Education.* The first systematic instruction in Education was given in 1891 under the Division of Philosophy. In 1906 the Division of Education was founded. 

In 1917 a proposal was made for the establishment of a Department of Education, but it was not until 1920 that the Division of Education was raised to the status of a separate school and the Graduate School of Education was organized with its own 

Faculty and officers.

CONCLUSION 

The growth in organization, efficiency, and scope of the work in vocational guidance in the Boston School System from the first experimental work in connection with social service agencies to the present highly organized and effective departments of guidance, placement, and follow-up is the best proof of the value of this type of service to the young people of our public schools, both while they are in school and after they leave to go on to higher education or into trades or industries.

It is a legitimate and highly necessary and indispensable department of the public schools of America today. 

The idea of guidance is gradually finding favor with college administrators, and young men and women are planning wisely their life careers. This will perhaps, more than any other single factor, contribute to making the world a happier place in which to live and serve.
