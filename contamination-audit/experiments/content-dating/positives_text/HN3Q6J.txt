​PAINTS 

88A First we might talk a little about your box of paints. 

You will want to try them almost at once. 

Before you begin, be sure to have a paint apron. You can always use the same apron, for it is bound to get spotted with paint. 

Every good painter has a paint apron. One that buttons down the back and has long sleeves. 

Of course, a good painter tries to be careful, but accidents will happen. 

Tempo paint washes out very easily in water.

Every good painter covers his table before he begins work. [7]

If he sits on the floor to paint he spreads plenty of newspapers on the floor. 

Then he can lay his paintbrushes down without harm. 

If a few drops of paint drip from the brush, while painting, the newspapers catch them. 

Every good painter thinks, a few minutes before beginning to work: 

I will not put my red paintbrush in my white paint. I will have a different brush for each color. 

I will be careful to wipe my brush on the edge of the paint jar before I begin to paint.

This will keep my hands from sticking to the handle of the brush. 

It will also save my paint and my clothes.

When I have finished painting, I will wash my brushes clean in cold water for another day. [8]

I will put the lids on my paint jars to keep the paint fresh and free from dirt.

Brushes require a great deal of care or they will harden and be useless for future work. 

Brushes used in tempo paints are quickly washed clean in water.

ANIMALS THAT STAND UP 

You have heard the story of Noah's Ark and the Flood. 

Did you ever make any of the animals that went into the Ark?

It is easy to draw animals if you remember what it is that makes one animal different from another: 

A giraffe has a long neck. 

An elephant has a trunk. 

A kangaroo has little short front legs, and long hind legs for jumping. 

A rhinoceros has a big horn on his nose, short thick legs, and a heavy body. 

A hippopotamus has a big head and mouth. [62]

A camel has a hump.

When you have decided which animal you want to make first,

Choose a sheet of construction paper from which to make this animal: gray or black paper for an elephant, brown or black paper for a bear, orange paper for a giraffe, with brown spots made with a crayon. 

Make a pattern on scrap paper.

Fold the sheet of construction paper in the middle.

Lay the pattern on the paper so that the top of the head is on the fold. గా [63]

If it is an elephant or bear, the head and back may all be on the fold.

After you have drawn the animal, color it with crayons.

Cut it out at the sides and bottom. Be careful to leave the fold of the paper uncut. This is what makes the animals stand up.

The animal is now double. The two sides are held together where the fold is. 

The feet will spread widely apart. 

You can make enough animals for a circus, and you can use them in many other ways. 

You can use them in your peep-show box and in your toy village. [64]

A CIRCUS WAGON 

If you have animals for a circus, you will want to have a circus parade.

And there is always a circus wagon in a circus parade.

A shoe box is a very good shape for a circus wagon.

On the lid of the box draw lines about 1 inch inside the edge of the lid. See the picture. [65]

At each corner punch a hole. Cut out this entire space, leaving a large opening. This is where the bars are to be placed. 

Have you noticed the iron bars on a real circus wagon that permit you to see the tiger or the lion and yet prevent him from getting out?

For the bars cut strips of black paper. Cut them all the same width.

Paste them on the inside of the box lid. The best way to do this is to brush paste around the inside edge of the lid, and then lay the strips in place while the paste is wet. 

The wagon wheels.

These are made by nailing large button-molds to a piece of board.

The board should be the length of the box and about 1 inch wider than the box.

Use large button-molds and nails 14 inches long. First, put the nail through a small piece of pasteboard. Then, put the nail through the button-mold and nail it to the [66]

board. The pasteboard keeps the wheel from slipping off over the head of the nail.

TO PARENTS 

This book was written to supply a definite need; in no sense whatever to compete with the many excellent books on this subject, but rather to supplement them. Its primary purpose is to offer suggestions for the beginning of toy-making and handwork for children under six years of age. Yet, while it is intended for little children, it is hoped that older children may find in it much of both interest and helpfulness.

Since in no other way does the child grow and develop intellectually so much as through the use of his hands, supply him with incentives for using them. Scissors are among the first tools a child can use successfully. Give him magazines, colored pictures, cards. 

Cut with him. Cutting is more interesting if there is something to do with the pictures when cut. Make an indestructible scrap book of paper cambric in some bright color. You will have to help the very little child to do this, but the older child can make one for himself. 

Handwork for children means something more than mere skill with the tools he is using. Constantly problems occur which call for thought and intellectual experiment. Adjustments must be made. 

Measurements must be taken. A knowledge of linear measurements comes to meet a practical need. Always the child is gaining in ability to make his hands carry out the demands of his intellect. 

The child enjoys the electrical toy, but he also enjoys, and has a sense of creative pride in, the toy he has made for himself. The crudest and simplest creation is best for the little child, as he attains his object before his interest is lost. 

Endless ideas will come to the worker for enlarging and varying these toys, and for making others with the same general directions. 

This book is offered without apology. It was written as a labor of love for little children. [117]
