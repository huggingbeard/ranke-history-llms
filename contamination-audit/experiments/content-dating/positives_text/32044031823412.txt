AUTHOR'S PREFACE 

HERE is an old saying to the effect that to err is human. Somewhat more to the point of this discussion, however, is the rule epitomized by John Selden, "Ignorance of the law excuses no man." 

Firms and all others who enter into contracts with the Federal Government for construction work of any description, or for the furnishing of supplies, must comply with the terms of their contracts, and with certain rules and regulations issued by the departments or establishments concerned which, if in accordance with law, have the force and effect of law, as well as with the laws governing such matters as declared in the statutes, decisions of courts, and decisions of the accounting officers of the United States. Ignorance of the applicable Government regulations, or of the law governing in the particular matter involved, does not excuse the Government contractor in the event he makes a mistake in the performance of his contractual obligations.

The United States Government is not based upon favors to individuals, but upon equal and exact justice to all-justice under the law and the facts. As individuals, the men and women in charge of administering the laws may sympathize with you in your mistakes, but, nevertheless, they must apply the law to the terms of your contract and the facts in the case.

The laws, regulations, and decisions applicable to your contract and your bond are beyond your control or influence and it is a faithless public servant who would strain them either in your favor or against you. Rarely, if ever, does such a man or woman reach any position of importance in the service of the United States and in even fewer cases do they remain long in such position. 

Generally speaking, one does not encounter in the performance of Government contracts any employees of the United States who will grant special favors. 

The facts of performance, however, are largely within 648478

your control. It is your business to know the terms of your contract and the specifications forming a part thereof, as well as your job. It also is your business to make an official report to the Government officer or employee in charge of your contract of all facts which may have a bearing on the determination of your rights under the contract. You should strictly comply with the terms of your contract and the specifications which are a part of it. 

My purpose in writing this monograph, therefore, has been to point out in a general way the principal things you should know and observe in the preparation of a proper record of the facts arising in the performance of your contracts with the United States.

CHAPTER I THE BID 

BIDS for contracts with the United States are required to be submitted on forms provided by the Government for that purpose and without deviation from the specifications unless alternate bids have been requested. 

Bids may be withdrawn prior to the date and hour fixed in the advertisement for the opening of bids. It is important for the bidder for a contract with the United States to know that, should he change his mind, his bid cannot be withdrawn after it has been opened and during the period therein named within which the Government may accept it. The rule in this respect is unlike that prevailing as between private parties.¹

It seems desirable to consider briefly the bid under the following subheads: Advertising; The Specifications; Pro tests; Preparation and submission of the bid; The bid bond; Mistakes in bids.

Advertising 

The law, section 3709, Revised Statutes, requires generally that all contracts on behalf of the United States, whether for construction work or for supplies, shall be made in peace times after advertising for bids, except in the comparatively few cases where the emergency is such as not to permit of the delay incident to advertising, and except in the case of small contracts, the amount of which varies as between the various departments and establishments but not to exceed $500 in any case. The actual advertising may be done in several ways: first, by sending a copy of the specifications and an invitation to bid to a

Certain trade journals also carry these advertisements for the information and guidance of their readers. 

The Specifications 

The actual advertisements contained in the newspapers, trade journals, or in the posted notices, do not undertake to describe in detail the work to be performed or the supplies to be delivered. As I have stated, such information is contained in the specifications drafted by Government employees and the specifications are required to state the needs of the Government in such terms as to permit of full and free competition by all qualified and responsible bidders. In plain words, this requirement means that the specifications may not be tricky, or in such terms that only a few favored bidders may be able to meet them.²

Protests Concerning Specifications 

A prospective bidder should carefully examine the specifications and if he believes they are unduly restrictive of competition, he should promptly write a letter to that effect, to the office named in the advertisement to receive and open the bids, setting forth all of the facts on which his conclusion is based. Copies of this letter should also be sent to the head of the department or independent establishment concerned and to the Comptroller General of the United States. The protest will be investigated and if found to be sustained, the defective specifications will be corrected, if not in that case, then in future similar cases. 

It is to be remembered in this connection that in comparatively few cases, is there a deliberate attempt on the part of the purchasing officer, with respect to a supply contract, to draft specifications around some particular patented or proprietary article, or in such a manner as to exclude many bidders, even though qualified, in connection with construction work. The specifications are not • Gillioz v. Webb, 99 Fed. (2nd) 585; Gilbert Engineering Corp. v. United States.

drafted by the head of the department or independent establishment concerned and rarely do such officials or their immediate advisers, see them unless some prospective bidder files a protest against the specifications. 

The drafting work is done by the purchasing officers or members of their particular office forces and in some instances by technicians who will use the equipment. Where construction work is involved, the specifications may be drafted by the architects employed in the Government service, or they may be drafted by private architects. employed for the particular project. 

While not universally true, it is generally true that these men do not have such pride of opinion that they are not eager to learn and profit by any mistakes which may be shown to exist in their specifications, but this does not mean that they will show any sympathy to a suggestion that has no real merit, or that has for its purpose the obtaining of some undue advantage at the expense of the Government or of other bidders. Where the exceptional case does exist, the head of the department or independent establishment concerned, and the Comptroller General—if the matter is brought to their attention in time-will cause the condition to be corrected. The Director of Procurement, who has taken over much of the purchasing of supplies for the civilian agencies of the Government, has established a board to hear and determine protests as to specifications.

There have been a few instances where a prospective bidder has filed a protest to delay the awarding of the contract. Investigation usually discloses the lack of merit in such protests, with the result that this practice does the protestant no lasting good. The men and women of the Federal service are generally honest and they expect honesty and good faith on the part of those who would do business with the United States. Fortunately, lack of good faith and honesty on the part of Government bid-

ders and contractors is rate and the offenders are detected sooner or later. Wrongful interference with the award of a contract by the United States may result in the payment of damages to the injured bidder by the other bidder responsible for the wrongful interference.7

Preparation and Submission of Bid 

With the specifications, the Government furnishes a folder containing the Government Standard Instructions to Bidders, a Standard Form of Blank Bid, and a Standard Form of Bid Bond, when the latter is required. If not furnished, they may be obtained at the office issuing the specifications. After having studied the specifications very carefully, the next thing to do is to study the instructions to bidders and the blank form of bid. It should be particularly observed that a bid cannot be withdrawn or amended after the date and hour fixed for opening, even though the bidder may discover that a mistake has been made in the bid. As between private parties, generally, a bid or offer may be withdrawn at any time prior to its acceptance. This is not true in Government contracting. 

The bidder is informed in the Standard Instructions to bidders that a mistake confers no right to the withdrawal of a bid after it has been opened, and he is cautioned to examine carefully his bid to see that no mistakes are contained therein. He is also informed that his bid may not be withdrawn after it has been opened and the Standard Form of Bid contains on the face thereof the express statement that the Government has sixty days after the date the bids are opened within which to accept the bid, unless a shorter period be stated by the bidder in his bid. 

This is very important, especially in periods of changing prices. Unless a bidder is willing to take the risks of changes in prices, etc., during the sixty day period, he should expressly fix a shorter period in his bid. 

7 Lewis v. Bloede, 202 Fed. 7, 25.

In addition to all this, the bidder is generally required by the terms of the advertisement or specifications and, in the case of all Navy contracts by the terms of the law as well, to submit a bid bond in a certain named percentage of his bid, conditioned that he will not withdraw his bid during the sixty-day period required by the Government in order to determine whether the bid will be accepted, or during such shorter period as may be named by the bidder. In nearly all cases, a certified check may be filed with the bid in lieu of a bid bond. Also, that if within such period his bid is accepted, he will execute the required standard form of contract and furnish a performance bond with good and sufficient surety to deliver the supplies in accordance with the terms of the contract. If the bidder should fail in any respect to do these things, he becomes liable to the United States for the difference between his bid and the amount the Government may be required to pay to have the contract performed. 

Multiple Bids

It has been held that competitive bids may not be received and considered from two corporations, one of which is a subsidiary-owned and controlled-by the other corporation.8 

Identical Bids 

Where identical bids as to price are submitted and none of the bids offer superior quality or greater advantages to the United States, the award should be as the result of drawing lots.9 However, in the absence of statute permitting such bids-and there are no such statutes-there may arise questions for the investigation and action of the Federal Trade Commission or the anti-trust division of the Department of Justice.

Bids Received Late 

If a bid is received at the place named in the advertisement as the place for opening of bids, after the date and hour fixed for the opening, such a bid should be returned unopened to the bidder. The only exception is when the postmark at the mailing office is such as to show that the bid was mailed in sufficient time to have been received before the date and hour fixed for the opening of bids. Even the postmark may not always be relied upon as being accurate. 10

The Bid Bond 

The bid bond is not a mere formality, as some bidders and their sureties appear to have considered to their sorrow and loss. Should the accepted bidder default in his obligation to execute the required standard form of contract and to furnish performance bond in the amount required the amount of the performance bond being generally stated in the specifications such bidder becomes liable to the United States for the loss sustained. 

In some instances the bidder has paid the amount of such loss, thus discharging the obligation of the bid bond, but in a far larger number of cases the sureties on the bid bonds have been required to pay the amount of the penalties named in the bid bonds, where such amounts did not exceed the actual losses. A loss of this kind may very easily cost the surety an amount equal to the premiums it would receive on several hundred of bid bonds. I know of two recent cases of this kind. In the one, a short time ago, the surety was required to pay $25,000, and in the other, $15,000. In both instances the loss was incurred because the surety on the bid bond would not become surety on the performance bond and the low bidder was unable to obtain a performance bond from any other surety.11 

10 Johnson v. United States, 82 Fed. (2nd) 500. 

11 Hickey v. United States, 65 Ct. Cls., 729.

Aside from causing heavy losses on bid bonds to the sureties and losses to the bidders, defaulted bids cause the Government a considerable amount of work and delay. The possibility of such unhappy occurrences could be reduced to a minimum by a more careful study by bidders of the terms of the specifications, the instructions to bidders, and the terms of the contracts which they will be required to execute in event their bids are accepted. 

Also, by the exercise of more care on the part of the agents of surety companies in the execution of proposal guarantees. Bid bonds should not be executed by any such agent unless he has in his possession data sufficient to justify the surety company he represents in assuming liability under a performance bond for the bidder as contractor. 

If the record and resources of the bidder are not fairly well known to the surety's agent, inquiries should be made with respect thereto before writing a bid bond and the inquiries should be submitted to sources having knowledge of the performance records of the bidder as contractor. In the case of Construction Contracts, inquiries should be made as a matter of course of the Bureau of Contract Information, Inc., Washington, D. C., which is maintained through the cooperation of some of the surety companies for the purpose of compiling and making available to the subscribing surety companies the records of construction contractors. 

The fact must not be forgotten that, in event the bid should be accepted and the bidder default, the United States will hold the defaulting bidder and his surety to the extent of the penalty of the bid bond for the loss sustained, and that in event of the refusal of either to pay, the appropriate United States Attorney will proceed in due course to collect through suit and execution on the property of the principal and surety. No officer or employee of the United States has authority of law to waive the responsibility of either the bidder or the surety on

account of such loss, although the Attorney General of the United States has legal authority to compromise such claims against defaulting bidders and their sureties, when the claim has been referred to him for collection.12 

Acceptance of Bids 

Acceptance of bids should be in the exact terms in which submitted and if submitted, they must be in accordance with the specifications with no substantial deviations therefrom. Where the specified date for the acceptance of a bid expires on Sunday or other national holiday, acceptance on the next succeeding non-holiday is a good acceptance. 13 

Mistakes in Bids

When one considers the thousands and thousands of contracts entered into annually by the United States through its many contracting officers in the various departments, independent establishments, boards, commissions, administrations, etc., it is not surprising that many mistakes in bids occur, but it is surprising that comparatively few of these mistakes are due to the action of such Government officers. This is accounted for by the fact that the work of the Government officers and employees is checked and rechecked by other officers and employees in the same branch of the Government, and finally such work is checked and examined by members of the staff of the General Accounting Office. 

Apparently, many bidders submit their bids without verifying the multiplication, subtraction, and addition of figures as to the various items of work or supplies, and without checking the completed bid against their esti-

The general rule of law is that a unilateral mistake, that is, a mistake made by the bidder, is not sufficient to relieve the bidder from responsibility for the performance of the contract in accordance with the accepted bid.14

Default of Bidder 

If the Comptroller General decides that the facts are sufficient to justify the conclusion that a mistake has been made in the bid, he then decides whether or not the bid may be rejected or reformed, depending on the facts in the particular case. If his decision is adverse to the accepted bidder or low bidder and the latter refuses to perform, demand is made for the loss sustained and in event of failure to pay the amount of the penalty named in the bid bond, the claim is referred in due course to the Department of Justice for collection through suit. The same procedure is followed where the accepted bidder is unable to furnish a performance bond. 

As above stated, the President of the United States, in an Executive Order of 1933, transferred to the Department of Justice the authority previously possessed and exercised by the Secretary of the Treasury to compromise claims in favor of the United States in charge of the Department of Justice for collection. It is possible for the defaulting bidder or surety on the bid bond to make an offer to the Department of Justice in compromise of such a claim at any time after it has been referred to that department for collection. Each case is considered on its merits and no general rule can be stated as to what will be the action on an offer in compromise of such claim. 

As the Comptroller General has stated in one of his published decisions concerning an alleged mistake in a bid, the Government contracting officers have other duties to perform than to act as guardians for negligent bidders. Losses in money to bidders and their sureties, as well as loss of time to the Government, could be avoided in these matters if bidders would exercise more care in the preparation of their bids.

CHAPTER II THE CONTRACT 

CONTRACTS between the United States and contractors are not matters of negotiation between the parties, as is generally the situation with respect to contracts between private parties. The Government, in the advertised specifications, generally notifies prospective bidders as to whether they will be required to execute a formal contract and furnish performance bond and if so, the particular form of printed contract in general use throughout the Government service is likewise named. 

Prior to 1926 there were generally as many different forms of contracts in use in the Government service as there were different departments and establishments of the Government. In fact, some of the larger departments of the Government had as many different forms of contracts as there were different bureaus in the particular department. In 1921, there was established by executive order an Interdepartmental Board of Contracts and Ad justments, although the board made few, if any, adjustments. This board consisted of representatives from various departments and establishments of the Government having more or less daily relations with the contracting work of the United States. The board consulted with representatives of the various trade associations, business men, and others. It made a study of the various forms of contracts in use by the Government and compiled the various scattered statutes relating to Government contracts.

After several years of this work, the board finally drafted a Government Standard Form of Contract for 

Construction Work, a Government Standard Form of

Supply Contract, a Government Standard Short Form of Supply Contract for use in making small purchases, and a Government Standard Form of Contract for the purchase of coal with the related standard forms for submission of bids, bid bonds, performance bonds, and instructions to bidders. An effort was made to make them as simple as possible and to limit the terms of the contracts to those essential things necessary to include in order to protect the rights of the Government and the contractor. Also, an effort was made not to include in these standard contract forms unnecessary details which had been included in some of the older forms of contracts and which imposed onerous conditions on the contractor unless deemed absolutely necessary for the protection of the public interests.

These standard forms were submitted to the President and he approved them, as Chief Executive, for uniform and obligatory use throughout the Government service and it will be noted that in the printed directions for the preparation of the contracts (which printed directions are contained on the contract forms below the place for the signatures) all contracting officers are prohibited from making any changes in the printed forms without the prior approval of the Director, Bureau of the Budget, obtained through the Interdepartmental Board of Contracts and Adjustments. This was done in order that no changes be made in the forms without careful prior approval by a disinterested agency of the Government and in order to preserve uniformity in contracting.

These facts have been mentioned here for a two-fold purpose: First-to assure the contractor that these contract forms were thoroughly and carefully considered by a group of men who had spent many years in the contracting and purchasing work of the Government. These forms are not agreements that were formulated overnight and which may be as quickly changed. Second-to further

assure the contractor and his surety that while the various articles, sentences and clauses of the contract forms have been so worded as to meet a particular decision of the courts, too much reliance should not be placed on decisions of the courts considering the different terms and conditions of the old forms of contracts whose use is now prohibited in contracting with the United States. 

There has been some litigation under the standard forms of contracts and there now are available decisions of the courts considering various stipulations in such standard forms. Also, there have been a large number of decisions by the Comptroller General of the United States with reference to the various terms of these standard forms. 

By an Executive Order of 1933, the work of the Interdepartmental Board of Contracts and Adjustments was transferred from the supervision of the Budget Bureau to the supervision of the Director of Procurement, Treasury

Department. With the establishment under the National

The meaning of the terms of the standard forms approved in 1926 and similar terms in PWA Form 51, if not otherwise entirely clear, has largely been settled. If the United States unduly delays in making the site available for construction work, or if the Government stops the work to test the bearing capacity of the foundations, etc., it is legally reponsible to the contractor for the excess cost occasioned by such delays for the convenience of the United States.¹

It is to be remembered that it is the duty of the contracting officer or construction engineer or inspector, whatever his title may be, to see that the work under the contract is performed in accordance with the specifications and terms of the contract. It is his business and he has taken an oath to faithfully perform it—to discharge his duty to his Government without fear or favor and regardless of all considerations, whether political or otherwise. If he fails in this duty, he is not only replaced by some other man who will not fail, but he may be discharged from his employment with the Government. It is a rare case where an employee of the

Almost without exception, such men who are faithless to their trust, or who are incompetent, are eventually detected.

A few instances have come to my attention where the contractor was so unjustifiably contentious that the departments recommended, and the Comptroller General approved, the rejection of his low bid for subsequent contracts. We, ourselves, may be in error and it requires facts and reasons to prove the contrary. Unsupported assertions will not provide satisfactory proof in any case. Rates of Wages Construction Contracts 

There came to a head in 1931 a definite conviction in some quarters that at least a few construction contractors were engaged in cut-throat competition and that since. wages constitute a large percentage of construction costs the low bidders were exploiting their own employees and through their subcontractors the employees of such subcontractors. The result was the Davis-Bacon Act,2 25 which was sponsored by Senator and former Secretary of 

Labor Davis and by the late Congressman Bacon.

In substance, this Davis-Bacon Act provided that every contract in excess of $5,000 with the United States or the District of Columbia which required or involved the employment of laborers or mechanics in the construction, alteration or repair of any public building should contain a stipulation that the contractor or any subcontractor on the public buildings covered by the contract should pay their laborers and mechanics not less than the prevailing rate of wages for work of a similar nature in the city, town, village, or other civil division of the State in which the work was located. Also, that if any dispute arose which the contracting officer could not adjust as to what were the prevailing rate of wages, the dispute should be referred to the Secretary of Labor for determination and his decision thereon was made "conclusive on all parties to the contract.”26

As might be expected, this statute gave rise to many disputes as to what were the prevailing rate of wages in any given locality and these disputes were referred to the

CHAPTER V SETTLEMENT OF CLAIMS  

IN N general, questions of fact are determined by the contracting officer and the head of the department concerned, as has been shown in preceding pages. The findings of facts made by these officers have substantially the same effect as the verdict of a jury as to the facts. 

Such findings of fact are made by express terms of the contract, final and conclusive on the contractor, and the contract means exactly what is says in this respect. However, the action of the administrative officers of the Government is not intended to be arbitrary and should they make a mistake, they are generally willing to be shown their error.

Questions of law as to the legal interpretation of the findings of fact and the terms of the contract usually are decided by the General Accounting Office and, in event of suit against the Government, finally by the courts. 

For instance, the question of whether or not the cause of a particular delay is a cause entitling the contractor to remission of liquidated damages may be either a question of law or a question of mixed law and fact. A decision as to whether or not the delay has resulted from the fault or negligence of the contractor is a legal conclusion to be drawn from the findings of fact. Whether or not there are changed subsurface conditions is not so much a question of law as it is a question of fact, as to whether the Government made any representations in the specifications concerning the subsurface conditions and if so, whether the conditions actually found to exist differ from the ones described. If the question of law be decided in favor of the contractor, there always remains the further and very

I 

important question of fact as to the amount of the excess costs necessarily incurred by the contractor, making it most important that adequate cost records be maintained in such cases.1

The general rule in doubtful cases is that a disbursing officer should not attempt to determine whether a question is one of law or of fact, and such claims are sent to the Comptroller General for direct settlement.2 If the latter refuses to allow the claim, the contractor may, within six years from the date the cause of action accrued, bring suit in the Court of Claims in Washington. If the claim is for less than $10,000 the suit may be brought in the District Court of the United States for the district in which the contractor resides. Since the adoption of the 

Government Standard Forms of Contracts, the Govern ment has proceeded on the theory that all doubtful claims should be settled by the Comptroller General, rather than paid by disbursing officers, for, in the latter event, should the payment be illegal or improper, the Government, the contractor, the disbursing officer, and the surety of the disbursing officer may become involved in litigation to recover the payment.3

Proceedings Before the Comptroller General 

The proceedings before the Comptroller General are on the written record, although requested interviews are generally accorded contractors and their sureties. However, final action is not taken as the result of such interviews. 

The decision is based on the written record and that record is preserved in the General Accounting Office. The contractor is not required to follow any particular form in whatever showing he may desire to make in any case before the Comptroller General. The contractor may state his side of a claim in a letter, or in a brief, or in both, but the facts should be carefully, accurately, and succinctly stated. If two typewritten pages are sufficient to state the salient facts, do not use three or four pages. The contractor should remember that his claim is one of thousands. It requires time to read and digest verbose statements. However, no matter what may be the length of the communication to the Comptroller General, the facts should be fully and accurately stated and the contractor should keep always in mind the fact that his statement will be carefully checked against the record to see whether he has correctly presented the essential facts in the case. 

This procedure makes it important that the contractor see to it that the contracting officer is promptly notified, during the course of the work, as to delays, changed subsurface conditions, etc., for, whatever may be the action of the contracting officer with respect thereto, it is safe to assume that the Comptroller General will require that record in event a claim comes before him with respect to these matters. Review of Settlements by Comptroller General 

Ordinarily, claims submitted for direct settlement are settled in the first instance by the Claims Division of the General Accounting Office, or in event the contract is with the Postoffice Department, by the Postal Accounts

The written requests for review should endeavor to answer the statements contained in the certificate of settlement. 

If the certificate of settlement has allowed a part of the claim, it will be noted that said certificate notifies the claimant in the printed language at the foot thereof that the check must be returned with the request for review. 

This is not always necessary for the reason that the Comptroller General ordinarily grants permission to the claimant to cash the check pending consideration of the request for review of the settlement. In such cases where the claimant needs the money represented by the check, he should so inform the Comptroller General and request written permission to cash the check without prejudice to the right to secure review of the settlement when the request has been prepared and submitted. In some instances it requires considerable time to properly prepare such a request for review of the settlement and there is no disposition to deprive the contractor of needed money

represented by the check during such periods of delay. 

Due to the fact that the various chiefs of divisions in the General Accounting Office may submit a matter to the Comptroller General for written instructions before settlement is made or other action taken and such instructions given, the request for review of such settlement or action may be in the nature of a request that the Comptroller General reconsider his former instructions as to which the claimant is not always advised. 

Equity Claims 

The Comptroller General has jurisdiction, under an act of April 10, 1928, (45 Stat. 413) to consider equitable claims against the Government and to make a report of the facts in such claims to Congress, with recommendation that an appropriation be made for their payment. 

During every session of Congress many such recommendations are made and approved, but no general rule can be stated as to what may constitute an equitable claim under this statute. Each such claim depends on the facts with respect thereto and the claim must be one where the Government in equity and good conscience should pay it, even though the contract and appropriation chargeable with the contract price make no provision therefor.

The fact that a contract is with the United States in no way renders it unnecessary for a subcontractor, materialman or laborer to make the same arrangement with contractors for the payment of their claims that they would make in event the contract was with a private party. In some instances, such subcontractors, materialmen and laborers have been able to collect from the surety. Such losses of subcontractors, materialmen and laborers could have been avoided in part, at least, by making satisfactory credit arrangements with the contractor.
