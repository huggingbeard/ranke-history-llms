HIGHLIGHTS: REVIEWING FISCAL YEAR 1974 

As fiscal year 1974 opened, the air transportation industry felt the full grip of the energy shortage that began to be felt late in fiscal year 1973. 

While the shortage began to ease somewhat during the latter part of fiscal year 1974, its long-term effects continued to be felt. Addressing itself to those long-term effects, the Civil Aeronautics Board continued its work of the previous year in the regulation of route adjustments and consideration of capacity control agreements. But rising costs of still scarce fuel (almost a 100-percent price increase domestically and steeper rises internationally) outpaced route and capacity adjustments aimed at compensating for increases in operating costs and led to requests for further fare and rate increases. In passing on those requests, the Board in the public interest adhered to its established policy of approving rate and fare increases only on the basis of cost increases already experienced, rather than on increases anticipated. 

The effect of sustained high prices for fuel fostered a number of industry and regulatory concerns, not the least of which were economic problems experienced by Pan American World Airways and Trans

World Airlines, which sought Federal subsidy from the CAB. 

Fuel considerations were evidenced in other Board actions where operating authority was concerned. The fuel situation was one of the factors considered in the decision not to authorize competitive service for the present in the Cleveland/Detroit-Atlanta Case. It was also a consideration in the requests by several trunk and local service airlines for Board approval of agreements to exchange certain of their route authorities. Five such cases moved through various procedural stages during the year-the American-Airwest Transfer Case, the American- 

Frontier Case, the American-Pan American Route Exchange Case, the TWA-Delta Transfer Case, and the Ozark-Eastern Transfer Case. 

In local service air transportation, the Board's major actions in the area of operating authority involved realignment of the route systems of three more carriers and replacement by commuter airlines of service which the certificated carriers were allowed to suspend at some points. 

The Board neared decision in the New England Service Investigation as the fiscal year neared its close. The case involves local and connecting air service to about 25 small communities in New England where commuter airlines have been providing most of the scheduled service. 1

Required consideration of environmental issues in the scope of route proceedings added a significant dimension to a number of cases, and several route proceedings previously ready for final Board decision were remanded for further proceedings so that an evidentiary record could be developed on environmental issues.

With the announcement of its Institutional Control of Air Carriers 

Investigation, the Board began a broad and in-depth inquiry into relationships between air carriers and financial institutions which hold substantial amounts of airline stock, are substantial creditors of the airlines, or which lease flight equipment to the airlines. Its aim is a determination of whether or how such institutions may influence airline managements and whether the Board should take action concerning any existing control or interlocking relationships. 

Board actions also increasingly reflected an attempt to broaden public knowledge of the nature of regulatory matters. An example of that was the adoption of a new procedural regulation which requires that agreements submitted for prior Board approval be docketed for more formal handling than before. Formal, docketed matters are readily available for public inspection and comment. 

Another case of interest to the general public as well as the airline and travel industry, the ATC Bylaws Investigation, gave the Board a vehicle to review various procedural regulations of the Air Traffic Conference of America and its relationship with travel agents. Among other agreements approved by the Board was one requiring travel agents to guard against large-scale thefts of tickets in their possession and to require use of a special ticket form for air tours sold by travel agents.

 was the first full year of the travel group charter (TGC) experiment. While fewer than 10 percent of the flights filed were operated, the percentage actually operated was a great increase over the percentage operated the year before. Reviewing the TGC experiment, the Board proposed a rule to liberalize the requirements for this type of charter. Late in the fiscal year, the Board also proposed another regulation designed to make charter travel more widely available on other than a prior affinity basis. The regulation proposed would create a new class of charter, the Special Event Charter (SEC), permitting groups drawn from the general public to charter aircraft in order to attend various kinds of special or unique events. 

The Board issued its opinions in the final phases of the largest investigation of any kind in its history as it issued its decisions in the 

Joint Fares and Fare Structure Phases of the Domestic Passenger-Fare 

Investigation (DPFI). Several more limited fare cases were in various stages of completion late in the fiscal year, and petitions for Board review were pending in the Airport Security Charges case and the

Emergency Reservation Practices Investigation. Late in the year, the Board also instituted the Domestic Night Coach Fare Investigation, but no procedural dates had been established. 

During the year, the Board permitted two general increases in passenger fares within the 48 contiguous States, a 5-percent rise to cover the overall rise in the cost of doing business and a 6-percent rise intended solely to cover experienced price increases in aviation fuel. 2

Several proceedings concerning air cargo rates and service were also in various procedural stages during the year. These included the 

Minimum Charges per Air Freight Shipment, Investigation of Premium Rates for Live Animals and Birds, the Domestic Air Freight Rates 

Investigation, the Rules and Practices Relating to the Acceptance and 

Carriage of Live Animals in Domestic Air Freight Transportation, and Air Freight Liability and Claims Rules and Practices. 

As with fares, the CAB also permitted a number of freight rate in- @creases to compensate the industry for escalating operating costs, but it I also suspended a number of increases because the proposed levels appeared to exceed industry costs of providing the service, including a - full return on investment.

International fare and rate activity increased as a direct result of the continued sharp rises in the prices of aviation fuel. During the last half 

I of the fiscal year, the Board approved seven agreements directly attributable to the fuel price situation and generally providing for fare or rate increases.

Subsidy payments to eligible airlines increased by $2.6 million overall during the fiscal year, but Allegheny Airlines received its last subsidy support as the year ended. Starting July 1, 1974, Allegheny

I became the first local service airline to go off subsidy. 

Economic enforcement continued its heightened activity and effectiveness, aided at least in part by a major organizational change in the Board's Bureau of Enforcement early in the fiscal year. A new 

Investigation and Audit Division was established in the Bureau, and the Investigation Division of the Bureau of Enforcement, the Field Audits Division of the Bureau of Accounts and Statistics, and the Field 

Representative Sections in Miami, New York, and Anchorage were transferred to the new division. During the year, the Board accepted civil penalties totaling $478,000, in amounts ranging from $900 to $101,000. That was a 16-percent increase over the total accepted in fiscal 1973.

Volumes 46 through 48 of the Board's formal case reports, "CAB Reports," were distributed during the fiscal year. Volumes 49 through 

51 were in various stages of publication and were expected to be issued during fiscal year 1975. 3

DOMESTIC AVIATION ROUTE ADJUSTMENTS 

The dramatic impact of the fuel crisis on operators and users of com mercial air service, beginning in November 1973, began to ease in the last half of fiscal year 1974, but its serious long-term effect-sharp rises in the cost of fuel-fostered major problems, including requests for sub sidy of Pan American World Airways, Inc., and Trans World Airlines, 

Inc. In addition, an unparalleled increase in the price of domestic fuel, to 22 cents per gallon, and even steeper rises in international fuel prices prompted substantial increases in passenger fares. One result of the fuel crisis was a series of short-term agreements to limit capacity in more than two dozen city-pair markets.

During the year the Board completed the hearing phase of the Capacity Reduction Agreements Case (docket 22908), a case designed first to deal with requests for renewal of the nonfuel-related capacity agreements in the transcontinental markets, and second to probe the desirability of capacity agreements as an instrument of general Board policy. 

INTERNATIONAL 

In international route activity, the most significant step was the move to the hearing stage of the Transatlantic Route Proceeding (docket 25908). The case's complex issues included questions on renewal of significant portions of the authority of Pan American World Airways, Inc., and Trans World Airlines, Inc., and of the supplemental airlines' authority, the certification of new domestic coterminal points, stopover rights in scheduled operations, and the certification of cargo service for supplemental carriers.

TRUNKLINES 

No new cases involving competitive authority were assigned for hearing during the year, but the Board issued decisions in certain competitive cases begun earlier. In the Detroit/Cleveland-Atlanta Case (docket 20724), the Board decided not to authorize competitive service now because of the fuel situation, the quality of service by the incumbent carrier, and the diversion which would result from a competitive award.

Among the significant matters affecting trunklines were proceedings nvolving the transfer of route authority. The American-Airwest ransfer Case (docket 25881), covering the question of authority in the outhwestern States, and the American-Frontier Case (docket 25397), ocusing on the exchange of route authority in the same general area, were the first such cases to be heard. Other route transfer cases intituted during the year were: the American-Pan American Route Exhange Case (docket 26245), involving the transfer of Caribbean uthority and South Pacific authority; the TWA-Delta Transfer Case docket 26479), embracing TWA's authority at Nashville, and the

Ozark-Eastern Transfer Case (docket 26668), involving St. Louis- 

Nashville authority. 

.OCAL SERVICE 

Board route action affecting local air carriers again concentrated on three major areas: the continued program of realigning carrier route systems (with decision nearing on applications of Allegheny Airlines, Frontier Airlines, and North Central Airlines at year end); continued favorable action on suspension applications involving air service to small communities where replacement service by commuter carriers was proposed as a substitute; and consideration of deletion of a city from a carrier's certificate where traffic is low and isolation not a determining factor.

The table on the following page lists Board actions in fiscal year 1974 permitting the communities shown to be served by commuter carriers in lieu of certificated scheduled carriers.

Board action in the New England Service Investigation (docket 22973), nearing decision as the fiscal year ended, will have a significant impact on local and connecting air service to some 25 small communities throughout the six-State area. In recent years, commuter carriers have been providing most of the scheduled service to these communities.

In a related matter affecting service to small communities, the Board, in the Frontier/Air Midwest Flow-Through Subsidy Case (docket 25755), established an experiment to test the feasibility of having service at three points provided by a subsidized commuter carrier in place of a subsidized certificated carrier. 

ENVIRONMENTAL ISSUES The National Environmental Protection Act requirement that the 

Board make determinations about the effect of its major actions on the quality of the human environment has added a new dimension to route proceedings. Environmental issues have been included within the scope of most new proceedings, and several major route cases, previously ripe for final Board decision, were remanded for further proceedings in order to develop an evidentiary record on environmental issues.

ALASKA SERVICE 

The Board took steps to bring the Alaska Service Investigation (docket 20826) to a close, making certain matters the subject of smaller, more limited hearing cases and moving to handle other issues by rulemaking proceedings. The actions should make for an early conclusion to the final (Bush) phase of this case. 

INFORMAL ROUTE MATTERS 

There were 300 applications for temporary authorizations of various types during the fiscal year. This was 64 more than in fiscal year 1973. 

Informal (nonhearing) route applications, which require the largest part of the analytical work on informal cases, increased from 200 in fiscal year 1973 to 255 in fiscal year 1974.

Informal route applications receive high priority, so that route modifications which promise to improve public transportation service and reduce costs for carriers can be timely effected. 

CONTROL AND INTERLOCKING RELATIONSHIPS The Board instituted the Institutional Control of Air Carriers 

Investigation (docket 26348) to inquire into relationships between air carriers and financial institutions, such as banks, which hold substantial amounts of airline stock, are substantial creditors of the airlines, or lease flight equipment to the airlines. In it, the Board will try to deter mine whether and in what manner various financial institutions may influence airline managements, and whether the Board should take action with regard to any control or interlocking relationships that may exist.

During fiscal year 1974, an administrative law judge issued his initial decision in the Air Carrier Reorganization Investigation (docket 24283), in which the Board is considering whether and under what circumstances the reorganization and diversification of air carrier businesses should be permitted. Oral argument before the Board on this case is due to be heard in the next fiscal year. 

The Board, having retained jurisdiction, also continued to evaluate financial transactions between some air carriers, such as The Flying Tiger Line, and their holding companies. The Board also approved the acquisition of control of a supplemental air carrier, McCulloch International Airlines, by the Black and Decker Manufacturing Company.

Among the noncertificated airlines, at the end of the fiscal year there was pending before the Board the proposed acquisition of two air taxis by motor common carriers, and the acquisition of control of Federal Express, a large freight-carrying air taxi, by a group of investors. An administrative law judge recommended approval of the proposed acquisition of control of Airborne Freight Corporation, one of the largest U.S. air freight forwarders, by I.U. International Corporation, a large holding company with affiliates engaged in many different businesses. The case awaits oral argument before the Board. Another administrative law judge recommended against permitting Liquigas, S.P.A., an Italian industrial concern, to indirectly acquire control of Ronson Helicopters, an air taxi operator. Petitions for review of the decision are pending before the Board.

INTERCARRIER AGREEMENTS 

An administrative law judge issued his initial decision in the ATC Bylaws Investigation (docket 23542), in which the Board is considering 0 whether the formative document of the Air Traffic Conference of America should be approved, particularly in view of its provisions for unanimous voting. On review of the initial decision, the Board heard oral argument, and was considering final decision as the fiscal year ended.

A hearing also was concluded in the Capacity Reduction Agreements Case (docket 22908), which involves the question of whether, as a matter of policy, the Board should allow competing airlines to limit by agreement the number of flights they operate in particular markets. 

The Board concluded its 4-year Express Service Investigation (docket 22388), deciding that the joint REA-air carrier system of providing air express should be ended in view of the expansion of the air freight services of the airlines and air freight forwarders, the inability of the REAairlines partnership to continue to provide a viable service, and the promise of more attractive high priority and small package, small community service by other means. In a related action, the Board authorized discussions among the airlines to provide for necessary interline coordination of a new high priority service.

In other significant cases, the Board authorized intercarrier discussions of ways to increase service at Chicago's Midway Airport, ap proved the formation of the Airline Industrial Relations Conference to serve as an information clearing house and, in some instances, as the representative of participating carriers in labor relations matters, and is considering an airline proposal to expand the Universal Air Travel Plan credit card program.

The Board adopted a new procedural regulation requiring that agreements submitted for prior Board approval (usually those having antitrust significance) be docketed and handled in a more formal manner than before. The aim is to broaden public knowledge of the nature of such agreements and allow for more orderly consideration by the Board.

AIR CARRIER SCHEDULING 

The impact of the national fuel shortage on the airlines, necessitating numerous flight adjustments, was one of the most pressing problems of the air transportation industry during fiscal year 1974. The Board helped the airlines to meet the emergency by authorizing discussions among competing airlines of ways to reduce capacity and by approving agreements ultimately reached. In related actions, the Board authorized discussions between Pan American World Airways, Inc., and Trans 

World Airlines, Inc., the largest U.S. transatlantic carriers, to let them consider ways of meeting the financial crisis, which the carriers said was caused by the greatly increased cost of fuel in that market. The discussions ended without agreement. The Board also authorized later, successful discussions among certain carriers to space their night coach flights to conserve fuel.

TRAVEL AGENT MATTERS 

The ATC Bylaws Investigation, referred to earlier, gave the Board a vehicle to review various ATC procedural regulations affecting the ATC's relationship with travel agents. This case awaited final Board action at year end. At the close of the year, the Board also was considering agreements (1) establishing a new, mechanized system under which agents will report airline sales, (2) requiring that agents and applicants pass tests for competency, and (3) amending the bonding program. 

The Board also approved air carrier agreements requiring all travel agents to take precautions against large-scale thefts of airline tickets in their possession, to require the use of a special ticket form for air tours sold by travel agents, and to increase travel agent fees. The Board denied a petition seeking to require air carriers to pay commissions on sales made by travel agents using Universal Air Travel Plan (UATP) 

credit cards. Pending at year's close was an air carrier agreement to establish a higher rate of commission on tickets sold by travel agents for "family travel," and to institute a study of the ATC commission structure.

The Board also approved an agreement among supplemental air carriers to establish through their trade association a uniform system for utilizing travel agents.

SUPPLEMENTAL AIR CARRIERS 

There were 11 certificated U.S. supplemental air carriers as of June 

30, 1974; however, three (Purdue Airlines, Inc., Standard Airways, Inc., and Universal Airlines, Inc.) were not operating. Separate applications to acquire each of the three are before the Board for decision. 

On December 28, 1973, at the request of the carrier, the Board revoked the operating authorities held by Southern Air Transport. 

Hearings in the Supplemental Renewal Proceeding (docket 23944) were completed in December 1973. The case is to determine (1) whether the supplementals' authority for international charter service (other than transatlantic), including international and domestic inclusive tour charter service, should be renewed, and (2) if so, which of the presently authorized carriers and new applicants should be designated to serve each geographic area. (The transatlantic charter authority of the supplementals is in issue in the Transatlantic Route Proceeding (docket 

25908), in which hearings commenced in June 1974.)

The certificated areas of authority of the supplemental air carriers are shown in exhibit 12, page 28.

AIR TAXI OPERATORS 

During fiscal year 1974, a total of 3,650 air taxi operators registered with the Board. Of that number, 226 were commuter air carriers which either transport mail or perform scheduled passenger or cargo services. 

During the year the Board amended its regulations to require all air taxis to submit annual statistical reports of their operations. Prior to 10

this amendment, only commuter air carriers were required to file. statistical reports.

The Board also issued 17 exemptions to air taxi operators to use "large" aircraft in specific areas or markets.

Nearly all have been authorized to operate split charters, some are authorized to operate inclusive tour charters, many hold limited cargo charter authority, and some have been authorized to operate charters from countries other than their homelands to the United States. 11

ECONOMIC CLIMATE 

International air transportation, like other economic activities, continued to be beset by severe cost escalation and sluggish traffic growth during the fiscal year. Pan American and Trans World Airlines, heavy reliers on international business, incurred substantial losses in their operations. Precipitous fuel price increases posed difficult cost problems for both those carriers and the international air transportation industry in general. Labor and other nonfuel costs for the industry continued to climb in the fiscal year, with no sign of abatement. The net effect, a noticeable increase in the price of air transport services, will test anew the price elasticity of the demand for such services in the international marketplace. 

The burdens and pressures on U.S.-flag carriers go beyond the recognizable worldwide problem of inflation. They also are called upon to operate in international air markets-notably, the transatlanetic-where excessive capacity has created a situation that has de- Th pressed their yields and reduced load factors while their operating costs have been rising. Some foreign governments also have imposed arbitrary restrictions on the frequencies they may operate and the capacity they may provide. Foreign governments sometimes also give their national air carriers preferred competitive positions in the marketplace. 

There has been a recognizable trend for such problems to increase in recent years, rather than to diminish.

Concurrently, a wide spectrum of improvements and actions are available to the U.S. air transport industry and to the Government. 

Both have been moving to meet the challenge. U.S.-flag carriers have been trimming employment rolls in all areas of operations and can be expected to continue doing so. The carriers are stressing more efficient use of aircraft to try to improve load factors and increase earnings, and they are continuing to automate operations-in managing space reservations, making ticket sales, conducting aircraft operations, and other areas to further trim costs and improve overall operations. Carriers 13

also are scrutinizing their air movement and ground support systems for all possible improvements.

The Board, in cooperation with the Departments of State and Transportation, has been acting to strengthen the economic position of the U.S.-flag carriers. Using its appropriate Economic Regulations, the 

CAB moved on countries which it determined had unreasonably restricted U.S. carriers. The Board also was studying preferential and restrictive practices which give foreign air carriers unfair competitive advantages so that it might formulate corrective actions. The Board hardened its negotiation positions in dealing with the demands of foreign governments for new or additional rights, and will seek to adjust existing exchanges of air rights with other countries under circumstances where exchanges are clearly out of balance. 

BILATERAL RELATIONS 

This is a summary of the current thrust of U.S. bilateral air transport relations with other countries. 

President's Policy Statement In a Statement of International Air Transportation Policy issued by the White House on June 22, 1970, the President called for continuation of the exchange of scheduled air transport rights with other countries on the basis of bilateral agreements embodying the "Bermuda" principles. Under "Bermuda" each airline is free to decide the number of flights it will operate on agreed routes, subject to ex post facto review of its operations to assure that, among other things, the airlines have a fair and equal opportunity to compete; the airlines of one country do not operate so as to affect unduly the operations of the airlines of the other country; the air services bear a close relationship to public requirement; and that the primary objective of air services is to carry traffic to and from an airline's homeland.

International air transportation has been well served by the economic environment which those principles have fostered. Competition among air carriers in domestic and international markets-can be expected to improve the quality of service and bring down the price in the long run. But those economic views on air transportation are not shared by all nations.

The Policy Statement, observing that charter or nonscheduled air rights have generally been granted or obtained on a unilateral basis, calls for bilateral arrangements to further the development of air charter travel.

Board Role 

The process of obtaining and exchanging air rights with foreign counries for new or additional route and charter air services involves the CAB in several respects. It entertains proposals for new or additional services from U.S. carriers and foreign governments, and it initiates such proposals on its own. It solicits the views of concerned and inerested parties, including air carriers, other government agencies, pitizen groups, and others. It conducts supporting market and economic studies and makes appropriate recommendations to the Department of State on desired air rights. In cooperation with the Department of 

State, it holds or takes part in aviation discussions and consultations with other countries, and participates as a member of U.S. delegations to negotiations.

Bilateral Agreements 

Bilateral air transport agreements have served, in practice, as the main basis for exchanging air rights with other countries and have provided the general framework for managing most U.S. commercial air transport relations with other nations, including the resolution of problems or disputes which may arise. Where no agreement exists, as in the case of Argentina, the Philippines, Central America, and parts of Asia and Africa, air relations have been conducted on the basis of reciprocity or unilateral grants of operating authority. 

The United States had in force, on June 30, 1974, a total of 73 bilateral air transport agreements relating to scheduled air services (exhibit 1). That included 15 bilateral agreements based on an assumption of rights and obligations of an existing agreement by a newly independent third country (e.g., as did Ghana following its independence from the United Kingdom). During fiscal year 1974, the United States signed a bilateral air transport agreement with Romania-the first with that country-and concluded a new route annex with Canada. A bilateral agreement pertaining to charter or nonscheduled air services was also signed with Canada and another with Yugoslavia (exhibit 1, footnotes). 

The two signings provide for the first time formal and full accord governing U.S. charter relations with other countries.

During fiscal year 1974, the following bilateral aviation consultations and negotiations took place: 

Western Hemisphere 

The United States and Canada amended the bilateral air transport agreement concluded in 1966, providing a new exchange of routes. The accord signed on May 8, 1974, was reached after lengthy negotiations. A separate agreement, concluded the same day, covers charter or nonscheduled air operations between the two countries. 

The route exchange with Canada provides for a major expansion of scheduled services between the two countries, involving 46 new routes and route extensions. Implementation authority for operation for 25 of the routes became effective May 8, 1974. Authority for the remaining routes will be implemented over 1976, 1978, and 1979.

The bilateral air charter agreement extends to the charter operations of both route and supplemental (or charter-only) carriers of both coun tries. It provides for the first time a comprehensive mutual understanding governing the long-term development of freight and passenger air charter traffic, and also encompasses the operations of over 500 air taxi operators of the two countries.

Negotiations conducted with Mexico in the latter part of fiscal year 1974 resulted in an extension of the present bilateral air transport agreement to December 1975. Scheduled air service between the two countries involves many air carriers and an extensive route system as well as a strong and growing community of economic and social interests.

Discussions involving Western Hemisphere countries (and political entities) were also held with Argentina, Barbados, Bolivia, the

Netherlands Antilles, and Panama. No agreements or understandings were concluded. The discussions served essentially to exchange views on route, capacity, and other matters relating to scheduled air services and rights.

Preclearance 

The United States and Canada signed an agreement May 8, 1974, continuing and expanding the provisions for the preclearance of passengers. Under the prior arrangements with Canada, U.S.-bound passengers were cleared through U.S. Customs and Immigration at airports serving Montreal, Toronto, Winnipeg, and Vancouver, rather than upon arrival in the United States. Preclearance was first estab lished at Toronto in 1952 and later extended to Montreal, Winnipeg, and Vancouver. The new agreement greatly expands the arrangements to include for the first time the preclearance by Canadian customs and immigration officials stationed in the United States of passengers going to Canada.

The agreement also will permit Canada to preclear passengers for 

Canada at 13 United States airports-in Boston, Chicago, Cleveland, Dallas/Fort Worth, Denver, Honolulu, Houston, Los Angeles, Miami, New York, Newark, San Francisco, and Tampa. The United States will be permitted to preclear passengers for the United States at seven additional airports: Halifax, Quebec City, London, Ottawa, Calgary, Edmonton, and Victoria. U.S. preclearance staff already in Canada, and facilities, will be expanded. 

Preclearance speeds the flow of aircraft and passengers, which can then move through gateway airports without delay, saving time and money for both airline and passenger. Preclearance is a practical application which can be desirably expanded to include other countries. 

No precleared flight has ever been hijacked.

A further understanding on preclearance was reached with Bermuda January 15, 1974, the basic arrangement for the preclearance of U.S.bound passengers in Bermuda by U.S. Customs and Immigration. The understanding also improves preclearance facilities.

Scheduled Passenger Traffic 

Scheduled air service far predominates in air travel between the United States and foreign points, accounting for 88 percent of the 23 million commercial air passengers carried in calendar year 1973 and of the 21.7 million passengers carried in 1972. The rest is charter traffic. 

U.S.-flag air carriers during 1973 carried 50.6 percent of this market, a slight decline from their 51.1-percent participation in 1972. Foreignflag carriers in this market showed the higher percentage increase in the number of passengers carried, 7.2 percent in 1973 over 1972. That compares with 4.8 percent for U.S.-flag carriers. Foreign-flag carriers also had a higher increase in the actual number of passengers carried in scheduled services (764,951), while the number of passengers on U.S.flag carriers increased by 536,260 passengers.

U.S. citizens as a percentage of the total scheduled market declined from 61.3 percent in calendar 1972 to 59.4 percent in 1973.

In the major market, U.S.-Europe, scheduled air passengers increased from 9.2 million passengers in calendar year 1972 to 9.4 million passengers in 1973. U.S.-flag carrier participation remained fairly constant. U.S. airlines carried 41.9 percent of the passengers in calendar 

1972 and 41.8 percent in 1973. In actual numbers, however, foreign carriers increased their carriage by 74,000 passengers, while U.S. carriers increased their number by only 37,000. 

U.S. citizens as a percent of the total U.S.-Europe scheduled market declined from 65.4 percent in 1972 to 63.6 percent in 1973.

Charter Passenger Traffic 

Passenger charter flights between the United States and foreign countries (excluding Canada) increased by 8 percent, from 20,090 flights in 1972 to 21,734 flights in 1973 (exhibit 15). The number of passenger seats operated increased by 15 percent, from 3.7 million in 

1972 to 4.3 million 1973 (exhibit 16).

The transpacific market was the only major charter market to experience substantial growth: flights increased 107 percent from 578 in 

1972 to 1,193 in 1973, and passenger seats operated increased 148 percent from 104,318 seats in 1972 to 258,573 seats in 1973 (exhibits 15 and 16). Traffic growth was substantial in the charter markets to Japan, Hong Kong, Australia/New Zealand, and the Far East Mainland (exhibits 17 and 18). 

Transatlantic flights increased by 8 percent from 13,654 in 1972 to 14,726 in 1973, and passenger seats operated increased by 13 percent from 2.7 million in 1972 to 3.1 million in 1973 (exhibits 15 and 16). During calendar year 1973 the charter market to the United Kingdom declined by 10 percent in number of flights and by 4 percent in passenger seats. The charter markets to Ireland and Greece also declined by 45 and 6 percent, respectively, in passenger seats, and 43 percent and 14 percent in number of flights (exhibits 17 and 18). 32

There was only marginal growth in the Latin American and Caribbean charter market: flights declined by 1 percent, from 5,858 in 1972 to 

5,815 in 1973, but passenger seats operated increased from 866,935 in 

1972 to 907,123 in 1973. Good growth marked the charter markets to 

Mexico and South America (32 and 61 percent, respectively, in passenger seats). But the charter markets to Bermuda/Bahamas, Central America, and other Caribbean points declined by 15, 19, and 2 percent, respectively, in passenger seats operated (exhibits 17 and 18). 

In the charter market between the United States and foreign countries, foreign route carriers had the greatest overall increase in traffic: 23 percent in flights and 45 percent in passenger seats operated. However, flights and passenger seats operated by foreign charter carriers declined in 1973 to the point that they constituted only a 4-percent share of the charter market (exhibits 15 and 16).

Both U.S. route and U.S. supplemental carriers experienced a significant increase in charter passenger traffic: 21 and 11 percent, respectively, in charter flights, and 23 and 22 percent, respectively, in passenger seats operated. The combined share of U.S. route and U.S. supplemental carriers in the charter market increased from 72 percent of the flights and 73 percent of the passenger seats operated in 1972 to 78 percent of both the flights and passenger seats operated in 1973 (exhibits 15 and 16).

The United States-Germany charter market became the largest for the first time in 1973, with 3,074 flights. The United States-United 

Kingdom charter market fell to second place with 3,046 flights in 1973, followed by United States-Spain in third place with 2,054 flights. The 

6,120 passenger charter flights between the United States and Germany and the United Kingdom accounted for 28 percent of the total passenger charter flights between the United States and foreign countries (exhibit 17). 

During 1973 transatlantic passenger charter flights accounted for 68 percent of the total number of flights, Caribbean and Latin America for 27 percent, and transpacific for 5 percent. Transatlantic passenger charter seats operated accounted for 73 percent of the total market. 

Seats in the Caribbean and Latin American market accounted for 21 percent of the total. The transpacific market accounted for 6 percent of the total seats (exhibits 17 and 18). 333

Freight Traffic 

Freight business is an increasingly important factor in the air transport balance of payments. The total value of freight exported from the United States by air was $10.4 billion in 1973; the value of imports was $6.9 billion. Thirty-seven percent of the exports and forty-eight percent of the imports were on U.S. carriers. 

U.S. route carriers in international/territorial operations carried a total of 2.3 billion freight revenue ton-miles in 1973 (exhibits 19 and 20). 

That compares with 2.5 billion freight ton-miles in the previous year, a decrease of 7 percent.

As a part of the total revenue ton-miles produced in all categories of movement, freight declined to 32 percent of the total revenue ton-miles in 1973, as compared to 34 percent in 1972. That contrasted with the steady increase in the percentage share of freight revenue ton-miles experienced since 1969 (exhibit 19). 

Passenger/cargo carriers produced over 1.4 billion freight ton-miles (61 percent of total freight carried), or about the same amount carried in 1972. The all-cargo carriers, however, declined from 1972's more than one billion freight ton-miles to 0.9 billion freight ton-miles in 1973, a decrease of 15 percent (exhibit 20).

The U.S. carrier share of U.S. foreign airborne trade, based on shipping weight data compiled by the Bureau of the Census, amounted to 

39.1 percent in 1973, a 2.4-percent decline from their 41.5 percent share in 1969. It reflects a decline in U.S. air carrier participation in most major markets, most notably the Asian and North American markets (exhibit 21).

The United States-Europe air freight market is this country's leading international air freight market. In 1973 it accounted for 48.8 percent of the shipping weight total. Air freight movements between the United 

States and the United Kingdom amounted to 11.4 percent, making it the leading component in the total United States-Europe market (exhibit 22).

The United States-Asia air freight market was the next most important market with 20.2 percent of the total market. Japan (8.9 percent), Canada (5.4 percent), and Venezuela (3.7 percent) were the leading

U.S. air trade country partners in their respective continents (exhibit 22).

MULTILATERAL RELATIONS Chicago Agreement 

The Convention on International Civil Aviation, commonly referred to as the Chicago Agreement, is the cornerstone of international aviation. Recognizing the importance of safe and orderly development of world aviation, the United States issued invitations to 55 countries to attend a conference on international civil aviation on November 1, 1944. From this meeting emerged the Chicago Agreement, which the 

United States signed on August 9, 1946. The Convention had been signed by a total of 129 countries by June 30, 1974, most recently by the Maldives in March 1974. The Convention resulted in the creation of the 

International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), which sets stand ards governing international flight. ICAO subsequently became an agency of the United Nations.

The work of ICAO has contributed very significantly to the development of international air transportation in the world. This is demonstrated in the unhindered, safe movement of aircraft (and freight and passengers) from one part of the world to another. Such movement would not be possible without the common communications and navigational aids made possible through ICAO's efforts.

International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) 

In fiscal year 1974 the ICAO Council issued a set of statements to contracting states on charges for airport and en route navigation facilities. 

The statements take into account the recommendations made by the 

Conference on the Economics of Route Air Navigation Facilities and 

Airports (EFA) held in Montreal in February 1973, and they supersede

Council statements published following the 1967 Conference on 

Charges for Airports and Route Air Navigation Facilities (CARF). 

Following an ERFA recommendation, the first session of the Panel on 

Route Facility Cost Accounting and Cost Allocation (RFCA) was held in Montreal in March 1974, with the first phase of its work devoted to development of route facility cost accounting and cost allocation to promote adequate and uniform guidelines. The CAB provided an adviser to the U.S. Panel member.

The 18th Assembly of ICAO adopted a resolution directing the Council to initiate a feasibility study on international air passenger fares and rates. Upon completion and review of the study, the Council had the 

ICAO Secretariat prepare a full statistical and comparative survey of international fares and rates in force in September 1973. The Council planned a second edition for 1975. It was also planning to propose to the 

ICAO Assembly meeting to be held in Montreal in September 1974 that the Secretariat undertake a program of annual studies dealing with international fares and rates.

NTERNATIONAL FARES AND RATES 

International fare and rate activity accelerated considerably in fiscal year 1974 due to the energy crisis which began in October 1973 and the resulting escalation in aviation fuel prices, which in some cases exceeded 200 percent. During the second half of the year, the Board approved no fewer than seven International Air Transport Association (IATA) agreements directly attributable to the fuel price situation. In summary, the agreements provided for a worldwide 6-percent surcharge on fares and cargo rates in January 1974 and further surcharges generally in the range of 7 percent in March and April. An additional 5-percent surcharge was imposed on North Atlantic cargo rates in June 1974.

Passenger Fares 

IATA carriers' efforts to reform the North Atlantic fare structure for effect from January 1974 were concentrated during September and October 1973, and culminated essentially in retention of the same fare structure with increases of varying amounts in all fares. The Board disapproved the proposed increases in normal economy fares, and deferred action on the 22/45-day excursion fare (the primary promotional fare) since the result would have been to widen the spread between normal and discount fares, a result contrary to the Board's objectives. The CAB also disapproved youth fares as unjustly discriminatory and in view of their uneconomically low level. The IATA carriers subsequently renegotiated an agreement which retained normal economy fares at status quo, and further increased the 22/45-day excursion fare as well as other promotional fares. The agreement, which also included a 6percent surcharge on all fares to compensate for fuel cost increases, was approved by the Board as representing a desirable step toward compressing the gap between normal and promotional fares.

The Board authorized the IATA carriers and the supplemental charter carriers to enter into discussions looking toward establishment of minimum charter rates in North Atlantic service. No agreement had been reached by the end of the year, but further discussions were scheduled. 47

Generally, fares on transpacific routes remained at status quo with the exception of fuel-related increases, although some downward adjustment in South Pacific fares resulted from the Board's requirement that the through fares not exceed the sum of the local sector fares constructed over Honolulu.

Most elements of a new Western Hemisphere fare structure were approved by the Board in April 1974 and involved increases of 5 percent in the Caribbean and 7 percent in United States-Mexico markets. A new individual inclusive tour fare replaced the group inclusive tour fare in several major United States-Mexico markets. The Board tentatively disapproved an increase of 3 percent in United States-South America fares, as well as increases in United States west coast-Mexico fares.

Final disposition of these areas was pending at the end of the fiscal year. 

The Board's disapproval of IATA agreed youth fares in foreign air transportation beyond December of 1973, and the cancellation of other youth fares, rendered inactive the previously instituted investigation of 

Youth and Student Fares in Foreign Air Transportation (docket 23780). 

The Board adopted the initial decision of the administrative law judge as its opinion in the Expanded Application of Category Z Fares 

(docket 24072), finding not unlawful the application of Category Z fares to Air Force Exchange Service personnel traveling on official business. 

Category Z fares apply to Department of Defense personnel traveling on Government business on scheduled services at a reduced rate keyed to a minimum rate established by the CAB in the interest of the national defense mission (General Policy Statements, section 399.16, CAB 

Regulations). Category Z fares differ from the military personnel rate, also a reduced rate, which applies to members of the Armed Forces traveling at their own expense, such as on furloughs, etc. 

Prehearing conference was held in the investigation of Baggage 

Allowance Tariff Rules in Overseas and Foreign Air Transportation 

(docket 24869), in which the lawfulness of baggage weight limitations and charges for excess baggage will be determined. 

Cargo Rates 

In October 1973, the carriers reached agreement on North Atlantic rates, which they had been discussing since May. The Board disapproved the major part of the agreement, since it showed no meaningful attempt to reduce reliance on low specific commodity rates and reduce the discount from general cargo rates, instituted a directional differential in all commodity rates, created anomalies in the relationship between high weight-break (30,000 kg.) commodity rates in scheduled service and the new minimum charter rates, and involved discrepancies in the structure of container rates for certain aircraft types. The Board later approved a revised agreement which substantially overcame those objections. The agreement also reflected a new approach to rate specification which satisfactorily implemented the Board's decision in the investigation of IATA North Atlantic Cargo Rates (docket 20522), which found the previous IATA structure unduly preferential and prejudicial to certain U.S. gateway cities. 48

On the North/Central Pacific, the carriers closed the open-rate situaon which had existed since 1971. The agreement retained general argo rates at status quo levels, and substantially increased specific ommodity rates to reflect escalation in operating costs. The Board aproved the agreement with the exception of rates for certain commodity ems, where the Board found a serious potential for undue preference nd prejudice to exist.

The Fuel Crisis 

By January 1974 with air carriers forced to cancel flights, reduce peronnel requirements and temporarily ground aircraft that would herwise be operating, the Board urgently needed fuel cost and fuel onsumption data to enable it to evaluate properly the impact of fuel rice increases on domestic and international rates and services and to Doperate with the Federal Energy Office in carrying out its responbility for the air carrier fuel allocations program. Airline Ownership and Diversified Activities 

Control relationships as they apply to regulated businesses have been matter of mounting concern to consumer groups, legislative bodies, nd regulatory agencies. The Board's evolving interest has recently exended beyond the question of airline ownership and into the relations etween air carriers and their parent and affiliated companies. 

The Board in fiscal year 1974 adopted new regulations which require persons controlling air carriers to disclose information on their shortterm and long-term indebtedness and on the security interests and holdings of their officers and directors in air carriers. This expanded the 

Board's capability to locate and identify those creditors who could exercise a material influence on the parent concern, which in turn could materially influence an air carrier subsidiary. In a related vein, the 

Board instituted the Institutional Control of Air Carriers Investigation 

(docket 26348), to try to further improve its ownership and control regulations.

In another related action, the Board adopted a regulation which requires greater disclosure of revenues and expenses directly related to the performance of services growing from and incidental to the air 61

transportation services performed by air carriers. Chief among these transport-related activities are hotel, restaurant, and food services; inflight liquor sales; limousine services; and airline agreements providing for mutual financial assistance in the case of work stoppages. 

Disclosure Standards for Lease Transactions 

Since 1972 new CAB disclosure standards on leasing transactions have helped close information gaps concerning the effects of lease com mitments on airline financial positions and operating results. Those regulations, plus heightened public concern over the absence o authoritative pronouncements by professional accounting bodies, have increased Board participation in that area. At the close of the fisca year, the Board was reviewing comments on a proposed disclosur standards amendment to the Uniform System of Accounts and Report which would require that obligations to pay for long-term financin leases and the corresponding rights to use leased assets be recorded a liabilities and assets on the face of the air carriers' financial statements 

The Air Freight Forwarder Industry 

The air freight forwarding industry has grown dramatically since 1963, with forwarding revenues and tonnage consolidated increasing a a compound annual rate of 23 percent and 17.7 percent, respectively 

But in that same period air freight forwarders have had one of the highest business failure rates, with considerable losses to the shipping public. 

In view of that growth and the increasing public reliance on air freight forwarders, the Board announced a proposal to revise the air freight forwarders' existing reporting system, establish a uniform system of ac counts for larger forwarders and, for the first time, establish regularized statistical reporting requirements for foreign air freight forwarders. A the close of fiscal year 1974, the Board had reviewed public comment on a draft regulation and was readying a final regulation. 

Charges by Foreign Governments for Airport and En Route Facilities Following the 1967 International Civil Aviation Conference on Air ports and Route Facilities, an increasing number of foreign gov ernments began to levy charges on international air carriers for en rout facilities and services-communication facilities and navigational aids meteorological services, and other ancillary aviation services. Both the number of foreign governments that levy such charges and, in some in stances, the charges themselves have continued to increase. The goal of those governments was complete recovery of costs associated with the facilities and services offered.

Rate of Return on Investment 

During the year the method for computing air carrier rate of return on investment was changed to conform with generally accepted accounting practices.

COMMUNITY AND CONGRESSIONAL RELATIONS 

In Community and Congressional Relations, the Board focused most of its attention in 1974 on problems arising out of the fuel crisis-severe service cutbacks to many of the Nation's smaller communities. To enable it to respond to scores of inquiries from Members of Congress, Governors, mayors, and airport officials, the Board sought information from each of the air carriers concerning their prospective service reductions. With that carrier data compiled, the Board was able to determine the future level of service and the impact on service to any community, congressional district, or State receiving service by any air carrier certificated by it. 

The usefulness of this data underscored the need to have such information available in the Board on a continuing basis. A computerized program has since been established which updates the fluctuations in the types and frequencies of service to all U.S. cities. 

The Board also dealt with a number of adequacy-of-service complaints not related to the fuel crisis. The Board has followed a procedure whereby service complaints are first brought to the attention of the carrier directly. If the problem remains unresolved, it is handled through the Board's Community and Congressional Relations Office. 

Informal meetings then are held involving the community complainant, air carrier officers, airport officials, and Members of Congress. Through moral suasion a compromise solution satisfactory to both the community and the carriers is often achieved.

COURT CASES 

Litigation during fiscal year 1974, for the most part, consisted of direct statutory review of certain CAB orders in the U.S. courts of appeals. Six complaints also were filed in the U.S. district courts against the Board, and on three other occasions, litigants who had been unsuccessful in appeals challenging Board orders in the courts of appeals, filed petitions for certiorari in the U.S. Supreme Court. In the latter three instances the Supreme Court refused to review the lower court decisions in favor of the Board. 

Fifty proceedings were instituted against the Board, compared with 59 in the preceding year. At the close of the fiscal year, 49 petitions for review had been disposed of, 55 were pending. In the preceding year 34 petitions were disposed of and 54 were pending at year end. 

An important case pending in the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit at the close of fiscal year 1973, Saturn Airways, et al. v. C.A.B., 483 F.2d 1284 (C.A.D.C. 1973), involved a challenge to the

Board's regulations establishing "Travel Group Charters," a new type of charter service in which participant eligibility is based upon various travel-related requirements rather than membership in a fraternal organization or club existing for purposes other than travel. In affirming the regulations, the court emphasized that "organizational membership is not necessary to insure the distinction" between charter travel and individually ticketed services.

Two significant cases involving passenger fares and one case concerning cargo rates were also decided in fiscal year 1974. The first of the two passenger fare cases, American Airlines, et al. v. C.A.B., 495 F.2d 1010 (1974), involved a challenge to the Board's orders in Phase 4 (Joint 

Fares) of the Domestic Passenger-Fare Investigation prescribing the es tablishment and division of joint fares between trunk and local service carriers. In affirming the orders, the District of Columbia Circuit accepted the Board's determination that the division of joint fare revenues on a cost prorate basis would be equitable to trunk and local service carriers alike. 

In the second fare case, Pillai, et al. v. C.A.B., 485 F.2d 1018 (C.A.D.C. 1973), the District of Columbia Circuit set aside a Board order approving an agreement among the member carriers of the International Air Transport Association extending the existing transatlantic passenger-fare structure through 1973, subject to a 6-percent increase based on the dollar's devaluation. The court found the Board's order 72

eficient for lack of findings and "record" support, and accordingly emanded the case to the Board for further proceedings. On remand, he Board reapproved the IATA fare agreements (order 73-10-55). 'etitioners' motion for summary reversal of the new Board order was ubsequently dismissed by a per curiam order of the court. 

The decision involving cargo rates was entered in Commonwealth of 

Virginia, et al. v. C.A.B., 498 F.2d 129 (C.A. 4, 1974). There, the fourth circuit affirmed a Board order which found that the existing transatlantic cargo rate structure was unduly preferential to New York and prejudicial to the municipalities of Boston, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington, Cleveland, Detroit, and Chicago and ordered the preference and prejudice removed through the application of new mileage-based rates. 

During the fiscal year, decisions also were forthcoming in the District of Columbia Circuit with regard to important route awards. In Delta

An EEO specialist added to the staff in October 1973 considerably furthered development of the Worker-Trainee Opportunities Program and the formalizing of the Upward Mobility Program. The Board's computerized payroll data system was used to update current statistical data and to provide the Office of Equal Employment Opportunity a feeder report on the work force, by organization, in totals of men, women, grades, and minorities.

LEGISLATIVE DEVELOPMENTS LEGISLATION ENACTED 

During the fiscal year, only one measure was enacted which affected the functions and activities of the CAB. This was Public Law 93-153, approved November 16, 1973, amending the Federal Reporting Services

Act so as to substitute the Comptroller General for the Office of Management and Budget in reviewing questionnaires proposed to be issued by independent Federal regulatory agencies, including the CAB. 

Shortly after the close of the fiscal year, a measure was enacted which was of substantial interest to the aviation industry and to the CAB. 

This was Public Law 93-366, approved August 6, 1974, which amended the Federal Aviation Act to implement the Convention for the Suppression of Unlawful Seizure of Aircraft (Hague Convention), to which the United States is a party. The public law also amended the Act to provide a more effective program for the prevention of aircraft piracy. 

The new law provided, among other things, that passengers could not transport weapons aboard aircraft unless such weapons were publicly declared and checked as baggage. With regard to that provision, the 

CAB issued regulations requiring air carriers to assume, for a reasonable charge, liability for loss or damage to property of a passenger which had to be transported as baggage.

EGISLATIVE PROPOSALS The Civil Aeronautics Board is required by the Federal Aviation Act o include in its annual report such recommendations for additional egislation as it believes to be necessary for discharge of its regulatory unctions. The Board's present legislative proposals for 1975 are encomassed in the following program. 

1. Authorization of the Board To Assess Civil Penalties ubila Legislation is needed that will permit the Board to impose civil lepenalties for violations of the economic regulatory provisions of the fort Federal Aviation Act, or Board regulations thereunder. The Act now spp provides that any person violating such provision of the Act or the luding Board's regulations shall be subject to a civil penalty, and it authorizes the Board to compromise any civil penalty action, but the penalties nd themselves can only be imposed by a U.S. district court in a penalty whiz proceeding instituted there. This procedure has seriously compromised or the the Board's ability to insure compliance with the requirements of the to laws which it has the responsibility to enforce. ers o аран ed th The general matter of the power of administrative agencies to impose in civil penalties has been the subject of study and consideration by the 

Administrative Conference of the United States, and the Conference has characterized the increased use of civil money penalties as "an important and salutary trend" and urged administrative agencies to conume sider asking Congress for authority to impose civil money penalties. 

This authority would help alleviate the already critical overburdening of the Federal courts by removing controversies which involve little precedential significance and only small sums of money, and it would help the Board to administer its statutory responsibilities more speedily and potently. The agency (rather than a district court) would resolve the disputed issues which require specialized knowledge and expertise. 

The requirement that the Board rely on de novo court proceedings for tit the imposition of civil penalties for violation of the Federal Aviation Act hampers the Board's ability to insure compliance with existing law and can result in both unnecessary duplication of effort and delay. abl sts pact and The legislation proposed by the Board would eliminate the need for a de novo district court penalty suit and would enable the Board to relate the amount of the penalty directly to the nature and circumstances of the violation. In many instances, this would reduce total litigation expenses to both the Government and private parties while preserving the safeguards of justice through the reviewability of Board decisions in the 

U.S. courts of appeals. 

572-410 O 75-7 87

The administration of the Federal Aviation Act, and the Board's regulations thereunder, are uniquely suitable to the administrative (rather than judicial) imposition of civil penalties. The eventual compromise settlements in Board cases has ranged from a low of $500 to a high of $101,000, and more than half of all claims involved a compromise settlement of $5,500 or less. Claims of this general size should not burden the already strained resources of the U.S. attorneys and the 

Federal district courts. Finally, almost all cases involve respondents who routinely come within the Board's regulatory jurisdiction (18 of the 23 respondents who were parties to a civil penalty compromise in fiscal 

1973 were direct air carriers, and others were indirect carriers), are fully familiar with, and are often the beneficiary of, the Board's processes. 

The legislation proposed by the Board would empower it, upon notice and hearing, to order the payment of civil penalties in accordance with the amount now fixed by statute. The legislation would specify that a 

Board order assessing a penalty would be subject to review only by the U.S. courts of appeals. It also would empower the Board to assess civi penalties in controverted cases. The Board also would retain its existing right to issue orders which include a civil penalty as part of a settlemen or other consent disposition of a proceeding. In either case, failure to pay a Board-ordered penalty would subject the person involved to an action in a U.S. district court for recovery of amounts owed to the 

United States.

The Federal Aviation Act of 1958 empowered the Civil Aeronautics 

Board to compromise civil penalties for violation of title VII of the Act (aircraft accident investigation). This function was transferred to the 

Secretary of Transportation by section 6(d) of the Department of 

Transportation Act, and retransferred by the same section to the 

National Transportation Safety Board. The proposed legislation would reflect this change.

Legislation that would have given effect to the recommendations of the Board was submitted to the Congress on August 8, 1974, but it was not introduced.

It is believed that the legislation would not involve any increase or decrease in appropriations. 

2. CAB Court Representation 

The judicial review provisions of the Federal Aviation Act do not expressly provide that the Board may participate in court proceedings through its own counsel as a matter of right. The Board believes that as an independent regulatory agency it should have the same right that other comparable agencies have to appear and be represented as a separate party in court proceedings involving review of Board orders. 

The CAB believes it essential, as recommended by the House Oversight Committee, that the Board be able to make its position known in addition to that of the Department of Justice. Such a procedure would allow the Board properly to carry out its functions and responsibilities, and at the same time permit the Department of Justice to make known to the court any policy considerations which it deems desirable or necessary in the public interest. 88

Notice of Tariff Changes 

Legislation is needed which would amend the Federal Aviation Act to quire a carrier to give at least 45 days' notice of any proposed tariff hange. The Board also would amend the Act to provide that, in the vent that it decides to suspend a proposed tariff for interstate or verseas air transportation, the Board will have to do so at least 15 days rior to the time the tariff would otherwise go into effect. 

If the Act were amended as proposed, the Board would continue to ave only 30 days within which to evaluate complaints requesting uspension of tariffs in interstate or overseas air transportation, since Bhe Board's suspension power would end 15 days before the expiration of the 45-day notice period. Both the air traveler or shipper and the carrier would benefit from this amendment. Air transportation users would benefit, since there would be greater certainty that the price which travelers paid for their tickets or which the shippers expected to card pay for the transportation of their cargo would still be the price at which the transportation would be provided.
