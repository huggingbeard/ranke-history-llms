YOU CAN WRITE 

Have you ever read advertisements which stated: "YOU, TOO, CAN ———————”? All that was needed was to insert whatever subject you wanted to learn-and presto-the job was completed. Perhaps you laughed at those advertisements? 

Funny or not, the fact remains that YOU CAN 

WRITE. If you will consider the successful writers you'll find that for every "born" writer there are dozens of "made" writers.

I have seen old men, young women-in fact people of all ages set out with this or that magazine as a goal and succeed. When they learned the slant of a publication they hammered at that publication until they managed to hit. It was not a matter of luck, it was not a matter of being a "born" writer. 

Look at yourself today. Why aren't you a writer? 

Can you give one good reason why you are not selling? 

The only reason you are not selling may be that you are not writing. You must first make your product before you can hope to find a market. Unless you produce daily you have a very slim chance of ever becoming a successful writer. The person who succeeds as a writer usually does so because he works hard.

YOU CAN WRITE... if you want to work. 

Courses can do you little good if you don't apply the knowledge you have gained. Wanting to write is not enough. You must have the courage and perseverance to work hard. The only way to learn to write is to write.

But, if you decide NOW to do something about this desire to write-if you really buckle down to hard work-you can write-and write to sell within the next three years. Breaks? Luck? Forget that. The only thing that will make you succeed as a writer is conscious application of story technique-plus a lot of hard work. 

There is no reason on earth why you can't sell. 

Show me an unsuccessful writer and I'll show you a person who has not applied conscious technique and who has not worked hard at this job of writing. 

Nine out of every ten persons who read this will not write more than 50,000 words a year. Yet, these people consider themselves writers. Despite all legal definitions, no person can consider himself a writer until he is paid for his material appearing in print. 

It is a simple matter to boost your average at least 

50 per cent this year. An extra fifteen minutes a day will mean at least 2000 words more each week. It means a novelette every five weeks-at just 15 minutes additional working time each day.

If you will work hard this year-produce a volume

You Can Write 

11 of material-I'll promise that you'll be selling at the end of the year.

The average person taking a course in writing is interested, primarily, in the financial return. Few people consider writing merely as an art form-they all hope to make some money from their writings. 

This is not an academic course in writing for those interested in the "arty" side of writing. It is an intensive course in fiction technique designed to show you the short way to writing. You will find no flowery passages in this course everything is terse, direct.

This course has not been prepared with the idea that it would automatically endow those who take it with the ability to write. In each person lies the ability to create something. It is the task of the instructor to find this ability and develop it. It may be a latent talent, awaiting someone to bring it to full life. That is the purpose to which we have set ourselves—the development of this latent ability so that the student may write to sell.

This is an age of specialization. Let us specialize on the short story. By concentrated effort directed along one line, we can hope to achieve better results. This course will give you results. I know, for many, many writers, trained by me, are selling their output every day.

LESSON I THE SHORT STORY FORMULA 

Unless you know what you are studying, there is no use in studying it, so let us first decide what it is we are studying. The short story is usually looked upon as a fictitious narrative, dealing with a dominant situation, starting at a given point and proceeding swiftly and dramatically to the logical conclusion without irrelevant interruption.

The short story is built about a dramatic plot. This sets it apart from the character sketch and the essay. 

The short story is unified to produce but a single effect. No matter which type of story you writelove, adventure, detective-it is developed by the characters in the story.

The beginning of the story must be definite. The characters should be identified as soon as possible. A clear, concise picture of the setting must be given. 

The reader should receive some hint as to what may develop in the story. The present conditions of the characters as the story opens must also be given to the reader.

Words, phrases, and incidents which are not essen- 12

The Short Story Formula 

13 tial to the story must be eliminated. Descriptions of setting and character, when overwritten, narrative discussion of plot, and all melodramatic material must be deleted from the story. Be forceful, dramatic. Get into the story as soon as possible and keep the reader interested in spite of himself.

The entire story should devote itself to the development of a single theme. The theme may be any one of the accepted axioms, proverbs, etc., which are in daily use. The theme serves the purpose of teaching a lesson to the reader but never lets the reader know, consciously, that he is being taught that lesson. 

Every story has five essential qualities. Unless you include all of them you have not written a story. A story deals with one main incident and tries to produce but one impression or effect. 

In addition to the single impression you attempt to create, you must have a clear, well-defined plot. A dominant character should be involved in the plot because of one dominant incident. The complication resulting and its solution will make your story. 

When you start your story, start either with a character, or some action. The best method is to start with a character in action, but this is not always possible. 

Search for a situation that suggests dramatic values. 

Place the character in this situation and let him react. 

Be certain that you have some setting, for to have a story without a setting would be like having a city without buildings.

It is better to suggest certain things than to make bald statements. For example, don't state baldly that the girl is five feet tall; show by comparison that she is short. You will create a more lasting impression if you use this method of writing. 

When you start the story, you bring up questions in the reader's mind as to the outcome of the story. 

You must be certain that every question raised in the beginning is answered in the end.

The beginning should indicate the setting, suggest the characters, and strike the tone of the story. These three factors will suggest the situation. The beginning may be one of two types. The beginning may be Narrative, and tell the story in expository fashion; or it may be Dramatic and describe dynamically all the events that are taking place. These are the two main types-there are countless variations in starting the story, but these are mainly little "tricks" of writing.

For example, there are beginnings which are also used as endings. This is the De Capo ending, so called because the ending goes back to the beginning and uses practically the same words.

The chief problem confronting the writer when he starts the story is to obtain Suspense. Suspense works through curiosity, anxiety, or apprehension.

The INCIDENT has been no little source of worry for writers. Too few of them know that the incident can be created mechanically, through a definite formula.

The Short Story Formula 

15 The Incident may be pictured as a mountain peak. 

At the foot of the mountain on one side, we have the Impulse. At the peak we have Climax, at the opposite foot we have Denouement. But, there are various steps to be followed in every incident created. 

First, start with the Impulse. On the way up the mountain, we have a series of steps through various difficulties. Then we reach the peak, or climax. At this point we give the reader a rest. But, remember, the incident is not yet completed with the climax. We must now descend the mountain on the other side. 

These are the steps in downward action, a trifle easier than the climb, but still as tricky. And then we are at the foot of the mountain, on the other side, with the Denouement. The point from the foot of the mountain to the peak is the struggle! Your story is a series of these incidents-a series of small mountains all building up into one big mountain. Reduced to formula, we have: 

1. The Impulse. 

2. Steps through difficulties (struggle). 

3. Climax (end of struggle with partial solution). 

4. Downward action (clearing up the questions). 

5. Denouement (the end of the incident). 

No incident is ever complete in itself. Each one demands an additional one, and each builds up into one big climax. In other words, a story is a series of small incidents building up to one big incident.

But, no story can ever be a true story without char-

acters. Too many writers fret about Plot and Slant when they cannot master the art of characterization. 

The most difficult problem facing the new writer may be voiced by the universal plea: "Where can I find story material?" The sooner the writer forgets his search for original material, the sooner he will begin to use the material already at his fingertips. It is very doubtful if you can find original, new material. All you should hope to do is to give a new treatment to old material. It is not the originality of situation or ideas that makes writers famous-it is the new treatment given to old ideas that makes them seem original. 

The problem confronting the writer, then, is not the discovery of new material, but rather, how to select the material.

There is more material available than any one writer can ever use. Any writer with an alert mind can be trained to look for material which lies about him. 

The greatest source book available to the writer is the Bible. In it are found love, adventure, and mystery-story ideas galore. Philosophical books also furnish excellent material, as do historical volumes. 

One must not take portions of these books and put them on paper. The task is to read and digest the volumes; then, with mind relaxed and senses attuned, the writer begins to imagine certain story ideas which are based on the material he has read. 

The newspapers furnish a fertile field for the gathering of story material. One little idea will usually

The Short Story Formula 

17 start thoughts that will develop into a salable short story. All a story source is intended to do is to furnish the inspiration.

To be a truly successful author, one must have the ability to feel sorry for his fellow man. This depth of feeling should enable you to feel and experience the problems of your neighbors. This doesn't mean "sob sister" sympathy-it means the real thing that reaches deep down into the soul and makes it hurt. 

Speaking from a strictly commercial standpoint, the writer who wants to succeed must give the public what it wants. The public wants to be entertained. 

The love story is the most popular type of story you can write. The magazines with the largest circulation (and which pay the highest rates) usually feature the love story. More motion pictures are made from love stories than from any other type of story. 

The love story is universal in appeal. The love theme may be any of a host of types-mother love, father love, brother or sister love, child love, love of country, sweetheart love. Take your choice and write the story. 

If you are to succeed you must work with this goal in mind to get your story into print. Unless you get your story into print and get paid for it, you will never be an "arrived" author.

LESSON II RESEARCH AND REPORTING 

There is an old saw to the effect that those who cannot write-teach. It is also said that those who know absolutely nothing about any given subject usually try to write about that subject. In both cases, however, it is soon apparent that those who teach must know everything about writing-plus the actual ability to write and sell—and that any person who is to write successfully about any subject must make a careful study of it and understand all points dealing with that particular subject.

It would be foolish not to write about any subject merely because one knew nothing about it. A bit of research and study will very often furnish the knowledge needed. If you want to write a story about 

Africa-you need not go to Africa-you may go by proxy. A study of reference books will soon give you a thorough knowledge of any locale or subject. Don't try to bluff through a subject-it can't be done. The reference room of the library should be your branch office if you are to write of things unknown to you.

As a writer, you must remember that all stories are 19

published because they have some special appeal. It is the editor's job to guess, and guess correctly, what will appeal to the readers of his magazine. He must satisfy the majority of his readers or lose his job. 

The writer who wishes to succeed must, therefore, give the editor something he can pass on to his readers. 

The only possible way you can give him what he wants is by reading his magazine. And that is the really hard task of the writer. It takes a lot of work to read, digest, classify, and SLANT for a particular market. 

At first, you may not know which type of story you do best. If you don't know, examine the stories you have written. If you have written more love than adventure, stick to love stories. Or, find out what you like to read, then try that type of story.

The successful author is also a reader. He reads the classics, the old master writers. From them, he gets his ideas about style.

Fictioneering is hard work. Once in a great while, an author sells the first thing he writes. He basks about in glory for weeks and months. Then, when he tries to repeat, he gets rejection slips.

In each of us, there is one big story struggling for utterance. It was this big story that the one-shot author wrote. He lived with this story, he mulled over it for a long time before he put a word on paper. 

He lived the story over and over again, changing, adding, cutting. But, with one story, he shot his bolt.

Research and Reporting 

21 He forgot the cardinal fact that one must work if he is to succeed at writing.

The writer is merely a reporter. He sees things and reports them to his notebook to be used later on when he writes. Newspapermen usually make good writers of short stories because they have been trained to observe details and to cull the good from the bad.

Any person can become a good reporter and become as efficient as the newspaperman. Be methodical in everything you do. Report happenings of interest in your notebook. You must be plodding and painstaking. Your collection of reported items may have quite a bit of useless material at first, but soon you will learn to discard items that would not help the plot factory.

A writer usually thinks in pictures. He visualizes the big scenes, knows exactly how they will look on paper before he puts down even a single word. Some writers even picture the illustrations to be used in the story and then write with those illustrations in mind. 

It is a good idea to write in this illustrative way. Write in pictures and you'll present a much clearer view of your material to the reader. Writing in this way, you will make your story more vivid and realistic. 

Practically everyone has day-dreams. Day-dreams are merely the pictures of an untrained mind. The selling writer seldom day-dreams. His mental pictures are for one purpose-to put them down on paper as a short story.

The student author must turn his day-dreams into something concrete. He must be the master of his brain and turn all imaginings into a specific channel. 

Only in this way can the struggling young author succeed. Unless you keep a close check on your imagination, it will run wild. The mind improves with use. 

The more you practice to keep your imagination in check, the more easily will story pictures form in your mind.

After a few weeks of keeping your imagination in check, you will be able to forget all about the outside world and concentrate upon the story problem. 

As a student you must work a certain period each day on this craft of writing. After you have done a day's work at the office, do your little stint of writing. 

Don't give up your job because you sell one story. 

You can write much better on a full stomach and a steady income. Concentrate on some objective and work toward it.

LESSON III VISUALIZING THE STORY 

You may have heard a lot about visualizationperhaps you haven't. Visualization merely means picturing the entire story in the mind. 

Of course, the untrained mind cannot visualize a story. If you haven't trained your mind to visualize, then you must do certain simple exercises to train your mind to obey you and concentrate on the story. 

If outside noises disturb you, then you will have to seek seclusion to practice visualization.

The first step in learning to visualize is to try to visualize the rooms you are now occupying. Locate the furniture, the wall coverings, the floor, the lights. 

Try to recall everything in the room without looking up from this page. Write it down on a sheet of paper. 

This done, go back and check to see if you have omitted any details. If so, you have not visualized the scene.

The next step is to visualize the characters. Suppose some people walk into the room. Visualize these people as you have visualized the room. Note the reactions of those who have come into the room. Do 23

they stare at you? Note that no two people react alike. See if you can grasp something of their character from the way they act. Let more people come into the room-let us say some friends drop in while you read this. Note their reactions. 

Now take three of these "characters"-three that stand out from the rest of the group by reason of something outstanding.

Now you have everything needed for the starting of the story-all except one thing. All you need do at this stage is to start something-just the slightest action-and you'll have a story unfolding. 

Visualize the window breaking, something falling. 

How would the people in your visualized scene react?

Their reactions will make the story. 

So far, we have visualized the room, the characters, and the first stages of characterization through action. 

Let us proceed one step further and visualize why these characters have come into the room. Perhaps a word or two from one of the characters will place them all. Let some character cry out-how will the cry be answered? Again, how the cry is answered will make the story. Visualize the dialogue. Very often you will have to call your notebook into play as you visualize dialogue. Jot down the scraps as you think of them. 

At first you will have difficulty in keeping your mind on the room—it may stray to the out-of-doors

Visualizing the Story 

25 or to another room. Keep the mind in check and force it to concentrate.

After you have learned to visualize in parts, then you can learn to visualize them all together. However, when you try to visualize the complete story, don't let your mind wander to the book at your side or to the conversation on the radio. Visualization and imagination are absolutely useless unless you can control them.

When the writer visualizes or imagines certain things, he must do so with one aim in view-that of expressing his imaginings. The author attempts to communicate his imaginings to others through the use of the printed page.

After you have learned to visualize familiar scenes, try to visualize the things that are far away from you. Try to visualize your early days in school or during vacation. Go to the library and read travel books on various countries. Read them, then try to visualize the country. Only through careful visualization can you obtain lifelike stories. Most good stories are carefully planned bits of visualization that grow step by step. 

Don't be discouraged if you fail in your first attempts at visualizing. You can learn only by long and arduous work. You should try to visualize only when your brain is clear. Never try it when the mind is clogged with other matters. You can't clutter up the

mind with two subjects at once and still visualize a clear story.

Stories are the result of imagination plus certain fundamental laws of writing. Imagination in itself does not create anything. Imagination is merely a method of selecting and assembling material that is ready to be used.

The writer is controlled by limitations similar to those which hold the architect in check. You can't create a new emotion any more than the architect can defy the law of gravity.

All creative thought must follow a definite pattern. 

It is only by varying the method of following the old pattern that the writer seems to create something new. 

There are three stages in the development of every story. The story must first pass through the inspirational stage before it can pass on to the second stage of actual writing. The third stage in the completion of a good story is the analytical or critical stage. 

No contractor would ever attempt to build a house without blue prints or plans to guide him. No author should ever hope to write his story until he has it before him in his mind. He must have some plan upon which he can determine the course of the story. 

Plan your story carefully. Consider the idea, the plot structure, the characters. With these before you, consider the complete story before you attempt to put a word on paper. 

Bits of exciting action don't make a story-but

Visualizing the Story 

27 they do help a story to move faster. Action is of interest only when it helps the story. Action for action's sake is worse than useless.

LESSON IV BUILDING THE STORY 

The actual building of the story-even before the author writes a single line of manuscript-is one of the most, if not the most, important factors in writing. For, unless the groundwork has been laid very carefully, the chances are that the story will fall down through some weakness in construction.

Perhaps the easiest way to build a story is by the scene method. This method of building is used rather unconsciously by most writers. A little of the conscious application of this method would improve the majority of stories. While few writers ever stop to think about the scene method, it is a fact that practically all of them use the scene as a natural division of story interest.

If you are interested in the conscious application of the scene technique, you may profitably study several published plays. Plays are written entirely in the scene method-and stories, to be successful, should be written in the same manner. By this I do not mean that you should write a play-not at all. Merely follow the play construction in short story writing. 28

Building the Story 

29 Writing by the scene method is comparatively easy. 

There are but few rules to remember. The chief rule is to have unbroken action in each scene. It must be remembered that when a new situation is introduced, the scene should be changed. 

Examine the play-and you will note the following: 

1. A description of each scene is given. 

2. All stage property is located. 

3. Number of characters present is given.

FREE SER AND MEDIA 1982 M 

4. Setting is made definite. 

5. Time is made definite. 

6. Condition of characters is presented.

Practically all of the above is mechanical. It would be folly to expect the author of a short story to list at the start of his scene the number of chairs, people, etc. What the author should do, however, is to incorporate these six points in his narrative in such a way that the reader knows all of the above points but is not aware that the author has told him of them. 

A study of Shakespeare's plays will reveal the finer workings of the scene method. As each scene closes, the last lines usually rhyme and "tap back" to the original story problem. When a new scene is presented, there is a complete scene reset. It must be remembered that in Shakespeare's time, there was little stage furniture-most of the stage sets were verbal explanations made by the actors- thus Shakespeare approaches most closely the actual short story methods. 

As stated before, the scene must present unbroken 

---

action. The scene should end when some definite change is to be made to further the story. Scenes in the story should be important scenes only. Unimportant scenes are better omitted from the story since they merely slow up the reading. Some scenes can take place "off stage" and may be mentioned by one character as having previously happened. In this way, you cut down on your number of scenes and make the story less confusing to the reader. A confused reader means that you have slipped up somewhere along the line in this matter of scenes. 

In your scene work, just consider that you have a bare stage to be set and peopled. Do it in a swift, clear manner. Don't waste time with long-winded descriptions. State the setting, the time, the place, in swift, terse sentences. Then, when you revise the story, you can do your polishing to eliminate the mechanical effects you have used in the creation of the story. 

To keep your reader from being confused, it is well to keep "tapping back" to the original story problem. 

These "tap backs" serve to increase reader interest and keep him posted on what the author is doing with the story people. When a character leaves a scene, be certain that the reader knows this character has left. 

When a new character enters the scene, let the reader know that this character has arrived. It is the small details that lend reality to your story. 

The scene method helps you with the time and place elements. For example, a period of time from

To reset the scene and give the place element: IN

Very simple, isn't it? A little practice will make you most adept at the scene method. In fact, you will find that the scene method has been a part of your unconscious technique for some time-so why not deliberately plan your story by this method? 

There will be a decided improvement in your stories if you plan them this way.

LESSON V PLOT AND THEME 

If you have a great deal of trouble in planning your story, turn to the magazine for which you intend to write and study it carefully. Take two or three issues of the magazine; see how the stories start, how the characters are introduced, how they talk, and what they say. After you have studied the plans of the stories already published in that magazine, you may use those plans in writing your own stories. You may even copy one of the stories word for word to get the "feel" of it. However, do not attempt to sell the story you copy.

The author alone determines the stories he will write. In some cases, the "hack writer" may be forced to write stories on order. Some of these stories may be abhorred by the writer, yet since he is in the business for the money he can get out of it-he writes the story and collects. As a general rule, don't write the stories you "hate" to write. Yet, it IS good practice and will help you develop your story technique if you will do a few of these "hated" stories. It helps you improve your technique since it is something with 32

Plot and Theme 

33 which you detest working-and as a result, you must use your technique to the utmost to prepare the story. 

I cannot over-emphasize the fact that there is an over-abundance of plot material. The writer may become confused because of this abundance. Merely learn to control your imagination and you will have very little trouble in finding story material. 

Plot cannot be stressed too much. It is plot which distinguishes short stories and novels from other literary patterns. Plot has been the loup garou of all writers simply because they could not understand it. 

All a plot should ever hope to be is a framework upon which to hang the locale, the situations, the characters, and the incidents. You should use your plot in the same way that the architect uses steel-merely as framework to be covered by other materials. 

There are said to be only thirty-six different plot situations, and no matter how much you think, you cannot conceive a single plot that has never been used. 

However, don't give up writing because of this fact.

The writer must get new effects with old plots. 

Plots will seem new because of treatment because of content. Many authors have made a fortune by using the same plot time after time. The personality of the writer also will color his work. 

After you have visualized your story carefully, the rest is mechanical. not 

Plot is very often confused with Theme. Plot is the framework-the Theme is the soul of the story. It is

the subtle something that runs through every great story. Its use is either conscious or unconscious. 

Theme is usually the great underlying purpose of the story. Theme must be present in every story-no matter how slight.

Theme is not a part of the characterization or of the plot. Theme may be defined as the idea underlying the plot. Don't try to preach. Make the theme unobtrusive-like an underlying song in a motion picture. Never make a definite statement of your theme in the story. It is better to infer than to state openly.

There is nothing at all mysterious about plot. Far too many struggling writers stand in awe of it. Given a fairly strong plot, you'll have a fairly strong story. 

If your plot is weak, your story is weak. But, creating a good strong plot is fairly simple work once you understand the mechanics.

Plot is problem; the stronger the problem, the stronger the plot. Plot may be termed the struggle of the characters to better their position. Plots must be easily understood. A complicated plot usually means a complicated story.

On every side the writer is faced with a host of mechanical and semi-mechanical plot devices. Many of these plot aids are very good. They have a definite purpose-but they should not be expected to do the 

THINKING for the author. Most of these plot devices suggest incidents from which the author pro-

Plot and Theme 

35 ceeds to elaborate and write the story. If you feel that you cannot think of incidents, then buy one of these plot devices. The average new writer, however, is better off without them.

I am not condemning plot aids, I merely suggest that the author try to use his brain first; then if ideas fail to come, resort to various plot aids. 

Salable plots are almost always simple plots. The story of a boy and girl in love is always a salable yarn.

So, all you, as the author, need do to create salable plots is to take these simple ideas and twist and develop them into a plot.

The confusion of plot arises from the fact that authors forget that a plot may be expressed in a single sentence. They confuse the plot of a story with the synopsis of a story. Too, some of them confuse Plot with Theme. Theme is the intangible thread that weaves the bright pattern. Plot, on the other hand, furnishes the design of that pattern.

I have had writers come to me and tell me that they have no plots, that they can't think of a plot. When I tell them that plots are all around us, they laugh. But it is a fact that a plot can be created from anything and everything.

On the lecture platform, I have given exhibitions of what may be termed "speed writing." In these exhibitions, I usually write a short-short story in fifteen minutes. The plot is complete and simple; the story usually sells. This demonstration is seemingly

remarkable. It is and it isn't. I can start from scratch, with no tangible plot, and, from a suggestion, write a complete story. Any professional writer can do this and so can you.

Over a period of years in writing stories, I have become rather mechanical in my writing. I usually start my stories with nothing more than a title, often with nothing more than an editorial dead-line staring me in the face.

As a general rule, I start with two people talking. I let them talk for a while-and something usually happens. Or else, put two people together in a room; something happens to one of these people-the other person tries to get out-complication-plot. 

Plot is merely a series of complications. A character gets into trouble; he struggles to get out of trouble and gets into deeper trouble; at last, after a desperate struggle, the character comes out, victorious. This, in general, is the original basic plot formula. From it, you can work out countless plots. 

The first step in developing a plot is to get an idea. 

Let us suppose that the idea is that there is no such thing as love. We get a character, a young man. He is through with love. Then the female character develops the first idea to the crisis. And that is all you do-build up some definite crisis. Your story will be one crisis after another, each crisis working up to the climax.

The moment you have two people working toward

Plot and Theme 

37 the same goal, you are bound to have conflict. The moment you have conflict, you start to have plot.

If writers would spend half the time on characterization that they do on plot, there would be a host of better stories appearing in editorial offices. In my capacity as editor, I have read scripts that had corking good plots but were woefully weak on characterization. The writer must remember that it is the characters who make the story-not the plot. 

I suggest that you write the story without giving much thought to the plot. Concentrate on the character action. Give the character something to do and have a definite reason for everything he does. 

Too many writers work hard at their task of story writing, and, with the story finished, they suddenly discover that the ending seems flat. The chief reason for this flatness is the lack of a definite goal for the main character. After all, your character must be fighting for something, and if after all this fighting there is no reward, then there is no use for the story. 

In ninety-nine out of every hundred cases plot is an individual problem. Some writers can't get started, others can't stop. Some can't compilcate, others overcomplicate.

LESSON VI TYPES OF "FICTION VIEWPOINT" 

Have you ever picked up a story written by a friend or fellow student and, after reading it, wondered what the story was all about? Have you ever asked yourself: "Where in the world did that character go? Who is telling this story?" If you have, then you will grasp the importance of Viewpoint.

After the story material has been gathered the author is confronted with the problem of choosing the proper place to start his story. Then he must select one character from whose viewpoint the story is to be told.

It's all in the way you look at it. And that is usually the answer to any problem. How many times have you heard someone say: "Well, put yourself in his shoes"? That is the trick of Viewpoint. You merely put yourself in one character's shoes. Viewpoint is merely the relation the author takes with his story. 

You'll find as you go along in this writing business that writing can be as hard or as easy as you make it. If you are looking for an easy way to write-forget it. 

There is no easy way, there are no short cuts. There 38

Types of "Fiction Viewpoint" 

39 will ever be writers who find writing hard, others who find it very easy. It is up to you, the author, to decide if it is to be hard or easy. And a great deal of this problem can be settled in Viewpoint. 

As a general rule, most would-be authors try to tell the story from every possible viewpoint. They assume that every story person should have a chance to tell the story. As a result, there is a mixed up yarn which is almost impossible to follow.

Viewpoint should be considered immediately after the plot has been planned and the characters selected. 

Select the character who will tell or carry the storyand that will be the viewpoint of your story. Choose the best possible viewpoint at the beginning. Don't change the viewpoint in the middle of the story just because you find you have given the story burden to the wrong character.

The story may be told by the Omniscient or allseeing author or by the Main Character. These two viewpoints are the most used. If you choose the Main 

Character viewpoint, you may have the action and story viewed either through the author, through the main character himself, through another character (this method is not very good since interest is then divided between the main character and the minor character), or it may be viewed objectively through some observer. In every case, however, the author will be telling the story of the Main Character. 

The disadvantage of this Main Character viewpoint

is that it is limited. You can tell only what the Main 

Character sees, hears, does, and says. It does, however, present the main character much more clearly than any other method. It gives an eye-witness account of the story and the reader can identify himself much easier with the main character.

Confession stories are main character stories told in the first person. They may also be Author Viewpoint stories-but not Omniscient Author. Since the first person story is so very easy to write, the author may become careless and become Omniscient. 

The Subjective viewpoint will show the inner thoughts, the Objective viewpoint will present a more limited viewpoint in that the author may use only the five senses of the character. Don't switch from the 

Objective to the Subjective in the same story. It has been done, yes, but it is not the best method. Stick to the single viewpoint throughout the story. 

In the objective viewpoint method, you make the characters act before the readers. It puts the characters on the same level as the reader. The reader sees things only as they happen before him, as observer. It presents the story action exactly as though he had been on the scene himself. The reader learns about the story just as he would learn it in real life through the use of his five senses, and he has a restricted viewpoint. The objective viewpoint presents action. As a result of this action, the story is speeded up. This fast action permits the fast changing of various scenes as

Types of "Fiction Viewpoint" 

41 the character travels from one story scene to another. 

The entire effect is of physical action: feelings are transmitted only by showing the outward evidences of feelings.

Inner thoughts are portrayed from the speech and actions of the character. The author may limit what the reader knows or sees by limiting the character's knowledge.

Complicated plots usually demand the omniscient author to advise and interpret. In the omniscient author method, the author may tell as much as he wishes his readers to know.

The viewpoint most used by the amateur author is that of the observer or other minor character. The minor character brings the reader as close to the story as he may come without coming in contact with the main character. This method of telling the story brings the reader right into the story, but it splits the reader's interest between these minor characters and the main character. You will find this method most used in the "frame" type of story. In this latter type, the story is usually told after it has happened. This is not considered a very good way to tell the story. 

If you have trouble in keeping the single viewpoint, use a very simple trick. Decide which character will tell the story, then write the story in the first person. 

With the story completely written in the first person, rewrite it in the third person. The single viewpoint is

thus maintained provided you have stuck to the “I” viewpoint in the original story. 

There is no "best" viewpoint. The story material makes its own demand. It is up to the author to decide which character will carry the burden of the story.

Some stories are written with a split viewpoint. A part of the story is told from the viewpoint of one character, another part is told from the viewpoint of another character. This method is very difficult to master effectively. You will find that the reason for this method is to increase the effect made by the main character. What really happens is that the main character is left in one scene while some other character is shown in another scene making preparations for the coming of the main character into that scene. 

Interest is increased because the reader wonders what the main character's reaction will be in this new scene. Remember that when you split the viewpoint in this way, you must bring the main character back into the story by leading him into the scene prepared by the other character.

LESSON VII YOUR "STYLE" 

Practically every author, or would-be author, labors along trying to "acquire" a style. Style is merely a distinctive or characteristic method of presenting the story to the reader. This quality of style differentiates one author from the others.

Style is the individual. It is your special way of telling a story. If you try to imitate other styles, your own suffers. If you would have a distinctive style of your own, you must be careful in your presentation of story material to the reader. 

Your words and sentences must be carefully chosen. 

They must express your thoughts forcibly. The reader must be able to see the picture you have in mind and feel your own emotions. A style must be uniform, it may not shift from the commonplace to the florid. It must be regular in that it keeps time with the story. 

Style must also be precise and balanced. If you guard these four qualities carefully, you will find that you have a distinctive style. Above all, write all your stories in the same style. Have a precise selection of 44

Your "Style" 45 words and phrases that convey the exact shade of meaning.

The only way you can cultivate your style is by practice. 

Many a now-famous author developed his style while serving his apprenticeship in the "pulps." These magazines have trained more writers than has any course in writing. Therefore, I instruct you now to write for the pulps-forget the "slicks" until you get a style that is suited to them. There is no reason why you cannot earn as you learn-and you certainly can earn and learn in the pulps.

Style may vary with the type of story written. 

Since style often varies with the narrative, we must then study the various important types of narrative. 

When writing in the third-person narrative the author is usually omniscient. Words and phrases may sparkle and shine and thus represent style at its best. 

Your style will determine whether or not your work is good literature or merely an example of the mechanics of writing.

A complicated style is not always a brilliant one. 

The simpler the style the better. A sentence that expresses its meaning clearly is much more effective than the ambiguous statement. Never leave a doubt in the reader's mind about anything. Don't be content with merely writing a story. Without style a story has little literary merit.

Style lies within the author. Read the classics and

contemporary fiction to study and imitate the styles. 

But, don't carry the imitation too far. Imitate for practice, but when you write your own material, do not become an imitator. Your own style must flow smoothly and the story must be read easily. If this is not true, then you have failed in your task. 

The dialogue of your characters may reflect your style or you may show it in straight description. In narration, the author expresses directly. In dialogue, the author expresses himself through his characters. 

Style in the first-person narrative is usually simple. 

There are no figures of speech, no colorful description. First-person stories are usually told as though based upon fact, and so the story must be told simply to be effective. The author may not become omniscient. All he is permitted to do is record the things he does, sees, or hears. The only emotions to be recorded are his own. The only possible way he may describe the emotions of others is by the outward evidences of these emotions. A study of first-person stories will show this style very clearly.

The first-person is generally considered a poor medium for developing style. Since the narrator must live to finish the story there is often very little suspense as to the outcome. Everything, then, depends upon being tensely dramatic and must play solely on the emotions. It is usually told in a chronological sequence and is usually a bald account. 

Still another narrative type is the "action type."

Your "Style" 47 This type of story distinguishes itself by short, snappy sentences which put the story across dramatically. 

Every paragraph attempts to have some dramatic action culminating in some punch. Action is usually suggested rather than baldly presented. A good portion of the action is left to the imagination of the reader. This puts the reader directly into the story and makes it more interesting to him. 

Mere movement is not action, nor is melodrama.

Action usually makes the story tingle with life. This type of narrative takes the commonplace and lifts it to actuality.

Few figures of speech or similies are used in action writing. It is the straight, bald action with a sharp staccato style that puts the story across. 

Style makes the story. You may know all there is to know about technique, but if you have no distinguishing style, your story is merely a mechanical thing that clanks across the page.

Don't become style conscious, however. Style will come gradually, almost unconsciously. If you strive too much to get a "clever" style, you may lose whatever style is naturally yours. 

Don't imitate too much. If you do, you will find yourself hopelessly mired in some archaic style that has long since gone in the discard. Merely try to be clear and virile. See that your thoughts are carried to the reader in the shortest, clearest manner. Leave no

doubt in the reader's mind as to what you actually mean.

Your own simple style may be more effective than the high-flown style of some professional writer who may be on his last legs. Imitate, yes, but only for practice. After you have practiced, then apply your knowledge to your own literary efforts.

LESSON VIII STORY ATMOSPHERE 

Have you ever noticed in the stories you have read that there is a certain intangible something that permeates the story? That, because of this vague something, you feel the story mood?

This intangible element that makes the story seem more real, that makes the characters seem alive, that makes the setting appear to be some actual place that you have known, is atmosphere.

Atmosphere is created through description. The more skilled you are in description, the more easily will you be able to create a lifelike atmosphere.

Your description must not be lengthy. Prose that is crammed full of description usually gets nowhere. 

Your settings should be described in a few bold, decisive strokes. Every word counts, so make every word carry its share of the story load.

When you write your story, try to weave the setting into the narrative without halting the flow of the story. Whenever this is done, you will find you have a much stronger story.

Skill, of course, is needed in the weaving of narra- 49

tive and description. Only the bare essentials are left; excess material must be taken out entirely. Sentences must take the place of paragraphs, paragraphs take the place of pages. No paragraph should ever be solid description. If you feel that description is needed, combine it with dialogue or action.

For example, let us look at this paragraph of straight description, which is then condensed through dialogue: "Up the trail, a little to one side was an overhanging rock jutting over the precipice. Tom and Sam stood for a moment looking at the massive boulder. 

Tom looked ahead to see if he could find Bruce. There he was, standing by the overhanging rock. "There's 

Bruce,' said Tom."

The above paragraph was quoted from the script of a new writer. Note how much better effect was obtained when he changed the above to read: " "There's Bruce, by the jutting rock.' Tom made a half motion toward the top of the canyon." 

Now, let us see how we can combine description with action without the reader becoming aware of the fact: "Trefton stumbled blindly through the maze of thicket. The grey desert sand stung his burning eyes. 

Twice, he almost ran head on into Joshua trees. When he reached the desert hole, he tumbled into the brackish water and drank convulsively."

He is trying to set the mood and atmosphere so that you will enjoy the picture to the greatest extent. 

Atmosphere may be bold or delicate. Whatever it is, it must have a subjective effect on the reader. It should make him feel the mood of the story. 

By the use of contrasts you will be able to achieve a better mood, a more subtle atmosphere. You'll get a better effect by describing a sunny afternoon and a very shady graveyard. Lights and shadows, used skillfully, always determine the effects you will obtain. 

The contrast of light and dark will create the mood. 

Through the use of staccato words and sentences you will be able to create the feeling of impending action. Dreamy and slow moving words and phrases will create a soothing effect. An air of tense expectancy can be achieved by using terse sentences, tense situations and words.

The background should be in harmony with the story. Establish the story tone, then make the setting fit that tone. Retain your dramatic appeal by establishing the right atmosphere through the setting. Your mood should be reflected in the story. LESSON IX SETTING YOUR STORY 

Many otherwise salable stories are very often rejected by editors simply because the setting is not definite enough. Some authors start their stories in a fine burst of action that plunges the reader into the story, but, as far as setting is concerned, the action might be taking place on a tight-rope or in a mine.

This question of Setting is a difficult one to settle arbitrarily. It takes quite a bit of practice to be able to draw the fine line between too much setting and not enough.

Briefly, Setting should be complete enough to tell the reader clearly where the action is taking place, show him definitely that the setting has some bearing on the story problem, and show that the use of setting reflects the atmosphere of the story. Setting should never be used with a lavish hand. 

If Setting is too difficult for you, it will be well to consider setting merely as the stage upon which all your action takes place. Keep the setting as simple as your stage setting would be. This matter of setting is directly related to the scene method of writing. If 53

you will use the scene method of writing and take care not to be too lavish with the use of descriptions of sunsets and mountain peaks, I believe you will have very little trouble with the question of setting. 

It is the little things that lend verisimilitude to your setting. Pay attention to the finer points, such as dialect of locale, use of local similes, and topography of that setting. Such detail lends the air of reality to the story. If you are too verbose and intend to be very dramatic about your setting, if you make your characters move from one setting to another merely to create the impression that you have travelled a great deal, then you are not making the most of your setting to help you with the story. Everything in your narrative must work for the ultimate good of the story. 

Your setting must reflect the story problem to such an extent that the reader will not be conscious of where the story begins and the setting ends. 

In using description of settings, try to combine the description with action or conversation. If you give lengthy narrative descriptions of setting, the chances are that the reader will skim over those paragraphs to get at the meat of the story. In this case, the reader may miss a vital story point which you intend to get over to him. I suggest that you use small bits of description of setting scattered throughout the story. 

Keep on building the single setting to such a point that the reader actually believes that the story could happen at that particular place. You must picture

55 the setting so clearly to him that he cannot help seeing it as you wish him to see it.

Setting is very often closely related to the type of story you are writing. A story set in the north woods would, of necessity, deal with logging, mining, or fast out-of-doors adventure. A similar plot, with a setting in the library of a New York mansion, would produce a sophisticated story which might have very little fast action. 

Most adventure stories are purely stories of setting.

The western story is of this type. The action is determined primarily by the setting. A western story in any setting but the west would cease to be a western story. 

If you are to write fast action stories, you must be terse and forceful with your setting description. Most of the descriptions should be given in action and dialogue, and viewed through the eyes of the characters. The author should keep out of the setting as much as possible. The moment the author becomes a part of the story, the reader keeps his eye on the author to the exclusion of all else.

It is well to remember that a story is not just setting, characterization, plot, etc., but rather all these things combined into the single whole. When we attempt to take the various ingredients apart to see what makes the story click, we must remember that fact. The various ingredients make the story, just as the various ingredients make the cake. Take one out

and you spoil the entire finished creation. Leave the salt out of the cake and you destroy its taste; leave the setting out of a story and you likewise injure it vitally.

The reader must be certain at the story's opening just where it is taking place. If you can state definitely that you can see where the action is taking place, then your problem of setting has been solved. There is nothing very difficult about this problem of setting. 

A simple setting is always much more effective than a very elaborate one. If you have too much setting, it detracts from the characters and plot. Too much of any one ingredient will spoil the entire product, no matter what it is you are attempting to create.

Readers who would ordinarily not read a single line of setting, will gulp it down when they are given it in small doses. A line or two at a time, coated with action or dialogue, will make the reader swallow it and ask for more. And when your reader asks for more—it is the editor's task to see that the reader gets what he wants—and that is where you start writing on order -with a definite advance on every story before you write a single line. 

There are many "order writers" today who were once struggling young writers. They showed editors they could produce material—and as a result, they are not exactly worried about their next meal. 

You must master setting completely. You can't have action happening if you have no setting for it.

LESSON X "COLOR" AND DIALOGUE 

Let us now talk about the author. If the time comes when the author decides he is not in the mood for writing, what then?

Anyone writing on order must stay in the mood for writing—or starve. To be a successful author you must be able to turn out work day after day and week after week. There must be no let-up. 

Practically all successful authors are specialists.

They all specialize in one or more story types and make their living from them. If they get out of the mood, they merely read the magazines and in a very short time inspiration returns and they start writing.

The average selling author reads with a pencil in one hand. Some little sentence or incident in another's story may suggest a host of salable yarns.

Any writer who tells you that he does not read the stories of others is either lying or he is a fool. You must study what others are producing in order to equal or surpass their material.

It is good training to see how many types of stories you can write. But to make money from writing— 58

"Color" and Dialogue 59 specialize! So much for the author; let us go on to technique. 

Have you ever picked up a published story and wondered why you enjoyed it? Have you ever read two stories with characters of equal appeal and found that one story appealed to you while the other did not? The reason for this appeal may lie in color.

Stories that do not live usually lack color. Many writers are content to be correct. Correct writers are seldom colorful writers. A writer who is well educated may sacrifice color for correctness, and destroy the chances of popularity for his stories.

The correct writer looks for a word that will express his meaning correctly. The colorful writer looks for the word that will not only express his meaning but will also be pleasing to the reader. Color words give tones of expression and feeling. Color words are not words denoting certain colors. Color words paint word pictures. They can help you put power in your style.

To be published, your stories must have color and romance. Romance is not love interest. Romance means make-believe. Romance pictures things not as they are but as you want them to be. Some things are more easily made to appear romantic than others. 

The wilds of Africa are much more romantic than your own back yard.

When it comes to description, you must use color words to get your effects. A maximum of effect must

be put into a minimum of descriptive sentences. The trick in doing this lies in giving the description through short, colorful sentences in such a way that the reader doesn't realize he is getting it. Bald description will become second nature after a bit of practice. 

Description can be cultivated only by observation.

Observe the common things about you, then try to describe them.

Perhaps the major fault of the new writer is the over-description of settings. While details should be observed they must not be over emphasized. Unless the description will have something to do with the action, forget it. There is no need to mention the walls of a room unless those walls have something to do with the story. The reader's imagination will usually fill in the small details. The best way to give description is to give it as the impression of one of the characters. 

Since the character is usually identified with the reader, the reader gets the same impression. Description can also be given in dialogue, and the reader will see the setting through the words of one character. 

Straight description should be limited. You must be able to "see" the things you have described. If, afer reading your description, you cannot see the picture, you must revise until the picture becomes clear. 

You must remember that you are trying to create an impression. Description cannot be imagined or created. Description is only learned through observation. One of the best ways to describe is through

"Color" and Dialogue 61 action and dialogue. Either that or as the impression of a character.

As description gives life to settings, so does dialogue give life to your characters. 

Remember: unless your character speaks he is not alive! The sooner the character speaks, the sooner he becomes a live person. 

Readers unconsciously take part in your dialogue if it is real, but will see it as a part of the story if it is artificial. Full ninety per cent of the rejected stories are rejected because of faulty and unconvincing dialogue.

The new writer thinks that dialogue is much too difficult to learn; therefore he ignores it. But anyone who can set down the conversation of others on paper can master dialogue.

Your reader participates in the story through the dialogue. If the reader enjoys the dialogue he will look for stories by that same author.

Much of our life, while awake, is composed of dialogue. We think, yes; but we express ourselves in dialogue. Have you ever noticed that people who live alone for some time develop the habit of talking to themselves? Then, why shouldn't your characters talk if they are to be live people? Readers look for quotation marks. 

People cry, shout, scream, grunt. Thus, some of these common sounds should be uttered by your story

people. Since real people do it-your characters should do it. 

We are a curious race. If we hear a snatch of whispered conversation we strain our ears to listen. So, if your reader picks up a story and sees a lot of quotation marks, he becomes curious and reads on. 

Be careful of your dialogue. Very often, a writer will describe a character who could act in but one way and then permit the person to talk entirely out of character. In using dialogue, don't salt it with "he said" and "she said." The best way is to follow up conversation with action on the part of the character who has just spoken. For example: " "Shut up!' Brent slashed out at the girl." Action follows the speech and does away with synonyms for "said." To study dialogue, copy down the speeches of your friends. Copy the fragments you overhear on a street car or bus. 

Then, when you know how people talk, you can use dialogue correctly. Until you know how people talk, your characters will seem like puppets and not like live people.

While dialect is a great help in dialogue, it can be overdone. A simple dialect is much more effective than a complicated one. Don't use dialect to ridicule. 

All dialect should do is to add to characterization. 

Don't have many characters using dialect-too many will spoil the effect.

Dialect is usually carried by servants or minor characters. Observe how people use dialect when they

"Color" and Dialogue 63 talk. There is often only one dialect word in a sentence. Our everyday speech is tinged with colloquialisms.

The only certain way to see if both dialect and dialogue are sound is to read the story aloud. Very often, if it sounds harsh to the ears, it is because the dialogue is faulty. 

Don't neglect your dialogue-if you do, you'll be neglected by the editors.

LESSON XI CHARACTERIZATION 

Look at your newspaper-what strikes you first?

It is an even bet that the name of some person stands out. In the newspaper offices the City Editor will tell you that names make news. In other words, it isn't the story itself that is interesting-it is the people in the story. 

Suppose you pick up the paper some evening and discover that someone named Jane Doe has won first prize in a beauty contest. Naturally, you are interested since Jane Doe is a good looking young lady. 

Then, someone tells you that Jane Doe is a half-cousin whom you distinctly remember, now, as having been an ugly little girl with stringy hair and freckles.

Now, because you know this woman, the story in the paper has an added interest for you. Because you could, in some way, identify yourself with the winner, you had a story that was much more important to you. 

Your reader must be able to identify himself with some character in some definite way. Unless the reader can make this immediate identification there is little chance that the story will be strong enough to 64

Characterization 

65 hold the interest of the reader. Remember to get your reader vitally interested and immediately concerned about the welfare of the character you introduce to him. Nothing can be very dramatic unless it is of personal interest to the reader. Drama is based upon the personal interest of the reader over some character in your story. Remember that, if you are to write salable fiction.

You must remember that the people are always remembered to the exclusion of all else. Characters are easier to remember than are plots. Ask any one person what the World War was about-and he can't tell you. But ask him about the people in the warand he'll recite a string of names. Events are not remembered for themselves-they are remembered for the people taking part in them. Plot is merely a mechanical device at best. Characters make the story. Poor characterization will ruin any story plot. 

As an author, you must have your own convictions.

You must not think with the great common mass of humanity. You must be able to stand apart from the group and observe. Your thoughts and speech must be your own and not that of others. You must not become a group thinker. You must be an individualist. 

The most successful writers are those who express their own thoughts and convictions.

Your fiction characters are created by you, and so they must reflect your inner self. When you create a character who is too pure, you are creating a false

character. No one person is all good or all bad. The average person is a mixture of good and bad-so are your characters. That is what makes them seem to be human beings. Characters should be handled in such a way that they seem to reflect life as it is. Nothing in a story should ever seem artificial. 

Every time you write a dramatic story you write about people. You can't write a dramatic story without them. It is the clash of man against man or against some stronger force that makes your story. There can be no drama without man interest. 

Your characters will make or mar your story. Very often, good characters can carry the story even though the plot is weak. Characters come only from your own observations. Each character must be alive, breathing. Characters may be created through imagination whereby the people you have written about, the people you have met, or the people you have seen on the screen are grouped to form a single story character.

A word must be said about the two varieties of story people in every story. You have characters, and you have types. Types are used as background. Types are typical of a certain class. However, if you give one of these types some peculiarities or characteristics that make him stand out clearly in the story, he then ceases to become a type and is a character. A type usually sticks to type, but the moment he does something unexpected or not in keeping with the type of

67 person he happens to be—and which is done convincingly-presto, you have a character. 

If you will read published stories carefully, you will note that the characters you remember are the characters who are not very beautiful or commanding in statue. Usually, there is something about the character that is rather unbeautiful. These irregularities make your character stand out. Eliminate details of description in your main character and the reader will have an easier time of imagining himself to be that person. And, when a reader actually becomes a story person, you have made a sale.

Your characters must act like live people. Let them have faults. The lead character may have some weakness, the villain should have some good points about him. It is this contrast that makes the story interesting. Because your lead character has certain weaknesses, he is always in difficulty-and the longer your lead character is in trouble, the more your reader will want to read on. 

Try to choose normal people for your characters.

Morbid realism is a very fine thing, but it has no place in magazine romance and adventure. Keep out the cripples, the blind, the sick. Readers want to be entertained.

The best way to develop a character to the actual visual stage is to work him out on paper. Write down his appearance in detail, then his little mannerisms. 

Thus, you will have a clear picture of your story

person. By working your story out on paper first your reader will not see the mechanics of character construction. 

Most stories have a man, a woman (the sex angle), and an element of struggle. This is the old dramatic triangle. Consider the triangle as being three elements rather than people. Naturally, it is not essential that a woman be in a story-it may be man and man against an element of struggle. The main thing to remember is the triangle.

Never introduce a character into a story merely to act as a foil for the hero or heroine. A character should have a part in furthering the plot action or else be eliminated. Every character should be able to carry on the story by himself without losing reader interest. 

The average writer must know something of characterization. Many writers consider the art of characterization as a mechanical method. You put in so much of this, so much of that, stir, and presto-a character. Yet it is difficult to teach characterization. 

While various characterization methods may be outlined for you, the real character drawing comes from the author, from within.

The writer must be a psychologist as well as a writer. He must know the feelings of his neighbors and be able to analyse these same feelings. In this process he must not let his emotions sway better judgment. He must be as cold in his analysis as the doctor in his laboratory. He must be able to realize why his

69 characters react to a certain stimulus. Not only must he know why, but he must be able to explain the reason logically and plausibly to the reader. It is a safe premise to assume that character drawing is about the most important part of story writing. If you fail in the drawing of your characters your story may be ruined completely. There can be no set or definite rules about character drawing. Perhaps the most logical method would be to draw from life-this may solve all character problems. Most certainly it is easier to write of people you know than to write of total strangers.

An author may spend hours drawing a character before putting one bit of him in the story. He knows why the character will do a certain thing in a given instance. You must know your story people intimately. If you find that by making a biography you can know the character better-do the extra work. 

You will have to experiment to find YOUR best way of creating the character. Creating, of course, refers only to the process of putting the character on paper. 

You can't invent a character. Characters are people you have met. A little bit of the man next door, a little bit of yourself, a little bit of half a dozen other people those are your characters. Characters are the result of your own observations. Characters-not puppets. Imagined story people cannot be termed characters. A character is not a character unless he lives and you can't make an imagined person live!

So, observe real people if you are to draw true characters.

Perhaps the most used method of introducing characters is to do it through a straight description of the character. This method is used to good advantage when minor characters are to be introduced quickly and then put aside. This simple method gives a quicker impression and thus helps save words when space is needed for story development. Thus: Sarah Allen was a thin old woman, gives you the picture in straight description. You have introduced the character to the reader and given the reader enough description upon which he can build the character to suit his own tastes. Very often, when the author must tell the reader something about what has happened to the character before the story opened, this descriptive method is combined with the expository method to gain the effect needed. Scatter your character descriptions throughout the story. Don't attempt to give the complete picture all at once. Break up straight description with story action and you'll find that the idea not only goes over better but will please the reader who hates to wade through a mass of dull straight description. 

Very often, you can show your reader a great deal about any certain character by giving a graphic description of his surroundings. While this method may be used alone, it is used to better effect when it is combined with some other descriptive method. People

The action method of presenting characters has become more popular of late. In this method we show the character through his habits, and his little everyday acts. In this way we speed up the story and create a live character since the character is in immediate action. This method is very useful in presenting the story crisis since the emotional load of the story is just that much stronger because of the action. It can be seen rather quickly that the following bit of character in action presents a much better picture to the reader than would straight description: Sam Doe bunched his shoulders as he ducked into the room. His single lock of dark hair fell, Napoleonlike, into his eyes. He slouched over to the desk, the small lines about his eyes ghastly white in his brown face.

Very often you may portray a character through his conversation or through the conversations of others. A man's talk will show much more than his schooling, it also shows his breeding, and his whole life. This method gives, not only an insight as to the character, but can also give some hint as to the story action.

Sometimes, the author may feel that the reader will not quite catch the meaning of a certain action on the part of the character. Then, it becomes necessary for the author to interpret the meaning for the reader. If it is a speech or an act on the part of the character then the author tells the reader, unobtrusively, just what is meant by such an act or speech. In this method the author must be most careful not to intrude too openly into the story, hence it is a most difficult method for the beginning author to attempt. Too, the author may become the omniscient or allseeing author and go into the minds of his characters and report what he finds there. In using this method the author presents to the reader just what the character thinks and what the character feels. If you want to have a tense moment in a story, then use this method. It does slow up the action somewhat but this has its compensation in heightened interest. 

Remember that your characters must stay in character at all times. When you present a character who can act in only one way don't try to have him change his character just because the story material makes that demand. No character may change his character unless this has been previoulsy prepared for by something mentioned earlier in the story. Don't make any character change without planting the possibility of a change in the reader's mind. 

You may feel better when you know that this problem of characterization is the same for the unarrived author as it is for the selling author. LESSON XII STARTING YOUR STORY 

For eleven lessons we have dealt with the ingredients of the story. We are now ready to take all that we have learned and combine it into a story. The big problem that now confronts the writer is the opening of the story. The average writer has more trouble with the opening of his story than with anything else. 

Some writers claim that the story should open as close to the end as possible. Others write action style. 

In this manner, the author plunges into action at the beginning of the story and continues with this action in chronological order. While this is a very nice way to start, all stories cannot be written in this way. 

We can, however, agree upon one thing-the opening paragraph or paragraphs should at least strike the theme of the story and strike a bit of plot action. 

Fast-moving, interesting openings are required of most stories. Stories with leisurely openings are seldom purchased by discriminating editors. No longer are long-winded descriptions and conversations wanted. To take the place of these last two, we have 75

an opening that literally snaps at the reader. Everything moves swiftly as the story opens.

Your first paragraphs are your sample case. It is a solid fact that a good opening will go a long way toward selling the story. The first paragraphs should not give away too much of the story, they should give just enough to urge the reader to continue reading.

In effect, you promise to give the reader somethingthe promise is given in the first paragraph-but you don't fulfill the promise until you reach the last paragraph. 

Don't start with descriptions of mountain peaks and sunsets. Don't explain to the reader what the story will or will not be-that is what the reader must discover for himself. Start with action-either actual or implied. Don't generalize. You must be able to arouse reader interest in the first paragraph. 

But, you ask, suppose we must tell the reader something that has happened before the story opens-what then? 

If what has happened before is fast or interesting enough, start your story there. You can give an insight as to what has happened before and what conditions are at present by the opening of your story. Or you may intrude on the action, and become philosophical. 

Don't carry this beyond a paragraph or you'll lose reader interest. 

Characters may be introduced in your opening paragraph-if they are interesting enough to make

77 the reader want to read on. Characterization through action is a very good method. Practically any magazine will consider a story starting in this way. A keen insight as to the character is given in such an opening. After all—your character makes the story. 

Your story may start with a minor incident which must be a part of your plot idea. A series of these incidents makes your plot. The incident must lead up to some action of great dramatic importance. Select for your opening incident one which will further the action of the story.

It is the consequences arising from the incident that make the story. Placed in combination with other incidents, it soon becomes dramatic. If you can remove any incident without destroying the story idea -remove it-for such an incident only holds the story back. 

Coincidence that bugbear of writers-may be used only at the beginning of the story-as long as it starts action. No coincidence should be used after the first two or three paragraphs. Coincidence should never be used in the development of the story. No element of chance should ever be used. No coincidence should ever help the character out of a tight situation.

Soon after your story opens, some complications should ensue. Unless complications present themselves immediately, the story is weakened. After a character has been introduced there should be some

complication. If a male character is walking down the street, something should happen to him that would cause a pretty girl to come to his aid. A complication will build up suspense-suspense will cause the reader to anticipate what will happen. The moment the reader anticipates the ending, the writer has him at his mercy, for the ending is usually something entirely different.

Very often a lapse of time must be allowed in a story. This must not be overdone. While a period of years may elapse in a novel, such a time lapse is not permitted in a short story. A check on published stories will show you that the length of time is seldom over a few hours.

In observing the unity of place, bear in mind not to move your characters about more than is absolutely necessary.

To preserve the unity of action, tell the story from beginning to end with one single line of story interest. 

Your story must move steadily without any backward or sideward steps. With the story finished, be certain that all loose ends have been caught up. Determine the way your story is to begin, arrange your action chronologically, and do not stray from your path. And, above all things, be coherent.

Your stories must be packed with drama. There must be plenty of action-and the reader must see the things as they happen! The average reader would prefer to see something happen than to read about it. 

If you write dramatically, the chances are that the reader will see things as they happen.

Narrative stories usually deal with one character in an incident or with a series of events. This narrative story becomes a dramatic story when the character becomes involved in a struggle with some other character. To be in a struggle, put the character in some dramatic position. LESSON XIII CONFLICT, CRISIS AND SUSPENSE 

If you want to attract attention, start a fight. A dog fight or a fight between two small boys will draw anyone. People pay hard-earned money to see two men slug each other. Everyone is interested in a fight. 

And so, to enhance the entertainment value of your story, you must have a fight. But, your fight is

CONFLICT. 

Physical conflict and mental conflict have the same interest in a story. A mere fight is not conflict. But, a fight for some ideal is conflict. We must have two persons or beings striving for the same goal. When one force tries to overcome the other, we have conflict. 

There must be some good and definite reason why the characters want to achieve their goal. When there is conflict there must be an objective. Conflict is the result of opposition to purpose. The greater the obstacle in the way of the characters, the greater the conflict. The object of conquest must be a worthy one. It must be something the reader would want. 

Conflict may be pointed up through the use of Contrast. There can be little drama without Contrast. 80

Conflict, Crisis and Suspense 81 It is contrast that offers the lights and shadows and heightens the struggle. The weak conflict with the strong. There is conflict when characters differ in opinions. So, to have contrasts, you must have characters that contrast with each other and your settings must contrast. Anything that contrasts will help your story, and your story needs plenty of help. 

But, although you may have conflict, contrast, and the other ingredients—if you don't have SUSPENSE, then your story is weakened.

Suspense is the quality which keeps the reader in a state of uncertainty as to how the story will finally turn out.

At times, suspense is achieved through physical struggle, at other times through mental. There can never be drama without suspense. The sooner there is suspense in a story, the longer the suspense is maintained, the greater appeal the story will have for the reader. If a man tries to rescue a girl from drowning, the reader is in suspense until he knows if both people will be saved; but if something happens meanwhile to cause the reader to wonder if they are still safe, then you will be building suspense again. But your readers must be interested in the characters. If they are not, then there is no suspense.

Suspense is that quality which makes the story interesting to the reader. Suspense is usually built up by fear. And fear is one of the basic human emotions. 

Fear, or Menace, is the commonest method of build-

ing and maintaining suspense. Don't cram your stories full of physical action. It is not action that determines the value to the story; it is what that action means. 

If thrills or action fit naturally, use them; but if you bring them in merely for effect, discard them. 

Suspense can be aroused in a great many ways, but unless the suspense has a definite bearing on the story, unless it is a natural outgrowth of the story problem, then the suspense has no place in the story. 

Suspense is usually at the highest point just before the story reaches the crisis. The crisis is the turning point in the story wherein all future developments are determined. Your story has a series of minor crises all building up to one major crisis. Suspense can be heightened by permitting first one character to win a point, and then the opposing character to win a point. 

A crisis is never complete in itself. Each one calls for another to follow. Unless action comes to a point that demands readjustment, you have not reached a crisis.

There is no limit to the number of crisis you may use in a story. As long as each one is stronger than the preceding one and each one builds up the story, they are permissible.

Your crises build up until a climax is reached. 

Climax is always followed by the denouement. 

A climax must be logical, unexpected, and convincing. This is a rather tall order. The best way is to have the climax in mind before you start the story.

Conflict, Crisis and Suspense 83 In the climax, the emotional strain of the story is relieved and the loose ends are tied up. Climax is the logical ending of any story. However, certain things must be explained in the denouement. 

Your ending should be the only one possible to the story. Your reader must feel that no other ending could have been possible.

When you write, write so that each situation prepares the reader for the ultimate end. Decide what effect the ending will produce and hold to this effect throughout the story.

Surprise endings are very interesting but very hard to produce. These endings must not only be a surprise, but they must be logical.

Endings must be plausible as well as possible. The ending should bring everything to a close. The reader should be satisfied that the story is finished. No loose ends should be left dangling to jar the reader. And, above all, the ending should end the story. 

In a great many cases, the writer will discover that he has a string of incidents and complications; a series of crises culminating in a major crisis-but no story. 

To be a real story, it must do something. The characters must have a goal; they must either reach that goal or else abandon the attempt. If the goal is abandoned some reason must be given for so doing. Ask yourself the question: Has my character accomplished what he set out to do? If he has, then the story is a

story. If not, then you have just an incident—not a story.

LESSON XIV WRITING TECHNIQUE 

Let us now consider the transitions in your story. 

You have completed a scene and you wish to make a transition to another scene. At this point, the average writer becomes afraid and makes an abrupt jump. As a result, the reader becomes confused. It is smooth transition that gives the story the continual forward movement. 

When you make a transition from one scene to another, just remember to prepare for the transition in the previous scene and to carry over some of the previous scene into the next.

As a writer, you must remember that clearness in your story is an obligation. If the story is vague at any point, you must remove that vagueness. If you write simply and clearly, the chances are that there will be no vagueness. You must remember that you are trying to give the reader a picture of life. If that picture isn't clear, then you have failed in your story purpose. 

But, how can clearness be maintained? One very important rule is to watch your character names. 85

Male characters are better known by their last names, females by the first. Don't call a character Brown in one paragraph and Sam in another. This confuses the reader. It is also a bad policy to have characters with names starting with the same letters; the reader may become confused by the similarity.

Very often, a reader becomes confused by dialect. 

Most dialect is complicated, and when the reader must slow up to understand the story, he may throw the story aside in disgust. In like manner, don't invent new words. You have plenty at your command. If you invent a name, you must go to the trouble of explaining what it means. The reader is not interested in that; he is interested in the story. 

Foreign words are often confusing. Use only foreign words which are used in everyday speech. Use words the reader can recognize. Use simple English words if they can be substituted for the foreign. Don't send the reader to the dictionary. If you use big words, the reader may get the wrong meaning-and thus the story effect will be lost. Avoid the use of technical terms unless you are writing for an audience accustomed to such terms. If you are writing for a specialized group, then such terms are necessary. Avoid the use of slang. Slang should be used only by one character-and only sparingly at that. If a word is ambiguous in meaning, avoid its use. It may be perfectly clear to you as the author, but the reader may be puzzled.

Writing Technique 

87 In the scripts submitted to me, 50 per cent have had dialogue that was not clear. Either because of lack of "tag" lines or even because the author forgot a "he said," it has been necessary to hunt back to see just who has been doing the talking. 

Avoid the use of too many dashes in a story. Never have a speech trail off unless you are positive the reader can fill in the gap. In too many cases, the author uses these dashes merely because he cannot think of anything to say.

Watch grammar, punctuation, and sentence structure. If you have trouble with your grammar, I suggest you get a book on grammar. Several good texts are available and a little study will go a long way toward improving your story. 

Watch your sentences. Don't make them all very long or all very short. Short sentences give the feeling of swift action, long sentences a feeling of slowness. 

A long sentence may also leave a reader breathless because of its great length. Newspaper offices insist on short sentences. I suggest you keep the sentences simple instead of complex. 

Avoid melodrama. The "unhand me sir" school of writing has faded. If you become over-melodramatic, your reader will turn away in disgust. Never lecture your reader. Don't intrude in the story as the author. 

Remember that you, as author, are the observer or the reporter. You have no business at all in the story itself.

Writing is an exacting profession. No one can “kid” himself into being a writer.

LESSON XV SLANTING YOUR STORY (The Choice of Your Markets) 

Have you ever heard anyone say: "Oh, you're wrong, you've got the wrong slant on things"? Did you have any trouble understanding what he meant? 

Then why should you have trouble understanding about slant in writing? 

To most writers, the problem of slant is difficult only because it entails hard work. Let me warn you at the outset, the task of learning how to slant means considerable effort on your part and unless you are willing to work, and work hard, you may as well forget all about slant—and all about your career as a writer as well.

Of course, it is possible to write and sell story material without the bother of slanting. But, you'll sell more stories, and you'll sell them faster and easier, you slant. if

Slant is merely a method of giving the editor what he wants to use instead of trying to force on him the material you think he should use. There have been cases of writers submitting juveniles to sex magazines 89

or sending sophisticated love stories to Thrilling

Mystery. It is just as foolish to send an unslanted story. 

Just what, then, is a slanted story? First, a slanted story is the result of your careful consideration of a particular editor's needs. When you go to the store to buy a certain brand of food, you usually resent any attempt of the clerk to offer you something "just as good." The same goes with the editor. He wants to buy a certain type of story; he knows what he wants. 

When you offer him something "just as good" he fires it back. If you do it again, he fires it back quicker -perhaps with a note. But, if you do the same thing again after he has sent you the note-your chances are nil at that particular office. 

You'll find that editors are rather human fellows, when you get to know them. Just because they return your material doesn't mean that they have a particular grudge against you. Just give them something they can use it may not be quite perfect-but they will work with you on that bit of material so they can use it. I know, for I have had editors work with meand as an editor I have worked with writers. Many writers have worked hard to get the particular slant of a given magazine-they had the material but the slant was a bit off-so the editor helped them out. 

You'll find this the case in practically every editorial office.

He'll give it gladly if he thinks the story worth while. If you ask, he will think you are one of the "something for nothing" school of writers and will promptly forget all about you.

Stories are sold which violate all rules, stories are sold which may appear to be poorly written. But the fact remains that most writers who have a steady output week after week, who make the big money in writing, are the writers who have taken the trouble to learn to write and slant-and who have written millions of words over a period of years. 

We have now determined two points in the matter of slant-the advertising slant, and the type of story used. The next matter is to study the illustrations in the magazine, the type faces, and the general make-up appearance of the book. By doing this, you get an insight as to the editorial mind. If the magazine uses few illustrations, it may be because the budget is low, because the editor cannot think in pictures, or because he prefers his particular type of readers to make their own mental pictures from the written words. Some magazines may use full color photography, regular photographs, paintings—either oil or water colorsetchings, woodcuts, etc.

It may now seem to you that the editorial content of the magazine is of very little importance in the matter of slant. It may appear that the real problem of slant is not so much the story itself but the externals. Don't you believe it!

In the first place, you may have a perfect slant for

a particular magazine and yet fail to sell because the story is poorly written. This slant business will help you write a better story, yes, but it cannot write the story for you. All slant attempts to do is to help a good writer make sales more easily.

LESSON XVI HOW TO SELL WHAT YOU HAVE WRITTEN 

No matter how many thousands of words are written on the subject of writing, the cardinal question remains: CAN I SELL MY STORY? 

It is the answer to this question that is so important to writers.

All learning is futile unless some definite use can be made of that acquired knowledge. Likewise, all the courses in writing mean nothing unless you can write a story that sells!

It is one thing to write an interesting story and another to write a salable story. All interesting stories are not salable—and all salable stories are not interesting. Most of you will agree to this last statement after reading several published stories. I know of writers who can produce good interesting stories but who cannot write a salable story. I have read stories by unknowns which compared favorably with published stories and yet the stories can not sell.

There must, then, be some magic method known to these writers who sell. There must be some method 97

available to the writer to help him write salable stories. 

Is it luck? Is there such a thing as editorial "pull”? 

No! There is nothing to the "pull" or "luck" idea.

There is no magic method. Writers will continue to write good interesting stories and they will continue NOT to sell. Some will read textbooks and fail to apply the tested methods. And that is the secretfailure to apply the knowledge.

You may learn a great deal about fiction, but unless you apply your technique you can have little hope of success. And applying the technique means that you must do all the work of slanting, too.

Before the story is submitted, we must have slanted it to some specific magazine. Slanting not only assures the writer of a better reception at the editorial office but it also lessens the chances of haphazard submission of material. A story slanted directly at three magazines of one general group cannot be offered to any other general group. While this may, at first, appear to be a drawback, it is really of great help to the author. 

By direct slanting, you learn to fit your stories to a particular group and thus have a better knowledge of the immediate needs of that small group. 

We have now decided upon definite technique and definite slant. The next thing to consider is intelligent revision of the story. It is here that most writers fall down. I have yet to see a new writer, or in a great many cases even a professional writer, who can sit

How to Sell What You Have Written 

99 down and write a story that will sell without revision. 

If you can't sit down and coldly survey your story for faults and weaknesses, get the services of a reputable critic. He will more than earn his money in showing you where you have erred. And, this work will not only help you with the story he is criticising but will keep you from making these same mistakes on other stories. However, if you can, with the help of various technical aids, revise your own story, go ahead.

With the story revised, rewritten, and typed we are now ready to take the final step in the selling of stories. And that is Intelligent Marketing. 

If you have slanted the story, you have narrowed down the market considerably. You may have, at most, about ten markets for the particular story. 

These ten submissions will cost you at least three dollars in postage and envelopes.

Get yourself a reliable market list-study the published stories of the magazines-check your story with the published ones—and then send it out with a return stamped, addressed envelope. Make things easy for the editor-easy to return, easy to accept. Don't write a long letter to send with the script. Don't ask him to criticise the story. Don't threaten. Be businesslikebe a salesman of your own product. If you were selling beans you wouldn't threaten dire punishment to all those customers who refused to buy. You are just a salesman-selling a product. It is up to you to con-

vince the editor that your product is superior. Convince him, not with a letter, but with the product itself.

Don't expect an agent to sell a story which is obviously unsuited for any market. Agents are not magicians. Given a fairly good story, they can, by suggesting revision, increase the sales chances of that storybut they cannot guarantee that a story will sell. In practically every case, agents depend upon their professional writers for a living. So, don't expect an agent to handle your material on speculation. He must maintain an office-a staff of market experts-and keep in touch with other writers through advertising.

This costs money-it is up to the author to pay his way until the time when he is selling regularly. Then, and then only, can you expect an agent to handle you on straight commission. 

I realize that most of you believe that you will not need an agent after you make a few sales. You'll need an agent even more. The agent will serve as a buffer between you and the editor-keep you posted on market needs-get you orders-keep your name before other editors-and generally boost your stock. He'll help you over the rough spots, show you the way, make sales for you. 

And that's all there is to selling stories. You must read-but that is not all. You'll succeed only if you remember this precept: 

You must apply your knowledge.
