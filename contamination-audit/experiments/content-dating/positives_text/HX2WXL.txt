INTRODUCTION 

Why write a book about an unknown shepherd? This may be the question asked by you, dear reader. In answer to the same, the author will not admit that the subject of his writing is unknown, for in truth "Monsignor Heinen " is a household word amongst thousands of families whose faith remains Catholic because of the missionary work of their patron and good shepherd. While we readily grant there are many priests in the individual dioceses of the Catholic world, who have done much for the cause of religion and yet are unknown because of their humility and possible reluctance to record their spiritual achievements, yet there is no reason to keep their work hidden when the living record of their pastoral successes will give inspiration and encouragement to a new or coming generation of shepherds in the 

Church of Jesus Christ. 

Is it not true that you draw your inspirations and encouragement from others? You have some ideal which you follow. Your philosophy of life is based upon your studies of life and your experiences in life. Need it be said that in our present time there is no need of both inspiration and encouragement? Our times are strenuous and our days are filled with discouragements. Therefore both shepherds and their flocks, both priests and people, all need some inspiration for the planning and doing of great things for Christ, and all need some encouragement to retain their pristine vigor and enthusiasm in the quest for souls. 

Monsignor Heinen, like St. Vincent de Paul or any other outstanding zealot for souls, left a definite record of his achievements and it is the glorious victory of his spiritual conquest which is bound to inspire and encourage us today. (5)

Introduction 

The words of David might be termed prophetic of Monsignor Heinen, for from the lowliness and humility of his missionary life he was lifted up and became mighty in the eyes of God. He seldom reverted to the achievements which he wrought from day to day, but placed the spoils of his victory into the hands of Almighty God. He rejoiced silently in the 

His work is monumental secrecy of his own heart and soul. in the memory of those whose faith Monsignor Heinen conserved.

It is the hope of the author of this book that a better knowledge of Monsignor Heinen will both inspire and encourage you, dear reader, in following Christ more closely.

May the prodigious work of Monsignor Heinen make us humble in the pastoral care of a single parish whilst we read of his vast missionary fields of action and the unlimited achievement of his dynamic personality!

LEO GREGORY FINK FEAST OF SAINT JAMES, ALLENTOWN, Pa., 1937.

MONSIGNOR HEINEN CHAPTER I THE LOOM of Life 

IT was in the land of the Crefeld Weavers in the Rhineland, Germany, where some of the most beautiful vestments worn by the priests in the Sacrifice of the Mass are fabricated, that the subject of this biography was born. To the child that was to reflect in his life the revocation and strategy of Ignatius Loyola in the Church Militant, the name of William Heinen was given. On October 12, 1839, he was born in the town of Willich, in the archdiocese of Cologne; the day was momentous in this respect that it marked the date of America's discovery by one Christ-Bearer-Christopher Columbus—and it augured the discovery of many souls in America by another Christ-Bearer the Right Reverend Monsignor William Heinen, V.F. He was truly to stand at the "loom of life" and weave into the fabric of the Catholic Church a pattern of missionary activity which the pioneer priests of Christ has designed in apostolic times. His work was to be truthfully recorded in eternity. Nevertheless it would also become monumental in the annals of Catholicism in the Philadelphia archdiocese. 

With the Catholic environment of the Rhineland, the child grew in years and wisdom under the paternal roof of his good and devout parents. It would be useless to draw upon one's imagination to set forth the influence of the Catholic Home upon the future missionary whose home would be wherever he found souls in need of shepherding. The years (11)

Monsignor Heinen sped onward and the school age was soon attained with the result that the foundations laid in the Christian family were formidable enough to bear the superstructure of a liberal education which was received in the local schools of Kempen and later at Muenster, Westphalia.

Deeply imbedded in his adolescent character was the desire of inculcating into the minds of others the need of "law and order " and the most natural thing to do in the pursuit of this vocational aspiration was to take up the study of law. It was in Arnsburg near Münster and also in Münster that the University Course in Law was undertaken in 1858, but in 1859 we find the young man inducted into the military service of the Prussian Infantry for the period of one year, after which he again pursued his studies of Law and Philosophy. 

This year's service in the army was only granted to those matriculated at the University for a course of professional study. The regular obligatory service for all Prussian subjects was three years. In view of the fact that the young student had served his term of military indenture later he was granted a pension which was withheld by the Chancellor Bismarck who made it obligatory for all pensioners to remain in Germany and spend their pension in their native land. 

In the year 1860 during his military service the young man met with an accident which turned the whole course of his ambitious life. It came about in this way. "A number of infantry regiments had been called out for the manœuvre. 

They had been on march since shortly after two in the morning when the reveillé had aroused them into line with knapsack and cloak. After leaving the field, late in the afternoon, they were suddenly commanded to halt and form a carré ". 

The movement was executed by one of those rapid evolutions in which the soldiers, breaking into a quickstep separate, single file, to make a square for the protection of the baggage train that follows them. It was a military exercise frequently practised by Napoleon in his Egyptian campaign, and calcu-

The unexpected turn, as the men took their positions while on the run, caused one of them to stumble. As he fell, the butt-end of his gun struck the ground, and the soldier behind him, leaping upon the point of the exposed bayonet, was spitted through the breast and shoulder." 

In this manner the author of " Pastor Halloft" tells of the accident which seriously wounded the hero of this story. 

Although he suffered much pain, yet he remained conscious. 

With mingled fear and hope he finally arrived at the military hospital and the thought uppermost in his mind was that if God spared him for a longer life, he would relinquish the world and its pleasures. His Father Confessor gave him the proper inspiration and as he suffered from the bayonet wound, the embryonic barrister turned his mind towards the serious consideration of the Divine Law instead of the intricate statutes of civil and military law. He contemplated the eternal laws of God and the unchangeable code of ethics as taught by the Church of Jesus Christ. "Law and order" was the countersign of his life and his penchant. conformity to the Will of God brought him to no other conclusion than that he would conform his whole personality to the Will of God in the religious life. He vowed allegiance to the Divine Leader of heaven's army and awaited the day of his restoration to health in order to pursue a new line of training for his priestly vocation. Perfect

In due time he consulted his confessor, an humble but learned Capuchin Father, who led him to believe that if God called him there would be some way of attaining to the ideal of his vocation. But as in all beginnings and especially with the formative period of great men's personalities, hardship, privation and trial must first be endured before the final masterpiece of personality is evolved. Accordingly during the period of years from 1860 until 1869, the youthful

Monsignor Heinen aspirant to a missionary career silently performed certain legal duties in the courts whilst he retained the military uniform; later he obtained a position as civil assessor in the garrison at Arnsburg, Westphalia, and thus awaited patiently the day of his redemption and opportunity to fulfill the vow which he made upon his one-time death-bed. 

Many stories were told concerning the hazardous labors of the missionaries in North America and the martyrdom of certain Jesuit Fathers in the cause of Catholicism in America only added new enthusiasm and gave the young man ambition to spend his life where the dangers were most numerous. His ambition became more and more pronounced to sacrifice himself in the missionary work amongst the Indians of North America or if the opportunity presented itself, he declared he would give himself to any foreign mission field where God would call him. With a burning zeal for souls and a vehement desire to dedicate his life to the priesthood of Jesus Christ, he lost no opportunity of reading and studying for his intended missionary life. 

At this time a young student of Louvain, wrote and published a brochure entitled "The Catholic Church in the United States of America ". The facts and statistics were gathered from reliable sources by the young student and gave a particular description of the needs of the missionaries in the new world and also described the various dioceses of the United States of America. The booklet was written by none other than the future missionary priest who won for himself the title of "The Church-Builder " of the Philadelphia archdiocese-Rev. Henry Stommel. A much quoted axiom of the author's own wording was herein oft repeated that “an industrious and upright man finds Bread everywhere, not only for the body but also for the soul". With similar sentiments and convictions deeply imbedded in his heart, the young William Heinen longed for the day when he could

The Loom of Life 

15 become a priest and also become a missionary priest in the 

United States of America. 

Would such an opportunity ever arise? Must he continue in the garb of a soldier and ply his professional art in the adjudication of civil assessment and taxes, or would he be released some day for the study of spiritual statistics and the proper evaluation of men's souls; could he ever hope to balance the budget of spirituality for bankrupt souls: could he ever pronounce the words of absolution to contrite hearts and bid happy souls "Go and sin no more". Many were the years of meditation and many were the long hours in those nine years of waiting for the fulfillment of his vow! 

Transformations of character as well as of vocations have always taken place and will continue to the end of time. 

What is thought impossible is still possible. The mountains of doubt and trial are still moved by faith in an ideal—faith in God! The design which is woven upon the fabric of man's personality is not revealed in the loom of his life. 

The weaver of Crefeld patiently kept on weaving at the loom of his anticipatory life, the sacerdotal vestments of his future ministry were in the loom. The Crefeld chasuble of trial, sorrow and sacrifice would soon replace the Prussian military uniform.

In the year 1869, the Most Reverend James F. Wood, Archbishop of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, visited Munster on his way to make his "ad limina " visit to Rome. During his stay in the Rhineland, the enthusiastic bishop had made known his desire to obtain several young students for the American missions. An old schoolmate wrote a letter to the young man and told him of the anticipated privilege of accompanying the American bishop over the Atlantic Ocean to Philadelphia. The letter was written in the form of a farewell to the friends of his youth. The militant youth determined that he would meet the American Bishop, even if it must be on the boat as it made its way across the ocean. The

Monsignor Heinen former civil assessor would now begin to fulfill his vow by leaving his parents and seeking the aid of the American prelate.

The youthful American missionary met the experienced American Bishop. The vague dreams of Indians and the heroic life on the western plains were exchanged for the serious and conservative missionary labor of the priests of the Philadelphia archdiocese.

The bishop saw the deep faith and the true desire of the young man; nothing could prevent him from granting the young man permission to come to America and engage at once in the preparation for the priesthood. Since he had already passed through a course of philosophical studies, the study of theology was absolutely necessary before the young man could be ordained to the priesthood. In spite of the fact that he had spent many years in the study of theological matters, yet he was obliged to spend some time and study under ecclesiastical supervision before he could enter the priesthood. An agreement was reached and it was proposed to pursue the theological studies in America at the Philadelphia Seminary.

CHAPTER II THE BRIGHT LEXICON OF YOUTH THE Voyage from Bremen to New York City was brief because of the ardent hopes within the young man's breast. 

No storm nor delay seemed severe or tiring enough to weaken the intrepidity of the young man who hoped soon to enter an American Seminary and prepare himself for the Catholic priesthood. As Bulwer Lytton once said "in the bright lexicon of youth there is no such word as 'Failure'", so the ambitious youth believed he would succeed. The steamship" Deutschland" arrived in New York harbor on August 21, 1869 and William Heinen was found in the safe keeping of Rev. Herman A. Depman of St. Clair and Rev. William

Pieper, who were also passengers on the boat. 

The Bishop's promise was soon to be fully realized, for the young student immediately entered the Philadelphia archdiocesan Seminary at 18th and Race Streets and took up his study of Moral and Dogmatic Theology. The building was comparatively large but in spite of the many rooms which were used for the class-rooms, halls and living quarters for both students and the professors, the ceilings were all very low. The chapel, refectory and offices were all crowded into the compact building and while some senior students had separate apartments, the twenty or more junior students were huddled into a dormitory with low ceilings and narrow windows, where when they were not engaged in study they were forced to also recreate. The brick-paved yard or court had a hand-ball alley. Many of the students were rather sickly, mainly because of the small quarters for the maintenance of a diocesan seminary, which with the advance of years would be entirely removed for the benefit of the beautiful (17)

Monsignor Heinen

Cathedral of Sts. Peter and Paul, adjoining the Seminary building.

The youthful theological student who had spent much of his life in the open country, soon broke down in health because of his close application to study and lack of physical exercise.

The great difference in climatic conditions was trying indeed upon the young student, whose miraculous escape from death had left him mindful of his weakened lung which had been punctured by the bayonet and the wound of which always showed the cicatrix. His life in Germany had been for the most part spent in the open air and in the spacious military quarters of the garrison, but the Seminary in Philadelphia was small indeed and crowded into the heart of a congested city. The damp rooms and poorly ventilated student's apartments were overcrowded and thus the youth whose handicap was his need of fresh air and exercise physical, became more and more weakened by the strain of study and close confinement. During these years it must be remembered that the Overbrook Seminary was in the course of construction, for the cornerstone was laid by Bishop Wood on April 4, 1866 and the same was opened for the students on September 16, 1871. While the young student did not have the opportunity of being sent to Overbrook, he sought the peaceful and healthful environment of Haycock 

Run along the Delaware River, where Rev. Francis J.

Martersteck who came from the young man's native town, was in charge of the parish church of St. John the Baptist. 

Here in the peaceful and healthful retreat of Haycock Run midway between the Mountains and the Delaware River the youth regained his health and privately took up his studies again. Here the spirit of the old Jesuits overshadowed him daily and from the history of the old Jesuit Missionary church, he learned many secrets which his books in theology

He covered the very training grounds of American patriots and the very soil which Washington's soldiers passed over when they marched from Valley Forge to the Delaware on that memorable Christmas night of 1776 and surprised the Hessians on the opposite side at Trenton. 

What a happy combination of environment! The Jesuits and the American patriots! Both were sources of inspiration for the young man.

The country air of Haycock Mouutain was invigorating and in a short time the student regained his health. In the following spring of 1871 he was notified to return to the Seminary and prepare for Sacred Orders. The happiest day of his life-the day of his ordination—was April 3, 1871 when at the age of 31 years he became a Catholic priest under the fatherly guidance of the Most Reverend Archbishop Wood. His first Mass was said in St. Vincent's 

Chapel of St. Vincent's Orphanage, Tacony, on Easter Monday, April 10, 1871, and as a "stranger in a strange land", he had the pleasure of having the orphan children as guests of his first Mass.

Unlike many other biographies, this sketch of the young priest shall be prefaced with a brief but psychological analysis of his personality. Instead of obliging the reader to assimilate three or four hundred pages of historical matter before the writer divulges the psychological inventory of his investigation into the characteristics of his subject, it shall be the author's purpose in this chapter to briefly sum up the personality of Monsignor Heinen. 

What character of man was the newly ordained priest?

Thousands upon thousands of priests quietly come and quietly go. They come from obscurity and they depart into obscurity. God alone knows the glorious achievements of

Monsignor Heinen their sacerdotal careers. It is indeed commendable that most men's lives are passed in silence, and only after death do we dig out of the ruins of a past century the buried treasures of history for the inspiration of the living generation. Such a personality was the future Monsignor Heinen-one that would be obscured in Life, but glorified in Death! 

From the writer's contact with persons who actually lived with Monsignor Heinen and from the definite records, monumental in the churches which proclaims his priestly work, we shall endeavor to give you, good reader, a preview of his personality for no other purpose than to make you better realize that more than human inspiration was the dynamic force which prompted him to begin and to untiringly continue his great missionary career until the very hour of death. 

In the words of David, the subject of this biography was first and last-" a man of God -"Homo Dei "—who kept before his mind the one objective of human life, namely, the salvation of souls. Rigorous in his own pursuit of other souls, tempered with kindness but always aggressive and persistent upon bringing more souls to Christ, he knew no such word as "failure". To his credit he knew how to utilize the latent or hidden forces in others and he used them to good advantage for the missionary work which he always had in view. No suggestion nor possible aid was left untouched, for in his mind the humble wayside flower was a possible instrument with which to convert a soul to God. 

Apart from the many persons with whom he came in contact and from whom he always received much assistance, certain ideals were firmly implanted in his mind and these ideals of principles or personalities could be traced to the historical and romantic writings of Alban Stolz, the theological and philosophical works of Franz Hettinger, the eloquence and rhetoric of Santa Clara, a celebrated Vienna preacher, the missionary zeal for the Slovak people as found in the life of Saint Methodius and the militant apostolate of Saint Paul.

21 A certain writer once said "tell me what books you read and I will tell you what you are ". This truthfully can be applied to the life of Monsignor Heinen. We can esteem his personality in proportion to the kind of books which he read, for the reflection of the ideals contained in the above mentioned books and writings can easily be found in the active and dynamic life of Monsignor Heinen. He became an informative speaker on secular affairs as well as religious topics, and although convincing indeed as his sermons were he never aimed at eloquence. 

Clad in the spiritual armor of God, the young priest awaited orders from his Bishop! He was anxious to save souls! He sought no glory for himself! His pleasure, his joy and his success would be for the "greater honor and glory of God!' 

With Saint Paul he stood in readiness and awaited the call to "go forth and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost"!

CHAPTER III FIRST MISSION EXPERIENCES 

It was providential that Father Heinen was first sent to the queenly city of the Lehigh Valley-Allentown-where the natural beauties of the country would mean more for the youthful priest than the palatial residences and commercial centers of a large city. His first appointment as curate was to the Sacred Heart Church, Allentown, of which the Rev. 

Ernest Hilterman was rector. While the Sacred Heart 

Parish extended over a large area and combined both city and country, nevertheless it was a parish which cared for the German-speaking Catholics who owing to internal dissension in their first church in Allentown, the Immaculate Conception Church, had broken away and with permission of the Most Reverend Bishop organized a new parish church at Fourth and Pine Streets, while the old parish church was sold at a sheriff's sale to the Catholics of Irish descent, who still hold the same at Ridge Avenue and Liberty Streets. In taking up his work in Allentown, Father Heinen realized that the utmost prudence would have to be exercised in order to keep peace amongst the none-too-friendly Catholics of the city. The good judgment and extreme solicitude for souls on the part of the pastor and his new curate soon won the high esteem of not only Catholics but also non-Catholics. 

Peace and harmony amongst the Catholics of both parishes was only a matter of time.

The first official record made by Father Heinen as a priest can still be found in the Baptismal Records for the year 1871. 

The entry was made as follows: 

1871-Record No. 25-Sacred Heart Parish, Allen- 

On April 23d I baptized Henry town, Pa. (22)

Stephen Latzer son of Ludwig Latzer and 

Mathilda Ridder, who was born on February 3d 1870. The Sponsors were Christian Wiedermann and Mary Kaiser 

(signed) WILLIAM HEINEN 

The records continued unbroken from April 1871 until October 1871, which shows that he was located in Allentown during that period of time. From October 15th 1871 until 

July 13th 1872 he was a curate at St. Paul's Church, Reading, but returned to Allentown at various times to assist Father 

Hilterman, especially from January 14th 1872 to April 28th 1872, after which he remained in Reading until July 13th 1872.

The records show that the baptisms were performed not only in Allentown, but also in Catasauqua, Bethlehem and the surrounding towns. Some pages of the records show that he brought whole families into the church, as in one specific case in 1872 he baptized five persons who were respectively, two, three, five, ten and thirteen years of age. He was always active and militant in his aggressiveness to win souls for Christ. Soldier-like in most things, even in his walk, he bore in his personality the conviction of his serious warfare against sin and vigorously tried to bring all men under the loving leadership of Jesus Christ. In his Pastor, Father

Hilterman, he found an exemplary priest who taught the new curate how to manage an American parish.

Father Heinen was held in high esteem in Reading as a most convincing and elucidating preacher, for the usual comment upon the two curates at St. Paul's Church was that "Father Heinen is our great preacher, and Father Fechtel is our best singer". During his stay in Reading, the young priest had the splendid opportunity of living under the same roof with the Right Rev. Monsignor George Bornemann, whose personality was that of another St. Francis De Sales and whose charity closely emulated that of St. Vincent de

Monsignor Heinen

Paul. From Monsignor Bornemann the young priest received much valuable guidance and experience which inspired him to develop the latent forces of his dynamic personality. 

In Monsignor Bornemann were found all those noble ideals of practical charity which any single priest might care to possess. Conservative, yet progressive, gentle but still determined, big-hearted and always just, such was the psychoanalysis of Father Heinen's pastor who was in the order of providence destined to guide the young priest in the most receptive and formative period of his sacerdotal career. In one thing both pastor and curate were like-natured. It was their military precision with which they began and completed everything they undertook. Monsignor Bornemann even as a student in the theological seminary had accompanied the 

Most Rev. Bishops Wood, O'Hara and Connor to Fort

Delaware where thousands of Union soldiers and Confederate warriors with a large number of officers were being hospitalized since the close of the Civil War. Monsignor 

Bornemann never forgot his work as a chaplain to the soldiers at Fort DuPont and Father Heinen had not forgotten his military life in the German army. They marched together as soldiers in the army of Christ! 

While engaged in parochial work under such admirable leadership, Father Heinen personally labored for the many afflicted people who were victims of the "Smallpox 

Epidemic of 1872". Under the direction of Msgr. Bornemann the young priest learned much in the school of parish experience and out of the epidemic he saw the inception of a hospital by Msgr. Bornemann which has since developed into the magnificent and spacious St. Joseph's Hospital which now dominates the hillside of Mount Penn and casts its beneficent invitation to stricken humanity from the length and breadth of Reading's population to come within its charitable walls and find peace, comfort, rest and health under the care of the city's best physicians and the Sisters of St. Francis

who minister to the sick and injured as well as administer the temporalities of the vast and growing institution. Father 

Heinen saw the first building which was the nucleus of the present institution; he visited the sick there very often and from his observance of the work done by the Sisters he often remarked that under God's care the acorn sown would some day become strong as the oak. This prediction has since come true in the present monumental service to stricken humanity in St. Joseph's Hospital.

In connection with the Smallpox Epidemic of 1872, Monsignor Bornemann always spoke in the highest terms of the indefatigable labors of Father Heinen for the sick and the poor. In fact, he overworked himself so that he too fell a victim of the dreadful disease which took so many lives. 

Fortunately for Mother Church, Father Heinen recovered from the affliction which at times threatened his own life. 

His experiences in Reading had well prepared him for the position of pastor and he had only to await the command of his Bishop who would soon send him forth as another apostle and good shepherd of souls. 

In July of 1872 Father Heinen was appointed pastor of the Church of Ss. Peter and Paul, Lehighton, where in the midst of a busy railroad town and literally surrounded by a vast farming and mining section of Eastern Pennsylvania, he found a portion of Christ's kingdom which he determined to rule over for the remaining days of his life. 

He had at the very beginning delighted in the work and always insisted that he neither craved for a city parish nor for the ease and comfort of a parish where the work was completed. He loved work and it was his happy state in life to labor not for the rich, but for the poor in the farming and mining regions of Eastern Pennsylvania. As a young priest he came to Lehighton with a determination of accomplishing something for the Church of Christ and he had the happy faculty of grasping a situation at a glance. Soldier-like, he

Monsignor Heinen could picture the advance of the army and could plan for the result of any emergency or development of the Church. 

Thus when he arrived at Lehighton he found that his parish church had a little mission church in East Mauch Chunk, which was cared for from Lehighton. Almost instinctively he saw the early development of East Mauch Chunk and saw the importance of doing something at once for the little mission and its growing number of Catholics. East Mauch 

Chunk was destined to be a prosperous community and his plans were to make the mission chapel of St. Joseph a parish church with his residence also located there. The church at Lehighton for the time being was to be made a mission chapel which he would visit every Sunday and holyday of obligation. Much to the regret of the Catholics of Lehighton but to the joy of those living at East Mauch Chunk, Father Heinen obtained permission to make the change and the mission chapel of East Mauch Chunk was raised to the dignity of a parish church in the year 1873 under the title of St. 

Joseph's Roman Catholic Church.

From St. Joseph's Church in East Mauch Chunk the future work of the Catholic Church was to radiate over hills and valleys: and, if ever a quasi-cathedral was established it was St. Joseph's Church. From this church, Father Heinen and his missionary curates would go forth and actually bring into existence so many parish churches in the Philadelphia archdiocese! Little did he realize that his country church would be a veritable cathedral from which the Catholic faith would be disseminated over the face of the earth. He would labor, teach, direct, organize, construct and maintain the growing forces of Catholicism, but he would never become a bishop. Like the Cure of Ars he would humbly live and humbly die for the salvation of souls! The mountains and the countryside would be his earthly paradise. 

The financial difficulties of the East Mauch Chunk Catholics vanished with the advent of Father Heinen, for he knew

how "to use a little money to great advantage". He did not plague the people for money, but made them realize their obligations. Hence, priest and people succeeded because they knew how to economize for the Church of Christ! Converts came into the church in large numbers; the Catholics even moved from other places to East Mauch Chunk: the good shepherd was the magnet of God! In a short time, exactly one year after his arrival, in 1894, Father Heinen insisted upon the establishment of a Catholic School in East Mauch Chunk. To many this was a dangerous undertaking and even amongst some of the priests it was deemed too hazardous for so small a country town as East Mauch Chunk. Father 

Heinen's belief was that "every Catholic Church should have a Catholic School" and he began the work under many discouragements from both the laity and the clergy. After much preaching upon the subject of Christian Education, Father Heinen obtained some Sisters of Christian Charity from the Mother-House in Wilkes Barre, who were appointed to teach in the parish school. 

The coming of the teaching Sisters was the one argument which satisfied the people of East Mauch Chunk about the necessity of a Catholic School. The people became ardent supporters of the idea and the parish school became exemplary in providing not only the fundamentals of Catholic doctrine but the requirements for practical work in the trades and professions. The opening of the Parish School is still well remembered by some who were privileged to be its first pupils, and the dedication of the same was marked by the visit to East Mauch Chunk of another priest who later in life became famous as a missionary and church-builder of the Philadelphia Archdiocese-Rev. Henry Stommel. What these two progressive priests planned at that time, was only known to God, but time has proven that their ideals of providing churches, small in size and great in number, were identical. Both were enthusiasts for the growth of God's

Monsignor Heinen kingdom upon earth. Both were untiring in their labors!

Both forgot themselves and never grew weary in advancing the army of Christ!

However, owing to the rough mountainous roads and the inclement weather, the irregularity of the Mass may have been the reason for the unsettled conditions amongst the people. With the coming of Father Heinen peace was established amongst the people and with a real united effort the parish went forward with leaps and bounds. 

The parish grew with the town. Mauch Chunk, once called by the Indians "Machk Tschunk "-Bear Mountain-became a prosperous railroad center of its day, since the Central Railroad of New Jersey as well as the Lehigh Valley Railroad made it a central point of transportation and pas senger service. The Switch-Back Scenic Railroad made the section popular with tourists and thousands upon thousands

of visitors came annually to admire the Lehigh Valley at that point. Everything was in favor of a successful parish in East Mauch Chunk and Father Heinen took advantage of every opportunity to bring about the achievement of his heart's desire.

In addition to the work directly done in East Mauch Chunk, the young pastor had also several missions on his hands which were never neglected because of his activity in the home parish.

The missions consisted of Lehighton, Slatington, Palmerton, Berlinsville and Bowmanstown. The curates were all given a genuine sample of missionary life during their residence in East Mauch Church, for the ambitious pastor expected his curates to follow him in gathering up the fragments of Catholicism "lest they be lost". The 

Lehigh Valley was scrupulously visited and every home, no matter how poor or shattered an appearance it made, the priest entered it for the purpose of finding every Catholic soul irrespective of race, color or nationality. In the olden times, the Indians hid themselves behind trees in their battles with the white-man, but in the missionary battles of Father Heinen he found the Catholics hidden in the company's houses or shacks near the slate quarries, the coal mines and the zinc works. Many poor souls were fearful in the country of their adoption; they could not speak the English language; they had no Catholic Churches; and it seemed as though little could be done for them. Father Heinen read the handwriting upon the wall. He determined that something should be done for the newcomers to America through the Catholic Church. If these strangers could not speak his language, he would study their language in order to help them. Just as truly as Paul was invited to come over to 

Macedonia", so also was Father Heinen invited by His Master to interest himself in the spiritual welfare of the many Catholics who did not speak the English language. He was to be their Apostle! 66

CHAPTER IV SLOVAKS IN AMERICA 

In the early eras of his pastorate in East Mauch Chunk, Father Heinen had on several occasions traveled westward through the Panther Creek Valley to Tamaqua and then farther on through the Schuylkill Valley to Pottsville. 

Whenever he visited Philadelphia he went southward through the Lehigh Valley to Allentown and then on to Reading and Philadelphia. In all these journeys, made purely upon the quest of souls, the young missionary found many people of Slovak descent who were without the proper spiritual care. 

Many were so far removed from a Catholic Church that it was impossible for them to consistently attend Mass every Sunday, while others owing to their timidity in entering an English-speaking church managed in some way to find a Slovak-speaking priest for the purpose of fulfilling their 

Easter precept.

In some cases the Catholic children were in danger of being led into non-Catholic churches. Father Heinen saw these conditions and knew that the one thing necessary for the Slovak Catholics was to have churches and schools for these people. The ideal was correct but to bring about the desired effect was a problem since in many cases the Slovaks lived in the coal company houses or in the crude barracks erected by the railroad companies for the men; the wages of the Slovaks were very poor, since most of them were hired for servile labor. 

What did Father Heinen do? Did he rest content that the sanctuary railing was the place of meeting these people? 

Did he insist that it was the people's duty to come to the church and if they did not come to the church they could (30)

Slovaks in America 31 remain outside of it? No! He determined to bring Catholicism which was understood by the people to their very doors! Father Heinen was both a soldier and a shepherd. 

He came down to the level of his people; he lived in their barracks; he said Mass in their frame homes and buildings, he taught them the principles of Christian Doctrine from the pulpit of their own dim-lit cabins and he broke the Bread of Life to them in the early morn before they left their families to begin a new day's work down in the dark and deep coal mines of the Panther Creek Valley. In other words, he became "all things to all men ". He studied the language of the people for he knew that he could make them better Catholics and better Americans-if he could be understood in their own language. He did not make the mistake of depriving the old folks of their mother tongue, and neither did he make the mistake of changing the trend of the younger generation's tendency to speak English. His vision for the greater glory of God prompted him to care for both old and young in their own specific way. He did not make the mistake which some others make when they refuse to give both old and young the language of their hearts, for it is out of the abundance of the heart that every human being speaks. Father Heinen followed in the footsteps of the pioneer missionary, Father Bally, and understood that the language question was a most delicate one, but could be solved by giving young and old what they desired to use for the better knowledge of their religion and better service to their country. 

We are told that Father Heinen was inspired to interest himself in the Slovak people through companionship with certain young men whom he numbered as his most intimate friends and whose aspirations were to study for the priesthood.

CHAPTER VI VICAR FORANE 

SUCH happy occasions as the laying of cornerstones, dedicating churches, preaching jubilees and anniversary sermons, attending conventions or rallies of Catholics throughout the archdiocese, were all very easily fulfilled by Father Heinen in his capacity of Vicar Forane and Commissary to the non-English speaking Catholics. He had been singularly honored by the Most Reverend Archbishop with the honor and authority to guide the destiny of the churches in which the German, Slovak, Lithuanian, Polish, Hungarian and Italian languages were spoken. In his own peculiar and thoroughly Catholic spirit he could meet the people of all these nationalities and induce them to serve their God and country to the credit of the Catholic Church in America. 

While it is true that the work of Father Heinen resembled that of St. John the Baptist in preparing the way for the coming of the Most Reverend Archbishop, nevertheless his work was always recognized as being fundamental and necessary to every advance of the Catholic Church. He organized the parish, gathered together the people, planned the location of the future church, taught the children their catechism of Christian Doctrine, collected funds for the purchase of land and later for the erection of the church. After he had the ground well prepared, then he would invite the Most Reverend Archbishop to preside at the ceremony which always placed the seal of ecclesiastical approval upon all his projects. He rejoiced silently when any project succeeded and he did not ask for any praise or honors when he actually achieved success. (41)

CHAPTER IX LIFE'S LAST Battle ONE of the most dramatic climaxes in Shakespeare's trag edy "Macbeth" is the last duel of the play wherein Macbeth cries out "we'll die with harness on our back" and goes down to death fighting. While we could scarcely agree with the motives of Macbeth's regal life, we must admire the tenacity and endurance of his life wherein he never turned coward, but fought to the very end of his mortal existence. 

If we were to psychoanalyze the missionary career of Monsignor Heinen we would find him "another Paul" fighting the good fight, running his course and keeping the faith until death. Literally he died with the "harness on his back." 

Once a soldier, he remained a soldier in the army of Jesus Christ.

His battles were many! His conquests were many! His disappointments were many and truly could he say that he began "to be aweary of the sun, and wished the estate of the world were now undone ", but he did not retreat. He had seen and conquered the world and the world had little to offer him in recompense for his worldly achievements. God blessed him, God consoled him and Jesus Christ was his Captain in the serried ranks of Life's last battle with death. 

It was in the wintry month of February 1910, that Monsignor Heinen was called to Philadelphia to investigate the proposal for the purchase of a parish school property. He inspected the property which was some distance from the railway depot, and owing to the trolley strike which was then in progress, he was obliged to walk a great distance in the inclement and stormy weather. He caught a cold which was followed by chills and fever. A doctor was called to attend (58)

Life's Last Battle 

59 Monsignor Heinen and all the necessary precautions were taken to prolong his life. A nurse was first obtained and then later a male attendant was obtained to care for the fast failing shepherd of souls. When he realized that he was growing weaker he directly asked his physician about the probability of death. "Does it probably mean death? I want to know. 

You are not a Catholic, doctor, and you may not fully understand. I am a soldier. Death means that I must make my report to my General. I want to be prepared, if I am to die. Tell me, is there humanly speaking, in your opinion, any hope? Yes or No?" "Very little, Father, I regret to say." "Thanks, doctor. It means that I am to go home."

He then prepared himself for the end by going to confession and received the Last Sacraments. After some time he asked: "Will you get me my last will and read it to me?"

Several things were changed and all preparations for the end were made. He completed his work with the exactness of a strategist and was militant to the end, he saw his friends standing about his bedside but never faltered nor lost courage in his fight. To one of his nearest friends, Rev. Paul

Licisky, now rector of St. Michael's Church, Lansford, he remarked "Nothing else remains for me, but to make a perfect act of contrition."

The young priest stood by his patron and spiritual guide.

And yet the men who as missionaries just before and after that preserved the faith of thousands of Catholics whose distance from church made their services of religion so difficult were really doing greater work for the Church by the saving of souls rather than the building of magnificent enduring structures.

You have the glory here in the Lehigh Valley of having among your great militant missionaries Monsignor Heinen whose work meant so much. His militant personality, as 

Monsignor Fink, one of his successors in the parish of Allentown, notes, won him the distinction of having founded during his career no less than forty-two parishes. No wonder that his career should have been chosen for description by Reverend Dr. Heuser under the nom-de-plume of Pastor Halloft.

Monsignor Heinen was a veritable incarnation of will power. He came on the missions to his finely prepared. 

He had had the advantage of military training in the German army and he had passed through the law school of a German

69 university when he decided that he would take up missionary work. In 1868 he was ordained to the priesthood and sent to take up his work in the sparsely settled region of the Lehigh Valley. His first residence was at Lehighton, and his next pastoral residence was at Mauch Chunk. Since my years as a boy these names have been familiar because four times a year going and coming to the college at Fordham I used to pass by these places so that they seemed very near home. From here Monsignor Heinen carried on his work. 

His missionary zeal radiated over the great middle portion of the Philadelphia archdiocese. His work was done among the poor, laborers who had been brought over by the companies or by various organizations in connection with the companies who wanted to have cheap labor. Before the mission of Christianity began, John the Baptist, you remember, sent some of his followers to ask Christ whether He was the Messiah or whether they were to wait for another to come. He told them to go back to John and tell him what they had seen, the blind see, the deaf hear, the lame walk, and the gospel is preached to the poor. It is that infallible sign that has always remained. Just look any Sunday morning on the streets of any of our towns and cities and note the churches to which the poor flock and from which they hurry homewards leaving place for others to come to the services, and you will find, of course, that it is the Catholic Church.

It was for these Catholics that Monsignor Heinen set himself bravely to work. Monsignor Heinen succeeded in keeping these Catholics from getting out of the Church. 

There were immense leaks, but it was not because the people were not Catholics in practice but because they had no priest to care for them. They were without the grace afforded them by the sacraments and their attendance at church and it was very easy for them to miss these duties, and hard to go on with the observance of religious duties. In the early

Monsignor Heinen days priests were few and it was almost impossible to afford the attendance at Mass and the sacraments. It was men like 

Monsignor Heinen who saved the situation and to whom the Lehigh Valley, Wyoming and Lackawanna, are indebted for the grace of Christianity."
