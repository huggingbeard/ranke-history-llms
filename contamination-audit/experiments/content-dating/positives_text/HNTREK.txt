FOREWORD 

This book is written in defense of Capitalism. Its precept is that 

Capitalism, or the private profit system, is in no way to blame for the vagaries of our economic condition, but that the operation of these methods are periodically clogged by our present blocked currency system which we insist on maintaining because of our false idea that by that system we foster our security.

Capitalism is sick. It has always been sick. It is being hampered and strangled, blamed and kicked about because of another's faultbecause of our muddled thinking on all things that relate to finance. 

Unless we recognize our fundamental trouble and take steps to correct it, there is no certain proof that all the present structure of our economic system will not be dashed 'mid the swirling of Communism, or Socialism, or chaos. 

No one will deny that something must be done, but let us take steps to study the problem before it is too late to revive the method that even in its hampered operation has brought us to a standard of living never attained under any other system.

No apologies are offered for the scant treatment of subjects in the shadows of whose magnitude the brief effort of this work is lost but if it charts our course of thought a little closer to that understanding which measures progress its object will have been more than accom plished.

THRIFT Let us consider that time-worn adage from Poor Richard's Almanac, penny saved is a penny earned.”

It is a remarkable tribute to the thoughtlessness of human nature that a qualification should be praised as an outstanding virtue when an analysis of its true meaning reveals it to be a startling sin against our fellow-men.

What is responsible for the mental and physical torture which a man experiences when no matter how hard he tries he finds it impossible to provide even the ordinary necessities of life for his family? What is it that causes his loved ones to go hungry-that causes him to lose that little home he has struggled so faithfully for years to provide? 

Thrift-because thrift says--"Do not buy the labor of a man when by letting him starve a while longer we can get his labor for less." 

There are billions of dollars of thrifty savings in this country that are waiting for what? Waiting for prices to stop declining so that they may be sure of no loss when they buy.

Let us tear aside the soft garments of righteousness with which this wolf in sheep's clothing is garbed and show the sharp fangs and cruel talons with which it tears at the hearts of men. 

Thrift-the word itself has a sharp sound. Thrift is selfish-it is thoughtless-it is unchristian and it is inhumanly cruel. 

Do you realize that when you spend a penny you give someone the work for which they have been hungering and striving for so long a time-the work they need to keep that little home-to feed the loved ones from whose little upturned faces they shrink when returning empty handed? Can you feel that pain or torture which is so keen that some birds will tear the flesh from their breasts to feed their young when no other food is available?

And then realize that all this suffering would be over if the people who have those billions of dollars in savings accounts would simply spend them. No wonder Jesus said: It is harder for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven than for a camel to crawl through the eye of a needle.

If he had been allowed to make this adage it would hardly have been, "A penny saved is a penny earned." It would more likely have been, "A penny spent is a blessed penny."

Unemployment is caused by the fact that we insist that money must possess two qualifications—one that it must be a store of value and the other that it must be a medium of exchange.

Money gives title to all goods and services and a person who keeps money and does not spend it is just as responsible for deaths due to unemployment as if he thrust a knife in the backs of those that perish. 

A man having money is in the same position as a man having a freight bill giving him title to a carload of oranges. Should he refuse to accept those oranges when they were delivered on the track in his city, and that refusal caused those oranges to rot and spoil, then that man would be the loser. But what about the man having money giving title to his fellow-man's services? Our medium of exchange is being used for purposes entirely antag onistic to its use as a medium of exchange and in fact we even go so far as to give a person a premium (interest on money) to prevent its use as a medium of exchange.

Take this store of value out of money and we will have a perfect medium of exchange.

Thrift doesn't build up wealth-it builds up debt.

PROPOSED

REMEDIES 

It has always been a matter of pride to Americans that their standard of living was considerably above that of the older nations, a general comparison being made with those in Europe. The forces which in the past have been responsible for creating this condition are becoming less and less active, and it is increasingly evident that the great American standard is being rapidly undermined and that it is becoming more and more equal to that of the older nations. 

We have prided ourselves for numbers of years that our people were not divided into classes, that all had an equal chance, but if we [5]

face a continuation of the forces which are at present affecting us, what will we produce? First, a class of unemployed with whom the farmers must be included, for anyone who is unemployed will be able to fall back on agricultural work, which, but for some miracle of legislation, will be faced with open competition. Second will come those employed under the rules covered by the N. R. A., who will in comparison with the farmers and unemployed subsist on the fat of the land; and third, those of more capable abilities whose knack of making money measures their reward at the highest point. 

The world today is facing a change in the social order. Governments are being redesigned-human relations changed, monetary experiments and bureaucratic control are the order of the day, and the whole underlying cause of the distorted condition which exists is the fact that we are fighting against a reduction in the standard of living which is facing a large percentage of our people. 

Having tasted the joys of a more abundant life it is only natural that we should struggle with increasing effort as we recede further and further from the enjoyment of those comforts which it is possible for us to visualize and of which we have had a taste. 

The reason for the increasing seriousness of the economic situation is the fact that we are emerging from a condition where many of our people have been self-sufficient, but today our dependence upon people whom we never see or will never know for practically all the things we need contrasts strikingly with the time we went to the store with a few dozen eggs and obtained in exchange material most of which very likely originated close to our home. Our self-sufficiency enabled numbers of our people to pass through a serious depression without changing their general condition to any great extent. 

Then, too, we were a young, undeveloped nation whose youth, when employed, could heed the advice of Horace Greeley who said, "Go west young man-go west," but today that development must be carried on by a much more complicated and intricate system of specialists whose dependence upon one another is complete. The standard of living which we have tasted and had dashed from our lips remains permanently planted in our memories and we are no longer satisfied with the life which self-sufficiency can evolve. And glimpses of that fuller life have been brought to us from time to time, not by any well ordered planning, but by accidents of fate which, in upsetting our economic equilibrium in the struggles between powers, created the condition which for a short time amid the raging conflict gave us the means of casting off the shackles which now hold us from enjoying the fruits of our ability to labor.

When the financial affairs of a country reach a point where the economic condition of a large proportion of the people is adversely affected there naturally comes a stronger demand for relief, but as business gradually improves and more people become reasonably satisfied then these demands subside, but in the history of a great country such as the United States a point has never been reached where the condition [6]

of the whole people could not have been materially improved in reference to the standard of living which we were capable of creating. 

Numberless explanations as to the cause of this fact have been advanced and, in the light of future knowledge, many of these theories and methods of remedy proposed and expounded by men held in high esteem will, if they are given any thought at that time, seem so ridiculous and outstanding examples of muddled thinking that we will wonder how they could have been given any consideration. Like the condition in 

France during inflation statesmen in good standing ventured every explanation for the cause of rising prices except the real reason, yet in spite of these things countries muddle through because life continues inexorably on its way. 

But anyone venturing an opinion or proposing a remedy is placing himself on record where his ideas are not judged in the present but in the future at a time when general knowledge gives everyone a greater and far more exact sense of perspective. 

The trouble with most theories and remedies is that a result is clearly visible and the most natural thing is to treat what appears as the very visible cause of that result, much the same as if a doctor, finding a patient in an extreme fever with a high temperature, would immediately say the patient is too hot and needs to be cooled and thereby proceed to apply cold water and ice bags. In this class would come the attempt to prevent over-production by reducing production when any serious analysis of the causes of over-production would show that no matter how much we reduce production there will still be overproduction at certain periods. 

Other theories attempt to create a remedy based on the fact that because something has changed and is no longer a part of our daily life that any adverse effect must be caused by that change which is much the same as a doctor who, being called to examine a patient who was sick and finding the patient had recently moved from another neighborhood, prescribes that the patient should return to the former neighborhood. Such a class includes those who feel that without foreign trade our country can never fully recover its stability, but those people never realize that foreign trade never was developed for countries to dispose of surplus goods but simply to obtain from other nations that which we do not produce or make as well as we do the things we send to them in exchange.

If a foreign country had something which our people wanted and did not produce is there anyone so simple as to consider that there is any obstacle to our obtaining that produce, providing we had something that the people of that foreign country wanted which they considered. as valuable as the material we wished to obtain? 

The talk about using foreign trade to dispose of our over-production is simply equivalent to trying to force a nation to take from us things which they themselves have in excess.

Gold has no place in finance today because it can accomplish nothing that could not by another method be accomplished in a more efficient manner. 

In primitive times people needed money with which to trade and adopted, in a general way, gold, for gold was known to most people in the world and possessed a reasonably stable value because of its limited production.

To explain the need of gold as a backing for currency the case of Ireland is perhaps the best and most recent example. 

When Ireland was fighting for her independence the people of that country were using English paper money as a medium of exchange and there was no hesitation in accepting this money, for it had a known value which the custom of its use had established. But when Ireland achieved independence her government demanded the right to issue money and proceeded to print Irish paper pounds, shillings, etc. 

When this new money was offered to the Irish people they naturally questioned its value, for they knew they could not use it to pur chase materials in England and they were not at all sure it would be acceptable to other Irish people, for, after all, it was simply a scrap of paper. So the question arose, "What is it worth?"

Thereupon it was up to the Irish government to establish a value for that Irish pound, and one of the most acceptable ways is to specify it as worth a certain amount of gold, in which case the Irish government must be willing to take the paper pound and give the holder the specified amount of gold at any time such an exchange is demanded. 

But after the Irish nation has existed for a number of years the value of that paper pound will become established by the custom of its use and accepted much the same as the English paper money was accepted, and for purposes of domestic trade the gold backing no longer is necessary as long as the government has the confidence of the people -maintains law and order and does not destroy the value of the money by printing more of these Irish paper pounds in the effort to get something for nothing.

But while nothing is necessary to maintain the value of the Irish pound within Ireland, its value in England is dependent upon certain definite factors, chief of which is the ability of the Englishman who takes the Irish pound to get something for it that he wishes. If he owes a man in Ireland a pound, then he will give for that Irish paper pound a value about equal to the value he received from the man in Ireland. 

Should the Irish government be willing to give the same amount of gold to the Englishman that the British paper pound was considered worth, then the purchasing power of the Irish pound in England would be equal to that of the English pound.

But if the Englishman was unable to get gold for the Irish pound the value of Irish pounds in England would be decided by the law of supply and demand. If the English held a large number of Irish pounds and owed nothing to Irish people, then the value of those pounds would [11]

decline. If, on the other hand, the Irish people held a lot of English pounds for which they could get nothing they wanted, and they owed no money to Englishmen, then the value of the Irish pound would tend to increase in comparison to the English pound.

But the value of this Irish pound in Ireland will be kept fairly equal to its value in England by the law of supply and demand unless artificial forces such as the gold standard or a fixed and guaranteed exchange for the English pound is arranged by the Irish government, in which case its value will depend upon this standard. But if the currency is allowed to find its own level, then its purchasing power in any country will quickly equalize itself, for if a bushel of wheat in Dublin costs one Irish pound, but that same Irish pound would buy two bushels of wheat in England, including the freight to Dublin, then all purchases of wheat would be made in England which, other commodities being in the same ratio, would soon result in an excess of Irish pounds in England and their value would decline until the price of wheat was reasonably near equal.

It has been the habit of countries in the past to control, as far as possible, the value of their money in foreign countries, and for this purpose gold has been shipped back and forth across the ocean innumerable times and at a cost entirely avoidable, for it can be easily understood that if the Irish government had arranged to give an English paper pound for an Irish paper pound, or vice versa, it would have been unnecessary to use gold with the attendant shipping expense. Not only that but gold laying in a treasury draws no interest, where, if it had been the same amount of English securities drawing interest which could be quickly converted into English money on demand, the saving by not using a gold backing would have been considerable. 

The confusion which attends the use of gold measures as remedies for our domestic economic situation arises in a measure out of the fact that gold is assumed to be money, while, as a matter of fact, gold has not been money either in foreign or domestic trade for a considerable length of time. The fact that gold has been given an entirely fictitious value because countries have controlled its supply does not change the picture, for in this case gold has been held level with money by artificial adjustments. Paper money is a commodity whose value is dependent in a general sense on the law of supply and demand. Gold, on the other hand, is a commodity entirely different from paper money which has a value also dependent on the law of supply and demand. The quantity of gold can change without the necessity of printing a larger amount of money; consequently, these two values can shift in relation to each other. 

A scientific definition of money would be that it is "The point of departure in a relation of values." There cannot be two points unless they exactly coincide and, since the value of money can change in relation to that of gold, one or the other cannot be money. In this case it is only necessary to use common sense to see that what we refer to in a relation of values is the token or paper money used in every-day transactions. [12]

In foreign trade the point of reference in a relation of values is not gold but the relation between the currencies of different countries. 

Gold is simply a commodity which is acceptable to any country when all other things offered in payment have been refused. 

It is on this misconception of gold that most remedies of this nature for our problem are based, but if it was understood that gold was not money it would be easily seen that any results by its manipulation could be more economically accomplished by other methods. 

If gold was money we would notice that if we offered a man in our country a piece of gold of a certain size it would obtain a certain quantity of materials. But if we offered him a piece half that size he would only profer half the amount in exchange. Not so with a gold dollar, for if we offer a man in our country a gold dollar half the size of our former dollar, we get just as much in domestic trade as we would get for the larger dollar. 

That is the reason why we are willing to give a dollar for a hundred pennies which are only actually worth about five cents—the value of the copper they contain. 

The fact is that increasing the price of gold places the country involved in the position of a factory that has reduced the price of its products, under which circumstances and particularly when the factory occupies an important position in the trade, it acts strongly to force the other factories to reduce their prices, placing the original fatcory in the position of a price cutter and chiseler. 

In order that the manipulation of gold may effect the price level of a country it presupposes a free flow of goods and gold or at least the psycholigical effect of such a condition but the present restrictions and tariffs on imports tends to block the effect that would normally attend the increasing of the price of gold in a country. 

The operation to increase the price level of a country by increas ing the price of gold might be illustrated by conceiving two bodies of water which are connected by a narrow river whose depth is equal to that of the lakes. It is evident that if the level of one of the lakes is lowered that water will flow from the other lake and the two will again reach a common level. But if the river was dammed so as to prevent the flow of water then the levels of those lakes could be held perma nently out of adjustment. It is also apparent that if the level of the lake which was lowered was considerably larger than the other lake that the level of the larger lake would not be raised appreciably. 

So it is with gold, for when a country raises the price of gold it lowers the price of its products, not in relation to its own money but in relation to all foreign money, causing a flow of goods to other countries which foreigners try frantically to prevent by tariffs, dumping duties and by adjusting their own price of gold. In the case of an important country like the United States the world prices are more likely to be dragged down to the level which we select than our price is to be raised. In the case of a small nation like Sweden the importance [13]

of her effects on world prices are so small as to pass almost unnoticed, for she represents a very small lake compared to the size of the lake which the rest of the world would present. 

If the majority of people were able to express an opinion as to the most serious aspect of our troubles they would probably agree that it was unemployment, but in the absence of a clear understanding of unemployment the remedy most simple to conceive is for the organization most capable the government-to provide jobs until everyone is working. So many people are advocating this remedy when any understanding of the real and underlying causes of unemployment would class such a remedy as exactly illustrated by the doctor who provides ice packs to cure a fever.

Aside from the financial chaos such a policy would involve we have another factor which is vitally concerned in this problem which is well illustrated by the old adage that you can drive a horse to water but you can't make him drink and as it might be stated to illustrate this point "you can give a man a job but you can't make him want to work,' which aptly illustrates the difficulties of socialism. 99 Therefore, just giving a man a job is not a cure for unemployment, for after we have given him a job he must have an incentive to work. 

CHAPTER III UNEMPLOYMENT 

If unemployment could be remedied in the sense that people desired to work there is no doubt that many of our problems would be eliminated and it is very necessary, therefore, that we understand the fundamental cause of this factor. In order to better understand this problem it must be stripped of all the complications and unnecessary elements that can be eliminated and be brought down to the simplest form possible. In order to do this we will visualize an island with three men, each of equal productive capacity, and these men are hedged about by all the restrictions which the use of money implies to civilized lands. Each of these men produces $7.00 worth of goods a year by working the entire time and it must be apparent that in order that a man may be enabled to dispose of all the goods he produces the others must purchase them from him by paying that man $7.00 and taking the goods which he has made.

In order to draw an analogy of this nature it must be understood that a man working in one of the larger factories today producing shoes generally does not personally use the shoes he produces but always sells his entire labor to another and thereby obtains money with which to purchase things he desires. Therefore, we are justified in saying that a man on the island being hedged about by the restrictions of civilized [14]

nations cannot use the goods he produces but must sell them to the other two in order to obtain the things he desires. 

It is therefore clear that if all the goods these three men produce are to be sold the total amount of money spent must be $21.00, the value of their combined production, and if this is effected each year these three men will be continually employed.

But suppose one of these men in a misguided way should feel that he ought to economize and decided, therefore, that instead of spending $7.00 that year he would save $3.00 spending only $4.00. In this case the two others will not be able to dispose of their entire production with the result that during part of that year they will be idle or in other words unemployed simply because that one man refuses to accept the goods which are represented by the $3.00 he has saved. 

Of necessity this example has been simple and complications have been eliminated, such as borrowing, excess spending and stored up capital, reduction of price level, etc., which in actual life enable us to to take up some of this excess under certain circumstances, but the vital result of this simple example has pointed to the clear fact that the saving of that money created the unemployment. 

Who benefited by this action? Certainly not the unemployed.

The one who saved the $3.00 received $7.00 in money from the others who did not save any money that year and it was only because the other two spent their entire salary and purchased that man's goods that he was able to save the $3.00. Beyond that point the other two will be unable to spend $7.00 next year for the income of one of the others is bound to be curtailed. In ordinary life this results in a decline in prices. because only $18.00 will be spent next year and the desire of people to sell their services makes them reduce the amount which they ask for their products so the total value of their products would tend to decline to $18.00

Another way of making the above points clear would be to lay the products of the work of these three men in a pile at the end of the year, under which circumstances there will be $21.00 worth of goods in that pile. In order to divide them equally each man must take $7.00 worth of goods, which is the exact equivalent of that man's total wages for the full year. Should one of those men decide to save $3.00 then there would be $3.00 worth of goods left in the pile and for what purpose-simply to rot- to be destroyed, which is called saving. 

Unemployment has no rhyme or reason and it must be evident from this analysis that unemployment comes from the refusal of certain people in a country to take their fair share of the country's goods perhaps because they have all the goods they desire and because they are selfishly unwilling to give any part of their share to others but prefer to let it rot or spoil certainly without benefit to anyone.

CHAPTER IV OVERPRODUCTION 

From the foregoing explanation it might be fair to state that the value of all production of all goods and services is equal to the sum total of all the wages, salaries, profits, rents, dividends, etc., in other words, is equal to all money received and unless the amount of money spent equals this amount something will be left over and this left over is called overproduction. 

If a man receives a salary of $50.00 a week he is producing new wealth at that rate and if he spends only $40.00 and saves $10.00 he is consuming only $40.00 worth of goods and creating an overproduction of $10.00 worth of goods, and this $10.00 worth of overproduction will remain on the market or have to be destroyed unless someone else is willing to spend $10.00 more than they receive, which means they must borrow the $10.00 or must go to the bank and draw $10.00 from their savings to spend for these goods. It is not sufficient that these people simply spend $10.00-they must spend $10.00 more than their income because they must balance their own consumption and production before they can eliminate the overproduction of someone else. 

Not only must this greater amount be spent but it is apparent that there is a time limit for this spending which is measured by the length of time it will take the goods to spoil or if they are not perishable goods the time it might take for them to become obsolete. It would also be measured in a more accurate way by the amount to which retailers and [17]

wholesalers limit their stock. If in normal business these people will carry, for example, a sixty-day stock then when business begins to decline a ninety-day stock might be accumulated, so it is very probable that if money that is saved is not spent either by a borrower or by the saver within thirty days that the effect of that saving creates the unemploy ment.

Overproduction and unemployment have practically the same effect as far as results are concerned because unemployment is the lack of production of goods that cannot be sold at the existing price level, while overproduction is the production of goods that cannot be sold at the existing price level.

The effect of overproduction or unemployment on the market is to cause a decline in prices because persons holding goods that cannot be sold will reduce their price in order to dispose of them and this reduction in the price of new wealth undermines the value of all other wealth, old and new. For if a man possesses a number of bushels of potatoes for which he paid $1.00 per bushel and more potatoes are being produced than can be sold then the person first offering a bushel of potatoes for 90c causes the others to tend to meet his prices. 

This is much the same in real estate and property for the existence of unemployment will cause people to compete with each other for jobs with the result that new houses can be produced for less than the old houses and the value of the old property must be adjusted to meet this changed condition.

It is this undermining of the prices which plays havoc with finances, for if banks are holding mortgages against this property and its values decline then these loans become unsound, and since our financial structure is built upon debts of this nature the entire structure begins to crumble. 

The simplicity of the effort to end overproduction by reducing production will be clear when these points are understood, for the amount of money saved represents, in a very exact manner, the amount of overproduction under conditions where loans are not being made for the purpose of consuming this overproduction. 

As was stated before, saving money can be counteracted by another person spending more money than they receive, accomplished by borrow ing or otherwise, but when everyone is saving and none are willing to lend this condition is considerably aggravated. If we consider that there is too much production and in order to remedy that condition we attempt to reduce the amount that is produced then the income of each person will be cut by the amount that their hours of labor are reduced, so that if a man received $25.00 a week and his hours are reduced so as to cut his wages to $22.50 per week such a reduction in his salary does not affect his will to save and if he continues saving nothing has been accomplished.

In a similar way the effort of the government to eliminate unemployment by giving everyone a job who wishes to work can only result in a financial wreck. This is exactly the same as the government [18]

buying all the goods for which there is no market at the prevailing price level in an effort to eliminate overproduction, for in order to ac complish this the government must spend more money than it receives, in other words it must borrow these savings which represent the overproduction. It is not sufficient to simply tax people and spend this money, for taxes represent the income of the government and the government must first balance its own consumption and production before it can assume the obligation of taking up the overproduction of its citizens. It is also a fact that no matter how much taxes we make a man pay he can still save something. If he receives $25.00 a week and has to pay $10.00 of this to the government his will to save remains intact. The only way to eliminate overproduction by this method is for the government to borrow all these savings and spend them and it is reasonably clear that this cannot continue without creating a desperate condition in finance.

When one person saves-someone else must be prepared to spend more than their own income to the exact extent that the other person saves if we wish to keep the income of the country at the existing rate. 

A great many people say that confidence must be established before business will pick up, but let us analize confidence.

Confidence means that people feel that conditions are such that a profit can be made in business transactions and that if they spend their money they will be able to earn more in the future. 

But waiting for confidence is to place our dependence upon a very delicate and elusive thing which can be startled and put to flight by the slightest sound. It is as if we went to the people who had money and said, as we might to a frightened child, "everything is going to be all right now- -go ahead and spend your money and invest it. Please hurry or more people will starve to death if you don't. Now please, please spend your money. 99 

Is not such a condition shameful? If spending money is necessary to prevent starvation is it right to depend upon such an uncertain thing as confidence? Why not give the government power to control the spending of money, then we could say in a manly way to those people who had money-"It is vitally necessary for the well being of our country that you spend or invest your money. If you do not you will wreck your neighbor's business."

There are those people who feel that a Planned Economy would solve the problem of leveling out our periods of slack business, but the clearest argument against this proposal is that in all the experience in history the execution of public works in immense volume has not shown even a tendency to encourage the feeling that a further step would have led to a consummation of the aim.

Those who advocate this plan are certain that the fault lies with its management and that with properly timed and co-ordinated enterprises such a method would produce results, but any fair-minded person would expect to find some encouragement from the initial steps of the plan. [19]

Another objection to such an idea is that the time to build and create public works is when they are needed. It would have been a very serious obstacle to the development of the automobile if we had deferred the building of roads in the period from 1920 to 1929 until business had receded sufficiently to make this work necessary. This is also evident in building warships of different types we must have them when they are wanted-not when people need the work, for then it might be well too late.

While there may be many public works which could be deferred until economic conditions made the employment necessary, it will be conceded by many people that planning work to be executed at an indefinite future period is very unsatisfactory, for when the time arrives for their construction they may be found to be seriously out of date and obsolete. Planning for what we will need ten years hence would require better brains than the politicians of our present period have as yet exhibited.

Added to the above arguments it must be clear that it is impossible for the government to offset saving for any length of time. 

It must create individual spending from capital or loans. And when it is realized that a depression usually follows a time when loans have been expanded to an unhealthy volume, this factor for spending must be left out of the calculation and spending from savings is the only force left to offset savings. And it is pretty clear that this effect will be badly crippled at a time when declining prices and activities are clearly in evidence. 

Therefore, the only effect of a planned economy would seem to be to defer the day of reckoning a year or two.

Take the example of a single farmer-a pioneer who is so isolated that his entire product must be used for himself and his family. By dint of intense energy he can produce a certain standard of living and let us suppose he and his family have produced sufficient food, clothing, housing, etc., to satisfy their wants. But the man gets an idea and invents labor-saving devices which enable him to produce what he has produced in half the time. What will happen? He has sufficient food, clothes and housing and it is not likely that he will double his production, not for long at least. He will, if he is energetic and dislikes idleness, find ways of producing better food or clothing and he will build a better house which means exactly that his standard of living has increased 100 per cent or to double what it was. 

Take the example of a factory that has been operating for a considerable time and has required 100 men to turn out its products. By the aid of labor-saving machinery and efficient methods they are able to produce the same amount of goods with the aid of 50 men, in consequence of which we will assume their products can be offered to the people for half its past price. Does this mean that 50 men are added to the ranks of the unemployed? This question can be answered by analyzing the market for the product of that factory and we find that where people have formerly bought the product of this factory, for say, $1.00 per unit, they now have to pay only 50c for what they need of the product. [ 20 ]

The question of whether the ranks of the unemployed will be increased by the 50 men depends entirely upon what people do with the other 50c which is left after they have purchased the factory's output. 

If they save it those men will be unemployed but if they spend it and buy something else then 50 other men will be employed and there will be no change in the total number of men unemployed. 

The whole question of whether we add to the ranks of the unemployed by labor-saving machinery could be answered by this simple question: Do the people continue to spend as much money as they did previous to the saving made by the machinery? 

For example, those who advocate a planned economy feel that it is necessary to determine exactly how much bread should be produced in a certain period. Such an idea fails to take into consideration that while the amount of business that was done in 1928 or 1929 may have been over double that of 1932 or 1933 the amount of bread made during either period was probably very nearly equal. But why didn't they produce twice as much bread in 1929? For the very simple reason that the profits in the making of this bread would have been far below that which existed in other industries, in consequence of which capital and labor would drift to those other industries. 

It is this factor which always has and always will control the actual overproduction in industries and makes unnecessary any artificial control, this force being just as immutable as the law of supply and demand of which it is a part. 

CHAPTER V SAVING 

MONEY

A clear knowledge of what saving money really means is necessary to an understanding of the problem under consideration, for it must be clear that if the variation in the total of the savings accounts in the banks is used as a basis of determining whether more or less money is being saved that our calculations will be considerably out of line. 

Most people think of savings as the amount of money in the savings accounts in the banks but these accounts have no relation to the amount of money saved. The bank credits to which we refer simply represent the amount of money that a bank has loaned or invested and the totals of these savings accounts fluctuates entirely according to the increase or decrease in the amount of money that a bank has loaned. If a person borrows money from a bank, or a bank invests money, the bank credits (savings accounts, etc.) will increase and if a bank calls a loan or the bank itself fails these savings accounts decrease. Saving money has nothing whatever to do with the increase or decrease in the total amount of the bank credits. [21]

Then in order to understand saving money we must have a clear conception of what it really means.

Saving money means being careful with money. It means a lack of spending or not spending. If we could buy a thing and don't, we have saved that much money.

In order to measure the amount of money that has been saved in a certain period the nearest approach we can get is to take the difference between the bank clearings in one period and those of the next period, but allowance must be made from this figure for exchanges of goods which do not affect employment such as stock and bond transactions. 

Saving money, insofar as it is detrimental, concerns only those things which create unemployment, and transactions in stock simply mean exchanges of money that is already saved. 

Saving money means a slowing down in the reciprocal velocity of the exchange of money, or in the velocity with which money changes hands, so that a person who received $25.00 a week and spent only $20.00 would cut down the income of the country 20% if such a condition was general with the rest of the people and no money was loaned to spend.

It may be recognized that such a saving is not permanent, for a person saves money in order to have money to spend later, but the trouble is that when the income of a country has been cut 20% it is equivalent to 20% of the people having no income whatever, under which circumstances the spending of the money that has been saved does not by any means replace that entire income or bring it back to the point that existed at the start of the saving. 

This is caused by the fact that 20% of the people have no money to spend whatever and this has resulted in the income of those who originally had $25.00 being reduced to $20.00, so that the spending of the money that had been saved would only replace the entire wages of 80% of the people and we would still have 20% without an income. 

Therefore, savings create a condition where it is necessary not only to stop savings to get back to a full division of income, but a condition where loans from capital and spending of savings must be sufficient to make up the income of all the people. 

Under a normal operation of the price level the amount of money being spent tends to spread over all people by the reduction in wages as the amount of money decreases, so that while a person's pay may be reduced from $25.00 a week to $20.00, the drop in prices causes his real wages to remain stable, but a drop in the price level is not accompanied by a drop in debts, which causes most of the trouble accompanying a condition of this nature. 

Saving money acts exactly on business in the same manner that friction acts on an automobile-it is a continual drag on the velocity of circulation-continually tends to retard the speed of exchange and, while rising prices and some other phenomena are a power to overcome this friction, just as soon as this phenomena subsides the action is exactly [23]

like turning off the power in an automobile-its speed continually decreases.

Perhaps this can be illustrated by the following example in order to show how the exchange of goods between people who are extremely anxious to make an exchange is blocked.

A man in Michigan, whom we will call Henry, has a bushel of potatoes, and these potatoes would be a godsend to a man in St. Louis, whom we will call Bill, and since Bill has a pair of shoes which Henry greatly desires there is surely no common sense reason why they should not make the trade. Bill does starve to death, self-preservation is the first law of nature and we cannot blame J. P. if he refuses to part with his dollar.

Another self-evident fact is that people who have money will not spend that money except for necessities unless they see the oppor tunity to get that money back again when they have something to offer in exchange. When prices are declining one is very apt to lose in an exchange of goods for money because money's value is the direct reverse of all other values. [24]

But is there one single redeeming feature in saving money that benefits the community or the people as a whole? If the community is prosperous then its people will be prosperous and while saving may temporarily benefit an individual it deals a death blow to the community and like a boomerang strikes back at the individual. 

It cannot even be considered that saving creates capital that may be invested and thus is the basis of activity for we have seen that before savings can be created someone must borrow money from a bank which conclusively proves that the activity and business must come before the savings can be effected. Business activity is what creates savings and business inactivity destroys them. 

Even the money which a government prints must be first used to pay for some activity before it can be saved. 

CHAPTER VI TAXING 

MONEY

If money could be made to lose its value at a slightly greater rate than the rate of decline in the prices of commodities then periods where people hoarded or saved money to the extent that it strangled trade would be eliminated. In fact if a control of the desire to get rid of money could be effected then the direct volume of business could be controlled because business is measured by the rate of exchange of money.

One method of reducing the value of money is to increase its quantity which means printing money or, as it might be called, inflation, but there are so many excellent instances of the effects of this method in history of which anyone interested may avail themselves that this book will not be concerned with this method. 

Suffice it to say that under a system of increasing the quantity of money the rate of decline in the value of money becomes a matter which can in no way be calculated. If the rate of decline in the value of money under this system could be definitely calculated and properly controlled then it could be a great factor to create prosperity but it has repeatedly shown its inability to hold within prescribed limits or to allow any measure for calculating its changes of value. 

However there is another method that can be used to reduce the value of money that can be definitely controlled, clearly defined and calculated in advance. That method is to tax money. 

If a person has, for example, $1,000 in cash on hand and knows that if he keeps that money over a certain date in the year, say January 3, that money will be worth only $900 then it could be said that the value of that money declined 10% in his estimation. Under the present system of no tax on money it would be manifestly impossible to drop the interest rate on bank credits below par, for under those circumstances people would withdraw their money from the banks and the banks could do no business-but under a tax of 10% on Government money and a tax of only 5% on bank money the value of bank money is 5% greater than cash which forces money into the banks.

While the Federal Treasury, under this law, can issue an un limited amount of money, it must be understood that the amount of money in actual use under such a system is very likely to decrease. 

This method seeks to control the velocity of currency entirely and the object of issuing money is for the single purpose of regulating this velocity. If the money which has been issued does not circulate fast enough then issuing an additional amount to loan to people who will spend this money will cause the total circulation to rise and when the amount of turnover of currency has reached its desired volume any further issues can be stopped.

It must be clear that if a billion dollars turns over once its effect is exactly the same as 100 million dollars changing hands ten times. 

The only way that the quantity of currency can increase under this method is for someone to borrow money because of the possibility of making a profit by such a transaction, and one would hardly be expected to borrow money to keep when it carries with it a penalty of possession of 10%. Again an increase in the amount of currency cannot take place unless a bank has no surplus funds to loan, for if a bank had funds they would be foolish to go to the government to obtain them. 

The theory is that the government stands ready to loan money at a specified interest rate which can be obtained by printing press operation and under such circumstances the interest rate on all money must follow the interest rate published by the government. So long as the banks have surplus funds no money will be printed by the government and only the demand by people for money in excess of the money already issued will create any increase in the currency. 

If business begins to boom to an extent considered undesirable by the authorities, any loans made by the government need not be renewed and the calling of loans will reduce the amount of currency in the country. [ 28 ]

The aim of the commission in charge of interest rates would be to keep business at a point that would absorb all unemployment, but it must be understood that when the demand for labor becomes such that there is very little unemployment the tendency is for those remaining unemployed to become independent and the wage rate level begins to increase which causes prices and the price level to begin to rise. 

When the amount of money offered for spending exceeds the value of goods offered for sale then prices will rise. When the amount of money offered for spending is less than the value of goods offered for sale then prices will decline.

An increase or decrease in the price level does no great harm under a system of taxed money, but it would probably be best to maintain a stable price level with the tendency, if any, toward an upward curve. 

Therefore the commission governing business could arrange to raise the interest rates or decrease them according to the general trend of the price level and, while there may always be a minor amount of unemployment, that unemployment will be among those who do not wish to work or who will not work unless they can obtain more than their fair share of the country's goods. Such unemployment will act as a regulator of the price level.

When a government prints money and spends it that government loses control of that money and in order to recover this control, excess taxes must be levied, but when a government prints money and loans it on good security which may be temporarily depressed in value it has only to call those loans when business is good to recover control of the volume of currency, no taxes being necessary to accomplish this object.

Where there is no unemployment an investment of funds in almost any material value would maintain itself at par except for the fluctuations common to human enterprise. Therefore if instead of saving money people would arrange to invest their savings they would have no difficulty in providing for periods of sickness or leisure, or old age, because investments where no unemployment is present will maintain a stable value.

By controlling these excess funds the commission could also control the expansion of bank credits with its inflationary tendencies.

CHAPTER VII CONCRETE EXAMPLES 

Let us take a definite example and assume that congress has passed such a law deciding that 10% tax must be paid on January 3 of each year and that the commission having been appointed by the President has met and decided in view of the stagnant condition of business and the prevalence of unemployment that the United States Treasury will loan money to the Federal Reserve at a discount of 4% which means that the Federal Reserve will give the Federal Treasury its note for $1,000.00 which does not bear interest, this note to run for a period of one year and receive in return $1,040.00 in currency-and [ 35 ]

this currency would be lawful legal tender of the United States for the balance of the current year through until midnight of January 3 of the next year at which time whoever might have possession of that currency, whether bank or person, must pay the government a tax of 10%.

It must be understood that this note cannot be repaid to the United States Treasury until the due date of the note which for purposes of illustration may be July 1, 1936, or until after the tax on money has been paid which will be due January 3, 1936. If it is for any reason desirable to repay this note before January 3, 1936, then $1,100.00 would be required to discharge the obligation. 

This tax of 10% must be paid after January 3, 1936, on all the currency paper money or coins which are at that time in circulation or that were coined or printed and put in circulation during the previous year and the person or institution holding the currency must pay the 10% tax. There can be no question of avoiding the tax by concealing the money because this money is stamped plainly with the year it is legal tender and if a person does not care to pay the tax on January 3 no penalty will accrue for this act but the very first time it is offered as payment it will only be accepted at 90% of its face value and as soon as it is turned into a bank it will be withdrawn from circulation. If a $10.00 bill dated as legal tender for the year 1936 was offered to another person in 1938-it would be accepted at 80% of its face value, in other words, the person who offered it would treat it exactly as if it was $8.00 and not $10.00. Should the date of the bill be 1936 and it was offered in trade in 1947-it would have lost its entire value and could only be considered as waste paper. 

The commission who decides the rate of interest which the Federal Reserve must charge for money would make that decision to cover the interest rate for an entire year if possible until the expiration date of the money, January 3. This rate could not be easily changed during the year since this would result in some confusion making it difficult and complicated to figure the interest on mortgages and all borrowed money which will be based on the published interest rates. 

If the commission decides the interest rate to be minus 4% it does not mean that small borrowers can borrow money at this rate. 

The charge to borrowers of all sorts will depend upon the expenses which accrue to the investigating granting handling and collecting of a loan based on its individual nature, each class of loan resting on its own merits and costing a certain percentage over the basic rate of minus 4%. 

Whatever the differential has been in the past in good banking practice will apply under a system of taxed money and good commercial loans have generally cost 6% at a bank while the bank has had to pay interest on deposits which we will say averaged 3% in the past. Since a bank is responsible for paying the 10% tax on cash which it has on hand, this item of expense will have to be charged against interest and will result in a reduction in the interest on deposits of about 1% which reduces the average interest rate to 2%, making a 4% spread between [36]

the interest rates to cover the expenses of banking and the tax. These illustrations are, of course, assuming that a bank will have expanded its cash into bank credits to a ten-fold figure. 

The cash which banks may have in excess of the 10% reserve requirements would undoubtedly accumulate as at present, in banking centers such as New York City, where large amounts of money may find a market and these funds would be handled by brokers for a small commission.

In the case of the depositor who wished to deposit the $50,000.00, the bank to whom it was offered, would not treat this amount as a deposit but would forward it to the New York Center for possibilities of disposing of it. 

These excess funds in New York City would be a decided block to issuing any additional currency by the Federal Reserve because they would always be offered at a slightly lower rate than that of the Federal Reserve, and if a large amount of this money existed the discount quoted would probably begin to exceed the Federal Reserve rate as the day to pay the tax approached.

However, as this rate declines, money becomes more and more a possibility of profit and the force to borrow and spend increases with each reduction in the price of these excess funds. Added to that is the chance that the original owner of these funds will recall them and find a method of disposing of them at a better advantage than that offered at the money centers. 

If one analyzes this excess reserve it will be fairly clear that it can never be a large factor and a very small amount will effectively block the printing of any additional currency which makes an ideal condition for vital control by the commission in charge of interest rates, for by only retiring a small amount of these excess funds by selling government bonds in the open market, any strain could be easily relieved.

It would undoubtedly be an object of the commission to prevent these funds from creating a demoralized money market since the interest rate set by the commission at the start of the year would be the desired rate and by creating a balance in these funds the rate could be closely controlled. 

The question of the price, as well as the yield on government bonds, under the system of taxed money, is an interesting point. These bonds at present hover around par with yields up to 4% at times, with short term bonds considerably less than this figure. 

There is no doubt that with the installation of this system there may be a readjustment in interest rates which will not be clearly defined until its operation is well under way. If we take the example of the bond yielding 4% at present interest rate, it would under a taxed money plan, be listed as bearing interest at 2% above the basic interest rate. Should this basic interest rate be minus 2% the yield on the bond would be nothing. [ 37 ]

Such a calculation assumes that the normal basic interest rate would be 2%, representing the rate which the Federal Reserve would charge banks under normal conditions. This rate would allow the banks to loan money for ordinary commercial purposes at the rate of 

6%, the difference, 4%, being used to pay the tax of 1% on money and the expenses of the bank which are assumed for purposes of illustration to be 3%.

Should the Federal Reserve wish to increase its interest rate, to say 6%, then the yield on a bond specifying 2% above the basic rate would jump to 8% unless that bond stipulated that no interest would be paid above 4%. But if this interest rate was not limited in these and other bonds it would be far more effective in controlling a boom in business which was considered for any reason detrimental than it would be under a condition where the rate was limited, for it withdraws farther and farther the possibility of the potential purchasing power tied up in these bonds being converted into active purchasing power and creating an additional stimulus to the already excessive rise in prices.

However, it is clear that under our present blocked currency sys tem where the lowest interest rate is near zero, the tendency for busi ness to expand seems to be lacking which would seem to indicate that the basic interest rate of plus 6% would stand extremely small chances of ever becoming a reality. 

If the basic interest rate should be placed at minus 4% a bond specifying 2% above the basic rate would be subject to an assessment of 2% and while such an assessment would not be paid it would apply against any future payments of interest on that bond and would act to reduce the market price of the bond by the amount of the assessment against it. It would also allow a concern to set up a credit on its books to the extent of the amount of the assessment reducing its bonded indebtedness to this extent which would enhance its position as collateral for loans.

Since most loans are written at 6%, which means 4% above the basic rate, the Federal Reserve rate would have to exceed minus 4% before an assessment would become a fact, but this would mean that all interest rates were 6% below normal which would be an inconceivable figure.

There would be no trouble collecting such an assessment from a bank loan since these loans will be renewed each year at which time settlement would be made. Should a private concern hold a mortgage against a home and refuse to accede to an assessment the mortgage could easily be placed with a bank or other party and the principal paid in full, less the assessment.

In case of a home loan organization an assessment would be placed against the deposit accounts which the company has which are prac tically held as deposits and paid to the persons owing the mortgage. 

There will no doubt be a great pressure to rewrite mortgages and all debts specifying a certain interest rate over the basic rate because [38]

banks will at practically all times, except during booms, have plenty of funds for loaning on such a basis and can always make a profit by such a transaction.

During a boom the basic interest rate could be placed at plus 2% which would set interest rates on loans which had been written at a percentage above the basic rate and loans by banks from the Federal Reserve could be as far as possible discouraged. Should the commission governing basic rates decide to try to discourage business further the basic rate could be raised but such an action will only come from further experience with taxed money and by the advice of the best financial brains of the country.

If a government bond should ever come to the point where its yield would be minus 1%, or when a person would be assessed 1% for its possession, the question of what its value or market quotation would be would depend upon conditions. One of the first factors to consider in this matter is what the possibilities are selling this bond and investing the money in a place which will not incur this liability. Bank credits will not answer for they are assessed and when it is considered that a negative interest rate clearly indicates declining business the possessors of corporation stocks are certainly in an even worse position. 

Even real estate offers no haven under declining business and while there will no doubt always be risky bonds which yield a high interest rate they certainly will not answer the requirements of possessers of govern ment bonds. In other words, no matter what interest rate is paid on government bonds, they maintain their ratio of yield in comparison with any other investment, and this condition will follow in all investments which represent debts.

Those investments which represent a participation in the profits are on the other side of the ledger and possessors of these investments will lose with a recession in business, but capital cannot find refuge in an investment which represents a debt because the minus interest rate represents on this type of investment the decline in value which an investment in stocks is undergoing because of the decline in business. 

Since all values are declining, no values can decline. 

A minus interest rate should quickly act to stimulate business, so this rate would not extend any considerable time, perhaps two or three years at an extreme estimate so if government bonds sold off say six points it would discount considerable of the assessments which might fall to its lot.

If these government bonds are written to conform to a specified premium over the basic interest rate it will result in their hovering around par, where if a stable interest rate was mentioned in the bond its market value would increase with each reduction in the basic interest rate.

A point that will be clear as our experience under this taxed money widens is that the risk in making loans will be considerably eliminated and holders of good securities will therefore find money easier to obtain [ 39 ]

and at much less cost than at present. There is reason to believe that as time passes very little variation in the interest rate will occur and adjustments will be less and less necessary. 

However changes in this rate may be brought about by the expansion in business caused by an invention or discovery which may create a large demand by people for the article which has been invented. In addition such an article may necessitate a tremendous investment in factories and methods of production generally called capital goods. 

An example of such an article is the automobile which together with the readjustments in real estate brought about the use of these automobiles was no doubt largely responsible for the expansion in busi ness during 1920 to 1929. Such a condition of course would cause a demand for capital and naturally affect the interest rate because of this reason, but it must be considered that our trouble in the past has not been created by a boom in business but by the after effects of this boom and it may be unnecessary therefore to seriously try to prevent a boom of this nature, but to correct the tendency to subsequent depression afterward by a lowering of interest rates until business is stabilized. 

Under the conditions created by this expansion in business the Federal Reserve or the Interest Rate Commission could entirely restrict increasing the currency, and to counteract overexpansion could contract the currency creating a high interest rate on borrowed money with the restraining effect on business which such a policy would have. 

Under conditions created by a minus interest rate wherein all bonds, bank credits, preferred stocks, etc., become an obligation and are subject to an assessment it might be considered that capital would attempt to find refuge in foreign currencies and that people would attempt to take their wealth out of the country. 

The technical effect of such an action would be to considerably increase the price of foreign exchange since there is a limited amount of this exchange available and a large demand would considerably increase the rate. Therefore a person who attempted to convert their wealth into foreign exchange would have to do so at a very disadvan tageous rate which would reduce his wealth. It is easily conceivable that only a small excess demand for foreign exchange would double its price under which circumstances in the operation of transferring wealth to foreign countries a shrinkage of 50% could be experienced which must be compared with the very small shrinkage due to the assessment from a minus interest rate.

Added to this is the fact that the demonstration afforded in the operation of this system in one country could easily influence foreign countries to copy its features under which circumstances the transfer of wealth would be decidedly hazardous.

The peculiar fact is that wealth which is of any importance cannot be transferred from one country to another. The only wealth we can send from the country is the product of its farms, mines, factories, etc., and at the present time under a blocked currency system this is one [ 40 ]

of the things we are trying very hard to do-against the opposition of the nationalism of every other country. What is wealth? It is represented perhaps popularly by money and from a previous analysis we have seen that the only money is token or paper money which has no intrinsic value since it only gives us title to the goods of our country. 

It gives no title to the goods of foreign countries for if a German brought some of his country's money to Detroit and tried to spend it there would be considerable difficulty. He must use his German money to buy American money before he gets to this country and this German money can only be used to buy something in Germany.

The fact is that most wealth cannot be transferred because among other things wealth is real estate and it would be interesting to see an American trying to pick up his farm and move it to Germany. Or his railroad or his department store or anything else of this nature. 

Taking stocks and bonds of American Corporations abroad would be useless for it makes no difference who holds these bonds they must pay the assessment.

When we consider that the only wealth that can be sent out of this country is the products of its farms, mines and factories and further realize that a minus interest rate clearly indicates declining business it must be understood that an attempt to transfer wealth to foreign countries under such circumstances could be but a benefit to business for there would be a large demand for these goods to export in order to obtain foreign credits.

Under a basic interest rate of minus 6%, to take a radical case which certainly never could exist, the small borrower would be entitled to a discount on his loans, on which loans he would have to pay no interest. 

The money for this discount would come from the assessment on bank credits which the banks collect themselves, and under a basic interest rate of minus 6% this assessment on these bank credits would be about 6%, from the proceeds of which the bank would pay its expenses andpay the tax on money and have a balance of about 2% of these credits which would be used to pay the discount on the loans which the bank has made.

The stable business conditions which must follow a system of taxed money, will enable a bank to grant a loan for a period of one year for values of the securities offered as collateral do not change, especially since the Federal Reserve itself will designate a basic price at which it will accept this security as a basis for a loan from the bank. 

The fluctuations in the value of securities under taxed money will be entirely due to the variation in human efficiency, the shiftings in population, etc., and such changes do not take place to any great extent in the short period of a year.

This would eliminate considerable of the banking expense of watching loans so closely, which in the past has made their continual renewal necessary and justify a loan being made for the period of a year.

While bonds, mortgages, preferred stocks, etc., can continue to be written at a set interest rate, this would tend to increase their market price considerably with each downward revision of the basic interest rate by the Commission, and while such a condition is not harmful, it is not desirable and will tend to interfere with the stimulating effect of a reduction in the basic interest rate. If these bonds are written to follow the basic interest rate by stipulating a certain premium over that rate, the method of control will be much more effective. The law by which Congress decrees this taxing money in effect could stipu late that all interest rates would be measured by starting from the basic rate, which would create a fair condition at once and eliminate much of the time a natural revision of interest rates would require. If the Pennsylvania Railroad had a 100 million dollar bond issue written, specifying a 4% premium over the basic interest rate, it can be understood that as business declines these bonds become much less of a burden, until, should the basic interest rate reach minus 5%, they would even be a source of income to the Railroad, making a much better condition than at present when as business declines they become an increasing burden.

In a previous analysis the reasons have been advanced to bear out the fact that there would continue to be a lively market for bonds subject to a minus interest rate, and should people try to sell them to avoid this liability there is obviously no investment which would offer any better ratio of return, since all values would be declining under a minus interest rate, which could only exist under a serious business depression.

Take the case of a merchant doing business at the rate of about $50,000.00 a month. At the start of the year this man would figure that at the end of the year he would have a bank balance of about $40,000.00 and would have about $10,000.00 in cash, which would be received too late to deposit in the bank. We will assume that the published basic interest rate at the start of the year was minus 2%. 

Therefore, he is going to have to pay because of the tax on money and on his account at the bank, a tax amounting to $1,000.00 on the cash on hand and a tax of 2% on his commercial account amounting to $800.00 making a total of $1,800.00. Since this situation is clear at the beginning of the year that merchant will arrange to pro rate the tax over his $600,000.00 of business, making 3/10 of 1% which would have to be added to the price of his product that year, so it must be apparent that this tax will not be a burden to that merchant.

Take the instance of a small concern in a normal city who owed the bank $1,000.00. Such an indebtedness would be of small matter when business was flourishing and a normal rate of 6% could easily be paid, but as business tapers off we find this loan becomes less of a burden to that concern until a condition may be reached where no interest at all needs be paid. This is in direct contrast to the conditions today, when, as business recedes, the debt becomes an increasing burden. 

Should the bank demand payment for this loan, the mortgagee could easily borrow from other sources, providing his security was valued not at depression levels, but at the level its value would bring under a predetermined price level, which means its approximate replacement value at a certain basic wage rate less reasonable depreciation, because the Federal Reserve would be ready to loan money up to a certain point, using this value, the bank receiving its compensation from the minus interest rate on bank credits.

It might be advanced that people would not take this money, but it must be clear that they already have the money in their possession and have given for that paper money a very definite value and must consider it as worth what they gave for it. It would be ridiculous to consider that business would stop dead upon the knowledge that a [43]

Since no more money has been printed, people have no more money than under normal circumstances, and since the debts which are owed them are bearing interest, they would be foolish to call these loans in order to get money that they must consider worthless in order to purchase something worth no more than the debt. In inflation entirely different financial relations exist and a government in a desperate effort to stimulate business or to obtain credit prints money and spends this money. In all the early stages of inflation the effect of this printing and spending dies down in a short time and in order to stimulate business it is necessary to resort to the same strategy and this condition continues until with a tremendous amount of money issued confidence is lost and chaos ensues. 

But it will be noted in this description, which can be verified by any study of the subject, that spending the money was what created the activity, and that activity subsided in a short while-in fact, shortly after the suspension of the spending. But notice that in taxing money there is no necessity for the government to spend or print money unless there is an entire absence of inflationary tendencies and under these circumstances none of the actual and real causes of inflation are pres ent. The decline in the value of actual cash is very real, but to avoid this decline it is simply necessary to deposit the cash in a bank to avoid the depreciation because the decline in the rate of check money or bank credits is at an entirely different rate than that of cash and the amount of actual cash carried on hand is generally very small. The main body' of cash is in the banks, being used as a base for the bank credits, and the tax on this base is spread over these credits.

Hoarding- meaning keeping money in strong boxes and mattresses-will be eliminated and it is very probable that the nation's currency can be reduced by the amount of this hoarding, and this in itself will be a great boon to banks, making them stronger and safer because, when a person takes money from a bank and keeps it out of circulation, they reduced the possibility of bank credits to 10 times that amount.

With the foregoing giving some basis to the argument that we will have no pressure for unusual spending unless it is desired to have that pressure, let us turn to the possibilities afforded by this act to create spending. It is a peculiar fact that a man of very high standing in the economic world has denied that this tax on money will create spending.

It would hardly seem necessary to have to bring arguments to support this point for it must be understood that this suggested tax of 10% on cash need not be followed if it is found to be inadequate, for it can be increased or decreased as found desirable in practice, and no limits as to its amount can be set up. Added to this is the fact that the tax on bank credits can be varied up to within a small difference, but bank credits must always be more valuable than cash in order to make it desirable to keep the cash in the control of the banks. Also there is no escape from this pressure, for anyone who has money to spend it so as to avoid the tax. Anything which represents money is subject to this pressure to get rid of money, for it is very clear that the value of money is declining and at a known and predetermined rate. It is not a rate which may suddenly impel itself on our senses, but a steady continual loss of value, and when money is losing its value it means that certain things opposite to money are increasing in value, in fact, the whole object is to give people the idea that money is decreasing in value faster than do other things.

It is a known fact that during the inflation in France people would take almost any reasonable chance to avoid a known loss or a loss that was sure to occur. If this is the case then it is a clear point in favor of the fact that a person having so liquid a thing as bank money or cash only needs to invest this money in material things to be able to avoid this loss even under a very slight burden on these credits. 

It would certainly be clear that a heavy tax would cause a wild flight from money which would cause a rise in prices equivalent to the desire to get rid of money. But we must never lose sight of the fact that these taxes can be controlled to a very fine point and the pressure to spend can be adjusted to exactly fit the occasion of its use. 

Beyond the fact that a pressure to spend money is present there is another element which adds to the importance of this feature and that is the fact that when the interest rate has been reduced to a point below the normal interest rate money is said to be cheap. This feature in itself is another factor for creating spending, for a person who bor rows money when business is bad knows that that money will not cost [ 46 ]

him much until business has recovered and is again functioning normally. This will be a force for starting the heavy capital goods industries when other things are moving slowly. 

But while examples of extreme conditions have been given none of these features will be present in the normal functioning of this system of taxed money, for a depression does not occur suddenly but is apparent from certain indications, particularly unemployment, many months in advance of a real decline. By correcting this unemployment through the method of reducing the interest rates a slowing down of business can never be experienced.

At the present time and under the present blocked currency system the reducing of interest rates to a very low figure would have no particular effect in increasing loans to industry, because there is no particular effect in increasing loans to industry because there is no bottom to the value of any security and banks are not justified in loaning money on anything but gilt-edge collateral, which is generally in the possession of very conservative people, who find it unnecessary to take any chances to advance their interests. But under taxed money the gov ernment has set a basic wage rate, from which is built a definite price level or security value, which is the basis of determining the amount of loans which the Federal Reserve stands ready at any time to accept as security.

It is commonly accepted by economists that any price level is satisfactory and works best which is the price level to which we are accustomed, so the commission governing interest rates would be justified in cutting those rates should the price level show signs of declining, but since this price level is tied up very closely to unemployment, the fact is that unemployment begins to decline before the price level tapers off so that either index could be used.

Suppose a man had a home which cost $20,000.00 to build in a normal period, and a bank had accepted this home as security for a loan of $10,000.00, but since business was very bad this house had declined to a market value considerably less than $10,000.00. At this time, however, suppose the interest rates for loans to banks from the Federal Reserve were set at minus 4%, under which circumstances the bank credits would be assessed 4%, which means in plain terms that the depositors are paying the interest on this $10,000.00 loan. Under these circumstances the loan on that home is sound because the bank is collecting the interest on that loan and should depositors at any time demand their money this loan could be discounted at the Federal Reserve at full value. Under these circumstances if the house could be rented for a little over sum of the taxes and insurance, it would represent no loss to the bank or if the bank had not foreclosed the owner could continue in possession, paying only 2% interest on the mortgage. 

It goes without question that the desire of depositors to avoid the assessments on their accounts would soon lead to a demand for investments and the real estate would rise in value until the price level had reached [47]

If the interest rate should be kept at a subnormal point after business had reached a proper activity, then prices would continue to rise with wages following for a period equal to the length of time the interest was subnormal and this rise would be marked by crashes at indeterminate periods caused by the price level running ahead of the wage level and having to recede until wages caught up with it. These rises would be marked at various times with inflation from the expansion of bank money, causing a more serious reaction because of the greater amount of money which could be issued as bank credits. Such a condition, however, could not continue unless the interest rates were subnormal, and an adjustment of these rates would prevent the continued rise.

During a serious rise of this nature, the crashes would be caused in a measure by the values of materials running far ahead of the estab lished price level which marks the lending point value of collateral at the Federal Reserve, but if this lending point price level was adjusted upward from time to time by the Federal Reserve these readjustments would take place without causing a serious reaction and prices would adjust themselves without serious financial discomfort to anyone and the rise would shortly continue.

In a rise of the price level of this sort an adjustment of the foreign purchasing power of our currency unit would be necessary as the prices of goods and materials rose in terms of our paper dollar, and such adjustments would be necessary to prevent our country from being flooded with goods of foreign manufacturers, the result of which would be that many people in our country would be working for foreign owned corporations. 

While the problem of the banks would be easily solved by assessing the bank credits, that of the insurance companies, and others dependent upon interest rates from loans is by no means so simple, but it must be realized that the stimulation of production might never require so drastic a reduction as a 4% minus interest rate, and a decline of one or two per cent in the interest rate at certain periods, coupled with an understanding of the crime of saving money, would be sufficient to stimulate business to any desired extent. 

While at the start of operation of such a system certain troubles, such as the problem of insurance companies may occur, the commission responsible for the adjustment of the interest rate could act very cautiously and reduce the interest much less than might be considered necessary to control the situation, but it must also be considered that the losses due to a lack of interest from investments would in no way [51]

compare to the loss of entire investments which exists under the present system of finance.

The response of business to the change in rates should be apparent within a year and when it is realized that the depression of 1929-34 took five years to run its course no serious consequences could have occurred if the decline had been stopped in 1930.

Also the insurance companies, under normal business, provide for a period of bad business by building reserves in anticipation of just such a contingency.

It must also be apparent that an understanding of the crime of saving money and the results of such an act will gradually result in a person naturally avoiding hoarding so that eventually no adjustment in the interest rate may be necessary and we will have a continual flow of business.

At the time this system is adopted by this country it is not probable that business will be of a satisfactory volume and its adoption will undoubtedly be a signal to speculators to push up values such as stocks and commodities. This is a perfectly normal activity as far as conditions are concerned for the shares of the stock companies will be at a low value when there is little business so an increase in business, which would come from a reduction in the interest rate, would very naturally increase the value of the shares. With the revival of business a basis of further advances in share prices would result in a further rise but the trouble with speculation lies in the fact that speculation feed on a rise and we can expect prices to be forced up to such an extent that a reaction must be expected. 

This decline will mark a critical point in the administration of this system because we will reach a point where it must be made apparent to the people of this country that no more capital is needed and that from that point consumption must increase considerably to the extent that people must accept a higher standard of living instead of continuing to pile up investments. Or those who do not wish to accept a higher standard of living must be made to understand that they cannot continue to produce if they do not intend to consume. 

There is no doubt that commodity prices will also increase due to speculation but these prices cannot run very far ahead of wages and it is best to keep the basic wage rate as near level as it is reasonable to do. During the past century wage rates in the United States have risen until at present they are about seven times what they were in 1840, but this rise was accompanied by an increase in the efficiency of production, which matched the increase in wages. If this increase in the efficiency of production had not occurred it would have been necessary to adjust our gold standard continually or our prices would have been far out of line with those of foreign countries who did not have a corresponding increase in their wage rates. 

Therefore, if the commission in control of the interest rates should increase the desire to spend money to the extent that there is [52]

a demand for goods exceeding the supply a continual rise in the wage rate will result. If, on the other hand, there is a less demand for goods than there is a supply, the inclination is for wage rates to decline. An increase in the wage rate level of moderate proportion is not harmful if accompanied with a similar change in the efficiency of production but if not then adjustments must be made in our foreign exchange rates to correspond with this change. 

If such changes are not made in foreign exchange rates then an increase in our investments in foreign countries will be continual if our added efficiency exceeds our wage increase or, on the other hand, foreign nations will increase their investments in United States values very materially if our wage level increases faster than our efficiency of production.

Such a condition would not exist under the operation of present currency because if a foreign nation should be able to sell a large amount of goods in our country there would be a very serious objection by different industries because since there is not sufficient employment to keep our people busy, imported materials would cause a still more extended state of unemployment especially if such a foreign nation should take their pay in investments and property within the United States. But under a taxed currency unemployment is perfectly con trolled and the importation of goods from foreign nations does not upset any existing relations but results in an improvement in the standard of living depending on the differences between the commodity cost of the imported material in the United States and that cost in the other countries.

Under taxed money a fairly large difference in the purchasing power of wages in different countries could go unnoticed for a con siderable time.

CHAPTER VIII SUMMARY 

Taxing money is not a scheme for creating business but the remedy of a fundamental defect in our currency system, a defect that at periodic intervals makes it profitable to harvest and store away the only material that we can use to exchange goods. 

This condition is caused by the fact that when all other values are declining, money, being the direct opposite of other values, may be said to increase its worth. In other words, money contains a store of value which when removed will leave but one use for money and that is a medium of exchange.

We must have something which can be definitely used to exchange our goods and services, something that is clearly devised to make these exchanges with no interference such as would be caused by trying to accomplish two things with the one device. The only thing that can [53]

be used to exchange these goods and services is money, for money alone answers every requirement for a perfect medium of exchange but when we also try to force that money to be a store of value in addition to the function of a medium of exchange there is a periodical interference.

In a conception of the working of a government under a system of taxed money it is necessary to visualize the economic position of a person of average intelligence whose normal efforts in business will guarantee an income of four to five thousand dollars a year, with this money having a purchasing power equalling that in existence today. 

We must also understand that at no time is there any question that any person of equal intelligence would not be able to obtain a position at such remuneration and it is clear to a person that his children will face conditions similar to this or as efficiency in production increases, even better living conditions. To provide for old age or leisure time that person will at various times secure bonds or stocks of good corporations or buy real estate and these investments will never have to face the devastating effects of a decline of price in material values except those naturally to be expected from human enterprise, such as shifting of populations and the human element of chance and inefficiency of management.

A person under such circumstances would have an entirely different outlook on life, soon finding that accumulations of wealth were a burden instead of an asset and, relieved from the worry and strain incident to providing for his family, would soon find considerable time for intellectual development.

The primal instinct to accumulate that has been passed on to us from generations back when our forefathers who were improvident in storing their food for the winter would endure the rigors of starvation-this primal instinct would soon leave us and a feeling of better fellowship would pervade the world. 

To accomplish this condition it is only necessary for us to create a condition in which the value of money can be controlled by a central agency and this change in the value of money can be made to correspond to the changes of other values. It will be impossible for these values of materials to change if the fundamental basis of those values, which is money, can be made to change in relation to those of other values. 

Such a system will entirely relieve the government of the necessity of providing employment because private capital, under strong incentives to find a place of security, would even be willing to build extensive roads, to be supported by tolls which, by reason of quicker transportation, would be but a small tax on industry or pleasure. 

In fact, every activity of the government except that of governing could be supplanted by private enterprise, with the greater efficiency which such operation affords, until such time that the people themselves feel that a government would accomplish these things in a more satisfactory manner. [54]

The condition created by a control of the interest rates simply gradually reduces the cost of capital until entrepreneurs who are willing to invest their own means will find ways to use this capital at a profit. 

Surely the small loss to investors is to be preferred to the tremendous shrinkages of value which has wiped out the entire capital of many people under the depressions which occur, with almost clock-like regularity, under our present blocked currency system, and it is not even certain that an assessment of capital would have to be made when such a device has been functioning for a reasonable period of time. 

Creating a condition under which we could control the willingness, and even anxiety, of banks to lend money with the method of control clearly understood and fair to all, would be far ahead of the present method where banks are severely criticized for taking a stand which is forced upon them to protect the funds intrusted to their care. 

While there is no denying the fact that banks are willing and anxious to loan money under the present currency structure, the price of that money is so high that people who are financially responsible cannot employ the capital at a profit and therefore, are not interested in obtaining that money. But with a tax on currency conditions are changed and, with each reduction in the interest rate, money becomes cheaper until a point is reached where it will profit those people to borrow the money and use it.

Every country is faced with a condition of acknowledged unemployment, which governments are making determined efforts to eliminate, but in spite of the various plans which are being tried not one country could be pointed out that has made what other countries would call outstanding success in bettering conditions. Facism, communism, socialism, inflation, production control have all been tried and found incapable of establishing conditions which do not require continual bolstering to work toward a standard of living such as it is possible for us to visualize and having conceived to rest until it has been achieved. Under these methods it is like climbing an escalator which is running downward while we wish to go up and, while by making heroic efforts we can gain only a few steps, the minute we stop to rest our progress is lost. 

Taxing money makes the escalator stand still while we continually go to greater heights of prosperity with no possibility of slipping backward.

Why not try a method for which the least that can be said is that it has never been tried? Surely the drift toward communism and such extremes is insidious, coming upon us when it is least expected. 

Let us avoid this state and give laissez faire a full play to show what it can do when the shackles are cast from it. 

Money must be left free to make exchanges of goods and services, and the length of time allowed for choosing a person's share of the goods or services of a country must be understood to be limited and not unlimited. [55]
