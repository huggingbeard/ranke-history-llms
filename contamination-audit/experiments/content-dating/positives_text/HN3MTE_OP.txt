4. Here are two different views of Amos' idea that a nation would perish if one class lived in luxury and oppressed the other. Both of these writers are young people from homes of wealth and social position.

I THINK AMos Was WRONG! 

I think Amos was wrong when he said a nation would perish if one class lived in luxury and oppressed the other. 

What nation has there ever been wherein all lived as equals having equal riches? Indeed it would be a veritably happy one to live in, but all people are not endowed with the same brains; and therefore all have not the same capacity for appreciating and possessing luxury.

Amos is right in the point of inherited wealth. No man should be idle, making no effort to work, but living on another man's toil. Of course, that is unjust. But after a man has striven hard to accumulate a fortune, if he has money should he share it with other people not worthy of possessing it? 

Indeed, no! If the Lord had wished us all to have equal shares in wealth, I think he would have endowed us each with an equal capacity for obtaining it and realizing its worth.

It is merely a socialistic idea to propose that every one should have luxury. If every one were wealthy who would there be to do the lower work? True, all men are born equal, but born equal not to remain equal; for they are born with an equal start in life. He who progresses farther evidently should have his reward. That is the way it has always been and always will be as long as some men have more brains than others.

Therefore, I maintain that a nation will not perish if one class has luxury and the other is oppressed. There must be leaders, there must be laborers; without either it could not remain, but with both, as centuries have proved, it will continue.

I THINK AMOS WAS RIGHT! 

Amos said that a nation would perish if one class lived in luxury and oppressed the other. This has proved to be a very true statement. In some cases the nations have not absolutely perished as did Judah, but have come to the gates of ruin.

In the book of Amos we read that Judah was to be captured and as an independent nation to be no more. The prophecies of Amos were carried out and this did come to pass.

Years after this early prophecy of Amos came another warning to a nation. The United States was in grave danger of ruin. The question was whether there should be slavery in the South. Abraham Lincoln said, "This country can no longer endure, half slave and half free." These words were indeed true for civil war soon followed, brought on by the bitter feeling of those who believed that slavery was wrong and those who thought that slavery was the only method by which to cultivate the South.

Just a few years ago in Russia, the question came again before the eyes of the world. The nobles were oppressing the serfs and lower classes cruelly. If the ruling class had only been able to see what was to happen, today we might still have a peaceful monarchy in Russia instead of the present 

Soviet Government.

Today the people are not slaves or serfs, but we have the same problem to face with a rich powerful class oppressing another. In our Constitution, all citizens of the United States are given the same rights: they are free and equal. Why then does one class, because they have money, live in luxury while the less fortunate starve and freeze to death? The people with education should not use their highly developed minds to see how much wealth they can gain by using their inferiors. 

They should put their education to better tasks and attempt to solve the question of how to make the poorer classes less oppressed. I don't for one moment advocate an equal division of wealth. That to me is foolish and cannot be done, but we cannot live in luxury at the expense of another class without trouble coming.

Amos spoke true words and the same thing will come to us today unless we better the lives of the oppressed classes in America.

"THE ACCURSED HUNGER FOR GOLD" 

Greed! In that one word Pope Pius XI sums up all the causes of the world crisis, which he describes as the worst calamity that has befallen man since the Flood. It finds expression not only in the accumulation of power and money in the hands of the few, but also in an exaggerated nationalism, which sets nation against nation and tramples on all the principles of social intercourse. Piling danger upon danger, it has afforded opportunity to Communism and given ground to Atheism. Pope Pius echoes a common thought. . . . Since the encyclical of October 2, 1931, says the Pope, "distress has increased, the number of unemployed has grown in practically all parts, and subversive elements are making use of the fact for their propaganda: hence public order is threatened more and more, and the peril of terrorism and anarchy hangs over society even more ominously." The Pope puts his finger on the same sore spot that others, too, have indicated, and his warning of a greater catastrophe to come unless moral guidance is assumed is in tune with other ill omens. The situation is grave beyond all measure in the eyes of this watchman of the world, and it may be recalled that economists and men of affairs have said that unless civilization sets its house in order it soon will have no house to set in order. "No leader in public economy, no power of organization will ever be able to bring social conditions to a peaceful solution," declares the Pope, "unless first in the very field of economics there triumphs moral law based on God and conscience. This is the underlying value of every value in the political life as well as in the economic life of nations; this is the soundest 'rate of exchange.' If it is kept steady all the rest will be stable, being guaranteed by the immutable and eternal law of God." The lust for earthly goods, "the accursed hunger for gold," in the words of the pagan poet, cause "the disorder

and inequality from which arises the accumulation of the wealth of nations in the hands of a small group of individuals who manipulate the market of the world at their own caprice, to the immense harm of the masses." LEST WE FORGET 

Nor must we forget that— 

1. A large proportion, perhaps a majority, of our people received in "prosperous times" less than sufficient to permit them to live lives of health and decency. 

2. The average farmer is living on a deficit. He is not bringing in enough to permit him to pay himself a small wage and a minimum interest on capital invested. 

3. From one and a half million to ten million are unemployed in good and bad years in the United States, and the bread line has increased by 650,000 from 1920 to 1927. 

4. Between one and two million children-perhaps a larger number-are forced to leave school and work for a pittance, while tens of thousands of other children toil long hours without any money compensation. 

5. A total of 350,000,000 days more or less are lost each year through sickness—most of which is preventable—on the part of those gainfully employed; between 20,000 and 25,000 workers are killed in our industries, while 2,500,000 are injured. 

6. Some millions of our children go to school in "good years" each day without proper nourishment.

7. Some four or five million residents over ten years of age in the United States are illiterate. 

8. Millions of workers in the cities and on the farms still live in tenements and in shacks unfit for human habitation in twentieth-century America, despite improvements since the war. 

9. One per cent of our income receivers obtained before the 

1929 depression one eighth of the realized income, while ten per cent received one third. Four thousand people in our richest class received in 1929 an average of nearly $400,000 a year, over 230 times the average received by the 99 per cent in the lowest income class. 

10. Perhaps one half of the energies of our workers is wasted as a result of the present chaotic competitive social order. 

11. Militarism, since the war, has become a grave and increasing danger. We are spending on our army and navy 270 per cent more than we spent before the war. 

These are some of the social evils which must be courageously faced. ONE MAN OR MANY 

After all, a millionaire has only one stomach. He can't possibly wear more than a few hundred pairs of shoes in a year. 

There is a limit to the clothes he can wear, the beds he can sleep on and the yachts and automobiles in which he can ride. 

There's one thing a millionaire can't do, with all his riches.

He can't consume enough goods to keep enough factories working so that there will be jobs and food for the rest of the people. 

He may scatter bank notes recklessly here and there, but no matter how much money he spends it does not have the effect on industry that the same amount of money would have if spent by a hundred thousand men, or ten thousand men, for shoes and overalls and shirts, and other necessaries of life. 

That is why income tax figures just announced by the Treasury Department have such grave import.

In the first eight months of 1929, 504 men with incomes of more than a million dollars a year possessed one twentieth of the nation's wealth. 

Where there had been 7,259,944 persons filing income taxes in 1920, there were only 4,034,702 filing in 1929. And where, in 1920, six-and-a-half million men and women with small incomes had almost fifteen-and-a-half billion dollars to spend, in 1929 the number of small-income earners had been reduced to three million, and they had only a little more than eight billion dollars.

In ten years the number of men with incomes of more than a million has been multiplied by fifteen, and the total amount of their incomes has been multiplied by twenty, while the foundation of the prosperity that made this possible has been rotting away because the people who could have kept factories humming and men and women working had no money with which to buy. 

A great economist predicted years ago that capitalism would commit suicide through greed.

Unless we are ready to see capitalism end, and with it the whole social order that we have known heretofore, the increasing concentration of wealth revealed by the Treasury Department cannot fail to alarm us.

She has explored the isles of the ocean for objects of commiseration; but amazing stupidity! she can gaze without emotion on a multitude of miserable beings at home, large enough to constitute a nation of freemen, whom tyranny has heathenized by law. . . . Danger Spots in America Today 1. The Use of Alcoholic Liquor 

People everywhere in America are discussing the question of the use of alcoholic liquor as a beverage. Many methods of controlling the traffic in intoxicants had been tried before the Prohibition Amendment and the Volstead Act became the law of the land, January 17, 1920. When this amendment was passed, it was felt that John Barleycorn had been buried, never to trouble America again. People forgot, however, that the friends of John Barleycorn might bring him to life, and this is just what has happened during the last ten years. The problem before us now is, "What are the effects on the individual and on society of the use of alcoholic liquor? Are they good or bad?" Is it wise or unwise to legalize the selling of beverages containing more than 2 per cent alcohol?

Some groups of young people have made the study of this problem a project and have carried out the investigation along the following lines: (1) A study of the physiological effects of alcohol and an investigation of its effects on health, mental and physical, on the death rate, etc. This investigation was carried out by consulting physicians, scientists, social workers and by studying books and magazines and the daily papers. (2) A study of the sociological (including the moral and religious) effects of alcohol; the effect on homes and schools and reports on the old saloons. This investigation was carried on by consulting ministers, social workers, school teachers, and other leaders in the community, as well as by reading articles in magazines. (3) A study of the industrial and economic effects, by consulting industrial leaders, business men, bankers, superintendents of mills and factories, and articles and books. (4) A study of methods used to control liquor traffic in the 

United States and other countries.

(5) A study of the background and history of the use of alcohol. (6) Reports on the results of national Prohibition-good and bad-and an evaluation of the opposing claims. 

After all these reports were in and had been discussed, the group decided upon their findings and suggested solutions of the problem of the beverage use of alcoholic liquor. (For bibliography and suggestions for conducting such a project, write the author at 14 Beacon Street, Boston, Massachusetts.)

The problem is at least 6,000 years old. Turn to the Old 

Testament, and you will find incidents recorded when Amos and the prophets attacked the wine-drinkers of their day. In the time of Jeremiah, the Rechabites took a solemn vow to drink no wine, neither they nor their sons forever (Jeremiah 35:6). 

Aristotle (384-322 B. C.) said in his Problemata "Why is it that, although the wine is hot, the drinkers are unable to endure cold and are readily attacked by pleurisy and other diseases? 

Why has wine the effect of stupefying and of driving to frenzy those who drink it?"

THE PHYSIOLOGICAL EFFECTS OF THE USE OF ALCOHOL 

1. Alcohol is a drug which acts on the brain and spinal cord; it is a progressive brain depressive. 

2. The immediate temporary reaction of drinking alcohol is to increase respiration-breathing becomes deep, eyes bright, face flushed, etc.; with larger quantity, the person becomes cheerful, satisfied, more active and talkative. 

3. Strong solutions irritate the stomach and intestines. Al-

cohol is very rapidly absorbed (fifteen minutes); after absorption it affects circulation, respiration and the brain. 

4. It affects first the higher intellectual activities, such as will-power, judgment, reasoning; then affects motor and sensory functions.

It lessens action of the entire brain and medulla. 

5. A small quantity leads to increased activity; a continued amount leads to stupor. 

6. There is an apparent increase in brain activity with drinking of alcohol, but this is not actual-only temporary. 

7. Alcohol removes normal restraints and inhibitions. Society demands that we set up certain inhibitions: we have to consider not our own impulses and satisfactions but the satisfaction of the group. Alcohol simply removes these inhibitions, as a result of diminished activity of the brain. 

8. It causes: (1) Loss of judgment, dulling of reasoning power, removal of inhibitions. (2) Loss of co-ordination and muscular control. (3) Paralysis of reflexes resulting finally in stupor. 

The first reaction from alcohol is to dull the intellectual centers; then it goes on to the second level, affecting walking and other muscular movements; finally, even the reflexes of the first level cease. 

9. First, a feeling of warmth comes from irritation to the stomach; later, a feeling of cold comes from heat being thrown off from the body-blood vessels are dilated. A drunkard would freeze much more quickly than a normal person. 

10. Alcohol is a habit-forming drug. We tend to repeat those activities which are pleasurable; therefore, one drink leads to another. A little drink does not dull the intellectual centers; the danger is in habit formation. 

11. Habitual drinking destroys the cells in different organs of the body, heart, kidney, blood vessels, liver, nervous system.

The cells are replaced by connective tissue which lowers the efficiency of the organ.

12. The cumulative effect of moderate drinking should be emphasized.

With adults, alcohol has not yet been found to cause more efficient action in mental or physical work. 

Doctors prescribe much less alcohol than formerly. Sometimes it is given to a patient who is an habitual drinker to cure him gradually, for it cannot be taken away all at once. 

As you read this study perhaps you will see why, while gin cocktails at the party may seem to "pep" it up, they really are having some very serious effects on the individuals who drink. 

As you study the whole problem, remember that the basic question is, What are the social consequences of the use of alcoholic liquor? Is it a social menace? If so, should it be abolished or controlled, and how?

TRAFFIC RULES OF BERLIN (GERMANY) POLICE 

1. The arduous and responsible calling of the motor driver makes it necessary for him to abstain completely from all alcoholic beverages (beer, wine, spirits, etc.) both before and during his work. 

2. The smallest quantities of alcohol are injurious for the motor driver. It is a widespread error that small quantities have no deleterious effect. THE PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECTS OF ALCOHOL 

Alcohol weakens the ability to form sound judgments. This ability is one of the highest attainments of man. It distinguishes a wise man from a fool. Good judgment on moral issues guides us aright morally. Good judgment on material values is an important part of financial success. Good judgment in ethical values distinguishes the gentleman from the boor. Alcohol blears all judgment; for it interferes with those delicate interactions of the brain cells upon which judgment depends.

Sir G. Sims Woodhead, M.D., of Cambridge [Eng.], says- 

"A man under the influence of small quantities of alcohol has no right to believe his senses and cannot trust them to give him straight facts."

Alcohol weakens self-control. Here is another capability of man that measures his advance in civilization. Self-control distinguishes the human race from the brutes, distinguishes civilized man from the savage. It not only measures his civilization but determines his success or failure in life. The lack of it makes the spendthrift and the libertine. It is a cardinal virtue of the Christian life. Alcohol destroys it. 

Alcohol creates a feeling of "I don't care"-makes one reckless of consequences. In the presence of a moral issue in his conduct, when a bleared judgment still whispers that it is

wrong, when self-control feebly reaches to throw on the brakes, this spirit of "I don't care" takes the wheel and rides like a demon, bringing ruin to the drinker.

Combine the psychological effects of alcohol in a person in the presence of physical, financial, or moral danger and watch the crash. At the wheel of an automobile speeding down the highway thick with danger! The eye sees a crisis, but cannot measure the distance accurately. He thinks that he will miss that car. His power of self-control is numbed. The crash impends, but a chance remains still to avoid it, if he acts quickly and aright. The demon spirit, "I don't care," takes chargecrash-death-anguish-an innocent life in the other car is snuffed out!

IS ALCOHOL A POISON? 

Doctor Abel, specialist at Johns Hopkins University, states that from one-half to one bottle of wine, or from two to four glasses of beer taken a day, not only counteracts the beneficial effects of acquired practice in any given occupation but depresses every form of intellectual activity. Therefore, every man who, according to his own notions, is only a moderate drinker, places himself on a lower intellectual plane and decreases the full and complete utilization of his intellectual powers. 

In considering this question, "Is alcohol a poison?" we shall inquire briefly into the effect of alcohol on the structure rather than functions; its effect on the digestive juices, the brain tissues, and the heart. 

Dr. William H. Welch, a collaborator with Doctor Abel at

Johns Hopkins University, states: "Alcohol in sufficient quantities is a poison to all living organisms, both vegetable and animal." 

No dissenting voice is heard that alcohol is a poison. Those who maintain that the moderate use of alcohol is not poisonous

might say the same thing of arsenic, strychnine, and opium; but no one argues that they are not poisons.

Doctor Welch maintains that alcohol is not merely a poison but the most far-reaching and virulent of poisons, because its effect is insidious and is cumulative as time goes on. 

HOW THE POISON WORKS 

Alcohol entering directly into the blood stream, and having a great affinity for water, causes a general scarification of tissues and let-down of vitality. The action of narcotics other than alcohol-opium, morphine, and cocaine-has a direct effect on the white corpuscles of the blood. These corpuscles are the leucocytes, or bodyguards against disease. In some manner they attack the various bacilli of acute diseases and in the normal body render these bacilli harmless.

Narco-maniacs (individuals who take narcotics of any kind) show a marked inability to withstand the infectious diseases. 

Opium paralyzes the white corpuscles of the blood, rendering them unable to combat the bacilli of germ diseases. Alcohol has much the same effect. Alcohol users show an inability to withstand lung diseases tuberculosis and pneumonia. In 

France, a country generally given to moderate use of alcohol, the deaths from tuberculosis have a direct bearing upon the amount of alcohol consumed.

The Tragedy in Hosea's Home 

A young man fell in love with an attractive young woman.

This love match began as all love matches do, and the couple were married. A son was born and was given a name which was as significant to his people as the name of George Washington or Abraham Lincoln is to us, and the young married couple rejoiced in the birth of the boy who was the first-born and through whom the family name would be continued. 

In a few years a daughter was born, but between the time of the birth of the boy and the birth of the daughter something had happened in the lives of this couple-something so tragic that when the daughter was born, she was given the name "She that never knew a father's love." In a few years another boy was born, but this time there was no rejoicing in the birth of the son, and he was named "Not my people," or "No kindred of mine."

Meantime, the husband of this family was pondering his domestic situation. He had seen his wife's love grow colder and colder, and her love for someone else grow more and more. 

He had seen the gradual breaking up of his home. Of two of his three children he knew he was not the father. Finally his wife left him altogether and went he knew not where, leaving a deserted husband with three small children on his hands; yet this young man still loved his wayward wife, and though he loathed her actions, he loved her still.

One day as he was walking down the street, he saw in the shambles of the town, clothed in rags and bearing the marks of dissipation, her who once was his attractive bride. He recognized her and she him, but what a difference from the days when they first met, and he courted and married her! What should this young man do? Should he take her back into his home or should he leave her to the lot which she had chosen? 

After a struggle within himself, he took her back to his home, but he said to her, "You shall not be my wife, although you may live in my house, until the time when once again we stand on the same basis as in the days when we were first married." 

So King Arthur faced his problem with his beloved wife

We live in a social world, and everything we do has some influence on another, direct or indirect, immediate or eventual, for better or worse. b. A FORGIVING GOD 

But the idea of forgiveness is much more than the mere remission of sins. It is not oblivion, just "letting bygones be bygones." It includes "forgetting the things that are behind," but it does more than that. Indeed, forgiveness is concerned more with the future than with the past. In the parable of the two debtors, Jesus likens sin to a bad debt and the forgiveness of sin to the writing off of a bad debt: "And when they had nothing to pay, he frankly forgave them both." But quite evidently this is no more than the threshold of forgiveness. 

A business man writes off a bad debt because it is no use keeping it on his books. It is no longer an asset; it has even

become a liability. It is a matter between him and himself, rather than between him and the debtor. He is simply dismissing the debtor out of his affairs. But forgiveness does more than that. It is still concerned with the debtor after the debt is written off; and, indeed, it only writes off the debt in order to recommence business with the defaulter on the old terms. It is not merely discharging the bankrupt; it is setting him up in business again. It is not merely cutting the loss, mere oblivion of the debt. It is restoration; it is the re-establishment of a broken relationship. It is reconciliation. 

But obviously the transaction is incomplete if the proffered forgiveness of God is not accepted. It takes two to make a quarrel; it takes the good will of two to make it up again. God's offer of reconciliation is profitless, if the offender rejects it. 

God offers his forgiveness to move the sinner to a change of heart, so that the change of heart may effect a full reconciliation. 

God is at one with the sinner; but that avails nothing until the sinner ceases to be at odds with God. The Proclamation of 

Slave Emancipation made every slave free in the United States; but it was effective only for those who chose to assert their freedom. "God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself," but the reconciliation is effectual only when we enter into it. 

—RICHARD ROBERTS, The Christian God, pp. 143-44. By permission of The Macmillan Co., publishers.

The dictionary defines "ideal" as "the goal of effort; the good, the true, the beautiful; the vision of perfection; something always in process of attainment, yet never completely attained." It is the star by which we guide our lives.

Most fundamental, most vital of all ideals, most far-reaching in their consequences to the individual and to society are the ideals of love. The Christian Ideal of Marriage 

Read Matthew 19:5, 6. These are Jesus' words and express the Christian ideal of marriage: 

1. "The twain shall become one flesh"-the physical, organic union. 

2. "What God hath joined together"—the spiritual union. 

3. "Let no man put asunder”—a lifelong relationship.

WHY MARRIAGE? 

The existence of a convention of society means that men and women, playing the game of life as we must all play it, came, through much bitter experience and hard thinking, to feel that the game would be fairer for all concerned if there were rules, and those rules were framed with the happiness of the greatest number in view. Without such rules, every young person becoming aware of sex inclinations would be thrown on his or her own resources of reason and experience. Such a course would quickly reduce our life together to chaos. Some conventions are admittedly effete and out of date and must be scrapped. But my experience is that this is not one of them; and I appeal to youth, in a phrase it greatly loves, to "play the game," to make this convention a voluntarily accepted code, and to suspect the individual egotist who scorns the rule and thinks only of himself just as he suspects the individual in Rugby football who has no thought for his side but is only out to get his own way, careless of the consequences to the team. 

To many such "playing the game" will be a hard test and seem a cruel limitation of instinctive energies; but in every game victory for the team depends on such sacrifices, and the finest members of our race will be ready, even at much personal sacrifice, to keep the rules, not only to preserve inviolate the innermost and most mysterious sanctuary of the temple of personality, the sanctity of which, once outraged, is never quite so beautiful again, but because they realize that however the individual may seem to justify his own conduct, that conduct cannot be considered right and cannot therefore be truly justified if its widespread adoption would wreck society. Nor must exceptions to the rules be allowed; for we should always claim our own cases as exceptional, and the rules would soon cease to bind any one. As Canon Streeter says: "If, for example, you say, in framing your rule, that engaged couples may behave as if already married, the rule will be stretched to cover

couples secretly engaged and very soon couples thinking of being engaged, couples wondering what it would feel like to be temporarily engaged and so on.'

Isaiah, the Statesman Prophet (740-701 B. C.)

He came on the scene with nothing told of his birth or parentage, and after forty years he vanished with nothing told of his death. Hosea's home was in the Northern Kingdom, Israel; 

Isaiah lived in the Southern Kingdom, Judah, and was evidently a resident of Jerusalem. He was a married man with at least two sons. He was on familiar terms with the king and at home in the court. 

1. CONDITIONS IN JUDAH WHICH ISAIAH MET 

In the year of the death of Uzziah, Isaiah received his call to be a prophet. Uzziah ruled in Judah from 782 to 740 B. C., and his reign compared with that of Jeroboam II in the Northern Kingdom, Israel. It was a time of great prosperity in Judah. Uzziah was a great organizer, very energetic. He developed the commerce of the country, extended the boundaries of the nation, rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem and strengthened them with towers. He provided a good water supply for the city, developed agriculture, and organized an army. Uzziah's son Jotham followed his father as king, but lived only two years. He was succeeded by Ahaz, a cowardly, superstitious, weak king, who ruled from 737 to about 727 B. C. He paid tribute to Tiglath Pileser and saved the country from destruction, but made it subject to Assyria. For thirty years Judah paid tribute, but when the Assyrian king, Sargon, was assassinated in 705 B. C., Judah revolted. Isaiah opposed the revolt of Judah. (See Isaiah 30: 7, 15; 31:1; 30:1-3.)

Sennacherib succeeded Sargon as king of Assyria, suppressed the revolt in Babylon and marched on Palestine, conquering city after city and carrying away thousands of captives and immense

spoils. Hezekiah was king of Judah at that time. The dramatic story of Sennacherib's demand of Hezekiah and Isaiah's part in the crisis of his nation is the basis of our next lesson. 

2. ISAIAH'S CALL AND COMMISSION 

(Isaiah 6:1-13) 

Read this passage and note the following points: 

A. The time of his call (6:1). It was the year Uzziah died.

The death of this king made a tremendous impression on the whole nation. He had been a strong ruler, and his death was looked upon as a great calamity. It became a dating point, like "before the War" or "after the War." 

B. The vision of the Lord (6:1-5). At this time Isaiah was a young man, deeply interested in the political and religious life of his nation. One day he was in the temple, evidently deep in thought, and he seemed to see "the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up," and "above him stood the seraphim; each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly." The young man seemed to hear one crying to another, "Holy, holy, holy, is Jehovah of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory.”

One of our great hymns is based on his vision: "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, early in the morning our song shall rise to thee." The thresholds of the temple seemed to shake and the house was filled with smoke. Then Isaiah was filled with dismay, and he said, "Woe is me! for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips: for mine eyes have seen the King, Jehovah of hosts." 

C. The cleansing of Isaiah (6:6, 7). Then one of the seraphim took a live coal from the altar and flew to Isaiah and touched his mouth with it, and said, "This fire hath touched thy lips, a symbol that thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin forgiven." 

D. Isaiah's call and commission (6:8-10). Then Isaiah seemed to hear the voice of the Lord saying, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" And Isaiah answered, "Here am I; send me." It was a challenge to a young man, and the

young man volunteered at once. Then follows his commission (vs. 9, 10.) He is to preach to a people who will hear but not understand him, a people whose ears are heavy and whose eyes are blind. 

E. The hardships he faced (6:11-13). To Isaiah's question, "Lord, how long?" he is told until the cities are laid waste and the land become desolate and the people are carried into captivity. He was to be the prophet of the decline of a nation. 

Not a pleasant prospect, to look forward to such a lifework! 

How many of your friends are accepting calls of this kind? 

The usual attitude in such a case is to say, "Oh, what's the use! 

What can I do?" 

F. Isaiah's relationship to the king (38:1-6, 21; 39:1-8). In the first of these passages we find a choice picture of a sick king who thought he was going to die. Isaiah comes and tells him at first that he will not live, and the king turns his face to the wall and weeps "large tears." He reminds Jehovah of what a good king he has been. Isaiah comes to see him again and tells him Jehovah has heard his prayer and is going to add unto his life fifteen years. Then Isaiah tells the attendants to make a plaster of figs and put it on the boil, and the king will recover. 

Like most men, when he was sick, he thought he was very sick! 

How human a king is after all, and how familiarly Isaiah takes charge of the situation! He was the real ruler.

The other picture shows Isaiah in the rôle of stern critic and bold denouncer. The king of Babylon sent letters and presents to Hezekiah when he heard that Hezekiah had been sick and had recovered from his illness. Hezekiah was flattered, and he showed the king's emissaries all his treasures of silver and gold and spices and oil. There was nothing in his house or in his whole kingdom that he did not show them. He is having a wonderfully good time when Isaiah appears on the scene and says, "Where did these men come from?" Hezekiah answers, "From a far country, even from Babylon." With fire in his eye, Isaiah says, "What have they seen in your house?" And

Hezekiah meekly answers, "Everything. There is nothing I have not shown them." Then Isaiah uttered words of doom as

a spokesman of Jehovah: "Behold, the days are coming when all that is in thy house and that which thy fathers have laid up in store until this day shall be carried to Babylon; nothing shall be left. Thy children shall be made captives." It was a terrible shock to the king, but he uttered no protest. Such was the power of Isaiah in the court.

ALBERT SCHWEITZER: CHRISTIAN SUPERMAN 

If we could enter his hospital today in the African forest we should find this "Alsatian giant, a lion who laughs," to take a phrase that Romain Rolland used in describing to me his muchbeloved fellow musician, toiling with his artist's hands to relieve the sufferings of the black race. Our souls would be stirred as we looked into the gentle and compassionate face. We should see lines of care written there by the eighteen years of herculean effort to redeem Africa.

At the age of thirty-four, Doctor Schweitzer began his study of medicine. After four years of preparation, he and his wife turned from a comfortable income and international recognition to a more radical form of human service. They went out alone to what they felt was the most neglected sphere of human need. 

It was a titanic undertaking to go without salary or any resources except their own savings. They made it a venture of

faith. When funds were exhausted they would go back to 

Europe for lectures and organ recitals. The project was so daring that in three years the world was watching them with keen interest.

The doctor told me in a letter of last year how he began in 1913. His first operation was performed in a whitewashed chickenhouse. Natives flocked to him and he was overwhelmed.

He and his wife struggled to meet the challenge and soon gathered helpers and built a haven of mercy in the jungle. In 1917 they came back to Europe, both of them shattered in health. 

Doctors said they could never go back. Unable to get out of

Africa because of the war, Schweitzer had kept the hospital going by borrowing money. When he reached Europe he found himself overwhelmingly in debt. In spite of his heart, that threatened to give out, he started to raise money to meet his bills. He traveled over Central Europe, Scandinavia, and England, playing the organ and lecturing on philosophy, religion and medicine. Everywhere his magnificent mind, his humble and gentle nature, and his contagious enthusiasms won him new followers. He made a tremendous impression. But his health was bad; he could not yet return to Africa. He threw himself into the creation of his monumental work on philosophy and gathered up new workers-doctors, nurses and helpers-and accumulated drugs and equipment. At length, after this long exile in Europe, he set sail for the jungle. His wife and daughter were too frail to go with him, but they willingly met the sacrifice in order that the hospital should be kept going. 

The doctor has become a titan of courage in the Ogowe District. Unflinchingly, he welcomes all sufferers. Children come, their bodies plastered with the ugly sores of frambasia. 

Laborers come from the forests, their feet masses of ulcers. 

Old people are brought by their families, dying, and left naked, with neither blanket nor mosquito net, before the doctor's door. 

He never turns them away; he is not afraid of their maladies, and is ever willing to assume the burden of their care. He keeps his courage up and exclaims: "Any one who wants to do good under our African conditions must fight against any

tendency to let his nerves and temper be upset by all the big and little difficulties of daily life, and must retain his full joy in his work."

As for Schweitzer, he answers with a life of Christian enthusiasm: "Those who have felt the discipline of suffering must enter the Fellowship of Pain and seek to deliver others from the fetters of sorrow!... It is my desire to be a forerunner, preparing the way for this renaissance, and to fling my faith in a new humanity, like a burning fire-brand, into the gloomy darkness of our times!" 

-ROBERT MERRILL BARTLETT, The Congregationalist,

The Crisis in Jerusalem 

Sennacherib, the able king of the Assyrians, was marching on Palestine, and his forces were drawing near to Jerusalem, which had revolted from under his rule. Hezekiah, the king of Judah,

at first resisted; then yielded and was forced to pay thirty talents of gold, three hundred talents of silver, and large spoil of every kind. Apparently, however, the Assyrian king then changed his mind and demanded the unconditional surrender of the city. (See Isaiah 36, 37). Perhaps he was afraid to leave behind him a fortified city as he marched on to Egypt. 

Whatever the reason, we find him in these chapters sending his chief officer with a great army to Jerusalem to demand its complete surrender. Read the vivid account in chapter 36. The officer stands outside the wall, and to him come Eliakim and Shebna, the scribe or secretary, and Joah, the chronicler. Very skillfully the officer develops his speech. First, he asks them to tell King Hezekiah that if he depends upon Egypt for help, he is leaning upon a staff that will pierce his own hand. But, he says, if Hezekiah is trusting in Jehovah, does he not know that the Assyrian king has come up against this land to destroy it by the very command of Jehovah? (See Isaiah 36:10). The Hebrew officials asked the officer not to speak to them in the language of the Jews but in Aramean; but the officer called the louder in the language of the people, telling them not to let Hezekiah influence them to trust in Jehovah, but to make their peace with the Assyrian king and to come out to him. He put fear and distrust in the hearts of the defenders by asking them, "Hath any of the gods of the nations delivered his land out of the hand of the king of Assyria?" How, then, should they expect Jehovah to deliver Jerusalem? But the people on the wall of the city kept their silence while Eliakim, Shebna and Joah returned to Hezekiah and told him the words of the 

Assyrian officer.

Then King Hezekiah rent his clothes, covered himself with sackcloth, and went into the temple to pray, meanwhile sending for Isaiah, the prophet. Isaiah immediately sent word to the king: "Thus saith Jehovah, 'Be not afraid of the words that thou hast heard. . . . Behold, I will put a spirit in him, and he shall hear tidings, and shall return unto his own land; and I will cause him to fall by the sword in his own land."

"Let not thy God deceive thee into thinking that Jerusalem shall not be delivered unto my hands. Behold, thou hast heard what hath happened to other lands, and shalt thou be delivered?"

GOD AND Daily Living 

There is a human inclination to seek God in the individual life, but the attempt to realize God in social living is rare. For this reason the Christian religion is sometimes thought to be an amusement for the leisure classes, for the trifling bourgeoisie, or confined to the hermit or ascetic. But religion is not that. 

John Fiske, in defining religion, said that religion is God working among men in everyday living. True religion is that which not only looks toward heaven but turns toward earth with a feeling of being in heaven.

Victor Hugo said, "Religion must be followed by all types of people. When we walk on the ground, one foot is always lifted and the other one adheres to the earth." Man would never become discouraged if he felt this way. Forgetting the nature of religious experience, he lives assiduously for earthly things only. When one foot is lifted and one foot is on earth the real nature of religious experience is revealed: we walk and go forward.

Jesus was a carpenter, but in the midst of this simple life he revealed God to man. He was born in a manger in a stable. His parents walked about looking for a place to stay, but found them all filled up, so there was nothing to do but go into a stable, and here the Baby was born. The image of God, however, resided in this Child. This one humble laborer has moved the world. Herein lies the realization of religion. Many think while they hold the throttle or the rudder God is not near nor needed. "While we are praying, he is near, but while we are working, he is not," they say. It is a mistake to think that although God is present in church, he is not present when we are making bean-mash. We cannot call anything true religion unless through it God may be found at the counter, in the kitchen, or in the shop. Jesus Christ revealed the image of God to the world, and his religion was in his everyday living. The 

Christian religion includes not only his teachings and ideals but also his daily life.

DOES GOD CARE? 

One thing can be asserted without wounding the most tender ethical sensibility; that God is present, even in the midst of tragedy, wherever remedial and redemptive agencies are at work, turning evil into an occasion for Christlike deeds of sacrificial love. The Cleveland Clinic disaster was a great evil, but God was in it-not in the sense that he designed it as a chastisement for certain people, but in the spirit of those physicians and volunteer workers who met the crisis so magnificently. 

One physician, working all day over the victims of the disaster, went home to die himself of the effects of the gas. When he realized that his own death was near, he turned to his wife and said, "My dear, these people must be cared for." Yes. God was in the Cleveland Clinic disaster! And God was in the war-not in the sense that he planned this great evil that good might come, but in the spirit of those who, whether in the trenches with Donald Hankey and Studdert-Kennedy, or in relief work with the Quakers, rose to the occasion and saw the great tragedy through, "with malice toward none and charity for all." So much faith can say without breaking the bruised reed or quenching the smoking flax: and it is sometimes best that faith should say no more. -Ibid., pp. 171-72

The Prophet and His Home 

(Micah 1:1) 

Micah was a native of Judah, a countryman from Moreshethgath, which was in the low hill country between Judah and the 

Philistine Plain. Seventeen miles away was Tekoa, the home of Amos. Micah lived in a fertile country, abounding in streams of water, good pastures, and olive groves. 

It was a region of farmers and shepherds. Although a small country place, it was at the southwest gateway of Judah and not far from the great caravan routes. Here one would expect to find an intelligent, resourceful, self-reliant people, unspoiled by the luxuries and evils of city life. 

Conditions in Judah

Micah prophesied during the reign of Hezekiah, king of Judah, thirty years after the time of Amos and fifteen years after Hosea, at the time when the prophet Isaiah was exerting his great influence in the city of Jerusalem. The Assyrian king 

Sargon had just captured the Northern Kingdom, Israel (721 B. C.), and on his march to Egypt was threatening the Southern

HENRY FORD ON FARM AND FACTORY 

Danger to our country is to be apprehended not so much from the influence of new things as from our forgetting the value of old things. At present, much that is blamed on the new thing-the machine-should perhaps be blamed on our

forgetting what we had before the machine came; namely, the land. These two belong together; they cannot live apart; there is no antagonism between them; they must be reunited. 

I do not look for less but more use of machinery. If the world is to have even a minimum supply of goods, it must utilize the machine. The people will never willingly forego the help which the machine gives them in their work. 

Agriculture needs a wider and steadier market; industrial workers need more and steadier jobs. Can each be made to supply what the other needs? I think so.

The link between is Chemistry. In the vicinity of Dearborn we are farming twenty thousand acres for everything from sunflowers to soy beans. We pass the crops through our laboratory to learn how they may be used in the manufacture of motor cars and thus provide an industrial market for the farmers' products. I foresee the time when industry shall no longer denude the forests which require generations to mature, nor use up the mines which were ages in the making, but shall draw its raw material largely from the annual produce of the fields. 

The dinner table of the world is not a sufficient outlet for the farmers' products; there must be found a wider market if agriculture is to be all that it is competent to become. And where is that market to be found if not in industry?

I am convinced that we shall be able to get out of yearly crops most of the basic materials which we now get from forest and mine. That is to say, we shall grow annually many if not most of the substances needed in manufacturing. When that day comes, and it is surely on the way, the farmer will not lack a market and the worker will not lack a job. More people will live in the country. The present unnatural condition will be naturally balanced again. Our foundation will be once more securely laid in the land.

The day of small industry near the farm will return, because much of the material grown for industry can be given its first processing by the men who raised it. The master farmer will become, as he was in former years, master of a form of industry besides.

An old Roman said that corn was never so plentiful in Rome as when the men who ruled the state were those who also tilled the fields. It will be so with our people when those who fabricate the utilities of the world are those who raise the raw material from the fields. . . .

We are pioneers, and pioneering has its hardships. This generation is composed of economic pilgrims, passing out of less desirable conditions into what we hope shall be more desirable ones. That is the meaning of these times. We are not going back, and false prosperity is not soon coming back. It would be well to interpret present conditions in this light and help each other in the good old pioneer way, as we pass through them. 

We are going to renew our stake in the land; for the land is the foundation of the economic security which we seek. -Christian Century, June 22, 1932, p. 807.

A BETTER SOCIAL ORDER 

What principles of social ethics are implied in the transformation of our collectivistic economic life into a more humane and genuinely democratic social order? 

1. Since every working member contributes, in a measure that cannot be accurately determined, to the welfare of the whole of society, every individual thus contributing is entitled, by virtue of his sheer humanity, to the conditions of a decent livelihood. This means a decent living wage for moderate hours of labor. It implies, for a mature man, an income sufficient to enable him to nurture a family under decent living conditions and to give his children a fair opportunity for an education.

2. Since the welfare of a society as a whole depends on each member's doing his bit, it is the duty of every competent individual to contribute as much as he can to the social wealth. 

It is the right of organized society to insist upon his fulfillment of this duty. 

3. More scope must be given, in the conduct of the common life, for the exercise of human individuality, for the expression of the constructive and other human impulses. This was one of the greatest values of the older American life, which is now fast passing away. No society can prosper and be permanent in which there is not wide opportunity for the expression of diverse human powers. The true meaning of individual liberty is opportunity for the realization and enjoyment of one's native human capacities. These exist in diverse degrees and combinations, and are the raw materials of our personalities. 

4. There should be a much greater approach to economic equality but not complete equality. Diverse individuals, with diverse powers and occupations, require differing periods of preparation, differing hours and conditions, differing ways of spending their leisure time. A scientist, an artist, a teacher and a handworker require very different periods and kinds of training, conditions of labor and of recreation. Beyond what is required for the training and exercise of one's special capacities for the fitting discharge of one's functions, there is no ethical justification for inequality of reward. Nothing more grotesquely illustrates the disease of acquisitiveness which so infects our industrial society today than the disparities between the rewards of an artist, poet, or scientist, on the one hand, and the rewards of an industrial or financial magnate, on the other hand. No wonder that, analogous to the antediluvian age in the earth's history, we produce commercial and mechanical monsters and spiritual pygmies. 

5. The surplus of wealth, over and above what is necessary to meet the above conditions, should be devoted to the common good-to enhancing educational and cultural or spiritual opportunities. 

-JOSEPH ALEXANDER LEIGHTON, Religion and the Mind of Today, pp. 87-88. D. Appleton & Co.

A Young Man Faces His Life Work 

This young man lived 2,500 years ago in a country no larger than the State of Vermont. He was well educated and came from one of the leading families of a small town three miles northeast of Jerusalem, called Anathoth. He might have had an easy time in life, but when he was less than twenty-five years old, he became convinced that God was calling him to give his life in service to his people. Very graphically he lays bare his inner struggle. Read Jeremiah 1:4-10. 

As you read this passage note the following points: 

1. Jeremiah's age (1:6) "I am a child"; that is, I am very young for such a great work. Apparently he was twenty-four or twentyfive years old. Jesus was about thirty years of age when he began his career.

2. The young man's attitude (1:6-8)

This young man was by nature somewhat timid, shrinking, lacking in self-confidence. Unlike Isaiah, who immediately answered "Here am I, send me," Jeremiah debates the question as to whether he is fitted for the work to which God seems to be calling him. This is quite characteristic even today; for every one must think of his own qualifications for the type of work upon which he is thinking of entering. It is the unusual person who has no inner struggle in the choice of his life work. 

3. His mission (1:9, 10)

In symbolic language Jeremiah tells how God has commissioned him to be his spokesman to the people. 

So conscious is he of God's presence, that he identifies himself with Him. Conscious of his own weakness, he found his strength in God. An interesting analogy is that of the call of Moses to lead his people out of 

Egypt. (Exodus 3:10, 11; 4:10-12) 

Conditions He Faced

Hezekiah, the great king of Judah, had long since passed away, and Isaiah, the great prophet of Judah, had been dead nearly seventy-five years. Jeremiah began his work when 

Josiah was reigning over Judah. This was a good king who was very loyal to Jehovah and very friendly to both the prophetic and the priestly classes. In 621 B. C. he had brought about a great reform in the religious life of the people, and the nation was enjoying peace and happiness; but unfortunately in the year 608 B. C., when he was only thirty-nine years old, he was killed at Megido in a battle against the Egyptian forces.

It was a tragic death, for it completely upset the conditions in which both priests and prophets rejoiced. Religious confusion resulted, and the nation became subject to Egypt. The new rulers of Judah were corrupt and unjust. Those were troubled times, and stormy days were to come, for the Egyptian king

was defeated at Carchemish in 604 B. C. by the Babylonian king, Nebuchadrezzar. In 597 B. C. came the first captivity of Judah, and ten years later, 586 B. C., after revolts and counter-revolts of puppet kings, Jerusalem was finally destroyed and the people carried into captivity. This was forty years after Jeremiah entered upon his life work. You will find in 2 Kings 22-25 the graphic history of this period. 

To Whom He Spoke and How He Wrote (See Jeremiah 1:5, 17-19; 26:7; 26:12)

Kings, princes, priests, people, all the people, were his audience. Indeed, he was sent unto all the nations (1:5), a wider ministry than just to Judah. This does not mean, however, that he was sent into foreign lands, but rather that he addressed his messages both to Judah and to the nations with which his people's destiny was so closely bound up, such as Egypt and Babylonia.

He had a private secretary, Baruch, who wrote his addresses and remained loyal to him to the very end. (Read Jeremiah 

36:1-4, 17, 18, 21-23, 27-32.) Imagine the scene when Baruch read Jeremiah's message to the princes and when the king, sitting in his winter house with the fire burning in the brazier, slit the roll with his pen-knife, page by page, and cast it into the fire until all the roll was consumed. Read Jeremiah 36:27-32, which tells us how Jeremiah took another roll and gave it to his secretary Baruch, "who wrote from the mouth of Jeremiah all the words of the book which Jehoiakim, king of Judah, had burned in the fire, besides many other words which were not in that book."

Jeremiah's Religion 

We are not to think of Jeremiah as finding a religion already made for him. On the contrary, like every one who has a worthwhile religion, he developed his religious thought and life through experience and observation and study and meditation. 

All through his life he wrestled with his doubts, and again and again he uttered his complaints to God. Once, at least, he even

rebelled against Him. (See 16:19-21.) Yet, through this stormtossed way, Jeremiah discovered some of the greatest truths of religion, which were unsurpassed until Jesus came and gave us the full revelation of God. 

Note the following points in Jeremiah's religion: 

1. God shapes the affairs of life. (1:11, 12)

Through the vision of the almond tree, Jeremiah learns that 

God watches over his word to perform it. The almond tree is the first tree to blossom in Palestine. One day it looks like a dead tree, then comes a warm rain, and it bursts into a beautiful mass of white blossoms. It suggests the power of God in human life. Thou art apparently a dead tree; thou shalt become a thing of life and beauty. "Thou art Simon," said Jesusimpulsive, hot-headed, well-meaning, but weak; thou shalt become Peter-a rock, a leader and a martyr of the early church. 

Thou art Saul, persecuting the Christians; thou shalt become 

Paul, the great Apostle to the Gentiles, and next to Jesus, the founder of Christianity. Thou art a poor country boy with few advantages and great hardships; thou shalt become the President of the United States, friend of the common people, and saviour of thy country.

Jeremiah believed in the transforming power of God in human lives. He also believed that people and events were shaped by God's hand. George Adam Smith says: "We find in Jeremiah blossom and storm, beauty and terror, tender yearning and thunders of doom up to the very end; and yet God watching over all. No matter how dark and troubled the world's affairs may be, God yet watches and rules all to the fulfillment of his will. Josiah might be killed in battle, the nation might be carried away into captivity, yet God watches and rules all according to his own purpose." 

Compare Lowell's "Present Crisis": "Truth forever on the scaffold; wrong forever on the throne; but that scaffold sways the future, and behind the dim Unknown, standeth God within the shadow, keeping watch above his own."

2. Prayer a refuge. (17:7,8)

In a beautiful passage he describes the man who trusteth in the Lord; he shall be as a tree planted by the waters, that spreadeth out his roots by the river and shall not cease from yielding fruit. Through all the lonely years of his life, Jeremiah found a sure refuge in prayer. Has the religion of the modern man a place for prayer or have we outgrown it? A young person said to me, "Once we prayed for rain; now we put in an irrigation ditch." What changes in our idea of prayer does her remark suggest? Has modern science made prayer unnecessary? 

Cromwell, Old Ironsides, went into battle feeling that God was in the saddle with him. The Puritans were devout believers in prayer, and our Thanksgiving Day goes back to the time when they publicly assembled in a day of thanksgiving to God.

If you want real help in the subject of prayer and wish to test it out by experience, which is the scientific way to do, get a copy of Harry Emerson Fosdick's book, The Meaning of Prayer, and use it day by day. 

3. God present everywhere. (23:23, 24)

Jeremiah asked, Can any man hide himself in secret places so that God cannot see him? Does He not fill heaven and earth?

The Hebrews thought that Jehovah was limited to their homeland and temple, and even the earlier prophets believed that when the people went into captivity they left their God behind them. Jeremiah has caught the idea of a universal God who was not limited to any one land or to any one place. "He fills heaven and earth," says the prophet. Compare this idea with Jesus' commission to his disciples: "Go ye therefore and make disciples of all the nations." (Matthew 28:18-20)

Closely connected with God's omnipresence is the thought that they who seek him may find him (29:13). "Ye shall seek me and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart." How close this is to Jesus' teaching: "Ask, and it shall be given you: seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you. For every one that asketh receiveth; and he

that seeketh findeth; and to him that knocketh it shall be opened." (Luke 11:9-11). 

4. Individual responsibility (31:29, 30)

There was a common proverb in Israel in the time of Jeremiah "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge"; meaning that the children were being punished for the sins of their fathers. This philosophy of life led to discouragement and to the attitude, "What's the use of trying?" Jeremiah flatly contradicted the proverb and said that each one is responsible for his own acts and cannot lay the blame on his father and ancestors. The prophet Ezekiel many years later confirmed this teaching of Jeremiah. "You may suffer," said Jeremiah, "because of what your ancestors have done, but it is not a punishment from God upon you." So we may suffer today because of the carelessness of an automobile driver, but the injury is not necessarily visited upon us as a punishment for wrong-doing. This thought of individual responsibility was a new truth in the development of religious ideas, and to Jeremiah we owe it. 

5. The new covenant. (31:31-34)

This passage is called the most profound and significant in the whole Old Testament. It is the high mark of Jeremiah's religious thinking. Note two important points:

A. Religion is to be found in the heart: in the inward parts and not in forms or ceremonies, burnt offerings and sacrifices. ...

B. The individual is to have direct access to God. "They shall teach no more every man his neighbor, and every man his brother . . . for they shall all know [God] from the least unto the greatest of them." This is really the heart of our Protestant religion, which refused to accept the priests or the Church or the Virgin Mary as intermediaries between man and his God.

The con- science of the individual is his final authority, and religion is a direct relationship between man and God.

What Others Say WHY CHRISTIANITY? 

Christianity is the only religion which teaches as a doctrine of great practical as well as scriptural importance that there is at work in the world a divine, universal Holy Spirit, indwelling, teaching, suggesting, reprimanding, inspiring, transforming, available for every individual who will open his heart to this divine inner influence. Jesus himself taught what every true 

Christian experiences that there is a powerful divine agency active in the world, seeking to apply the principles of Jesus and leading on to a fuller appreciation of truth.

These three essential and distinctive features of Christianity may be stated systematically in relation to God, the chief essential of all religion, as follows: (a) In God there is something eternal. That aspect of God which perpetually is the creator and loving ruler of human life may best be known as "Father." (b) In God there is something historic. That aspect of God which has come most fully into the compass of human life, in the gracious character of Jesus Christ, may best be known as his "Son." (c) In God there is something progressive. That aspect of

God which like a continual companion is leading human life forward may best be known as "Holy Spirit."

These three features of Christianity, on the one hand, are highly theoretical; they involve momentous theological implications. On the other hand, they are thoroughly practical; they may be understood and tested in experience. Together they constitute a comprehensive summary of what Christianity itself really is. But Christians also believe that these three Christian

beliefs represent exactly the finest aspirations among the religions of the world; accordingly, together they constitute a prophetic fulfillment of the religious history of the world. 

A compact statement of the essence of Christianity, and also of the blessing which Christianity seeks to give unto the world, has been formulated in the New Testament in the well-known benediction: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of God and the communion of the Holy Spirit be with you all" (2 Corinthians 13:14). 

-ROBERT ERNEST HUME, The World's Living Religions, pp. 276-78. Charles Scribner's Sons.

A THOUGHTFUL RELIGION 

The most unfortunate outcome of this false reading of the Bible is the prevalent opinion that to be religious it is not necessary to think. You can be a good Christian and not think at all.

Indeed the notion is abroad that it is dangerous to think in the realm of religion. You have got to think in business. You go to the dogs in business if you do not think, and think hard. 

You have got to think in science. You get nowhere if you do not think. But in religion you must not think too much. You must not think deeply. If you think too deeply you may become an infidel. Religion is doomed as soon as men cease to think. 

Superstition yawns and swallows them up. The Bible does not make its appeal primarily to the emotions. Its primary and deepest appeal is always to the intellect, the reason, the mind. 

The Old Testament expresses its most thrilling appeal in the great exhortation put into the mouth of God: "Come now, let us reason together, let us argue this matter out, let us think this subject through, and let us see if we cannot arrive at some rational and satisfactory conclusion." In the New Testament, our 

Lord is always asking: "How do you think? What do you think? Why do ye not of yourselves judge what is right?" 

You cannot be a good Christian unless you think.

PERSONAL RELIGION 

Jeremiah was the first prophet to raise this question. In him personal religion came to self-consciousness. It is this fact that gives to him his unique significance in the history of religion. 

The earlier prophets had laid down the essential principles of religion, had made religion a matter of ethics, of holy love, and of moral faith, and had no doubt exemplified these qualities in their own private life. But they did not apparently look upon their own experience of religion as sufficiently important to be worthy of a place in their recorded utterances. Religion with them seems to have been primarily a national affair. It was 

Jeremiah who first gave to it the personal note. It was he who first made the soul of the individual the true seat of religion. 

But this does not mean that he gave up the national point of view. Through all his ministry he continued to address himself to the nation as such. It simply means that he made the conception of religion deeper and more inward. He made its essential nature consist in personal fellowship with God. This implies the ascription of new importance to the individual. It also implies that true religion is not a matter of race, but is as broad as humanity itself. But Jeremiah does not especially

concern himself with these implications. He leaves them to be worked out by his two great successors. What he is himself especially interested in is the actual, vital experience of God. 

And this he finds in himself, in his own soul. He, as Duhm says, "first discovered the soul and its significance for religion." -ALBERT C. KNUDSON, Beacon Lights of Prophecy, pp. 16667. The Methodist Book Concern, 1914. Used by permission.

THE BEGINNING OF CHRISTIANITY 

Religion, according to Jeremiah, is an affair of the heart. 

By "heart" I mean what the Bible means, an affair of the interior life, of the soul, of the innermost chamber of personality. 

There has always been a tendency to make religion something external, formal, ritualistic. The human race has always been haunted and plagued by the delusion that God is angry with men, angry because of their sins, and that he must be appeased by making him presents. You must give him something to placate him. You must offer him a gift to induce him to be friendly.

And so through the ages, men have been giving God things, sometimes a sheep or a goat or a bullock, sometimes a sheaf of grain or a pot of oil or a basket of flowers, or sometimes they have even sacrificed their eldest sons, thinking that in this way God would come round and show his favor. That was the delusion which held sway through thousands of years, and the delusion still prevails in many parts of the world. 

But nineteen hundred years ago in Palestine there lived a Man who broke away from this age-long tradition. He believed that God does not ask or expect material gifts. What he wants is love. All he asks is the heart, the mind, a man's own self.

This idea soon spread and a Jew by the name of Paul gave it classic expression in an address recorded in the book of Acts. 

Paul declared boldly that God does not dwell in temples made with hands; he dwells in the heart. He does not want things made with the hands; he wants the heart. He does not need any material thing for he owns everything; all he wants is the

fellowship of the heart. That idea went out into all parts of the world. A new religion had taken its place in human history- Christianity. 

-CHARLES E. JEFFERSON, Cardinal Ideas of Jeremiah, pp. 5354. By permission of The Macmillan Co., publishers. 

THE ESSENTIALS OF RELIGION 

The ideality and universality of religion-these are the two new grand apprehensions which Jeremiah has given to the world. Every man, as such, is born a child of God. He does not become such through the forms of any definite religion or outward organization; but he becomes such in his heart, through circumcision of the heart and of the ears. 

A pure heart and a pure mind are all that God requires of men; let his piety choose what form it will, so long as it is genuine. Thus we have in Jeremiah the purest and highest consummation of the prophecy of Israel and of the religion of the Old Testament. 

After him One only could come, who was greater than he. 

-CARL HEINRICH CORNILL, Prophets of Israel, pp. 98-99. 

Open Court Publishing Co. 

Our age is in need of a Jeremiah. We need some one to make clear to us the essentials of religion. We have lost our way in a wilderness of superficial and inconsequential things. 

Many good people are uneasy, some of them are sorely troubled, and a few of them are in keen distress. They are worried and dejected because so many things are being shaken and other things are losing their grip upon the modern mind.... Hallowed customs are being surrendered. Some are going and others have already gone. The old custom of teaching the children the catechism and having the pastor examine them on his round from home to home-that went long ago. The custom of family worship is going. It has already gone from a majority even of our most religious homes. The practice of attending two services on Sunday is going. Many churches have discontinued the second service altogether. It seems to many that religion itself is going. Why then should we marvel that many of the saints are in distress?

But we should remind ourselves that no new thing is happening. Religion, to its contemporaries, has always seemed to be going. So it seemed to the devout Jews of the first century. 

They had grown up to think of religion in connection with the altar and the sacrifices and the priesthood and the temple. Without these, religion could not get on. These were indispensable to the true Church of God. But a new interpretation of religion arrived, and all these things considered indispensable were forthwith discarded. . . .

And now in the twentieth century we have come to another crisis. Things long counted indispensable are being left behind. 

The authority of the Bible is being questioned. In many quarters it is being openly repudiated. We can now sympathize with the Roman Catholics four hundred years ago. They were thrown into a panic by the denial of the authority of the Church. 

What can you do, they asked, without an infallible authority?

The Protestant replied: "We have an authority and it is an infallible authority. Here it is the Bible." But now the authority of the Bible is denied, and what can the Protestant say? He can say: "We do not need any external authority.

The seat of authority lies within. Religion is an affair of the heart, and cannot be linked forever with any institution, whether church or book, which lies outside of itself. Christianity is safe so long as we have the human heart." 

-CHARLES E. JEFFERSON, Cardinal Ideas of Jeremiah, pp. 

62, 63, 65. By permission of The Macmillan Co., publishers.

What Jeremiah Tried to Do 

(Read Jeremiah 1:16; 2:4-8, 13, 22, 23)

We are to think of a young man, living in or near to the capital of his nation; a man of high social standing, welleducated, and reared in a religious home. He was destined to play a large part in life; it was the hope of his mother and his training as a child. Not until he was nearly twenty-five years old, however, did he finally decide to throw his life into the boiling caldron of public affairs. 

Note what he set out to do: 

1. Bring the people back to a worship of the true God. (1:16; 2:4-8; 2:13) 

2. Get them to live clean, upright lives. (2:22, 23)

In these two, Jeremiah found the roots of all the evils that threatened the welfare of the nation. 

Suppose you were deciding to throw your life into America's public affairs, would these be the places on which you would focus your efforts? A great deal is being said and written today about the need of America's getting back to God; that she has been off on a grand debauch of greed and pride and selfishness, with money and possessions as her god. Do you think this is a just picture of conditions up to the time of the financial depression? If so, what is the way out? What shall we try to do?

His Personal Feelings (Read Jeremiah 1:19; 4:19-22; 8:20-9:1; 11:18, 23; 15: 

10-21; 20:14-18)

Jeremiah was intensely emotional-a loyal, steadfast but storm-tossed soul. In the expression of his feelings we look through the windows of that soul. He had chosen a hard task, for he was by nature timid and shrinking, a lover of peace. 

Note the play of his emotions: 

1. (1:8, 19) The conviction of God's presence was with him through all the events of his life, keeping him steadfast and loyal. Hold this in mind as you follow the ups and downs of his emotional life. 

2. (4:19-22; 8:2-9:1) A picture of a yearning soul, pained to the very depths by the conditions of his people, crying out, "My heart is disquieted in me; I cannot hold my peace." "For the hurt of the daughter of my people am 

I hurt; I mourn; dismay hath taken hold on me. Is there no balm in Gilead? is there no physician there? ... Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people!" 

Compare Jesus' lament over Jerusalem as he came over the Mount of Olives and looked down on the city of Jerusalem (Luke 19:41-44). We are told that as Jesus saw the city, he wept over it and said, "If thou hadst known. in this day, even thou, the things which belong to peace!"

Compare also Jesus' lament over Jerusalem which is told us in Luke 13:34, 35. “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, that killeth the prophets and stoneth them that are sent unto her! how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her own brood under her wings, and ye would not!" 

3. (11:18) The picture of an innocent man who is like "a gentle lamb that is led to slaughter," unaware of the plots against him.

4. (15:10, 15-18; 20:14-18) To what depths was the prophet stirred in his soul when he could curse the day wherein he was born, and curse the man who brought tidings to his father saying, "A man-child is born to thee!" But this is his outcry in the midst of the troubles which surrounded him. "O woe is me, my mother, that thou hast borne me a man of strife and a man of contention to the whole earth! I have not lent, neither have men lent to me; yet every one of them doth curse me. . . . Why is my pain perpetual and my wound incurable?" he complains. "Wherefore came I forth out of the womb to see labor and sorrow, that my days should be consumed with shame? Would that my mother had been my grave and that I had never been born!"

Perhaps you can think of leaders of our national life or those who have stood out in the history of the world who have been as lonely as Jeremiah and felt that every man's hand was against them. Even the Father of Our Country had his troubles, and grievous ones, during the war for independence and afterwards. 

Think also of Joan of Arc as portrayed by George Bernard

Shaw, when she is surrounded by accusers who tell her that now she is alone: "Yes"; [she replies,] "I am alone on earth: I have always been alone. My father told my brothers to drown me if I would not stay to mind his sheep while France was bleeding to death: France might perish if only our lambs were safe. I thought France would have friends at the court of the king of France; and I find only wolves fighting for pieces of her poor torn body. I thought God would have friends everywhere, because he is the friend of everyone; and in my innocence I believed that you who now cast me out would be like strong towers to keep harm from me. But I am wiser now; and nobody is any the worse for being wiser. Do not think you can frighten me by telling me that I am alone. France is alone; and God is alone; and what is my loneliness before the loneliness of my country and my God? I see now that the loneliness of God is

his strength: what would he be if he listened to your jealous little counsels? Well, my loneliness shall be my strength, too: it is better to be alone with God. His friendship will not fail me, nor his counsel, nor his love. In his strength I will dare, and dare, and dare, until I die." 

-GEORGE BERNARD SHAW, Saint Joan. Brentano's. Used by permission.

His Methods of Work 

Some prophets used one method, some another, to impress their message upon their hearers. Jeremiah had his own methods. Some of them are given below: 

1. PARABLES 

a. The Rotten Girdle (13:1-11) The girdle was a symbol of the people, who once clave unto Jehovah their God, but have become rotten and good-for-nothing. 

b. The Potter (18:1-12) Sitting in the potter's house watch ing him at his work, Jeremiah sees in the clay the people of Judah and in the potter, Jehovah their God. "Behold, ye are as clay in the potter's hand. If you do good, you will become a good vessel; if evil, you will be broken to pieces." 

c. The Broken Bottle (19:1-13) A graphic portrayal of the destruction of Jerusalem. Jeremiah takes a potter's bottle and gathers an audience around him, both the elders of the people and the priests. He tells them they have forsaken their God. He then breaks the bottle in their midst and declares "Thus saith Jehovah of hosts: Even so will

I break this people and this city, as one breaketh a potter's vessel, that cannot be made whole again."

d. The Baskets of Figs (24:1-10) The prophet sees in a vision two baskets of figs-one of very good figs, one of very bad figs. Jehovah tells him that the good figs are the people that have already gone into captivity; the bad figs are the remnants that remain in Jerusalem. They will be "a reproach and a proverb, a taunt and a curse in all places whither I shall drive them." This parable was spoken after the first captivity in 597 B. C. and just before the final captivity in 586 B. C. 

e. The Purchase of the Field at Anathoth (Chapter 32) 

Jeremiah buys from his cousin a field in his native town, Anathoth. He signed the deed, sealed it, called witnesses, paid the money for it. At that time Jerusalem was besieged by Nebuchadrezzar, and Jeremiah had prophesied that it would be captured, and the people carried into exile. 

Undaunted in the midst of gloom, however, he looks forward to the time when the captives shall return, and "houses and fields and vineyards shall yet again be bought in this land." This came to pass a half century later, 538 B. C., long after Jeremiah's death.

Catch the spirit of this prophet, now nearing the end of his life-the flower of the people already carried into captivity, the destruction of the capital imminent, the rulers and the people hostile to him, friendless, lonely; yet in spite of it all, he boldly and publicly buys a field as a symbol that a time shall come when the repentant people will return from captivity. Perhaps you will think of 

George Washington in that terrible winter at Valley Forge at the low ebb of the fortunes of our American Revolution. How much would any one have given for a deed of land at that time, made out in the name of an independent 

American? There is something gripping and challenging in the faith of these great men who refuse to be daunted by circumstances. 

f. The Book Sunk in the River (51:59-64) The book is a symbol of Babylon;

its casting into the Euphrates, a sym-

bol of the fall of Babylon. This was the parable sent to the captives in Babylon by the old prophet. Fearlessly he had attacked their faithlessness to Jehovah; he had pleaded with them, counseled with them, given his life for them, and now they were reaping what they had sowed. "But better times are coming," says Jeremiah. "You are learning your lesson in captivity, and God will forgive you and restore you to your land, and the city of your captivity shall be destroyed."

2. WRITTEN MESSAGES 

The modern business man was not the first to make use of the private secretary. Jeremiah's friend, Baruch, son of Neriah, was his private secretary through all the years of his public career. Read how he wrote down word for word the messages of Jeremiah, and then read them to the princes in the temple (see Chapter 36). After the king burned the roll, Baruch wrote another at Jeremiah's command. The written messages could go where Jeremiah could not; for while the king was reading his words, the prophet was in hiding. 

3. ADDRESSES AT STRATEGIC PLACES 

The prophet did not wait for his hearers to come to him, he went to them. Perhaps it was at one of the populous gates of the city or at some public assembly, or at a street corner where he could attract an audience. Like Paul, he worked in season

and out of season, year in and year out, using whatever method he could to save his people from destruction and to restore them to the "good life."

The Fate He Met 

Of new ideas some one has said that first every one violently opposes them, next some of the people accept them, and finally every one says that is what he always believed. Most of Jeremiah's life was spent in the first stage of this process. Like most reformers, he passed away before people accepted his ideas. Note how he was treated and what finally became of him. 

1. Ridicule (20:7) "I am become a laughing stock all the day; every one mocketh me." Is there anything harder to bear than ridicule from your friends? 

2. Persecution (20:10, 11) Even his familiar friends watch for his fall. 3. 

Plots (18:18) His enemies conspire against him, trump up charges, and agree not to listen to what he says—somewhat of the experience Amos had at Bethel when they tried to suppress his speech. 

4. Imprisonment (20:1-3) He was scourged and put in the stocks over night by Pashur, one of the officers in the temple.

At another time he was arrested and threatened with death by other prophets and priests. He narrowly escaped with his life (26:8-19). Later on he was seized and accused of treason. For many days he lay in the dungeon, until King Zedekiah let him out and confined him to the guardhouse. Not long after this he was again thrown into a dungeon and would have died there, if an Ethiopian servant in the king's house had not prevailed upon the king to release him. 

5. Exile (chapter 43) Against his will the prophet and his secretary were forced to go to Egypt with the last survivors of the people. The prophet had pleaded with them not to go and had warned them that in Egypt they would become a curse and a reproach and would never again see their native land. 

Although in exile, Jeremiah continued to prophesy with the same high courage that had distinguished his whole life.

In that country, after forty years of service to his people, ended the career of this great man. What an example of loyalty to truth! What a challenge to timid souls today, who fear to face ridicule, unpopularity, opposition, and persecution! What an indictment of those who are unwilling to give up anything for the welfare of others! That timid, shrinking soul, with a tender heart of love and compassion and a will of iron, enters upon one of the stormiest times in the country's history and leaves in seeming defeat. He is a dramatic and tragic figure. 

Naturally social, he was denied a family (16:1, 2). He was by nature peaceable, yet he was thrown into the midst of strife (15:10). He was a persevering, courageous man, who found his refuge in prayer and his strength in God. Of all the prophets he comes the nearest to the spirit and experience of Jesus. With his message Jesus was very familiar. In fact, so similar were 

Jesus and Jeremiah, that some people in the time of Jesus said he was Jeremiah (Matthew 16:13, 14).

As you complete the study of this great prophet, ask yourself, Does America need a prophet like him today? What would he say to America if he were living today? 

Jeremiah's Service to the World

The life of this great man seemed to end in defeat. He never lived to see his people returned to God and restored to their land. 

Tradition said he died at the hands of his own countrymen. 

Yet he left behind him ideas which have continued through the centuries and have molded the thoughts and lives of people the world over. 

The great truths he taught are: 

1. That God is not limited to any locality, but is the God of the whole earth. This was Jeremiah's contribution to the idea of the Universality of God. 

2. Religion is an affair of the inner life, for which forms and ceremonies and external things can never be a substitute. A person's religion is the relationship between himself and God.

3. Individual Responsibility. Each one is responsible for his own actions. The greater the person's privilege, the greater is his responsibility.

What Others Say 

1. John Wiclif was born in 1320. He entered the clergy and became a great reformer. He defended the right of the state to deprive unrighteous clergy of their goods. Attention was first called to his views on theological questions in 1375, and in 1377 he was summoned to give account of himself. Popular riots arose, but nothing was done. At a second hearing in 

1378 he was protected by the queen mother. His doctrines were condemned. His body after death was disinterred and burned, and his ashes were thrown into the river. He believed in the right of the individual to form his own opinions on the basis of Scripture and reason. He uncovered the evils of society and showed contempt for organized authority. 

2. William Tyndale was born about 1493. He was educated at Oxford and Cambridge and became a preacher. Since his opinions differed from those of the other clergy, he became involved in religious disputes and was censured for indiscretion. 

He then determined to translate the New Testament into English. There was no help in London, so he went to Germany where the New Testament translation was printed in Worms in 1525. It was carried to England and denounced, but Tyndale continued writing tracts on reform doctrine and also published parts of the Old Testament. In 1535, he was arrested and put in prison; in 1536, he was burned as a heretic. A man of learning, talent, courage, he became a martyr to freedom. 

3. Galileo was born in 1564. He went into medicine, but gave it up for general sciences. He turned his attention to the laws of bodies in motion in opposition to the accepted notions. 

The enmity of the Aristotelians caused him to give up the chair at the University of Pisa, and when he advocated the Copernican system, he was denounced as a heretic. The Grand 

Duke, however, protected him. In 1632 he appeared a second time before the Inquisition and was forced to abjure his scientific theory. 

4. John Wesley was born in 1703, and entered the ministry at twenty-two. Then he taught Greek at Oxford. He and a

band of friends who wanted to help people, visited the prisons, the poor, and read the Bible to them and prayed and preached.

Many people did not like this. They jeered and laughed at 

Wesley, the leader. After acting as a missionary in America, he returned to England, and began preaching his "Methodism" everywhere. His ideas were different, and although many came to hear him, were helped, and converted, many others who did not approve of his ideas and ways, persecuted him everywhere. 

People followed him, hooted him, stoned him. His enemies would ring bells and make a great noise so his sermons could not be heard. They tried to annoy him, to harm him; they mobbed him and surrounded his house. Just because his ideas were different, and he had courage to express them, he suffered these persecutions all his life; yet he did more for England than many more popular men.

GANDHI'S MESSAGE TO ENGLAND 

If we are intent upon completing our independence, it is not from any sense of arrogance. It is not because we want to parade before the universe, that we now are severed from all connection with the British people. On the contrary, you will find in the mandate that the congress contemplates a partnership. 

The congress contemplates a connection with the British people, but that connection is to be such as should exist between two absolutely free peoples. The time was when I prided myself on being, and being called, a British subject. I have ceased for many years to call myself a British subject. I should far rather be called a rebel than a subject; but I have aspired, and still aspire, to be a citizen, not in an empire, but in a commonwealth, in a partnership, if possible, and, if God wills it, an indissoluble partnership, not a partnership superimposed upon one nation by another. Hence, you will find that the congress claims that either party should have the right to sever connections and to dissolve the partnership. I do not for one moment minimize the ability of Great Britain to hold India under subjection with the sword. But which leads to the prosperity of Great Britain and to the economic freedom of Great Britain-an enslaved, but

rebellious, India, or an India as an esteemed partner with Great Britain?

I, personally, would wait, if need be, for ages rather than seek to attain the freedom of my country through bloody means. I feel in the innermost recesses of my heart, after a political experience extending over an unbroken period of close upon thirty-five years, that the world is sick unto death of bloodspilling. The world is seeking a way out, and I flatter myself with the belief that perhaps it will be the privilege of the ancient land of India to show that way out to the hungering world. I have, therefore, no hesitation whatsoever in inviting all the great nations of the earth in giving their hearty cooperation to India in her mighty struggle.

THE FATE OF JEREMIAH 

In Egypt the prophet closed a life full of suffering. Bitter contentions arose with his countrymen. Jeremiah still fearlessly discharged his office as incarnate conscience of his people, and was, according to Jewish tradition, stoned to death by an infuriated mob.

Thus breathed out this great soul Jeremiah, solitary and alone on Egyptian soil under the blows of his own people, for whom during his whole lifetime he had striven and suffered, and from whom, for all his love and faith, he had but reaped hatred and persecution. Truly he drank the cup of suffering to its dregs. 

But, undismayed and dauntless, he fell in his harness, a true soldier of truth. He had become as an iron wall, and as pillars

of brass against the whole land. They had struggled against him, but not overcome him. He fell as a hero, as a conqueror; he could die for the truth, he could not abjure it. 

-CARL HEINRICH CORNILL, Prophets of Israel, pp. 106-07.

WILL OUR CIVILIZATION LAST? 

Will our civilization go down? We do not know. We know that other civilizations have vanished. The civilization of 

Assurbanipal, of Nebuchadnezzar, of Rameses II, of Cyrus the 

Great, of Pericles, of the Antonines, of the Goths and the Saracens and the Aztecs-all these arose, flourished and dissolved. Will ours go down? We only know it cannot go on as it is. It is too dishonest. Society is honeycombed with hypocrisies and shams and lies. It cannot go on indefinitely. 

It is too unbrotherly. The men at the top are too indifferent to the way in which the men at the bottom are obliged to live. 

Races are too snobbish in their attitude to one another, and classes are too cruel in their treatment of one another. It cannot go on. It is too stupid. Great Britain and France and the 

United States are spending hundreds of millions of dollars on the armies and navies, notwithstanding they have just come out of a war loaded down with debt. Here is stupidity in its densest and deadliest form. It cannot go on. Unless our civilization is improved, it is doomed. 

What can be done? One reply is that nothing can be done but to pull it down, rip it up, blow it to atoms, and make room for something different. That is what the Bolshevists say, and the Communists and the Syndicalists and the Anarchistsgroups of alert and determined men who are working in every country to disseminate the philosophy of destruction. 

The majority of men, however, are not concerned with the problem at all. They do not want to be bothered with it. They go on eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, making money and spending it, willing that civilization should drift on, God only knows whither. 

-CHARLES E. JEFFERSON, Cardinal Ideas of Jeremiah, pp. 

71-72. By permission of The Macmillan Co., publishers.

LINCOLN'S FAREWELL TO HIS FRIENDS (NOTE: Thus Sandburg describes Lincoln's departure from Springfield for Washington.)

A path was made for Lincoln from the station to his car; hands stretched out for one last handshake. He hadn't intended to make a speech; but on the platform of the car, as he turned and saw his home people, he took off his hat, stood perfectly still, and looked almost as he had at the Bowling Green burial services when tears had to take the place of words. He raised a hand for silence. They stood, with hats off. 

Then he said slowly, amid the soft gray drizzle from the sky: "Friends, no one who has never been placed in a like position can understand my feelings at this hour nor the oppressive sadness I feel at this parting. For more than a quarter of a century 

I have lived among you, and during all that time I have received nothing but kindness at your hands. Here I have lived from my youth till now I am an old man. Here the most sacred trusts of earth were assumed; here all my children were born; and here one of them lies buried. To you, dear friends, I owe all that I have, all that I am. All the strange checkered past seems to crowd now upon my mind. Today I leave you; I go to assume a task more difficult than that which devolved upon General Washington. Unless the great God who assisted him shall be with and aid me, I must fail. Who Was Jonah?

Jonah was a prophet who lived during the reign of Jeroboam II in Israel, just before Amos and Hosea, or between 780 and 

750 B. C. (2 Kings 14:25). Unlike these two prophets, however, he prophesied victory and prosperity for Israel. He appears to have been a supporter of the king, a one-hundredper-cent type of patriot.

The Book of Jonah 

This is not prophecy nor history but a parable, or story with a lesson. It does not claim to be written by Jonah and apparently is the work of an unknown writer, several centuries after the time of Jonah. It was a customary literary device of Jewish religious writers to take some character of an earlier time and through him bring a message for a later day. Scholars date the book between 400 B. C. and 200 B. C.

When it was written the Jews were becoming intensely and narrowly nationalistic. Their leaders forbade marriage outside their own race and tried in every way to develop a strictly Jewish nation, concerned wholly with its own affairs, not interested in other peoples, but actually antagonistic to them. It was a narrow, exclusive selfish policy. Even Jesus' disciples held the same exclusive feeling (Acts 10:28, 34, 35). Perhaps you can find instances of a similar type in American life, if you note the attitudes and actions of some senators and congressmen. 

Echoes of the popular cry for "one-hundred-per-cent Americans" are still heard through our land-these one-hundred-percenters to be white, native-born, Protestant Americans. Compare this slogan with the attitude of the Jewish leaders centuries ago.

The Book of Jonah was written to combat this narrow nationalism. It teaches that God cares for other nations-even the hated Babylonians—as well as for the Jews, and that the Jewish nation had a mission of service to the world. This teaching is in line with that of our best thinkers, who sincerely believe that America has a world service to perform; that her opportunity is unique, her responsibility great, her mission inescapable, and that this mission is to be accomplished not by force but by unselfish good will. 

Jonah's Experience

Read this dramatic story (1:1-4:11), noting the following points: 

1. Jonah's Mission 

(1) He is commanded to go to Nineveh, the Babylonian capital, chief city of the nation which had carried the Jews into captivity-naturally not a pleasant journey, especially as he is sent to save the city from its wrong-doing (1:1, 2). (2) He flatly disobeys the command and goes in exactly the opposite direction. Nineveh was east, Jonah goes west, en route to Tarshish, a Phoenician colony at the western end of the 

Mediterranean Sea. (3) He is overtaken by a storm at sea. Note the sudden tempest, the storm-tossed boat, the sailors throwing overboard the cargo, calling on their gods for deliverance, casting lots to find who is the one against whom the gods are angry-and Jonah is asleep in the hold of the ship. A graphic picture. Note the swift ensuing action and the calm after Jonah was thrown overboard. 

2. Jonah's Prayer 

Remember that you are studying a parable, not an historical narrative, and the characters and events are symbolic. Jonah symbolizes the nation, Israel. His sojourn in the great fish represents Israel's exile (Jeremiah 51:34, 44). The return from captivity is symbolized by Jonah's escape from the belly of the great fish. This figurative language was perfectly plain to those for whom this book was written.

3. Jonah's Message and Nineveh's Repentance

This time Jonah obeys and brings to Nineveh Jehovah's message of doom. Note that the people repent (3:5); the king repents (v. 6); the nation, as a whole, prays for mercy (vs. 

7-9); and God forgives them (v. 10). 

4. Jonah's Disappointment (4:1-9) 

Jonah was angry; petulantly so. He even wanted to die (vs. 3, 4). He did not want God to love and forgive the enemies of Israel, and he could not see why he should care for any other people except Israel. What a picture! On the one side, a merciful God, slow to anger, abundant in loving kindness; on the other, a mere man, proud in his own conceits, blinded by his prejudices, warped in his soul, vindictive in his spirit. Use your imagination and see little Jonah building a shack in the hot sun on a side hill overlooking the city. There he sits and perspires, waiting for his hopes to be fulfilled and the city destroyed. God even gives him a little shade (v. 6), so thoughtful is he of his prophet; but the gourd (or shrub) withers in the heat and Jonah is sun-struck (v. 8). What a spectacle! What a miserably small soul he shows! Angry again at God and wanting to die because the little shrub has withered up! 

5. Jonah Learns a New Lesson (4:10, 11) 

Swiftly the story reaches its climax and teaching (vs. 10, 11). "Jonah, you are greatly concerned over a little plant which came up in a night and perished in a night; should I not care for the fate of a great city and its inhabitants? Israel, you are so steeped in your own narrow ideas, your outlook on life is so selfish, that you care nothing for the welfare of other nations. 

But I am the God of all the nations and peoples of the earth."

America, you have refused to take your share of the world's burden. You have said that the fate of other nations was no concern of yours. What Others Say THE MOST CHRISTIAN BOOK of the Old Testament 

What, then, is the chief lesson of the Book of Jonah? Certainly it is possible to point out several truths taught and emphasized; nevertheless, there is one central truth that "runs like a red thread through the whole and at last becomes a knot whose unloosing in 4:10, 11 forms the glorious finale." This truth is the universality of the divine plan of redemption. Nowhere else in the Old Testament is such continued stress laid upon the Fatherhood of God, embracing in its infinite love the whole human race. The Book of Jonah is indeed a "missionary book," teaching that God does not wish that "any should perish, but that all should come to repentance." Some of the post-exilic writings of the Old Testament indicate that there was growing among the Jews a narrow, exclusive tendency, which produced the idea that salvation was for the Jews only, an idea against which early Christianity had to battle with all her might. To counteract this spirit of exclusiveness is the aim of the Book of Jonah; to show that the belief that Jehovah would save only the Jews and destroy all other nations was an unwarranted assumption. "The national limits of the old covenant are here wondrously broken through; the entire heathen world opens as a mission field to the messengers of Jehovah. Thus the book, with its wide-hearted outlook on God's ways, and the sharp criticism of the selfish spirit of the Jewish people, as a didactic work, is itself a miracle in the literature of this people." No

one but a prophet, filled with the Spirit of Jehovah, could have written this, the most Christian of all Old Testament books.

NATIONALISM OR PATRIOTISM 

Patriotism must not be compared with nationalism. The one is natural, the other artificial. The former is a healthy instinct, the latter, in its modern form, is something consciously and deliberately inculcated. It is a profound mistake to regard it as one of the permanent features of human nature which we cannot hope to change. . . .

Certain it is that whether we regard nationalism as a political system, a religion, or a business enterprise—and it is all threeit has certainly been the most fertile cause of war during the last century and a quarter. It is possible to count no less than fifty-four wars and rebellions during that period directly caused by the advancement or the suppression of nationalist aspirations.

During that last tiny fragment of the known history of our race, all the astonishing inventiveness of the human spirit has, in the hands of nationalism, been turned to mutual destruction. The steam engine, electricity, the internal combustion engine, all have been seized upon by the national state to provide the monster battleship, the treacherous submarine, and the horror of aerial bombardment for use against its neighbors. The new developments of transport and communication have made it possible to mobilize the manhood of a nation for war within a matter of hours, and have helped to clamp the curse of conscription upon the neck of Europe.

The difference between such nationalism and real patriotism is as great as that between love and lust. The true patriot desires the greatness and prosperity of his country in the largest sense. He is not satisfied with mere material wealth. He wishes to see his country in the van of intellectual and moral progress. 

He desires to see her lead the world in all good ways. He believes that to hope for continued riches without this spiritual health is futile and worthless. He conceives of his country as the trustee of all her wealth and power for the benefit of mankind. In other words he recognizes no difference in the ethical principles applicable to the individual or the state. 

If then our patriotism is to be worth having, if we are to love our country intelligently and to good purpose, we must not merely seek what are usually called her interests. Or rather we must recognize that her interests stretch far beyond her immediate growth in prosperity and power and include the acceptance of responsibility for such things as the maintenance of world peace and the support of international justice. 

-VISCOUNT CECIL, The New Patriotism, pp. 170-72, 177 

Harper's, July, 1932.
