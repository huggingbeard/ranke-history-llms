The Commonwealth of Massachusetts RESOLVES OF 1961 CHAP. 116. RESOLVE PROVIDING FOR A STUDY AND INVESTIGATION BY AN UNPAID SPECIAL COMMISSION RELATIVE TO MUNICIPAL HOME RULE. 

Resolved, That an unpaid special commission to consist of three members of the senate, to be designated by the president thereof, five members of the house of representatives, to be designated by the speaker thereof, and six persons to be appointed by the governor of whom two shall be municipal officials or officers who are members of the Massachusetts League of Cities and Towns, one shall be a member of the Massachusetts Mayor's Association, one a member of the Massachusetts Selectmen's Association, is hereby established for the purpose of making an investigation and study of "home rule" for cities and towns of the commonwealth. Said commission shall consider the subject matter of current senate document numbered 580. 

Said commission may call upon any department, board, commission and officer of the commonwealth or its political subdivisions for such information as the commission may desire in the course of its study; shall be provided with quarters in the state house or elsewhere; may travel within and without the commonwealth; may hold public hearings; and may expend for legal, clerical, and other services and supplies such sums as may be appropriated therefor. 

Said commission shall report from time to time to the general court the results of its study and investigation and its recommendations, if any, together with drafts of legislation, and of proposals for amendments to the constitution suitable for introduction by a member of the general court, necessary to carry such recommendations into effect, by filing the same with the clerk of the senate and shall file its final report not later than the last Wednesday in January, nineteen hundred and sixty-two.

Procedure 

The Commission determined that the content of the report of the Legislative Research Council, Senate No. 580, should receive the special consideration that it merited under the terms of Resolve 116, and for this reason a very close liaison was kept with the Director of the Legislative Research Bureau. The Commission wishes to acknowledge the splendid spirit of cooperation which the Legislative Research Bureau displayed throughout the existence of the Special Commission. 

Further, the Commission did agree that it would be in the public interest to hear from individuals and organizations interested in municipal home rule for the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, as well as from persons outside the Commonwealth experienced in, the ramifications of home rule. 

The Massachusetts Federation of Taxpayers Associations is one of the civic organizations throughout the Commonwealth which has exhibited a continuous and concerned interest in municipal home rule, an interest demonstrated by the legislative proposals which it has submitted for the consideration of the General Court through the years relative to home rule. Mr. Lyman Ziegler, municipal consultant, was invited to represent the Federation at a hearing of the Commission and to present to the commission the philosophy of the Federation in advancing two (2) specific pieces of Constitutional proposals (Appendices "A" and "B" of this report).

Mr. Bolan related to the Commission steps leading to the approval of a proposed constitutional home rule amendment by the Constitutional Convention, called in Rhode Island in 1951. This amendment was subsequently ratified by the electorate of that state by a vote greater than that given to any other amendment which T had been simultaneously submitted to the voters for ratification.

In summary, Mr. Bolan offered these ideas: (1) that municipal home rule gives to citizens the opportunity to improve their local governmental structure without bothering the State Legislature; (2) that home rule of itself does not introduce violent change, but that change comes as a result of local action in the various communities by interested citizens; (3) that oftentimes, cities and towns do not take full advantage of home rule, except to change the basic law of the community, namely: the municipal charter; (4) that local governing bodies assume a very cautious approach to the full employment of home rule powers; (5) that the opinions and decisions of the Supreme Court have a certain ameliorating effect on home rule; and (6) that even with a grant of municipal home rule, the legislature still retains many powers with respect to the cities and towns.

Mr. Reynolds likewise presented data on municipal home rule, as it has worked in the State of Connecticut. He stated that home rule came about in Connecticut not from any local demand but because the State Legislature saw in home rule a means for improving its own procedure. The case in point was the 1955 Session of the legislature when a number of important state-wide proposals were shunted aside due to the fairly sizeable number of purely local bills submitted for the consideration of the assembly. The result of this blockage was the enactment of a home rule statute which authorized some fifty-seven (57) local powers for cities and towns throughout the state. Mr. Reynolds commented that a home rule amendment is also being considered, and the only obstacle to its passage is the fact that amendments have been offered which necessitate concurrence of both branches of the legislature before final passage.

Mr. Reynolds commented that home rule in Connecticut did not deny to the State the power to act uniformly on matters affecting the cities and towns, nor did it deny to an individual city or town the right to petition the legislature. In answer to a Commission question as to the principal reason for the enactment of home rule in Rhode Island and Connecticut, Mr. Bolan and Mr. Reynolds agreed that the motivating factor in both states was the tremendous growth of population in the cities and towns, accompanied by the need for additional powers by local governments to cope with the problems attendant to such growth. 

At a succeeding hearing, held at Boston College, the Commission was privileged to hear from the Honorable Francis Bergan, Presiding Justice, Appellate Division, Supreme Court, State of New York. 

Justice Bergan had served as vice chairman of the Temporary Commission on the Simplification of the New York Constitution, and as chairman of the sub-committee on Local Government of this Commission. Immediately prior to his appearance before the Commission, Justice Bergan had been the principal speaker at the National Conference on Government held in Miami Beach, Florida on the topic: "The Legal Basis for Local Governments in Metropolitan 

Areas". 

Justice Bergan, in his presentation to the Commission, stated that there were two (2) basic schools of thought in providing a grant of home rule to cities and towns: (a) the Fordham School, and (b) the Bromage School. Bromage school of thought would provide explicit and stated home rule powers for local government units, whereas the Fordham school would provide for a general grant of legislative powers to the cities and towns, but would reserve to the state legislature restrictions on such grant through general law. Justice Bergan commented that after serious study, his Commission leaned strongly toward the Fordham approach and incorporated into its final report a suggested local government amendment accordingly (Appendix "D" of this report). He offered the opinion that municipal home rule was often emasculated by court decision and opinion where an attempt had been made to detail specific local powers in home rule provisions. Evaluation of Replies 

As can be ascertained from the report, not every question was asked of each and every grouping of municipal officials. However, one inquiry was addressed to all groupings, as follows: “Do you believe that the State Constitution should be amended to provide home rule for the cities and towns of the Commonwealth? Or, do you believe that home rule could be provided better in another manner? Please stipulate." It was significant to the Commission that when this question was so structured, there was an overwhelming consensus in favor of the constitutional change approach to local government. No attempt was made to define the term, "municipal home rule". This lack of definition was purposely intended so as not to establish for these officials any pre-conceived notion as to the content of home rule, which could be interpreted as a Commission point of view. Further than this, opportunity was afforded to each respondent to indicate another means for a grant of home rule, and the fact that only 38 out of a total of 265 replies stipulated either legislative enactment or a change in joint rules is a most important and weighty finding. 

On the whole, local officials generally embrace the principle of constitutional home rule, and in questions dealing directly with a given facet of home rule, these same officials exhibited a great deal of discretion in allocating to the State Government certain powers, while allocating to local government other designated powers. 

One of the local powers normally included in any grant of municipal home rule is that of local determination of the acquisition, retention and disposal of real estate by the municipal corporation. 

Out of a total of 154 replies, only 19 replies favored this power being retained by the State, and 125 replies favored this power being delegated to local units of government. 

Perhaps, one of the most important findings, as a result of the questionnaires, is the overwhelming consensus of municipal officials that municipal personnel administration, in all its aspects, should be left to the individual municipality. Questions dealing

[Mar. 

with municipal personnel matters were framed in several ways, and there is no doubt but that there is a well-defined feeling on the part of local officials that violations of home rule have occurred with greater regularity in this area of state-local relations than any other single area.

Another important finding had to do with the field of municipal finance, as it is related to municipal home rule. Again, several questions were so structured as to elicit opinions of these officials on many financial matters of a local nature. In general, local officials feel that the municipal finance provisions, as spelled out in the general laws, are basically sound. The inference that may be glimpsed from such replies is that cities and towns are generally satisfied with the controls that are exercised through general legislation, that they are not particularly stringent, and that they do not constitute a home rule issue. A certain amount of caution must be exercised, however, in giving more weight to this position than can be culled from the responses, due to the fact that (1) there was not an overwhelming vote of confidence in this matter, (2) there were spasmodic suggestions where the general laws could be improved, and (3) that a portion of this sentiment was due to the sympathetic treatment on the part of those state officials handling the day-to-day finance problems in conjunction with local officials. 

The Commission finds that local officials had strong feelings against the passage of so-called "acceptance statutes", and the existence of such statutes constitutes a friction point in the gamut of state-local relations. A listing of such statutes is appended as part of this report. The Commission advises caution in the use of this list (Appendix F), on the basis that it may not be the complete enumeration of all such statutes. 

On the matter of referenda on the salaries of mayors and councilmen in cities, respondents felt that such referenda are unnecessary, that salaries of locally elected officials are a matter of internal administration and that the local legislative body should be the logical agency for final determination of personnel matters, especially those dealing with the setting of salaries and wages.

The Commission is very much concerned with the fact that there are two (2) separate and distinct schools of thought relative to providing municipal home rule. Senate No. 580 offers a completely unbiased analysis of both types of home rule grants. 

CHAPTER II. CONCLUSIONS Home Rule Definition 

The Commission finds that municipal home rule is one of the most intricate areas in government, one in which definition cannot be applied with universal agreement. Authorities neither agree among themselves as to approach, definition or scope of home rule. 

Jefferson B. Fordham, Dean of the Law School, University of Penn sylvania and a noted authority on home rule has this to say in definition: "When we speak more broadly of the home rule move-

1962.] 

ment in the United States, we usually have reference to a method of providing for municipal government, which involves power in the electorate of a municipality to frame, adopt and amend a charter of government." In this sense, Dean Fordham looks upon home rule as that type of grant, whether it be constitutional or legislative, whereby cities and towns can prepare and adopt their own basic law, providing for the organization and structure of local government without the intervention of a higher level of government. Dr. Rodney L. Mott, in his treatise, "Home Rule for 

America's Cities", comes very close to this definition in the statement: "A locality may be said to have 'home rule' when it has the authority to determine for itself the organization, procedures and powers of its own government. . . How much home rule a locality. has depends on the extent to which interference with it on the part of other governments is reduced to a minimum, or the extent to which it is able to settle important issues for itself, and on the extent to which it has the largest possible amount of self-government."

This same train of thought apparently can be glimpsed if we look to an authority, such as Dr. Howard L. McBain, who writes in his monumental work, "The Law and Practice of Municipal Home 

Rule": "Broadly construed, the term 'municipal home rule' has reference to any power of self-government that may be conferred upon a city, whether the grant of such power be referable to statute or constitution. In American usage, however, the term has become associated with those powers that are vested in cities by constitutional provisions, and more especially provisions that extend to cities the authority to frame and adopt their own charters." 

It may be said in passing that the Council of State Governments, in its report on state-local relations, restricts its definition of home rule to constitutional grants of local autonomy because of the fact that any grant of home rule by legislative act alone may be elimar inated by subsequent legislation. The definition offered by the 

Legislative Research Bureau, namely: "the autonomy of local governments within the sovereign state over all purely local matters, whether established by constitutional or statutory provisions" is i not in contradiction to the others offered, and can even be apve praised as being more succinct and meaningful.

[Mar. 

When the Commission compares these concepts of municipal home rule with the wording of Article 2 of the Articles of Amendment to the Massachusetts Constitution, we find the relationship that exists between the Commonwealth and its political sub-divisions is in contradistinction to home rule, in that it places in the hands of the General Court full power and authority to erect and constitute municipal and city governments in any corporate town or towns in the Commonwealth, and to grant to the inhabitants thereof such powers, privileges and immunities not repugnant to the constitution as the General Court shall deem necessary or expedient, for the regulation and government thereof. If we should compare this wording with that contained in Article XXVIII of the Articles of Amendment to the Rhode Island Constitution, commonly referred to as the Home Rule Amendment, the difference of approach to municipal government under modern conditions can be readily observed. Section 1 of the Rhode Island constitutional grant of home rule is cited verbatim: "It is the intention of this article to grant and confirm to the people in every city and town in this state the right of self-government in all local matters." 

Under existing circumstances in Massachusetts the cities and towns are creatures of the General Court; they have no powers of their own. In a case involving a similar relationship of locality to the state legislature, the City of Clinton v. Cedar Rapids and Missouri Railroad Company, (24 Iowa 485) the Supreme Court of

Missouri held: "The state legislature might, by a single act, if we can suppose it capable of so great a folly and so great a wrong, sweep from existence all the municipal corporations of the State, and the corporations could not prevent it." In another case, worthy of mention, Trenton v. State of New Jersey, (262 U.S. - 182), the 

Court re-affirmed this position in its decision, which reads in part: "The State, at its pleasure, may modify or withdraw all such powers, may take without compensation such property, hold it itself, or vest it in other agencies, expand or contract the territorial area, unite the whole part of it with another municipality, repeal the charter, and destroy the corporation. All of this may be done, conditionally or unconditionally, with or without the consent of the citizens, or even against their protests."

1962.] SENATE - - 

Here in Massachusetts, there have been several court decisions, dealing with the powers (or lack of powers) of the municipal corporation. In the case of Commonwealth v. Plaisted (148 Mass. 

375), the Supreme Court held: "The several towns and cities are agencies of government, largely under the control of the legislature. The powers and duties of all the towns and cities, except so far as they are specifically provided for in the Constitution are created and defined by the legislature, and we have no doubt that it has the right in its discretion to change the powers and duties created by itself and to vest such powers in officers appointed by the governor, if in its judgment the public good requires this, instead of leaving such officers to be elected by or appointed by the municipal authorities."

In an earlier case, Weymouth and Braintree Fire District v. 

County Commissioners (108 Mass. 142), we see very much of the same language: "There can be no doubt that the power to create, change and destroy municipal corporations is in the legislature. This power has been so long and so frequently exercised upon counties, town and school districts, in dividing them, altering their boundary lines, increasing and diminishing their powers and in abolishing some of them, that no authorities need to be cited on this point. The Constitution does not establish these corporations, but vests in the legislature a general jurisdiction over the subject by its grant of power to make wholesome laws, as it shall judge to be for the general good and welfare of the Commonwealth." 

In a recent decision, Atherton v. Board of Selectmen, Town of 

Bourne, (337 Mass. 250), the Court held that municipalities are subordinate agencies of the State and have only those powers conferred upon them by statute.

In substance, therefore, the Commission finds that municipal home rule, as visualized by leading authorities, does not exist in Massachusetts. The Commission finds that while the several states and the Federal Government possess unto themselves the machinery for preparing, revising and amending their fundamental laws (i.e. constitutions), the cities and towns of the Commonwealth have no authority to make provision for their own organization and structure of government. In other states, where cities and towns have been given grants of home rule powers, there do not appear any serious problems which would make municipal home rule any less feasible or advisable here in the Commonwealth. While there

[Mar. 

is no apparent demand on any widespread scale for municipal home rule in Massachusetts, it is apparent that other state legislatures have assumed a leadership role in bringing about this desirable change in state-local relations. The lack of agreement between authorities in this field as to the content of "local powers" within the meaning of any grant of municipal home rule should not serve to deter the Commonwealth from seeking a workable solution to improve the relations between the State Government and its municipal corporations through either a constitutional amendment, or through legislative enactment, or through both means. 

Metropolitan Area Problems 

The Commission finds that ever-increasing attention is being given by political scientists, by municipal officials, by state legislators, by the United States Congress and even by the Office of the President to the problems being created by population growth in urban areas. 

These are commonly referred to as "metropolitan area problems". Such problems are very real and most pressing. 

As a result of the 1960 Federal Census, authorities in the field of government visualize an existing megalopolis, extending from Portland, Maine, as far South as Washington, D.C. The number of standard metropolitan areas have been added to, and the boundaries of existing standard metropolitan areas have been extended. 

It is estimated that in Massachusetts, 85.2% of the people reside in such areas.

It is not within the providence of this Commission to consider in any depth either the impact of regional growth or to advance proposals for coping with attendant problems. However, the Commission would be remiss in its responsibility if it did not give some recognition and weight to the relevance of metropolitan area problems to home rule.

In July, 1961, the Advisory Commission on Intergovernmental 

Relations published a report entitled: "Governmental Structure, 

Organization and Planning in Metropolitan Areas", and one of the most salient findings in this report is as follows: "The Commission subscribes firmly to the principle of maximum flexibility and freedom of action for local units of government in meeting the needs of their citizens; however, the Commission also believes that certain limitations must be introduced against the historical concept of home rule as

1962.] 

SENATE - 

The applied to political subdivisions located within metropolitan areas. 

Commission recommends that the States, when considering either general constitutional revision or undertaking constitutional changes with regard to local home rule, reserve sufficient authority in the legislature to enable legislative action where necessary to modify responsibilities of and relationships among local units of government located within metropolitan areas in the best interests of the people and the area as a whole." 

This is no isolated finding. Persons genuinely concerned both with the future of the many state governments and with the vitality of the countless number of cities and towns throughout the nation understand that there must be a change in basic thinking with regard to the extent that home rule should be conferred upon the municipal corporation. Up to this point, there has been a marked tendency to incorporate into state constitutions grants of home rule powers to municipalities which would make effective cooperation between communities in a given geographical area dependent almost entirely on the whims and attitudes of a relatively few local officials, perhaps to the detriment of the region as a whole. 

It is obvious, at the same time, that when such home rule grants were originally framed and ratified, the intent was not to minimize effective regional action, but rather to furnish to the political subdivisions those powers which they needed to cope with problems brought about by their own particular growth and the subsequent demands for better and improved municipal services. 

The extent to which authorities in municipal government are concerned with the advent of problems of a regional nature viewed in the context of some positive delegation of home rule powers by the State Government upon its cities and towns is evidenced by the following excerpt from a speech given by Dean Jefferson B. Fordham to the National Conference of Government in 1954: "Perhaps the most important challenge in America today to our capacity for governmental organization and administration comes from the increasingly metropolitan character of American life. The complex problems of metropolitanism are pressing hard upon us and it would be shortsighted indeed, to formulate a constitutional policy as to municipal home rule which did not take them into account. Within the scope of a metropolitan area there may be a primary city and a number of lesser municipalities. The geographical reach of each of them is far short of the scope of metropolitan problems. . . . . 

It is significant that the 1961 National Conference on Government was dedicated almost in its entirety to the complex and come plicated issues encompassed in a reasonable approach to regional

[Mar. 

problems. It is likewise indicative of this concern that the National 

Municipal League is considering some abandonment of its traditional pattern of a self-executing home rule provision and a grant of specific local powers in re-shaping its "Model State Constitution." 

While the finished product is not available, current thinking is to so structure a constitutional grant of home rule that will still permit the state legislature to act expediently and expeditiously on matters that affect two or more municipalities' problems which conceivably transcend existing municipal boundaries. 

In this light, it is of interest to note a primary finding of the Temporary Commission on the Simplification of the New York 

Constitution, as follows: 

"Facilities for going to and from work and making daily purchases of food and supplies are but part of the total challenge to local governments. The changes that are going forward require cooperation spread out over larger areas to provide health facilities, including hospital services, water supply, sewage disposal, transportation, and to set up traffic and police regulations and zoning and land use controls. "These problems are at once the joint concern of the city and of the related or affected counties, towns and villages which have close economic ties with it. They increasingly involve the State, in respect both of its own services and of its responsibility for the adequacy of the basic structure and resources of local government. This interdependence in public problems makes essential new forms of public cooperation. The prob- lems grow progressively more acute, especially in the very largest center, but they exist everywhere in which the city or other urban community has become a business and economic nucleus for a surrounding area of growing and expanding population. And the impact of the basic forces at work is such that no part of the State is so remote as not to feel the need for new vigor and effectiveness in local government. "These developments point to three basic requirements of a sound Local Governments (i.e. Home Rule) Article in a modern constitution for the 

State. Briefly, these requirements are: 

1. It should promote local self-government by providing for a broad unambiguous grant of power to local governments that will stimulate initiative and vigor in meeting new and expanding responsibilities. 

2. It should permit maximum intergovernmental cooperation in meeting problems that cannot be handled properly by each local government acting alone. 

3. It should free the Legislature of the burden of acting on hosts of local bills so that it may concentrate on matters of importance to the whole State."

While these observations were made by a Temporary Commission sitting in and for the State of New York, the findings are just

1962.] 

as applicable to Massachusetts. The Temporary Commission, in re-writing portions of the New York Constitution, envisaged the need for both a strong and vigorous State Government, as well as a need for strong and vigorous local units of government. The same needs exist in Massachusetts to no lesser degree. 

Effect on Legislative Workload 

The Commission is particularly interested in the third finding of the New York Temporary Commission, dealing with freeing the legislature of the burden of considering innumerable local bills. 

In Connecticut, the enactment of a home rule measure by the State Legislature came about because of a log-jam within the legislative arm of government, wherein important bills pertinent to state-wide issues did not receive the proper attention of the legislators due to the tremendous number of local bills that had been submitted and were being considered. The legislature, in fact, did call itself into special session for the express purpose of doing something concrete about the reduction in the number of local bills. 

Out of this situation developed the Connecticut Home Rule Law, enacted by the 1957 General Assembly and subsequently improved by amendments by both the 1958 special session, the 1959 and 1961 sessions of that body.

Following the adoption of this piece of legislation, the Commission notes with interest the decreasing ratio of local bills to total bills submitted to the 1959 and 1961 biennial sessions of the Connecticut Legislature, as follows: 

1957 Session Total Bills: 1,352 

Local Bills: 234 

1959 Session Total Bills: 1,268 Local Bills: 110 1961 Session - Total Bills: 1,027 Local Bills: 76 

Comparative figures are offered for Massachusetts in Senate No. 

580, as follows: 

1956 Session Total Bills: 4,130 Local Bills: 781 1959 Session Total Bills: 3,907 Local Bills: 859 

1960 Session Total Bills: 4,205 Local Bills: 828 

Thus, about one out of every five bills submitted for the consideration of the General Court can be properly labeled as a "local bill". Furthermore, the proportion of local bills to total bills has not varied appreciably in the last decade. The Legislative Research

[Mar. 

Bureau points out that of the bills submitted in 1956, 1959 and 1960, those enacted into law ranged from 28% to 35%, dealing with almost all phases of strictly "local affairs."

Dr. Rodney L. Mott, again in his work, "Home Rule for America's Cities" points out that "legislative control of cities is fully as bad for the legislature as it is for the municipalities themselves. 

The system of special legislation opens the door to special privileges which legislators find it hard to refuse. The sheer volume of these demands tends to swamp the lawmakers and prevent deliberation on the major issues of state policy. These local and special bills sometimes comprise half of the bills introduced in each session. Legislative interference with cities tends to turn state legislatures into spasmodic city councils. Home rule, as a device for returning local business to the city, is the obvious remedy for these evils."

CHAPTER III. RECOMMENDATIONS 

Completeness of Research

The Special Commission on Municipal Home Rule is convinced that Senate No. 580 of March 22, 1961 contains all the basic research that needs to be done with regard to the philosophy of home rule, its development, home rule issues in Massachusetts, a critique of proposals submitted to the General Court and the many other aspects of the subject. In the opinion of the Commission, this report is one of the most comprehensive analyses of this area of statelocal relations. While this statement appears earlier in this report, the members are convinced that it bears repetition because of the effort that must have been applied to have resulted in a presentation replete with details of home rule, while at the same time, giving due consideration to the principal points at issue. 

In like manner, the Special Commission feels that it has heard personally from leading authorities in this field, who not only possess an insight into the theory of municipal home rule but who have had a fairly broad experience in dealing with the concrete problems attendant to home rule. By means of this report to the 

General Court, therefore, the Commission offers its sincere thanks to Honorable Francis Bergen, State of New York; to Mr. Robert P.

1962.25 

Bolan of Providence, Rhode Island; to Mr. William J. Reynolds of 

Hartford, Connecticut; and to Mr. Lyman Ziegler of the Massachusetts Federation of Taxpayers Associations. In conjunction with this expression of gratitude, the Commission is of the opinion that having heard from such experts, there is no need of further consultations with persons known to possess knowledge in this field of endeavour, recognizing, at the same time, that there are other authorities whose advice and counsel may have been of considerable value to the Commission in its deliberations. Rather, the calibre of those persons who did make an appearance before the Commission afforded sufficient and ample opportunity for the members to obtain a good sampling of alternative approaches to the solution of the home rule problem in the Commonwealth. 

Finally, in acknowledgment of the 303 replies to questionnaires submitted to the Commission from 166 cities and towns, the Commission is agreed that there is a well-defined need for municipal home rule, as can be readily gleaned from the answers submitted to the Commission from the many diverse groupings on municipal officials. Through the responses received in answer to twenty-four (24) distinct inquiries presented, the Commission is persuaded of the necessity of dealing with home rule through both constitutional and legislative change.

The recommendations, therefore, of the Commission are: (1) that no further research need be entered into with regard to the theoretical aspects of municipal home rule, (2) that there is no further urgency to discuss home rule in its many details with other noted authorities in this field, and (3) that municipal officials, particularly the municipal administrators, have indicated in no uncertain terms the necessity to deal forthright with the matter of a positive grant of home rule to the cities and towns. 

Current Proposals 

There are before the 1962 Session of the General Court two (2) proposals dealing with municipal home rule. These measures submitted by the Massachusetts League of Cities and Towns were first introduced by the Massachusetts Federation of Taxpayers Associations in 1951, and with the exception of few sessions, the same pro- P posals with minor changes have been examined by the General

[Mar. 

Court over the period of a decade. The propositions, in bill form, have been included as part of this report (Appendices "A" and "B"). In addition, the same proposals are dealt with in Senate No. 

580, Pages 101 through 108.

In essence, both bills are intended to provide a degree of home rule by restricting the legislature in two (2) areas of state-local relations: (a) the method of passage of laws of purely local concern, and (b) the intervention of the General Court in specified spheres of local interest.

For the purpose of this report, these are referred to as negative home rule proposals. While their intent is to restrain the hand of the General Court, they do not grant to the cities and towns any substantive home rule powers. If the application of these bills were to hinder the General Court from necessary and effective action with respect to the cities and towns, which are creatures of and responsible to the State Government, this Commission would recommend that they be disapproved. The Special Commission is of the opinion that there are instances where the most effective remedy to problems that seemingly are of a local concern lies with the legislative power vested in the General Court. However, with relation to the aforesaid proposals, even though the intent is to check the General Court somewhat, the Commission feels that there are sufficient safeguards written into both measures to permit the legislative arm of the State Government to act expeditiously even in areas of local significance, when and if the need should arise.

The Commission is of the opinion that should these proposals be incorporated into the State Constitution, the workload of the General Court will be considerably lightened. The amount of time that would be required to examine bills of local importance would depend, for the most part, on action taken by local legislative bodies in accordance with the procedure outlined in these proposals. 

Both bills would place a heavy responsibility on local legislative bodies to give weighty consideration to those propositions, submitted by local officials or by private citizens, which demand legislative action at the State level of government. This shifting of accountability, even though in limited areas, would present to local legislative bodies a better appreciation of the types and kinds of de-

1962.] 

mands made upon the General Court, sitting in annual session on some 5,000 bills of both state-wide and local interest. 

In this light, then, the Commission recommends passage of both these proposals to the General Court. 

Positive Grant of Home Rule 

The Commission has indicated that passage of the two current proposals (Appendices "A" and "B") would, at their best, offer a negative form of home rule to the cities and towns of the Commonwealth. If the Commission were to restrict its recommendations to the approval of said measures, it would do a great disservice to the 351 municipal corporations in the Commonwealth. Moreover, in light of the research that went into the preparation of Senate No. 580, and in light of the counsel offered by the experts who appeared before the Commission, and finally, in light of the replies from municipal officials here in the Commonwealth to specific questions asked by this Commission, the members would be remiss in their duties if they did not give recognition to the fact that there exists the need for a positive approach to municipal home rule. 

The Commission has taken note of the fact that municipal home rule exists in either constitutional form or in statutory form, or in both forms in some thirty-six (36) other states throughout the nation. Most, if not all, these provisions have been made available to the Special Commission. The better provisions make the municipalities partners with their respective state governments in seeking solutions to the complex problems of our atomic age. The 

Commission has given heed to the admonition offered by both Mr. 

Bolan of Rhode Island and by Mr. Reynolds of Connecticut that the single most important reason for the advent of home rule in these New England states could be reduced to the single important fact of "urban growth".

The problems attendant to such growth cannot always await the sitting of the General Court, for to a great extent they must be met and solved in an atmosphere of immediacy. While concerned with the urgency for affording cities and towns the tools necessary for coming to grips with dynamic issues, the Commission is likewise concerned that the authority of the General Court, with respect to the cities and towns, should not be diluted in the process.

[Mar. 

Therefore, it became necessary for the Commission to appraise the three (3) approaches, offered in Rhode Island, Connecticut and New York. In Rhode Island, the approach was through a self-executing constitutional amendment. In Connecticut, the approach to date has been through legislative enactment. In New York, the 

Special Commission has offered a combined approach. 

There is little doubt that the Home Rule Amendment to the Rhode Island Constitution is a well-framed grant of home rule to its municipal corporations. A great deal of progress in municipal government can be glimpsed from the municipal charters that have been formulated as a result of the charter-making provisions of this amendment. In the long run, such an amendment may prove to be the best way in which municipal home rule can be effected. 

Like other self-executing amendments, nonetheless, the Rhode Island grant has opened itself to court action, and the courts have assumed a narrow construction of the grant of local self-government. In addition, there have been no precedents offered to date, to determine whether such a grant of home rule will or will not deter effective regional action on problems that transcend municipal boundaries. It would appear that lacking such precedents, a home rule city or town could conceivably hinder appropriate areawide achievement by strict adherence to its own home rule powers. 

In Connecticut, the home rule act seems to have worked out well thus far. While it was enacted by the General Assembly in 1957, the 1959 and 1961 amendments to the act seem to have strengthened rather than diminished the home rule powers of cities and towns. It is significant, however, that the need has been felt to provide a constitutional framework within which home rule can be made more meaningful and of lasting permanence. 

In New York, the Temporary Commission on the Simplification of the State Constitution, on the other hand, seems to have originated a novel approach to the home rule problem, and reference is made to the Local Government amendment offered by this Commission (Appendix “D”).

The provision would furnish an outright grant of legislative power to the cities and towns, but would leave to the province of the State Legislature, by means of a general statute, a means for restricting local governments in areas where the State Government

1962.29 SENATE ― 

deems to have an over-riding interest. The wording of the proposed amendment offers a simple and non-legalistic method of spelling out the details of home rule. It presupposes the enactment of a general statute of local government. 

The Commission, therefore, is of the opinion that the best and far-sighted approach to municipal home rule in Massachusetts is in the manner indicated by the proposed amendment for the State of New York.

Inasmuch as time has not been available to the Commission for either drafting a proposed amendment similar to the New York proposal for the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, and inasmuch T as there is the accompanying need to draft a general statute of local government that should be submitted simultaneous with any such constitutional proposal, the Special Commission does recommend to the General Court that the Commission be revived and continued in existence and offers the accompanying proposal resolve in line with this recommendation.
