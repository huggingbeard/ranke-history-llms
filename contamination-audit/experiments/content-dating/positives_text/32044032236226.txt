A STUDY OF THE RELATIVE BURDEN OF THE RETAIL SALES TAX UPON WASHINGTON STATE FAMILIES THE PROBLEM 

The purpose of this study was twofold: (1) to determine relative tax burdens among families of different income and size who were living under the retail sales tax as applied in Washington state in 1958, and (2) to determine how relative tax burdens for the same families would change under various modifications to the Washington sta retail sales tax.

PROCEDURE OF ANALYSIS 

Relative tax burdens were computed by consulting family budget studies to determine how families of different income and size spent their money and by then applying to these budgets various types of retail sales taxes. The budget studies included a detailed summary of family expenditures from which the taxable items were identified and totaled. A sales tax rate of 3-1/3% and 4% was applied to this tax base to obtain the family's tax bill and this, in turn, was divided by the gross family income to determine the effective tax rate. These rates were then plotted on Charts 

1 to 21 to facilitate an easy comparison of tax burdens among families. 

DESCRIPTION OF THE BUDGET STUDIES 

The survey of consumer expenditures for 1950, conducted by the United States Bureau of Labor Statistics, was the source for family expenditures data. This material had been published recently in an 18-volume series covering over 12,000 families living in 91 cities of the United States. However, to compute relative tax burdens for the citizens of Washington state, only that part of the B.L. S. expenditure study representing large cities of the West and small cities of the West was used. It should be noted that this part of the sample represented over three thousand family interviews. 

Budget data were presented for each family group varying in size from one to six or more at each of nine income groupings. The expenditure datum for each size and income group was an average calculated by dividing the aggregate amount of income or expenditure by the total number of families in the group. 

The average current expenditures for each family size and income grouping were divided into the following eight broad classifications: 1

To compute the relative tax burdens of the retail sales tax on Washington state families from the above listed current expenditures, only those items taxable under the 1958 tax laws were included in the tax base. For example, expenditures for housing were deleted, but household operations were included. Personal care, medical care, admissions, education, newspapers, and miscellaneous items were not included. Although transportation was deleted, automobile purchases and a computed auto operational expense were included in the tax base. Therefore, the following expenditure items were included in the tax base; (1) Food, tobacco and alcoholic beverages; (2) Household operation; (3) House furnishings and equipment; (4) Clothing and footwear; (5) Toilet articles and preparations; (6) Recreational expense plus radios and T.V.; and (7) Automobile purchases and automobile operations (not gasoline and insurance).

The total expenditure for the above items by each family class was multiplied by 3-1/3% to compute the tax bill which in turn was divided by the family income to find the effective tax rate. RELATIVE TAX RATES UNDER THE 1958 RETAIL SALES TAX 

Chart 1 revealed the fact that as family size increased the rate schedules also rose. The lowest rate schedule was for the single individual, and the highest schedule of rates was for the family of six or more. In the middle-income groups the rate differential was one percentage point which, at the current rate of 3-1/3%, means the typical single person paid 1/3 the tax rate of the large family. This characteristic of the retail sales tax is labeled "perverse" by some writers in the field of public finance.

Among the low incomes the tax rate differential between small and large families was quite significant. For instance, the "single individual" class was paying while the families of two and four members were each paying 5%. Moreover, the family of three paid as high as 19.77% of their income in retail sales taxes. A closer look at the data revealed that this particular group had an average annual income of $400 and an average annual expenditure of $2,500. Because this part of the sample was very small --- covering only four families --- caution in the use of this data is recommended. One can say with assurance that there are families who are exposed to very high effective tax rates because their expenditures are essentially from past savings or borrowings. However, it might be incorrect to say that such high rates typify the family of three with incomes below $1,000. This tax situation does show the similarity between the sales tax on low-income families and the B: O tax, or sales tax, on low-profit firms doing business in Washington. 

Chart 1 also revealed the regressive nature of the sales tax. The low-income families in the sample were paying between 3% and 20%, whereas the high-income families were paying between 1% and 2-1/2%. All of the schedules sloped downward to the right indicating the regressive factor in the tax. For the middle-income groups, however, the tax is only moderately regressive. 

Chart 14 was virtually the same study but applied to the budgets for families living in the small cities of the West. Similar results were found in this study, verifying the regressive nature of the tax higher incomes paid proportionately less

RELATIVE TAX RATES UNDER A WASHINGTON SALES TAX WITH FOOD EXEMPT 

Effective tax rates were computed using the same budget expenditures mentioned previously with the exception that all food consumed by the families (at home and at the premise where sold) was removed from the tax base. Charts 2 and 15 summarized the computed rates for the various families in the large cities and small cities of the West. (Charts 3, 4, and 5 compared the rates when food was exempt with the rate under the present law.)

The plotted tax rates showed that for the middle incomes the tax became surprisingly proportional. Between $2,000 and $10,000 income families there was little differenc in the effective tax rates paid. All incomes paid approximately 1% to 1-1/2%.

Moreover, the perverse factor was almost eliminated as families of different size paid about the same rates. However, wide discrepancies remained among the low incomes where the regressive element prevails. It should be noted, however, that even

RELATIVE TAX RATES WHEN FOOD IS EXEMPT AND THE BASE IS EXTENDED 

An attempt was made to determine the changes in relative tax burdens which would result from a food exemption when the base was extended to cover legal fees; newspapers; fuel, light, and refrigeration; barber expenses; and amusements. The results have been recorded in Chart 6. 

Comparative tax burdens under the above conditions, but with the tax base extended even further by the inclusion of medical care, have been plotted on Chart 7.3 These charts reveal the following information: (1) The rate schedules on Charts 6 and 7 for middle incomes are more level indicating that regressivity has been modified. (2) The rate schedules on Charts 6 and 7 are clustered (closer together) for middle incomes suggesting that the discrimination by family size has been reduced. (3) Among the low-income families, the problem of regressivity and perversity still remains. For instance, the family of three paid 19.77% under the current system, and they paid 18.76% under the alternative plan of exempting food and extending the base.

RELATIVE TAX BURDENS UNDER A FOUR PER CENT TAX COMPARED WITH THE BURDENS UNDER AN EXTENDED TAX BASE 

An attempt was made to compare the effective tax rate schedules under a 4% tax with those of the 3-1/3% tax extended to cover the six categories mentioned earlier.5 

The results can be seen on Charts 8 through 13, and 16 through 21, comparing schedules for condition (1) with the schedules for condition (2). 

The results were inconclusive. It could be seen that the schedules tended to overlap, suggesting little significant difference. Twelve cases are involved; six for the large cities and six for the small cities. In only 4 of the 12 cases could it be said that raising the rates noticeably raised the effective rates to all income groups more than did extending the base, and these 4 cases were restricted to single individuals and large families.
