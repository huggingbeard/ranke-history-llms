I. INTRODUCTION 

Under authority of H.S.R. No. 96, the House Judiciary Committee was directed by the 63rd Legislature to conclude its program of constitutional and statutory reorganization of the judicial branch of government. 

Pursuant to that authorization, the staff of the committee served the Judiciary Committee of the Texas Constitutional Convention and is presently preparing implementing legislation for the Article V tentatively adopted by that convention.

This report includes the judiciary article tentatively adopted by the convention and an analysis of that article. Because the convention judiciary article reflects five years of labor by the committee and the bench and bar of Texas in an attempt to restructure the state's judicial resources into a more functional system, the committee recommends its submission to the people as a constitutional amendment. 

Because some parts of the present Article V were treated in other articles by the convention and because of various references in other articles to matters which were changed by the convention judiciary article, certain "conforming amendments" must accompany submission of that article. A discussion of those amendments follows the section-by-section analysis included in this report. 

The report concludes with an appendix containing the text of the article recommended by the committee, its transition schedule, and necessary conforming amendments. 1

A. 11. 

1974 CONSTITUTIONAL CONVENTION JUDICIARY ARTICLE Historical Background 

The court system established in the 1876 Constitution was the sixth judicial structure implemented in Texas within 40 years. In 1891, the article was again substantially revised. This last revision attempted to relieve overcrowding of the supreme court docket by creating intermediate courts of civil appeals and the court of criminal appeals. In addition, the legislature was authorized to create courts other than those enumerated in the constitution. Altogether, a total of 21 amendments have been proposed to the 1876 judiciary article, and 13 have been adopted. 1 The resulting court structure consists of two courts of last resort, having a total of 14 judges; 14 intermediate appellate courts, having three judges each; 227 district courts; 10 criminal district courts; 254 county courts; 26 special domestic relations courts; 6 special juvenile courts; 62 other variants of "county courts at law" exercising parts of county court and district court jurisdiction; approximately 925 justice of 2 the peace courts; and approximately 1,000 municipal courts."

Since 1918, repeated proposals have been presented to implement a unified judicial system in Texas. 

Although these proposals have varied widely, typically they have included elimination of specialized courts, requiring all judges to be lawyers, and, in general, creating a state-financed, simplified court structure amenable to coordinated internal management.` Twice in the past two decades such proposals have been the subject of State Bar referenda." B.

Recent Developments 

The recent history of this movement centers around two related developments. First, the 61st 

Legislature (1969) directed the Judiciary Committee of the House of Representatives to conduct a study of the need for court reorganization. After a series of hearings throughout the state and three years of research, the committee made an extensive report to the 63rd Legislature calling for substantial changes in the judicial branch of government.5

In a companion development, a committee of the Judicial Section of the State Bar sought extensive modification of the judicial structure during the 62nd Legislature.

Later in 1971, this effort led to the creation of the Chief Justice's Task Force for Court Improvement. 

The result of the task force study and public hearings was the introduction of a joint resolution proposing a new judiciary article in the 63rd Legislature. While the resolution passed both houses in varying forms, it was not ultimately adopted." 

Following this failure and preparatory to the 1974 Constitutional Convention, the Texas Constitutional Revision Commission conducted further hearings and research into the matter of court reorganization. In common with the task force and judiciary committee proposals, the C.R.C. judiciary article included one court of last resort, intermediate appellate courts with both civil and criminal jurisdiction, and a two-level trial court system financed by the state. Municipal and justice courts would remain part of local court structures outside the "unified judicial system."

C. Constitutional Convention 

In this context, the judiciary committee of the constitutional convention began its work on January 11, 1974 and reported its recommendations on May 6. During that period, the committee met 49 times, heard nearly 100 witnesses, and refined its work through three complete draft proposals. 

After approximately 10 days' consideration of the committee's report, the convention tentatively adopted an Article V by a vote of 98 to 53. That proposal, in common with the C.R.C. and task force drafts, created one supreme court and directed the creation of one or more intermediate courts of appeals, both having jurisdiction as provided by law. District courts were retained as the primary trial courts in the state and lesser jurisdiction circuit courts were permitted. These four courts comprised the unified judicial system.

The local court system was retained outside the unified system. County courts were preserved "unless otherwise provided by law". There were to be one or more justice courts per county, and municipal courts were permitted as provided by law. 

D. Section-by-Section Analysis of Convention Proposal Sec. 1. JUDICIAL POWER. (a) The judicial power of the state is vested in the judicial branch. The state unified judicial system is composed of a supreme court, courts of appeals, district courts, and circuit courts. All courts have jurisdiction as provided by law, but jurisdiction of courts of the same level within the unified judicial system must be uniform throughout the state. 

No courts may be created except those authorized by this article. (b) The legislature by law may grant the supreme court the power to answer questions of state law certified from a federal court. (c) Courts that have original jurisdiction of criminal cases may (1), subject to regulation by law, suspend imposition or execution of sentence and place a defendant on probation or (2), if authorized by law, modify, set aside, or reimpose sentence.

COMMENTS 

Subsection (a) implements the separation of powers directive which had been included in Section 1 of the convention's proposed Article II. The unified court system is defined, and courts within it are required to have uniform jurisdiction. All courts in the state have their jurisdiction set by statute, and no courts may be created except those authorized by the article: Judges of locally-financed, statutory courts would be absorbed into the unified system in the manner prescribed by the transition schedule. 

Subsection (b) permits the legislature to authorize the supreme court to answer questions of state law certified by federal courts. The provision effectively overrules the decision of the Texas Supreme Court in United Service Life Insurance Co. v. Delaney holding that use of a declaratory judgment proceeding for the purpose of advising a federal court on Texas law was an advisory proceeding and beyond the constitutional power of Texas courts. Texas would be the tenth state to adopt such a procedure.

Subsection (c) restates the sentence suspension authority of criminal courts as presently included in Article IV, Section 11A. In addition, the legislature is authorized to permit those courts to "modify, set aside, or reimpose sentence." This latter provision permits the granting of broad judicial resentencing authority, in part concurrent with executive power, which is not presently permitted. 

Sec. 2. SUPREME COURT. The supreme court is the highest court of the state and consists of the Chief Justice of Texas and at least eight other justices. The court may sit en banc or in sections of not fewer than five justices. The concurrence of a majority of the justices sitting is necessary to decide a case.

COMMENTS 

The present supreme court has been expanded from three to nine members since 1876; its jurisdiction is established both in statutes and in Sections 3 and 3-b of the present Article V. Sections 4 and 5 of the present Article V establish the five-judge Court of Criminal Appeals; it has jurisdiction as provided by law in criminal matters. 

The detailed jurisdictional provisions of the the present judiciary article are not retained in the convention article, which grants the single high court both civil and criminal jurisdiction. The transition schedule provides that judges now on the court of criminal appeals become justices of the supreme court and, by attrition, the size of the court is to eventually stabilize at nine justices.10 If necessitated by the expanded jurisdiction of the court, it may sit in sections of not fewer than five justices. Also, the number of justices on the court can be increased. 

Elimination of two courts of last resort would leave only Oklahoma with such a system. The arguments for and against retention of dual high courts have been often reviewed. At the heart of the . issue, as the judiciary committee stated in its report to the convention, is the fact that efficient operation of the judicial branch of government depends on effective internal management. The retention of two courts of last resort frustrates that goal.

Sec. 3. COURTS OF APPEALS. The legislature by law shall establish one or more districts and in each provide for a court of appeals consisting of a chief judge and at least two other judges. The court may sit in sections if authorized by law. The concurrence of a majority of the judges sitting is necessary to decide a case.

COMMENTS 

In common with most populous jurisdictions, Texas has long utilized intermediate appellate courts. 

Since 1891, a network of regional courts of civil appeals has handled civil case review in the state. They are constitutionally restricted in size to three justices. Presently, 14 of these courts are organized in 13 geographic districts.

With the provisions of Section 3, the convention proposal continues the system of intermediate appellate review in civil matters and extends it to criminal cases. While separate courts could have been created for this prupose, it was felt that the better course was to utilize the existing framework of courts of civil appeals and place criminal jurisdiction in that system. 

Testimony of civil appeals justices appearing before the committee indicated that the additional criminal work which would be vested in the 42-judge intermediate appellate system could be absorbed. 

Because the size limitation on the courts is removed, they could be expanded if necessary. 

Three factors should help allay the concerns of those who fear a difficult transition from the present civil appeals system to a general jurisdiction system. The court administration provisions included in 

Section 7 permit trial and appellate judges with extensive criminal law experience to be assigned temporarily to the courts of appeals. Some civil appeals justices are now utilized as special commissioners 11 on the court of criminal appeals and are thereby gaining experience in criminal case review." Finally, decisions of the courts of appeals would be subject to supreme court review. During the transitional period, the high court would retain the criminal law specialists currently serving on the court of criminal appeals. If necessary, the court could sit in criminal and civil sections. 

Sec. 4. DISTRICT COURTS. The state is divided into judicial districts, each with one district court having one or more judges. The legislature from time to time may determine by law the number and location of districts and the number of judges in each district. 

COMMENTS 

Organized into semiautonomous geographic units with one judge per court, district courts have existed 12 in Texas largely unchanged since the Constitution of the Republic. In Wheeler v. Wheeler, the supreme court held that more than one district court could have jurisdiction over the same territory. Since that time, a pattern of multicourt urban counties and overlapping districts has evolved. 

Article V, Section 8 of the 1876 Constitution details at length the jurisdiction of district courts. In 

1891, the legislature was authorized to create "other courts" besides those enumerated in the constitution. 

The result has been the proliferation of locally financed courts having concurrent jurisdiction with the district court in specialized areas. Examples of these are special domestic relations and juvenile courts. 

County courts at law also exercise considerable concurrent jurisdiction with district courts. 

The House Judiciary Committee study concluded that the present organization of district courts and specialized district courts results in large part from jurisdictional rigidity in the present constitution. The failure of the state to create adequate numbers of district courts, the report noted, left a void to be filled by local government.

Section 4 avoids the one judge per court structure and jurisdictional inflexibility. Existing district, criminal district, domestic relations, juvenile, and probate courts become, under the transition schedule, full district courts within the unified system. Specialization could well be retained within this system but would be an administrative device rather than a jurisdictional barrier to efficient management of resources.

Section 5. CIRCUIT COURTS. The legislature by law may provide for circuit courts, each with one or more judges, and from time to time shall determine the number and location of circuit courts. A circuit court may serve one or more counties, but no county may be served by more than one circuit court.

COMMENTS 

The C.R.C. and task force proposals mandated a lower-level trial court system but did not require that each county be served by such a court. The Convention Judiciary Committee Report mandated such a system and required that each county be served by the system. 

A principal compromise during convention consideration of the committee proposal was to make the establishment of circuit courts permissive with the legislature and to retain present county courts until otherwise provided by law.

Section 5 empowers the legislature to create a lower-level trial court system as part of the state unified judiciary. Judges of this system are required to be lawyers. As provided by the transition schedule, judges of the various county courts at law become the initial circuit judges in the state. The transition schedule also provides that lawyer-judges of county courts can elect to become circuit judges where circuit courts are authorized by the 64th Legislature.

Sec. 6. OTHER COURTS. (a) The county courts established by the 

Constitution of 1876 are continued unless otherwise provided by law. The county courts have jurisdiction as provided by law. The county judge is the presiding officer of the county court and has judicial functions as provided by law. (b) The governing body of each county shall (1) divide the county from time to time into not fewer than four nor more than eight justice precincts and (2) establish and maintain one or more justice courts, each serving one or more precincts in the manner prescribed by law. (c) Municipal courts may be established by law or by charter as authorized by law.

COMMENTS 

Section 6 establishes or authorizes the courts in the state "local court system." When read in conjunction with the last sentence of Subsection 1(a), this section enumerates those courts which may function outside the unified system.

Subsection (a) retains present county courts. The courts may be modified by law, however, and their jurisdiction is not frozen in the constitution. County judges have judicial duties as provided by law. This latter provision would permit the vesting of magisterial functions in the county judge even if the court were abolished.

Subsection (b) retains four to eight justice precincts and requires at least one justice court in each county. The precinct structure was retained because of the special significance of that structure in local option alcoholic beverage elections.13 The transition schedule provides that existing justices of the peace serve for the terms for which elected. Thereafter the governing body of the county can maintain the number of justices and justice courts it deems necessary. 

Subsection (c) authorizes the creation of municipal courts. Constitutional treatment of this subject is necessary if the potential for creating municipal courts is desired because of the language of Subsection 1(a) restricting the courts in the judicial branch to those constitutionally authorized. 

Sec. 7. COURT ADMINISTRATION AND RULE-MAKING AUTHORITY. 

(a) The Supreme court shall provide for the efficient administration of the judicial branch. The supreme court may delegate administrative authority to the chief justice and the administrative judges. (b) The supreme court may direct the transfer of cases between courts of the same level. The supreme court may temporarily assign justices or judges within or between levels. (c) Each court of appeals district constitutes an administrative district for the management of trial courts. The Chief Justice of Texas with the advice and consent of the senate shall designate a judge to serve as the administrative judge of each district. (d) The legislature by law may provide for an agency of the judicial branch to propose rules for administration of the unified judicial system and to perform other duties as provided by law. (e) A rule of administration may not be inconsistent with general law or rules of procedure and does not take effect until approved by the supreme court. (f) The supreme court may promulgate rules of civil procedure for all courts but may not promulgate other procedural rules except as provided by law. A A rule of civil procedure promulgated by the court may not be inconsistent with general law and has no effect if expressly disapproved by the legislature. COMMENT 14 Section 7 establishes the framework for internal administration of the judicial branch of government. 

Presently, there is considerable statutory recognition in Texas of the need for judicial management. 

Despite this recognition, there is no coordinated system of internal administration. 

Under the management system proposed in Subsections (a)-(e), the supreme court has the constitutional duty to provide for administration of the judicial branch of government. The court may delegate all or part of its authority, supervise the transfer of cases horizontally, and assign judicial officers horizontally or vertically. Trial court administration districts are created in each court of appeals district. If it chooses to do so, the legislature may create a court administration agency to propose rules of administration which would become effective upon Supreme Court approval, and perform other duties prescribed by law.

The House Judiciary Committee study observed the need for such management assistance. That report noted that efficient operation is not the ultimate goal of the judiciary but observed: "Many areas of the state either face today or shortly will face a challenge to the functional capacity of their justice system which cannot be met by the mere creation of more courts. The first remedial step must be the institution ,,15 of an effective court system."

Subsection (f) provides for approximately the same procedural rule-making power in the supreme 16 court as is presently provided. The court would be confined to promulgating civil rules not inconsistent with general law, and the legislature could disapprove a rule. The court could promulgate other procedural rules as provided by law. 

Sec. 8. QUALIFICATIONS OF JUDGES. To be eligible to serve as Chief 

Justice of Texas or as a justice, judge, or justice of the peace, a person must be a citizen of the United States, a resident of this state, and otherwise qualified as prescribed by law. No person may serve as Chief Justice of Texas or as a justice or judge in the unified judicial system unless licensed to practice law in this state.

COMMENTS 

Section 8 requires judges of courts within the unified system to be attorneys. All judges are required to be citizens and residents of the state. With the exception of the legal training requirement, the convention judiciary committee found no broadly accepted principles of enduring quality associated with qualifications for judges. Thus, detailed constitutional directives concerning qualifications were left for statutory implementation. Until the legislature acts to provide for the new qualifications for office, present qualifications would remain in effect. 

Sec. 9. SELECTION AND TERMS OF JUDGES. (a) The Chief Justice of 

Texas and the justices of the supreme court are elected for six-year terms by the qualified voters of the state. The judges of each court of appeals are elected for six-year terms by the qualified voters of the court-of-appeals district. A district judge is elected for a four-year term by the qualified voters of the judicial district. A circuit judge is elected for a four-year term by the qualified voters within the geographical area served by the circuit court. The legislature may prescribe by law a mandatory retirement age for the Chief 

Justice of Texas, justices, and judges of these courts. (b) A justice of the peace is elected for a four-year term by the qualified voters of the precinct or precincts. A municipal court judge is selected in the manner prescribed by law or by charter as authorized by law. (c) A vacancy in the office of Chief Justice of Texas, justice of the supreme court, or judge of a court of appeals, district court, or circuit court is filled by appointment of the governor with the advice and consent of the senate. A vacancy in the office of justice of the peace is filled by appointment of the governing body of the county. A justice, judge, or justice of the peace appointed to a vacancy serves until the next statewide general election.

(d) The Chief Justice of Texas, justices, judges, and justices of the peace are elected in the manner prescribed by law. 

COMMENTS 

The provisions of Subsection (a) maintain the present four-year terms for trial judges and six-year terms for appellate judges. The elective method is retained for the selection of both trial and appellate judicial officers.

The C.R.C. and task force documents contained provisions, either in the body of the article or as an alternative submission, which called for "merit selection" of appellate judges. This proposal was considered both in committee and by the full convention but was not adopted. 

The grant of power to the legislature to establish a mandatory retirement age for justices and judges is necessary to clearly indicate the branch of government in which it is vested. 

Subsection (b) retains present terms for justices of the peace and permits them to be elected from one or more precincts. Municipal judges may be either appointed or elected as provided by law or charter. 

Subsection (c) provides that vacancies in judicial offices within the unified system are filled by appointment of the governor, and vacancies in the office of justice of the peace are filled by the county commission. All vacancies would be filled until the next general election. 

Subsection (d) permits complete statutory control over the manner of election of judges. As with the 

1876 Constitution, nonpartisan judicial elections could be instituted by statute. 

Sec. 10. REMOVAL AND DISCIPLINE OF JUDGES. (a) The governor shall remove the Chief Justice of Texas or a justice of the supreme court on the address of two-thirds of the members of each house of the legislature for willful neglect of duty, incompetency, oppression in office, or other reasonable cause not a sufficient ground for impeachment. (b) The legislature by law (1) shall provide for a judicial qualifications commission and (2) may provide for the removal, suspension, or censure of the Chief Justice of Texas, justices of the supreme court, judges, and justices of the peace.

COMMENTS 

The 1876 Constitution contains numerous overlapping provisions concerning removal and discipline of judicial officials. Article XV, Section 2 authorizes impeachment of supreme court and court of appeals justices and district judges. Section 6 of that article authorizes the supreme court to remove any justice or judge, and Section 8 permits the removal of supreme court justices for less than impeachable offenses by the method of legislative "address." 10

In addition, Article V, Section 24 of the present constitution authorizes district judges to remove county judges. Section 1-a of that article, added in 1965 and since amended, creates a judicial qualifications commission to assist the supreme court in judicial disciplinary matters. 

Subsection 10 (a) of the convention Article V retains the present "address" method of removal. 

Subsection (b) requires the legislature to to create a qualifications commission, and the transition schedule continues the present one in existence until altered by law. In addition, the legislature is empowered to make cumulative provisions for removal, suspension, or censure of all judges. 

Sec. 11. DISTRICT ATTORNEYS. (a) In each district as defined by law, the qualified voters elect a district attorney for a four-year term. (b) Each county must be served by a district attorney. The district attorney represents the state in such criminal actions as provided by law and performs other duties and functions provided by law. (c) A district attorney must be licensed to practice law in this state. 

Other qualifications of district attorneys; the grounds and procedure for disqualification, suspension, and removal; and the filling of vacancies in office are as provided by law.

COMMENTS 

The district attorney has been provided for in the judiciary article in every constitution since 1836. 

The county attorney has been provided since 1866. While the convention judiciary committee continued this practice, the convention moved county attorney provisions to Section 3 (a) of its local government article.

Article V, Section 21 of the 1876 Constitution does not mandate any district attorneys. "Resident criminal district attorneys" exercise the duties of both county attorneys and district attorneys.17 

Subsection (a) of the convention judiciary article authorizes the legislature to define district lines for district attorneys and retains four-year terms. Subsection (b) is a departure from existing law; it requires that a district attorney serve the state in each county. Subsection (c) requires that district attorneys be licensed to practice law in Texas and leaves all other incidents of the office to statutory regulation. 

Section 12. DISTRICT CLERKS. (a) The qualified voters of each county elect a district clerk for a four-year term. The legislature may provide by law for the election of a single clerk to perform the duties of both a county clerk and a district clerk. (b) The district clerk is the clerk of the district and circuit courts of the county and performs such duties as provided by law. (c) A clerk holding office under this section may be removed on a jury finding of incompetence, official misconduct, or other cause defined by law. A vacancy in the office is filled by appointment by the judges of the 11

district and circuit courts of the county in the manner prescribed by law. A clerk appointed to a vacancy serves until the next statewide general election. 

COMMENTS 

The principal departure from existing law in Section 12 is that district clerks serve both the district and circuit courts. The office is thus "unified" in order to permit greater economy and standardization in the collection of data necessary for management of the unified court system. 

Subsection (a) retains the elective nature of the office and broadens the present authorization for combining the office with that of county clerk. Subsection (b) refers to the duties of the office, a subject not covered in the 1876 Constitution. Subsection (c) carries forward present law concerning removal of clerks and filling of vacancies. 

Sec. 13. JURIES. (a) The grand jury is impaneled only in the district court and consists of 12 persons. Nine members of a grand jury constitute a quorum. At least nine members must concur in a bill of indictment. (b) A party has a right to a trial by jury on demand made in the manner prescribed by law. The legislature by law shall provide for trial juries. (c) A jury verdict must be unanimous except that in civil cases the legislature by law, or the supreme court by procedural rule promulgated under this article, may authorize a jury verdict to be rendered by not less than three-fourths of the jurors sitting in the case. Alternate jurors are not permitted unless authorized by law. 

COMMENTS 

Ostensibly, the right to trial by jury is guaranteed in Article I, Section 15 of the 1876 Constitution.

18 19 However, the cases of White v. White and Hickman v. Smith have held that the right of trial by jury included in the Bill of Rights is limited to the right as it existed at common law or as provided for by statutes when the constitution was adopted in 1876. 20 21 On the other hand, the cases of Tolle v. Tolle and Hatten v. City of Houston have held that the right of trial by jury enumerated in Article V, Section 10 of the 1876 Constitution does not depend on the existence of the right at the time of adoption of the constitution. The guarantee extends to any "cause" instituted in the district court, and "cause" is defined as a suit or action concerning any civil or criminal question contested before a court of justice. 

Although Subsection (a) in no way changes the present constitutional posture of grand juries, it does clarify the requirement that nine members concur in any indictment issued by the grand jury. The direction of grand juries remains in the province of the district courts. The only change made by 

Subsection (b) is to remove from the constitution the procedural details of making a demand for jury trial. 121

Subsection (c) permits the continuance of the present rule on nonunanimous jury verdicts in civil cases. The 1876 Constitution would allow a 9-3 verdict in civil cases in the district court, although 10-2 is presently provided by court rule.22 Felony and misdemeanor cases are required to be decided by a unanimous jury; this is a departure from present constitutional law on misdemeanor cases. 

Sec. 14. APPEAL BY STATE. Subject to the guarantees of the Bill of 

Rights of this constitution, the state may appeal in a criminal case only (1) from a trial court ruling that a law is unconstitutional or (2) from a court of appeals decision to the supreme court, which appeal is at the discretion of the supreme court unless otherwise provided by law. 

COMMENTS 

Article IV, Section 3 of the 1845 Constitution permitted the legislature to grant the state a limited right of appeal in criminal cases. The legislature did so in 1856. Article V, Section 26 of the 1876 

Constitution expressly denied this right.

Testimony before the convention judiciary committee indicated that Texas was the last remaining American jurisdiction to deny absolutely any appeal by the state. Nonetheless, the committee continued to treat this subject in the constitution and continued the prohibition. 

As written during debate by the full convention, Section 14 permits the state to appeal a trial court ruling that a statute is unconstitutional. Unless the legislature otherwise provides, supreme court review of courts of appeals decisions will be wholly within the high court's discretion. Any appeal by the state, of course, is subject to the double jeopardy limitation included in the Bill of Rights. 

With this limited appeal, the drafters hoped to facilitate final adjudication in challenges to the constitutionality of criminal statutes. In addition, discretionary review by the supreme court of decisions of the courts of appeals in criminal matters provides the means for achieving uniformity in the application of criminal law throughout the state.

Sec. 15. APPEAL BY ACCUSED. (a) A person convicted of a criminal offense in a trial court has a right to an appeal to the court having jurisdiction. An appeal under Article I, Section 11a of this constitution is to the court having jurisdiction. (b) An appeal to the supreme court in a criminal case is at the discretion of the supreme court unless otherwise provided by law. 

COMMENTS 

Presently, a person convicted in a criminal case may appeal to the Court of Criminal Appeals. Article 

4.03 of the Code of Criminal Procedure places this appellate jurisdiction in that court, except for misdemeanors in which the fine imposed does not exceed $100 and which have been appealed to a 13

county court, county criminal court, or county court at law. Article I, Section 11a of the 1876 

Constitution, added in 1956, also confers appellate jurisdiction on the Court of Criminal Appeals to review orders denying bail to an accused convicted twice before of a felony. 

Section 15 assures a person convicted of a criminal offense of at least one appeal. Review under 

Article I, Section 11a is continued, but the court having jurisdiction of this review is not specified. As with appeals by the state, review by the supreme court is discretionary unless otherwise provided by law. 

Sec. 16. APPEALS FROM ADMINISTRATIVE ACTION. Notwithstanding any other provision of this constitution, the legislature may provide by law for the method of appeal to the courts from rulings, decisions, or other actions of state agencies or political subdivisions of the state. 

COMMENTS 

Statutes directing appeal to the courts by trial de novo from actions of administrative agencies have not found favor with the Texas Supreme Court. Such statutes have been held to empower the courts to determine legislative questions. Thus, such review has been held to violate the separation of powers provision of Article II, Section 1 of the 1876 Constitution. H.J.R. No. 32, 57th Legislature, Regular 

Session (1961) proposed that the legislature be empowered to prescribe any manner of appeal to the courts, including de novo appeal. That proposed constitutional amendment was not adopted. 

The convention judiciary committee's preliminary draft of Section 16 was identical to H.J.R. No. 32. 

That version was shortened in the final committee report and redrafted subsequent to preliminary adoption by the full convention. The section is not self-enacting. It permits complete legislative discretion in determining the manner of appeal from administrative actions. Conceivably, de novo appeal might be prescribed in some instances as a judicial check on abuse of power by an administrative agency. Because of decisions of the Texas Supreme Court, if the discretion to prescribe this manner of review is to lie with the legislature, specific constitutional authorization is necessary. 

Sec. 17. FINANCING. The state shall pay the basic compensation of justices and judges of the unified judicial system and district attorneys and shall pay such other expenses of the system as provided by law. 

COMMENTS 

The convention judiciary committee originally considered a section on court financing in the context of prohibiting supplemental compensation for judges. When that proposal failed to receive majority support in the committee, a financing section was retained to give specific direction for the state to assume the basic costs of the unified court system. 

Under Section 17, counties retain the responsibility of providing for county courts and justice courts, and cities retain the responsibility to provide for municipal courts. Since all statutory courts would be merged into the unified court system, county commissions would be relieved of the responsibility of 14

E. providing for the various county courts at law and special district courts. As under the present constitution, the disposition of fines and fees collected by the various courts would be determined by statute.

Comparison of Proposal and Bar Poll Results 

In February, 1973, the state bar membership was polled in regard to judicial improvement issues then pending before the 63rd Legislature in the form of a resolution proposing a new Article V. The results of that poll compare as follows with the work product of the 1974 Texas Constitutional 

Convention: 

1. Seventy percent of bar members responding favored merger of the two courts of last resort. The convention document effectuates this merger. 2. 3. 4.

Seventy-six percent responding favored the conversion of courts of civil appeals into courts of appeals having both criminal and civil jurisdiction. The convention agreed with this recommendation, and its 

Article V mandates the creation of one or more courts of appeals. 

Eighty-one percent responding in the bar poll desired adoption of the following district court proposition: (a) eliminate overlapping districts; (b) make criminal district, domestic relations, and juvenile judges full district judges; and (c) eliminate the one judge per court concept, in order that an urban county could have one court with multiple judges. 

Under the convention proposal, the legislature may eliminate overlapping districts; domestic relations, special juvenile and secial probate judges are made district judges; and a district court may have one or more judges. Thus, the principal provisions of district court organization approved by the bar membership are either executed or facilitated by the convention proposal. 

Eighty-four percent of bar members responding favored replacing all courts below the district court level with a statewide system of county-level courts. Here, both the constitutional revision commission and the convention disagreed with the bar recommendation.

Under the convention proposal, a state-financed system of circuit courts is authorized but not mandated. However, present county courts are retained until otherwise provided by law; each county commission is directed to establish one or more justice courts, and municipal courts may be established.

The differences between the convention Article V and the bar referendum position in regard to lower-level trial courts are not as great as may first appear. By statute, county courts and justice court judicial jurisdiction could be eliminated. Municipal courts need not be established. All trial jurisdiction below the district court thus could conceivably be vested in a state-financed circuit court system. The convention Article V, unlike that in the present constitution, would permit full legislative implementation of the bar's recommendation on lower-level trial courts. 15

5. 6. 7. 8.

Eighty-three percent of bar members responding desired to allocate existing judicial officers within the new system. Justices of the peace would have been phased out under a "grandfather clause." In accord with the recommendation, the transition schedule of the proposed article designates existing trial and appellate judges as the initial officers in the new system. Justices of the peace are not phased out by the terms of the judiciary article or its transition schedule; but as noted above, neither the jurisdiction nor the number of justice courts is frozen in the constitution. 

Eighty-three percent of bar members responding in the 1973 poll favored making the Chief Justice of Texas the administrative head of the court system. The chief justice would implement administrative directives through local presiding judges. The convention proposal establishes substantially the same system, with administrative responsibility placed in the supreme court. Authority may be delegated to the chief justice and to administrative judges within court of appeals districts. 

Ninety-seven percent of bar members responding desired all judges down to and including the county level to be lawyers. The convention proposal does require all judges in the unified system to be lawyers. As noted above, however, the proposed Article V does not go to the added step of merging all "local court" functions into the unified system. With plenary control over judicial jurisdiction and qualifications, the legislature could place all judicial functions in courts having lawyer-judges. 

Eighty-six percent of bar members responding desired the state to pay judicial salaries and other expenses provided by law. The legislature would have authority under this recommendation to provide for full or partial reimbursement of expenses from fines and fees. 

The convention adopted a provision similar to this recommendation. Local salary supplements, however, are not prohibited. Specific reimbursement authority was deleted from the judiciary committee report by action of the full convention. In effect, the legislature retains authority to provide for disposition of fines and fees, and the convention's action merely removed a limitation on utilization of these funds. 9.

In regard to selection of judges, the convention varied most substantially from the State Bar recommendations. Sixty-two percent responding in the bar poll did favor either partisan or nonpartisan election of all judges. The convention agreed with this recommendation, and its Article V requires election of all judges. The legislature could stipulate either a partisan or nonpartisan format. 

While only thirty-eight percent of bar members favored "merit selection" of appellate judges, sixty-one percent felt the public should have the right to vote on this issue. The convention did not agree with this position on alternative submission of the merit selection issue. 

Article V of the constitution proposed by the 1974 Texas Constitutional Convention differs in form substantially from the document considered by the 63rd Legislature and which was the subject of a referendum of the state bar membership in February, 1973. In regard to the issues considered in that referendum, however, the convention document, in substantial part, executes or facilitates the legislative execution of the majority desire for judicial reorganization as therein expressed. 16

ARTICLE V THE JUDICIARY Section 1. JUDICIAL POWER. (a) The judicial power of the state is vested in the judicial branch. The state unified judicial system is composed of a supreme court, courts of appeals, district courts, and circuit courts. All courts have jurisdiction as provided by law, but jurisdiction of courts of the same level within the unified judicial system must be uniform throughout the state. No courts may be created except those authorized by this article. (b) The legislature by law may grant the supreme court the power to answer questions of state law certified from a federal court. (c) Courts that have original jurisdiction of criminal cases may (1), subject to regulation by law, suspend imposition or execution of sentence and place a defendant on probation or (2), if authorized by law, modify, set aside, or reimpose sentence.

Sec. 2. 

SUPREME COURT. The supreme court is the highest court of the state and consists of the Chief Justice of Texas and at least eight other justices. The court may sit en banc or in sections of not fewer than five justices. The concurrence of a majority of the justices sitting is necessary to decide a case. 

Sec. 3.

COURTS OF APPEALS. The legislature by law shall establish one or more districts and in each provide for a court of appeals consisting of a chief judge and at least two other judges. The court may sit in sections if authorized by law. The concurrence of a majority of the judges sitting is necessary to decide a case. 

Sec. 4.

DISTRICT COURTS. The state is divided into judicial districts, each with one district court having one or more judges. The legislature from time to time may determine by law the number and location of districts and the number of judges in each district.

Sec. 5. CIRCUIT COURTS. The legislature by law may provide for circuit courts, each with one or more judges, and from time to time shall determine the number and location of circuit courts. A circuit court may serve one or more counties, but no county may be served by more than one circuit court. 

Sec. 6. OTHER COURTS. (a) The county courts established by the constitution of 1876 are continued unless otherwise provided by law. The county courts have jurisdiction as provided by law. The county judge is the presiding officer of the county court and has judicial functions as provided by law. (b) The governing body of each county shall (1) divide the county from time to time into not fewer than four nor more than eight justice precincts and (2) establish and maintain one or more justice courts, each serving one or more precincts in the manner prescribed by law. (c) Municipal courts may be established by law or by charter as authorized by law.

Sec. 7. COURT ADMINISTRATION AND RULE-MAKING AUTHORITY. (a) The supreme court shall provide for the efficient administration of the judicial branch. The supreme court may delegate administrative authority to the chief justice and the administrative judges. (b) The supreme court may direct the transfer of cases between courts of the same level. The supreme court 37

may temporarily assign justices or judges within or between levels.

(c) Each court of appeals district constitutes an administrative district for the management of trial courts. The 

Chief Justice of Texas with the advice and consent of the senate shall designate a judge to serve as the administrative judge of each district. (d) The legislature by law may provide for an agency of the judicial branch to propose rules for administration of the unified judicial system and to perform other duties as provided by law. (e) A rule of administration may not be inconsistent with general law or rules of procedure and does not take effect until approved by the supreme court. (f) The supreme court may promulgate rules of civil procedure for all courts but may not promulgate other procedural rules except as provided by law. A rule of civil procedure promulgated by the court may not be inconsistent with general law and has no effect if expressly disapproved by the legislature. 

Sec. 8.

QUALIFICATIONS OF JUDGES. To be eligible to serve as a justice, judge, or justice of the peace, a person must be a citizen of the United States, a resident of this state, and otherwise qualified as prescribed by law. No person may serve as a justice or judge in the unified judicial system unless licensed to practice law in this state. 

Sec. 9.

SELECTION AND TERMS OF JUDGES. (a) The justices of the supreme court are elected for six-year terms by the qualified voters of the state. The judges of each court of appeals are elected for six-year terms by the qualified voters of the court-of-appeals district. A district judge is elected for four-year term by the qualified voters of the judicial district. A circuit judge is elected for a four-year term by the qualified voters within the geographical area served by the circuit court. The legislature may prescribe by law a mandatory retirement age for the justices and judges of these courts. (b) A justice of the peace is elected for a four-year term by the qualified voters of the precinct or precincts. A municipal court judge is selected in the manner prescribed by law or by charter as authorized by law. (c) A vacancy in the office of justice of the supreme court or judge of a court of appeals, district court, or circuit court is filled by appointment of the governor with the advice and consent of the senate. A vacancy in the office of justice of the peace is filled by appointment of the governing body of the county. A justice, judge, or justice of the peace appointed to a vacancy serves until the next statewide general election. (d)

(2) The county commissioners court shall from time to time (a) determine the justice precincts to be served by each constable and (b) divide the county into four separate and compact commissioners precincts, each containing as nearly as practicable an equal number of inhabitants. (3) A sheriff and a county clerk are elected by the qualified voters of each county. In those counties designated by law, a county attorney is elected by the qualified voters. Constables are elected by the qualified voters of the precincts they serve. (4) The qualifications, duties, and functions of the county officers authorized by this section are as provided by law except that (a) among other qualifications a county attorney must 46

7. be licensed to practice law in this state and (b) among other duties and functions of a county clerk are those of county recorder and clerk of the county and commissioners courts. (5) The county officers authorized by this section are elected in the manner provided by law for four-year terms. Vacancies in county offices authorized by this section are filled by the county commissioners court until the next general election. (6) A county officer is removed from office after a district court trial and jury finding of incompetency, official misconduct, habitual drunkenness, or other causes defined by law.”
