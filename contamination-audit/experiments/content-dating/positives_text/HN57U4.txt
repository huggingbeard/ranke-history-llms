Rt. Rev. Wm. Mercer Green, D. D. (Member of Board of Trustees of State Institutions of Higher

The progress of the Negro race in this country has in it distinct elements of true romance. Mr. Mark Ethridge, a son of Mississippi, now with the Louisville, Ky., COURIER-JOURNAL has spoken this true sentence: "In spite of every obstacle that has been thrown in his path, it spite of every effort to make him obsequious-a Lazarus at the table of Dives-rather than positive in voicing his rights, the Negro has made more progress in the seventy-two years since the feudal system of the South ended than any other race has made in a similar period in the world's history." 

The progress of the Negro in the field of education has been astounding. More than ninety per cent illiterate in 1866 and seventy per cent in 1880, in 1930 Negro illiteracy had dropped to 16.3 per cent. The eagerness with which he has sought an education, and the success with which many have acquired through education training for efficient and meritorious activity in many fields, attest a stability and strength of character, a will to go forward, an indomitable spirit of ambition and industry in the face of desperate handicaps, which deserve the admiration as well as the encouragement of all magnanimous people. The following figures given by Mr. Ethridge are taken from the census of 1930: "There were 351 technical engineers, 361 chemists, 430 artists, 545 photographers, 1,038 social workers, 1,347 lawyers, 2,164 dentists, 3,805 physicians and surgeons, 4,130 actors, 5,728 trained nurses, 10,548 musicians, 25,034 clergymen, and 54,683 teachers." These facts bespeak fine potentialities.

The Negro people constitute a goodly percentage of our national population, and an important factor in our

PUSHING FORWARD 

wholesome civilization. Especially in the South they touch and condition our happiness and well-being in many intimate ways. Consideration for our own welfare, as well as for the well-being of this portion of our citizenship, demands our deep interest in the progress of the Negro people, in their educational and religious, as well as in their physical, economic and moral progress.

Professor Davis tells the story of the founding of Alcorn A. & M. College. It is a story well and faithfully told by a man of scholarly mind and generous spirit, giving his sacrificial service to the education of his people, under Christian leadership and ideals, while well trained in the principles of impersonal sociological approach to the issues involved. It is a story rich in value and inspiration. It records the interest taken by splendid Governors and other civic and religious leaders in providing adequate educational opportunities and leadership-training for the Negro people. It recounts the patient and hopeful efforts of the Negroes to secure recognition by the dominant race of their need for a proper educational endowment for fighting the battle of economic and industrial life, for an efficient fulfillment of the duties of citizenship, and for the development of leaders who might hold aloft the beacon light of progress and achievement along the difficult pathway of their lives. The few instances given of the successful alumni of Alcorn College are rich in inspiration. 

The list could be much larger.

Alcorn was the first Land-Grant College for Negro education established in the United States. It has moved steadily forward to greater usefulness in the training of Negro leaders and in offering opportunities to those capable of seizing them, for appreciation of the more abundant life, and for assuming a worthy part in the business and professional life of the State and country. Under a fine and able administration it is increasing in a wholesome integration of purpose, in the ability and training of faculty, in the size and quality of student body, and in adequacy of equipment. INTRODUCTION 

Educating the people through the use of institutions of learning is a principal agency evolved by society to assure social progress. When education is thought of in this light it has two distinct aspects: (1) giving to the largest number of people a basis for effective membership in the social group-general education, and (2) giving to the members of society the training whereby they may render most effective service in the several vocations-vocational and professional training.* 

The function of a college is to take up these two aspects where the high schools leave them, and to train young men and women for social leadership and give to them a thorough preparation in those vocations which depend very largely for their successful operation upon the completion of at least a college education.** 

As a part of our educational scheme, general education is to guarantee reactions to ordinary social situations in line with the best interest of society. Since general education of a college grade is attained by only a relatively small fraction of our citizenry, that small fraction is bound to wield a disproportionately large influence in setting educational, ethical, scientific, social and technical standards for all.

The Alcorn Agicultural and Mechanical College, Al corn, Mississippi, has established its place as one of the important Negro Land-Grant Colleges to help fulfill the above-described purposes. Because of its historical background and its present range of services, it is committed to the task of providing a first class collegiate education for the Negroes in its section of the country.

History and Development of Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College 

The history and development of Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College may be divided into four periods. The first period was the Reconstruction Era which closed about 1871; the second was the founding of Alcorn University; the third, from 1871-1899 witnessed the early development of the college; the fourth period dates from 1899 to the present, and is called by some the period which characterizes the making of the "Greater Alcorn".

Some analyses of Negro education in Mississippi prior to 1871 will serve to throw light on conditions which led to the establishment of Alcorn University. 

Negro Education In Mississippi Prior To 1871 

Prior to the civil war, almost all of the free schools in the state were those maintained out of the proceeds coming from the sale or lease of the sixteenth section lands granted to the state by congress during the early part of the century. These schools were open to white children only.

The work of teaching the Negroes began with the occupation of the state by the Federal armies, and the first schools established for them were at Corinth, shortly after the occupation of that town by the Union troops in 1862. Other schools were were established for

Negroes about Vicksburg before the close of the war by such organizations as the American Missionary Association, the Freedman's Aid Society, and the Society of Friends. With the organizing of the Freedman's 

Bureau, a more systematic and comprehensive plan of [6]

Negro education was undertaken. Joseph Warren, chapiain of a Negro civil war regiment, was appointed superintendent of the Freedman's Bureau schools for the state at large. As to the number of schools and the pupils enrolled therein, Superintendent Warren made the following report on March 31, 1865: *Vicksburg, eleven schools, twenty-two teachers, and an enrollment of 1,854 pupils; camps near Vicksburg, four schools, nine teachers, and an enrollment of 720 pupils; Davis 

Bend Colony, four schools, nine teachers, and an enrollment of 739 pupils: Natchez, eleven schools, twenty teachers, and an enrollment of 1,080 pupils. 

Since there was no state public school system, the teachers were, for the most part, supported by the 

Friends' Society, United Brethren, the American Baptist Home Missionary Society, the Northwest Freedmen's Commission, the National Freedmen's Relief Association, and the Reformed Presbyterian Board.

HISTORY AND DEVELOPMENT The State Teachers' Association*, a representative group of southern white teachers, on January 17, 1867, went on record as favoring a state public school system for white and colored children alike.

In spite of this favorable attitude, however, toward Negro education, it would be incorrect to conclude that there was no opposition to the education of the Negro at any time before 1871, or that there was not a certain element of continuous opposition thereafter. This was shown by reported efforts made to keep the Bureau agents from finding places to teach; and the fact that teachers were abused and intimidated.**

Public education for Negroes in Mississippi was not in practical operation prior to 1870. The Freedmen's 

Bureau had, however, already provided a centralized scheme of organization which the state authorities could easily take over, and which prepared the ground work for the organizing of the public school system.† 

The "Black and Tan Convention" drafted the constitution of 1869. This constitution was ratified by the electorate, December 1, 1869. This same convention authorized the legislature to establish a uniform system of free public schools for the benefit of all children between the ages of five and twenty-one, and to establish schools of higher grade as soon as practicable. On 

July 4, 1870, the legislature passed an act "to regulate the support, organization, and maintenance of a uniform system of public education for the state." This new system of free schools went into operation in October, 1870, with Henry R. Pease elected as the first 

State Superintendent of Education. Thus began the public school system of education for Negroes and whites in Mississippi.

Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College had its beginning as a seat of learning in 1830 as Oakland College, for the education of white male students, having been founded by the Presbyterian Church. As early as 

1829, a committee was appointed by the Presbyterian Church to study the question of establishing an institution of learning that would carry the branches and courses that would make for general culture, and for the training of ministers for that church. 

BETHEL CHURCH, WESTSIDE, MISSISSIPPI 

Where Oakland College had its beginning in 1830 (The church is still standing) In January, 1830, this committee met again, this time at old Bethel Church, which stands now just two miles north of the college grounds. They decided definitely at this meeting to open an institution. The site for the school was set two miles south of Bethel Church, and a Board of Trustees was elected to see to [12]

the establishment of the college. In July, 1830, the trustees met and brought some of their slaves to clear the forest from the grounds and to lay the foundation for the college, which was named Oakland College because of the beautiful oaks which grew thereon. 

The Rev. Jeremiah Chamberlain, eminent and wellknown Presbyterian minister, was elected the first president of Oakland College. Later, because of a heated dispute in the college chapel on the slavery question, he was assassinated at the gate to the yard which surrounded the president's home. 

OAKLAND CHAPEL 

Constructed in 1830 and is still used as a College Auditorium This institution, however, grew with remarkable success until the beginning of the Civil War. With the firing at Fort Sumter, the doors of Oakland College were closed, and the young men left their desks to answer the call to arms. When the smoke of battle had cleared away, Oakland College was unable to re-open for the reception of students because of the economic conditions which prevailed over the south. The Presbyterian Church had this college on hand with no money to operate it, so the institution was offered for sale. 

The state was ready to establish a school for the education of boys and young men of her Negro citizens; therefore, the state became a willing purchaser of Oakland College for $40,000.00, and re-named it Alcorn University in honor of the late James L. Alcorn, who was instrumental in making the college a reality and who was then governor of the state. [13]

President Revels set out to make Alcorn University mean to the recently-enfranchised Negro citizens what its predecessor, Oakland College, had been to the white citizens the leading college in the state. 

When the university opened its doors for the reception of students, 179 enrolled and, so far as the state of Mississippi was concerned, another Negro advance was set in operation. The fame of the institution for converting rural and urban boys into sensible and educated young men, fairly capable of caring not only for their own affairs but also those of others, was spread throughout the whole state, and even into Louisiana, Tennessee and Ohio.

By Section 2922 of the Revised Code of 1871, the Governor of Mississippi was required to "appoint biennially, three persons in each representative district, or county, in the State, to act as a Board of Examiners whose duty it shall be to examine, in an annual convocation of representatives of the common schools of the representative district or county, all candidates for free scholarships to Alcorn University. ... Who shall grant to the best answerer a certificate of free scholarship for a matriculation term of four years in Alcorn University $100.00 per year, from the common school fund of the district or county." This law made it possible for worthy and ambitious students to secure an education at the expense of the state. [16]

HIRAM R. REVELS 

One of the main provisions of an act passed by the state legislature February 28, 1878, pertaining to the university, was as follows: To reorganize Alcorn University (for Negroes) into an agricultural college, under the name of "The Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College of the State of Mississippi." After 1878 the legislature divided equally the fund obtained from the sale of lands granted by Congress between Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College and the State Agricultural and Mechanical College at Starkville (college for white students). The reorganization of Alcorn to have it conform more closely with the Morrill Act provisions brought a change in the administrative policy of the institution in regard to curriculum offerings. 

Under the University, provisions were made for and it was required of every student that he complete five years of Latin and four years of Greek, whereas under the Agricultural and Mechanical College regime offerings were made for the study of at least theoretical agriculture and students who elected to take the course were not required to present any qualifications in the study and mastery of languages. The university curriculum represented the following offerings:

Aside from the classical course offered in the college, a scientific course was also available for those who desired it. In both of these courses declamations were stressed and the quarter system used. The buildings consisted of a chapel, three brick dormitories, a number of frame cottages, two college literary halls, president's home and a refectory. The farm consisted of 235 acres upon which cotton and corn were the chief crops grown. A year, peach and apple orchard consisting of 500 trees was started during the 1872-1873 session. The college catalogue for that year states: "It is the intention as it is the duty of the institution to make the farm a model of agricultural beauty and fertility, and to develop a high order of scientific as well as practical agriculture.' 

Under the Agricultural and Mechanical regime, an agricultural course was added and it is interesting to note that the literary courses in high school and college required courses in agriculture. Military tactics was also required of all students in accordance with the requirements of the State law. [18]

JOHN H. BURRUS 

John H. Burrus of Nashville, Tennessee, was elected to the presidency of the institution in the fall of 1882.

An important event during this administration was the passage by Congress of the first Morrill Act amendment, known as the New Morrill Act, in 1890, which gave to the state a direct appropriation out of the national treasury amounting for the first year to $15,000.00, with the provision that this sum should be increased by $1,000.00 a year for ten years when the total appropriation from this source would be $25,000.00 annually thereafter. It was through President Burrus' keen insight and prompt action that, while this Act was before Congress, a rider was put on the appropriation which provided that in states having separate schools for the races the funds allotted to the several states should be divided between the races in proportion to the relative educable population of the races in question. Hence owing to the predominant number of Negroes in the state Alcorn College has always derived from that appropriation a little more than half of the total.

Although the institution was known as the "Agricultural and Mechanical College," unfortunately the mechanical division had been sadly neglected. It was during this administration that the mechanics departments were made realities. In 1893 the departments of carpentry and blacksmithing had their beginnings.

The graduates of Alcorn College were making wonderful contributions to the Negro race as evidenced in the trustees' report. They reported in 1893 that a graduate of Alcorn was professor of Agriculture at Prairie View Normal School of Texas; another was a minister and editor of a magazine in Tennessee; another was president of Gibsland College of Louisiana; another was a prosperous furniture merchant of Nashville; another was a physician and a clerk in one of the governmental departments at Washington; while in our own state a number were heads of schools or prospering farmers. The Administration of E. H. Triplett, 1896-1899 

E. H. TRIPLETT 

President Triplett, formerly a minister in the Baptist Church, was, according to the record, a self-made man who had never attended college. His knowledge of college administration was very limited. His faculty, practically all college graduates and some from the best mid-western institutions, regarded him as having been made president because of favoritism and not because of any fitness which he possessed for the job. Trouble started between the president and the faculty early in the new administration, and the Board of Trustees dismissed practically the entire faculty and gave President 

Triplett a clean slate. During Christmas week, 1897, the president was shot by an unknown assailant. At the end of the next college session, conditions had not [31]

PUSHING FORWARD 

improved at the college and the trustees decided, as the spokesman for the board put it, "to reverse the order of the sweeping." This time the president was removed and the faculty retained. Thus ended an administration of turmoil and distraction and a general lack of tangible accomplishments. A standard three-year

Business Course was offered in 1897-1898. [32]

Perhaps the most outstanding event of this administration was making the school co-educational. Since the establishment of the institution in 1830 as Oakland College, down to 1902 as Alcorn College, the school had been (for the most part) for the education of males only. There may be some who might honestly debate the wisdom of making the school co-educational, yet there could certainly be none who, when it is learned that the state had theretofore made no dormitory provision for the higher education of her Negro womanhood, would hold for a moment that President Lanier did not act wisely in forcing the issue as far as possible and insisting upon the opening of the doors of the institution for the admission of girls. The nurse training school was established in 1903 and opened in 1904.

The Administration of John A. Martin, 1911-1915 

J. A. MARTIN 

J. A. Martin, another alumnus of the college, became president under most favorable circumstances.

He had a Board of Trustees of unusual strength and a harmonious faculty. President Martin's health began to fail him during his second year as administrative head, and he was forced to relinquish active supervision of the affairs of the college. In this emergency, ex-President Rowan was drafted. During the next year, President Martin's health improved and he was able to assume his full duties again. However, during the early part of the fourth year of his administration, his health again failed him and in December of that year he died. Doctor Rowan carried out the unexpired term of President Martin. [39]

PUSHING FORWARD 

During this administration, athletics (baseball) was put in the hands of a professor and efforts were made by the faculty to elevate this phase of physical training to a place equal to that of the leading colleges in the country. Intramural and inter-collegiate matches were encouraged. The aim was to increase bodily vigor, the quickness of vision and intellect of the students, as well as to give the college valuable publicity. A healthful, social and friendly spirit was also begotten through these matches. The two outstanding achievements of this administration were the establishment of a manual training department and a modern steam laundry, (1915), both of which are distinct credits to the institution, even to this day. 

The requirements for graduation were as follows: (1) All students graduating from any department must spend the entire scholastic year in college. (2) All orations for graduation must be prepared by the students themselves, passed upon and graded by the proper committees. During the sixty-seven years of its operation as an institution for training Negro youth, Alcorn College has undergone considerable changes, and has registered remarkable growth. Each president contributed something to its development, but the late Dr. L. J.

The institution is partly located upon and operates what is known as the General N. B. Furlong Farm-a name given in honor of one General N. B. The Smith-Hughes Act was passed by Congress in February, 1917, and provides for the co-operation of the Federal Government with the states in a plan for the training of teachers for positions as leaders, directors and supervisors of the three lines of work most essential to a well-ordered civilization, viz: Agriculture,

Home Economics, Trades and Industries. In accordance with the provisions of the Act, Alcorn has been designated the Negro teacher-training institution for Mississippi.

In 1919, Dr. A. E. Butts, now Chancellor of the University of Mississippi, was high in his praise of the work done at Alcorn. Says he:* "The Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College has developed steadily since its establishment; the enrollment has increased from year to year; the courses of study have been broadened. and the Negro race has been benefited by the training along industrial lines which the establishment of the institution has made possible."

The federal authorities, as well as state officials, who kept in touch with the work done at the college, were complimentary both of the work done by the students and of the economical management of the college.

It was in 1920 that the faculty adopted the Hour System for the college department and the Unit System for the high school department, making it necessary for one to obtain credits to the amount of 180 term hours in order to graduate from the college department, and twenty units in order to graduate from the high school department.

The purpose of the college was re-stated by the ad ministration in 1920 as follows: 

"It is the aim and purpose of the management of this institution, in accordance with the larger aims which called it into existence, so to fit the girls and boys, the young women and young men, who attend here, mentally, morally and industrially that as they go from this institution they will automatically fit into the economic scheme of the state and nation so that they will be well-nigh indispensable in their several communities. To that end this institution, as far as possible, is made a home for those who attend. Not only their intellectual, but also their social, moral and intellectual training receive careful attention." 

It is quite evident that Alcorn was enjoying a very marked degree of prosperity and that the work done was of a much better grade than during any former period. The state of affairs in connection with the work at Alcorn was made possible by several circum- [44]

(2) A higher type of students from year to year, made possible by the fact that Alcorn had begun to teach her grandchildren-the offspring of former Alcorn students and who, because of more intelligent parents and better home environment, were making better material for Alcorn, were being attracted to the college. Another reason for this better type of student, was the better public schools found in the rural tions of the state. sec- (3) Another factor that contributed to the superior grade of work at Alcorn was the change in the organization of the work. Before 1922, since the time that 

Alcorn was made more largely industrial, the workday was divided into two parts--the first half devoted to academic instruction and the other half to the industrial departments. (4) All courses offered at the college were materially strengthened and revised.

Harmon, secretary to the Board of Trustees, called attention of the legislature to the fact that Alcorn College was rendering useful service despite its limited facilities and insufficient support fund. He begged the legislature to deal more generously with the institution and did not hesitate to say that every dollar thus appropriated would be honestly spent in making possible a larger sphere of [45]

usefulness for the college. The Board of Trustees called attention to the fact that the deficit at Alcorn was due to the lack of an increase in support funds to keep pace with the increased operating costs incidental to the continued growth of the college. Investigation showed that the college had been running behind one biennium after another. The Board felt that the time had come to liquidate the college's indebtedness and to increase its support fund by $25,000.00 per year in order to avoid future deficits. Secretary Harmon gave additional reasons for the deficit. He reported that it was due partly to the fact that the equipment was in a badly run down condition necessitating constant expenditures for emergency repairs from the meager support fund. He stated further: "If Mississippi Negroes are to be kept on the farms, they must be trained to meet the new era of dairying and intensive agricultural development which is at hand, thus establishing the necessity of purchasing for the college a Jersey herd of twenty-five animals."

He also called attention to the very great need for a gravel road to connect the college with Highway 61, either to Port Gibson or to Lorman. During the winter, the roads were well nigh impassable and, at all seasons of the year, following a heavy rain, people going to and from the college would bog down in the mud. During the session 1930-31 a gravel road was completed which connected the college with Port Gibson and Lorman. 

In spite of the deficit, at a special meeting of the trustees the college officials were instructed to continue the operation of the institution, in the firm belief that the legislature in its wisdom would recognize the inadequacy of the last support fund, make an appropriation to pay the deficit, and provide sufficient funds for the next biennium without any deficit whatsoever. 

In 1928, President Rowan called the Board's attention to the fact that Alcorn had outgrown its present facilities and accommodations and that the time was ripe for general expansion if the school was to keep pace with other institutions in the state and with other land-grant [46]

colleges for Negroes. He also called their attention to an offer made by the General Education Board to aid ten of the southern states in the building programs of their land-grant colleges for Negroes. All of these states accepted the offer except Mississippi. The General 

Education Board at this same time offered Tougaloo College and Southern Christian Institute grants which both accepted. Although the legislature failed to accept this offer for Alcorn in 1926, some of the legislators told President Rowan that they felt if the General Education Board would renew its offer in 1928 Mississippi wou'd be inclined to accept it. In 1928 the General Education Board did renew the offer, making known the fact that it would give the college $100,000.00 if the state would put up $200,000.00. President Rowan gave this information to the Board. Said he: "Fortunately, we have at this time what we did not have two years ago-a more friendly ally in the person of a gentleman well-known to this Board, to all the schools of the state, and to the state at large. Without any solicitation, he has manifested a kindly interest in this institution, has already done something to arouse the interest of certain forces in our behalf, and I believe is going to do more in that line. If this Board would join forces with his, I believe this help could be secured."

President Rowan in his report to the Board of Trustees in 1929 called their attention to a survey made by the Department of Interior about two years prior to this time of the leading institutions for the higher education of the Negro. According to that survey Alcorn

College fell short of the high mark in several matters. 

There was not a single delinquency that could not have been removed had the proper financial support been provided. The four weak points in the college, according to this report, were: (1) The organization was not in keeping with modern school organization. Several additional officers were needed-a dean, a registrar, a superintendent of buildings and grounds, a full-time librarian, and a sufficient number of additional teachers to reduce the sizes of classes, and to segregate the high school from the college. At President Rowan's insistent demand the Board of Trustees gave Alcorn a full-time librarian in 1930, and a dean and registrar in 1932. (2) There was not a strong department of education, nor a standard teacher-training course. (3) All of the courses, especially the Liberal Arts and Teacher-training courses in Agriculture and Home 

Economics, needed to be greatly strengthened. [49]

It is almost impossible to know Alcorn College without knowing some individual Alcornites. These brief portraits which follow are taken almost at random from a large list of busy Alcornites who are not only interesting personalities, but are personalities well worth knowing. [63]

Doctor Levi John Rowan comes to our thought in review as a man, as a teacher, and as an administrator. 

Any one of these divisions might be emphasized to the limits of this case study. However, the writer shall attempt to portray some aspects of his many-sided activities under these different headings. 

AS A MAN-L. J. Rowan was born of slave parents on August 7, 1871, at Rodney, Mississippi, just four and a half miles from Alcorn College, the very year in which the college was purchased and dedicated to the educational development of the Negro race. His first days were spent in an obscure log cabin, which was previously used as a living quarters for slaves. He was forced to work his own way all through his early life and young manhood. His early training was received [64]

in such public schools as Rodney, Mississippi, and St. 

Joseph, Louisiana, afforded for Negroes at that time.

Having completed the course of study offered him in these schools at sixteen years of age, on September 18, 1887, he entered Alcorn College. At Alcorn he came in contact with men of Negro leadership and culture, including the Burrus brothers, one of whom was John H. 

Burrus. then president of the college. The faculty as a whole was well-fitted to assume the training of future leaders for a race who but yesterday were slaves. Rowan's love for music; his ability to write and speak effectively; his keen intellect, manliness and fineness of character; his high respect for law and order-all made him an outstanding student among his fellows, and a favorite of his instructors.

His college career, however, was not lacking of its share of financial worries. He found it necessary to make his own way through school by doing house work for white families, and whatever other work he could find to do. At times he was compelled to leave school in the midst of the session and seek employment for one or two quarters in order to earn support for himself These conditions did not discourage him, but made him more determined than ever to reach his goal.

His college goal was reached in May, 1893, by receiving the degree of Bachelor of Science. talented

AS A TEACHER-He early found his calling and dedicated his life to it. Even before he graduated, as soon as he could qualify for public school teaching, he entered the teaching profession. The first five years after graduation he taught with much contentment and joy in the rural schools of the state in the remote sections thereof, located many miles from a railroad. He taught better than he knew; because of services wellrendered in the obscure places he had attracted attention, and consequently in 1898 he was called from obscurity to serve on the faculty of his alma mater. Here was a varied experience and yet exceedingly helpful to him in later years. He was always at his best in the classroom, and exemplified in himself that article of his educational creed which insists that a real teacher should make his subject both interesting and instructive. He rose rapidly from the position of tutor to assistant professor of English by 1901, just three years after his return to the college as instructor. He rose from the rank of assistant professor of English to that of professor of English, and 1905 found him promoted to the presidency of the institution, just twelve years after graduation. 

AS AN ADMINISTRATOR-At a time when the developing institution--Alcorn College-and the Board of Trustees required above all else a president superior in scholarship, a leader of men, and eminently fitted to assume the training of future leaders, Doctor Rowan supplied that need. He served with distinction from 

1905 to 1911, to the entire satisfaction of the Board of Trustees. Up to 1910, each state educational institution had its own Board of Trustees, but as a means of making the administration of these institutions more [66]

uniform, economical and efficient, the legislature reorganized these several Boards into one for all the state educational institutions. Because of efficient management of the affairs of the college by President Rowan under the old and separate Board, when the new and old Boards met-the old to turn over and the new to receive the administration of the affairs of the various state educational institutions-the outgoing Board, through its spokesman, pronounced quite a eulogy upon President Rowan for his careful and efficient work as an administrator and, in recognition of the same, conferred upon him then and there the honorary degree of Doctor of Philosophy.* 

He was moved a step down the ladder by the new Board at the close of its first year, through no fault of his own, to the position of professor of English and vice-president. Following the death of President Martin in December, 1914, Doctor Rowan was again chosen president by the same Board that had demoted him just four years before. From 1915 to 1934 he guided the affairs of the institution and became its most distinguished president. With his aid, an expansion of the physical plant and the student body unparalleled in the history of the college was achieved. During his administration, a summer school, an extension school for teachers in service, and a night school of four weeks each year for the adults of the Alcorn community were inaugurated. During the twenty-five years of his administration, he corferred degrees upon 517 graduates.

A. E. PERKINS 

A. E. Perkins was born in 1879 near Smithdale,

Amite County, Mississippi. He is the second child of a family of nine children. His elementary education began in 1885 at Smithdale, and his first teacher was a Miss Fannie Barrett. Young Perkins' mother stopped him from school after he had attended only a week. 

She was very eager to send him to school, but she felt it quite unsafe to trust him daily crossing a treacherous river with a slippery foot-log as a bridge. 

His progress that week astonished his teacher, his mother and others. He had quite mastered the alphabet and had been promoted to reading such lines as: [80]

"An old sly fox; can he hop?" "A bad dog; he bit a man." His mother soon moved across the treacherous 

Amite River which was robbing her children of what she felt to be a priceless heritage, namely, a substantial education.

His mother had great faith in him, probably because the people called him bright and full of possibilities. 

He says that he was doubtless no smarter than some of the other pupils, but he was a harder worker, more energetic and persistent, and thus he outstripped the other pupils, including his brothers and sisters. He kept the leadership of his class during the four or five sessions he attended public school. His teachers were in the main poorly lettered, but they had that which well supplements book learning, namely, vision, character and common sense. These things they infused into the very life and thinking of their pupils. 

He was taken out of school very early in life and hired out for wages, but he studied all the more while working. Much of his zeal he credits to his mother and teachers. The white people were not unkind to him. 

They spoke to his mother about his aptness, and some of them, particularly Mr. Grif Terrell, Mr. G. H. Butler and Mr. Billy Badon said that he should be sent away and educated because the community could not afford him the proper opportunity. Education in this day called for much sacrifice, and not infrequently severe hardships on the part of Negroes.

October 14, 1884. He received his elementary training at Waterproof, while his high school work was done at Alcorn College, he having finished the high school course there in 1902. He was admitted to the freshman class at Alcorn College in the fall of 1901 and completed his college work with the Bachelor of

After graduation, he went to South Central, Mississippi, and began school work in a wealthy community in Jefferson Davis County. Feeling that something [98]

worthwhile could be done, he proceeded to interest and stimulate the citizens of the community in the effort of getting a better school building. He was a very young man, and most enthusiastic. His enthusiasm was contagious and within one session he raised $400.00 with which to build one of the first Rosenwald schools in 

Mississippi in 1908-1909.

His work attracted attention and for the session 1909-1910 he accepted the principalship of the public school at Collins, the county seat of Covington County,

Mississippi. He served the people at Collins in a very satisfactory manner, and was called from there to 

Kentwood, Louisiana, in 1910.

This modest, unassuming, though energetic Alcornite entered the town of Kentwood to begin a movement which, I dare say, has had a more potent and outstanding influence upon race education than any one other influence which has been, or is now, working in the field of education in the south for Negroes. 

Prof. Strange had been a close student of the attempts of school men who had tried to establish and operate Institutes (as they were called) which were private schools with advanced literary training, with a smattering of industrial training. These schools were supported entirely by donations from both northern and southern white people. He noticed that, for a year or two, they would apparently do well and then they would fail. The building in which the work had been carried on was left standing by the roadside in an almost indescribable state of dilapidation and was a sad reminder of the days when the boys and girls of that community had hopes of advanced education in books and an opportunity to learn a trade. 

Finally Prof. Strange came to the conclusion that no matter how worthy the aim of the founders of such schools happened to be, they were doomed to failure when they had to depend entirely upon donations for their existence. He, therefore, began to think of a way that would insure their permanency. He took the mat- [99]

Thus, Alcorn College gave to this country O. L. Coleman, and through him and his most efficient wife, Coleman College, which in itself is a great contribution to the higher education of the American Negro.

P. S. Bowles, Teacher-Trainer, Vocational Agriculture, 

Alcorn A. & M. College, Alcorn, Mississippi 

The above is a picture of one whose modesty so constrains him that he would be the last to claim it, but of whom it can be truthfully said that without his sketch no history of Alcorn College would be anything like complete; because as a matter of fact it cannot be gainsaid that owing to the different positions he has [112]

worthily filled in the educational life of the State, to his long and continuous connection with the College, his high idealism, and his aggressive spirit, there is no one personality who, wearing the yoke as a mere staff member, has exerted so great an influence in the establishment of the life ideals of the students of the College, nor whose convictions and conceptions of life and its responsibilities have been more thoroughly built into the warp and woof of the fabric of thought and feeling of the Negro manhood and womanhood of this State of this day and generation as the subject of this sketch-Professor P. S. Bowles. 

Professor Bowles is a native of Claiborne county, Mississippi, having been born there on a plantation known as Caledonia, in 1869. His parents moved away from Claiborne county up into Warren when the boy was about four months old. There he lived and played and spent his boyhood as other boys, and there was begun the tedious struggle of climbing the educational ladder in the rural schools of his home community, at the age of seven years. His attendance at school was of a rather desultory sort, as the free school system at that early date, after the Civil war, was just getting established and the terms were rarely more than three or four months long, and the teachers poorly prepared. 

Moreover, Professor Bowles had been born of slave parents who had received their emancipation only four years before the boy was born, and who recognized that any chance at all to learn the "Three R's" in any sort of school was far beyond anything they had ever enjoyed; and therefore, they could not adequately appreciate the necessity of having their son take advantage of what meager opportunities for real training the poorly-organized school system at that time offered. 

It was in the midst of such an uninviting educational environment that the boy was forced to make his initial efforts at education; and early gave evidence of a precocity beyond his years, and stimulated the hopes of his parents, friends, and teachers that he would yet make good and that there was more truth than poetry [113]

PUSHING FORWARD 

in the oft-repeated saying that "Honor and fame from no condition rise; act well your part; there all the honor lies." He was sent to college at the age of seventeen, evidently entering what would now be called the tenth grade, or second year high school. He remained in college seven years, graduating and receiving his Bachelor's Degree in 1895, at the head of his class. He entered the profession of teaching in the rural schools of the state and there brought the same painstaking assiduity to the task of teaching that he had given evidence of in the job of learning. 

He assisted in building and organizing the first high schools in Lawrence and Marion counties, where he spent the first ten years of his after-school life, and where the results of his labors are still in evidence in the communities which he served. 

After having been away from the college ten years, he was recalled by his alma mater to join the forces of those on the college staff who were to "train Mississippi's future citizens." He returned to serve his alma mater as an assistant professor in the department of English. It so happened that much of the English taught to students at the time when he joined the department was in the form of emphasis upon formal grammar. His keen perception in the appropriate use of words enabled him to blaze new paths in the work of the English departiment and shift the emphasis from that of mere statement of rules and canons to discovery of the purpose of those rules and bring about considerable improvement in the use of the English sentence by the students in the departinent. 

Nevertheless, in addition to the new highs which he was able to establish in the mastery of English speech, his many-sided interests in the major purpose for which the institution was established constrained him to discover that the use to which the facilities of the department of agriculture were being put were falling far short of reasonable expectations. He at once began to call the attention of the administration to this serious lack and to insist upon a more purposeful use of [114]

the department's facilities towards the training of the students, in the business of farming and upon an enlargement of those facilities for more adequate use. 

He had the prescience, if we may call it that, to see that in a State that was predominantly rural, and whose major interest and industry was that of farming, and whose population was more than half Negro and mostly untrained, there was nothing that opened up so great an opportunity for his people as adequate training for maximum achievement in the field of this major industry. He had the faith to believe and the courage to assert that Alcorn A. & M. College, which by its name and profession was committed to the training of the Negro citizenship in the vocation of farming, was lying down on her job, and asleep as to her most precious opportunity if she did not turn a greater proportion of her resources and energies to the training of her young manhood for valiant service in this important calling. His position was bitterly assailed and he was maligned and traduced by the majority of the then faculty staff, and denounced as a cheap "time server" and a "player to the galleries" to win favor with the white South. But being convinced of the rectitude of his course he refused to be deflected from the path he had chosen. He continued to inveigh against an educational philosophy which sought to prepare girls and boys for stations beyond any probable attainment, or for no particular station at all; and insisted that they should be directed and constrained to prepare for the opportunities that were at hand, and thus by utilizing these to the best of their abilities they would be preparing themselves for places of higher responsibility, if such there be where they should present themselves. 

In a communication addressed to the president emphasizing this point, on being read before the Board of Trustees for clarity of statement of need in this field, to secure favorable action, his masterly presentation of the case so gripped the attention of the Board that they passed an order in that meeting directing that the author of that communication be transferred to the department of agriculture, and passed other measures re- [115]

PUSHING FORWARD 

commended in the communication, and having the president's approval. He was thus made an assistant professor in the department of agriculture and placed in charge of the institution's newly purchased dairy herd of Jersey cattle. And, as he had done in the department of English, so he did in his new field. He "stepped up" production, and methods of care and management of the herd to new highs and won for himself deserved commendation. While occupying this position Professor Bowles maintained for a number of years an active membership in the American Society of Agronomy.

A vacancy, by death, occurred in the English department at this time, and, the president remembering the good work Professor Bowles had done as under professor in that department, requested him to return to the English department and to accept its headship.

Professor Bowles, however, remained at the head of the English department only one year; when the pass age of the National Vocational Education Act in 1917, and the necessity for the organization of a new department for the training of teachers of vocational agriculture at Alcorn College, caused the recall of Professor Bowles to head up that new department. The lamented Dr. H. O. Sargent, who had been placed in charge of the development of this new field of service among Negroes, after visiting the college and conversing with a number of the members of the teaching staff with a view of selecting a suitable head for the new department, recommended to the administration and the state officials the appointment of Professor Bowles. He accepted the responsibility with a full knowledge of his limitations for the new service and began at once as best he could to qualify for the job while on the job, and the results of his labors to date bear incontestable evidence of the extent to which his ambitions in that direction have been realized.

He took hold of the department when there were only three schools in the State carrying departments of vocational agriculture with a total enrollment of about [116]

fifty students; and largely through his tireless efforts, both in the field of preparation of teachers for the service and the encouragement of boys to enter the work, he has built up the number of departments in the State to more than a hundred, with a total enrollment of persons now reached through organized instruction to several thousands; and in the face of an unsympathetic faculty staff, and even sometimes openly hostile, he has built up the morale of the work at the College until there is now no further hostility on the part of teachers, no further embarrassment on the part of students in enrolling in the course, and it is safe to say that of the male students enrolling in the institution in any one year the Agricultural department will have a respectable plurality.

Mississippi stands out among the foremost states in the number of depart.nents of vocational agriculture established and the number of teachers trained and placed. And in the first national contest for efficiency of training among the boys being trained in farm skills by the teachers heading these departments, held at 

Tuskegee Institute, Mississippi won all first honors in practically every event.

Having built up the system of vocational agricultural departments in the State to reasonably adequate proportions, the constant grind of necessary travel in connection with his official responsibilities to those schools becoming too exacting, Professor Bowles voluntarily offered to retire from the service. 

He appeared before the Board of Trustees of the 

State's Institutions of Higher Learning and explained that he had come to feel at his advanced age that a choice would soon have to be made between slighting the schools to save himself, or sacrificing his health to do his full duty to the schools; and that while he had heard no serious complaint regarding the efficiency of his work from any angle, yet his own consciousness of the actual situation constrained him to prefer retirement from the service, rather than have the efficiency of the service to the system which he had labored so [117]

PUSHING FORWARD 

arduously to build up, in the slightest bit impaired. He thanked the Board for the liberal and generous consideration shown him in returning him for thirty-one consecutive years to his post, and bowed his adieu.

The Board thereupon drew up and unanimously voted a set of resolutions, a copy of which was later furnished 

Professor Bowles, and which he prizes very dearly, commending him for his years of faithful and unselfish service rendered the institution, and for the fidelity to every trust which he had shown the while, and wishing him that happiness and contentment in his retirement which in his years of service he had so richly deserved. 

Thus to all appearances Professor Bowles had come to the end of a long period of service to his adma mater and prepared to take up the less strenuous duties and responsibilities of private life. 

But when it became generally known that Professor Bowles' final retirement from the service of the institution, and especially from the department with which he had been so long connected, was in the offing there came showers of letters and personal requests from faculty staff members and graduates on the field urging that he reconsider the proposed plan to retire, and pleading that he remain, at least for a while longer with the institution and the department of Agricultural Education. Finally the balance was tipped and the decision to retire was abandoned when the 

State Director of Vocational Education under whose direction Professor Bowles had worked for a period of eighteen years, and others connected with the State Department of Education, and who knew of his worth, also joined the fast increasing ranks of the protestors. 

A vague sense of the real appreciation held by those under whom, for whom, and with whom whom Professor

Bowles had worked during these years, was breaking full upon him and he could not remain heedless to its far-reaching implications. He quickly donned his professional armor and prepared to do battle for his alma mater in the usual way at the same old stand until a [118]

more convenient season shall dictate the wisdom of his retirement from the service.

While Alcorn College proudly claims Professor 

Bowles as one of her most worthy products, and rightly so, because he graciously says of her that "she made me all the little that I am;" and while he has given her in continuous service the rich benefit of the years of his most active labors, his services have not altogether been confined to routine duties within her confines. 

He was one of the few progressive teachers of the State who when the banner fell from the hands of his worthy chief, John H. Burrus, for a long time President of Alcorn College, and the organizer of the first State Teachers Association of Mississippi, picked up that banner and assayed to carry on; and labored to reestablish and build up, and did build up that association to its present proud strength and proportions. He has filled at one time or another practically every station in the organization from bench member to President. And during the years that he was President of the State Association it was a common thing to hear him paid the compliment that his administration was the most constructive of any that had yet been. Several of the State-wide movements now in action in the sphere of Negro education are the fruits of policies and ineasures initiated by him during his administration. 

He was the first to officially bring the collective influence of the organization to bear upon the eradication, or at least the minimization of several evils that had fastened themselves in our education system, to the serious detriment of the group concerned, as well as upon the institution of certain positive reforms that have since gained state wide sanction. 

His piercing vision has always enabled him to look beyond the frills and gewgaws of life and see the fundamentals, and to hold his life and purposes steadily to their achievement. [119]

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 

The facts and factors discovered in this study of Alcorn and her graduates will be commented upon briefly.

The rise of Negro leadership and the development of the American Negro depend upon opportunity; but opportunity for him has two aspects: (1) Preparation on the part of the Negro to meet and to make opportunity for himself, and (2) Opportunity provided for him through outside sources.

Part I of this study presents a brief record of what a limited number of the race and one educational institution have achieved. For the individuals who have been students at this institution, the opportunities it has offered them have been provided largely by others. 

Part II of the study, on the other hand, cites cases of a few who have met and made opportunities for themselves. In this they are also making and spreading opportunities for others of their race.

All of the schools maintained in Mississippi prior to the civil war were schools established for the education of white children. The first schools established for

Negroes were at Corinth shortly after the occupation of that town by the Union troops in 1862. 

Among the best element of southern whites, there was a favorable attitude toward Negro education prior to 1871; however, there was also a certain element who opposed Negro education before the year 1871, the reconstruction period. Both of these forces are still in evidence in the south. A free public school system of education for Negroes and whites went into operation [120]

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION 

in October, 1870, resulting from cooperation between the Negro and white members of the "Black and Tan" convention of 1869, which authorized the legislature to establish this system of education. The legislature of 

1870, composed of Negroes and whites, passed the required act which put this system into operation, and Henry R. Pease was elected the first State Superintendent of Education.

The first Negro land-grant college founded in the United States was established by the State of Mississippi in 1871-the Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College (it being originally named Alcorn University)named in honor of James L. Alcorn, who was then governor of the state. Each president of the college has contributed something to its development, but Dr. L. J. 

Rowan served longer and guided its larger development for nineteen consecutive years.

The college sent out its first graduating class, consisting of three members, in 1882. Since that date, Alcorn has graduated 833 from its college department. 

These graduates are found in 31 states and the District of Columbia of the United States, and in 54 of the 82 counties of Mississippi. They are listed in 40 different occupations. Alcorn graduates have made some outstanding contributions in Negro leadership as evidenced by the facts used in the case studies that appear in this report. These case studies have been recorded in, what has seemed to the author, necessary detail. 

L. J. Rowan did outstanding work in the building of the "Greater Alcorn". In 1928 he secured from the 

State Legislature and the General Education Board of 

New York City more than $300,000.00 as an initial start in Alcorn's building program. 

R. S. Grossley was the first Negro appointed Assist ant State Supervisor of Public Schools of Mississippi a position created by the State Legislature in 1919.

was he who started the Rosenwald schools moving at a rapid rate. His real work has been done at the State 

College for Colored Students in Delaware, where he has been president since 1923.

P. W. Hill has distinguished himself in the medical field by his special efforts in the practice of Preventive Dentistry among Negro children of the public schools of Clarksdale and Coahoma County. He is also a depu ty of the Coahoma County Health Department. 

A. E. Perkins furthered the advancement of Negro education in the south by erecting two of the finest public school buildings for Negroes in this section of the country-Biloxi Public School, Mississippi, and the 

Danneel Public School, New Orleans, Louisiana. At the same time, he helped to raise the general status of the Negro so as to redound to the good of the communities in which he wrought, of the south, and of the nation.

J. E. Walker is a prominent figure in Negro business. 

As a physician, he built a fine practice; as a business man, he built a bank; and as a Negro capitalist, he built a leading insurance company.

J. H. Moseley has done outstanding work as a builder and promoter of Negro schools. His most outstanding work has been done at Mound Bayou, Clarksdale, Grenada, and as president of Natchez College, all in Mississippi. 

A. M. Strange is called the "Dean of Colored Public

Schools". In 1908-1909, he built one of the first Rosenwald schools in Mississippi, and it was he who built the first county training school for Negroes in the United States in 1911-1912. He was employed continuously in this system, which he worked so diligently to improve, until May 10, 1933, when he was elected Principal of the Okolona Industrial School at Okolona, Mississippi. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION 

P. S. Bowles has gained the admiration of high governmental officials in Agriculture because of his tireless efforts in the projection of a sound agricultural policy for Negro Mississippians. "By their work ye shall know them," or, "A workman is best known by his chips," can be most tangibly applied to Alcorn College and the above-listed Alcorn graduates. The author has attempted herein to present a tangible measure of their respective achievements. [123]
