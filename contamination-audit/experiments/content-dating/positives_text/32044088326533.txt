THE BEGINNING OF IT ALL 

Only a few months after the armistice of November 11, 1918, an unimportant individual named Adolf Hitler began to make himself a minor nuisance in Munich,

Germany. He created the nationalist socialist workers' party, and added to it a semi-military organization known as the 

Hitler volunteers. Less than five years after the armistice which declared the 

World War to be "unfinished business," he organized armed revolt in Munich. 

The uprising failed and Hitler was con victed of treasonable behavior and condemned to jail for five years. 

Unfortunately, he was released in less than nine months, or else the history of the past quarter century might have been greatly different. Immediately he went back to his mischief-making, assisted by other unpleasant and conniving characters. Among them was Dr. Josef Goeb bels, who joined the Nazi movement in 1922 and stayed with it to the bitter end in bombed and besieged Berlin. In July, 1945, only a few days after the Russians admitted their comrades in arms to the battered city, Berlin was not a pleasant place to visit. Particularly, the office of the Ministry of Propaganda, heart of the spider web of Dr. Josef

Goebbels, was a wreck and ruin. The end of the building fronting on the Wilhelmstrasse was a heap of rubble. Bombs had hit the rest of it in a dozen places, and a pitched battle had been fought through its long corridors. But, unlike thousands of lesser buildings in Berlin, the home of the Ministry of Propaganda had not burned.

So there was rubbish deep in most of its rooms, left there by the Russians when they helped themselves to everything else of apparent value. There was a stack of 

Dr. Goebbels' personal scrapbooks. There were dozens of his pictures, suitable for framing. There was a vast disorder of waste paper, letters, bookkeeping rec ords, and photographs.

A visiting correspondent picked up in one rubbish-strewn room a small carton containing a roll of film. A month later, back in the United States, he found it to be a complete pictorial history of the Nazi party, from its ominous beginnings to its peak of power in Nuernberg, in 

1936. Pictures on this and the preceding page are from this Nazi-made film. AS

POSTWAR CORRESPONDENT 

It is a long way from Philadelphia,

U. S. A., to Berlin, Germany. The jour ney from here to there started nearly twenty years ago, on a Sunday afternoon. 

That was when people with nothing better to do amused themselves by watch ing airplanes perform the miraculous feat of getting off the ground, and the more. remarkable triumph of getting back to earth again, right side up. On this his toric occasion an over-zealous press agent for a near-Philadelphia flying field had hinted in the newspapers that daring acrobatics and perilous parachute jumps might be expected, and ten thousand peo ple came to see. 

One was a teacher of Latin and Eng lish, who sat in a hangar's shadow and watched airplanes go up and down with monotonous lack of incident or accident.

Eventually it became evident there would be no parachute jumps or fatal accidents. 

At this moment arrived the owner of the flying field. Swiftly he fired the press agent and looked around for another.

There was nobody suitable in sight, except a tired school teacher who was wondering how to spend a summer vacation with nothing else to spend. Would the teacher take temporary employment in aviation, writing publicity stories and persuading editors, if possible, to publish them? 

To write intelligently of aviation requires some skill with language, an attentive ear for the knowledge and experience of better men, and enough mechanical ability to mend a broken bicycle. There fore it is possible that a Latin teacher might become an aviation writer in emer gency and get away with it. 

Nineteen years ago I did not passionately approve of the airplane, and am doubtful still whether its invention and development have been worth their price. 

Nor do I think it necessary to be able to fly one, any more than I feel called upon to qualify as motorman of a street car in order to ride in one. In spite of these suspicions, it was seen fit a dozen years ago to make me a member of the Aviation

Writers' Association. 

In the late spring of 1945 came an invi tation which was a compliment to the services rendered by A. W. A. in peace and war. The function of the Association is declared to be "to establish and maintain high standards of quality and ve racity in the gathering, writing, editing and dissemination of aeronautical infor mation." The War Department thought well enough of A. W. A.'s work in these respects to invite a dozen of us to tour 

Europe and study what strategic bombing had done to win the war against Ger many.

It aroused some apprehension when the news got around that I was planning a trip by air to Europe. Nervous neighbors shivered at the idea of flying so far, with no place to sit down except a wave on the Atlantic Ocean. This is because some people were scared by airplanes in infancy aud have never got over it. 

But airplanes are not dangerous.

Probably there have been more killings and maimings by bicycles and roller and maimings by bicycles and roller skates than were ever charged up against the airplane. So I am not afraid of planes, though desperately in dread of automo bile rear bumpers. I can step into a plane and ride through rough weather without turning a hair or my breakfast, but I stand paralyzed on city sidewalks, afraid to cross the street lest somebody's car should hook me with a bumper and drag me three blocks to total destruction. 

Especially in London, where the right side of the road is the wrong side and the left side is the right side, which can be very confusing to a traveler who left the United States only twenty hours ago.

Before then there had been some painful preliminaries to the journey, including a series of stabbings with hypodermic needles, intended to protect us from typhus, typhoid, smallpox, tetanus and yellow fever. It was worth it to join the brotherhood of men who have served their country by submitting to mayhem from the medical profession. Soldiers and sailors and some civilians-we have all had our shots in the arm and should live forever, or until we fall down the cellar stairs and break our senile necks. 

A civilian who travels under military management is required to dress the part in an officer's uniform, though without the fancy trimmings. There are eight pockets in the summer uniform and in the full-dress ensemble for cool climates there are a dozen. It is a fine thing to be patched all over with pockets, except that a welldressed correspondent is not permitted to put anything in them. Somehow he must secrete about his person his pocketbook, passport, watch, handkerchief, glasses, fountain pen, vaccination scar, pencil, comb, cigarettes, matches, lighter, letters of credit, military orders, aspirin tablets and small change. But there may be no lumps to mar the military appearance of his uniform.

We ran into trouble with these regu lations on the autobahn leading from 

Munich to the Southern Redoubt. There was shrill whistling behind us and it could not be Junior, the jeep driver, making propositions to a girl on a bicycle. 

It was a military policeman on a motorcycle, a grim fellow wearing white helmet and gloves and a small cannon on his hip. He disapproved of the fact that two members of our party were riding bareheaded. Ours not to reason why or make reply. Meekly the offenders put on their hats, the traffic cop saluted and whirled away.

The War Department's Regulations for Correspondents Accompanying the Armed 

Forces says that "the proper uniform for accredited correspondents is that of an officer, but less all insignia of grade or arm or service." The hat or cap is part of the uniform.

Moreover, we were not truly and properly war correspondents. We were postwar correspondents.

Hurry Up and Wait

Washington is full of people who find passionate satisfaction in statistics, but nobody seems to know how much free coffee and how many gratis doughnuts are handed out by the American Red Cross during an average war. My own gallonage for a month's drinking and dunking might be impressive. 

There is a phrase of military language which explains why Red Cross coffee is a popular beverage at military airports. 

The slogan is, "Hurry up and wait." 

You hurry to the field so that you can wait two hours for the plane to take off. 

You go by plane so that you will have plenty of time to wait when you get there. You hurry so much that you have no time to see what you want to see, but plenty to sit staring at nothing, or drinking coffee. 

You also hurry to Washington on 

Thursday so that by good luck you can get away to Europe on Sunday afternoon. 

Washington could explain, of course, that there are papers to be signed and photos and fingerprints taken. There are credentials to be issued. Passengers for trans-Atlantic travel by air must be briefed, a depressing ceremony explaining how to meet all imaginable emergencies on the way over. There are discussions of being "ditched" in mid-ocean which make first-time passengers quietly thoughtful. There is a checking of physical fitness, a counting of vaccination scars and inocu lation punctures. There is also a scarcity of airplanes, due to heavy traffic and bottlenecks.

The result is that the correspondent is "alerted" and may stay that way for days. To be alerted is to be available by telephone within reach of the airport. 

Sometimes his alertness may be relaxed by the promise that there will be no plane space available for four hours, or until tomorrow, which permits the poor devil to sleep a little or go to the movies.

In the last hours of our waiting there arrived by air a load of soldiers relieved from duty in Europe. They were weary and grimy. But under the dirt on their faces was a fixed grin of delight in being back home. 

Outbound passengers were requested to wait until they were cleared through the necessary formalities. It was done in

Five hours of flying will take you from super-heated Washington to the cool quiet of Harmon Field, in Newfoundland.

The big ATC base is circled by forest and when we got there was canopied by a velvet sky, thick with stars. It is an elegant airport and some day you may be using it as a short cut to happy hunting grounds and fishing streams. These are among the war's dividends the many acred squares of concrete which mark the world's new highways, located anywhere that a military airplane might find it convenient to sit down.

There were twenty-three of us, bound for Europe on sundry errands some ferry pilots, two Polish officers, half a dozen correspondents and a flight nurse in trousered uniform and a mop of curls.

Past midnight is bedtime, even on a 

C-54 cargo plane flying the Atlantic.

Going to bed on a cargo plane consists of draping yourself with a blanket and lying draping yourself with a blanket and lying down on the floor. A briefcase or folded coat may be your pillow. Somehow you sleep, lulled by the muffled slumber song of four mighty engines. 

But the Polish general snored loudly and with a foreign accent. A ferry pilot nestled his foot in the back of my neck.

So I sat up for a thousand miles or so, watching a strange picture. Sound sleep reduces all men to the least common denominator of humanity. There is little to choose between generals and sergeants, soldiers or civilians, if you bundle them in blankets and lay them out on an airplane floor. 

You get up in a cargo plane as a dog does-by standing up and stretching. 

You do not wash, unless you care to lick your own face and mop up with a handkerchief. There is no stampede to the bathroom because there is no bathroom. 

There is a curtain and a can. There are chemicals in the can. 

You fold your blanket and turn it back to an unhappy sergeant who travels back and forth between America and Europe, doing odd jobs for the passengers and sweeping up after them. You look from the window and assume there is an ocean beneath you, but the plane flies over a rumpled carpet of clouds and there is nothing else to be seen.

The pilot had promised us in Newfound land that we would land in Europe in eleven hours and twenty-three minutes.

He made it with eight minutes to spare.

RETURN OF THE NATIVE 

Prestwick, Scotland, is a port of entry to the United Kingdom, and here the air traveler settles with the customs inspec tors. Nothing dutiable was discovered in my own baggage, though the customs man went politely blind when he met a bottle of whiskey beneath my socks and shirts. But the Polish general found him self in difficulties. In innocence or igno rance he had loaded his bags with stock ings for his lady friends and many of them were silk. At last sight he was digging deep to pay duty on them.

Nothing could be done to help the general. But there was also the little flight nurse, alone and forlorn in a strange land and surrounded by menfolk minding their own business. Her curls had lost their jauntiness on the long night cross ing and she looked as though ten minutes with a mirror would make her feel much better. "The powder room is down the corri dor, around the corner, second door to the left," I told her. She smiled wanly, said "Thank you," and departed down the corridor.

There was fickle weather as we flew from Scotland to Povington, but the skies cleared as we neared London and the scars of war could be seen below. Unlike those of other wars, which were lines of trenches and battered fortresses, these half-healed wounds were the broken roofs of houses and the pock marks where bombs had fallen. Patches on hundreds of homes showed where fragments had fallen from air battles overhead, telling there was an air field not far away, a constant target for the enemy's attention. 

From Bovington we went on by mili tary truck, noting other signs that a hur ricane of air warfare had swept over England. It left a lot of useless ugliness in its wake. Almost it seemed stupid that air-raid shelters were still standing in countless suburban and rural streets in the midsummer of 1945. The postwar visitor wonders why the British people still put up with them. But they have put up with the Tower of London for 

800 years and are rather proud of it.

These dank and dismal shelters were the fortresses of Britain during a long war. 

But now England is at peace and it is possible to put off till tomorrow, or ten years from now, what would be better done today.

Something of the long fight to keep London itself alive we learned from those London itself alive we learned from those who directed the battle. They were spe who directed the battle. They were specialists, now able to speak of their skill in extracting citizens of London, living or dead, from bomb-ruined buildings.

They talked of special cranes built for the job and of strategies of attack on a tangle of tottering wreckage. They had grim samples to show the visitors, great areas empty of homes and factories, historic buildings full of holes and the amazing space around St. Paul's which will look, in a few years, as if it were intended to be there from the beginning.

Nearing London at eventide, my fellow passengers ate sandwiches from a satchel and apologized for not sharing them with the stranger. The lady left the satchel open; I dropped into it a bar of soap and tried not to blush for doing it. It is not a simple matter, even in wartime, to present a cake of soap to a middle-aged lady of obvious breeding and social standing. But she beamed with gratitude and her farewell word in Euston station was, "O, what a simply beautiful bath I'm going to have tomorrow morning."

We had been advised by the War De partment to take along plenty of soap for personal and social purposes. Of my own two dozen cakes the first was laid on the altar of Anglo-American friendship, which was a good place for it.

HAMBURG, GERMANY

The Wake of The Storm 

Though Biggin Hill, southeast of London, was a dreary place in the aftermath of war, it had seen its days of glory. 

If you remember the movie, "Mrs. Miniver," you know something of Biggin Hill already. When we were there it was a base for the Air Transport Group, which had handled military traffic into the combat areas. But during the Battle of 

Britain it was the home for many of the 

Spitfires which saved England. Nearly 

200 German planes were knocked down during the battle by Spitfires from Biggin

Hill. It might have been in the pleasant. valleys around the hill that Mrs. Miniver lived and heard her pilot son go by on his deadly business, or waited for her husband's little boat to come back from 

Dunkirk. 

Here was waiting the plane, a C-47, which would ferry us over the Channel and fly us wherever we cared to go in 

France and Germany. First to the sands of Dunkirk, where the British were backed into the sea and lived to fight another day. 

There it was discovered that second-hand battlefields are disappointing. Tourists will find them so who try to identify the decisive battlegrounds of this war, as we did when looking down on the leafy for est of the Argonne and the valleys of the Meuse and Marne, where there was very bloody business a generation ago.

So it was with Dunkirk, even so soon. 

Also we flew over Belgium, by way of Nieupoort and Ostend, noting where bombs had left their marks in clusters and strings across the countryside. Here and there a luckless house lay smashedperhaps because it was used for an ob servation post or Gestapo headquarters. 

Or an air field would show its pattern, pitted mercilessly with craters.

At Antwerp we circled to see the pattern of robot bombing, or its lack of pat tern. 

Then the trail turned southward and at last appeared brief zigzags of trenches and tiny gashes which were foxholes. They seemed pitifully inade quate from high overhead, yet men lived in them in some sort of safety for weeks and months. Mother Earth's brown shoulder is still a good hiding place for the soldier, even from the weapons of modern war. 

THE SKELETON OF CIVILIZED LIVING 

A dozen rusted tanks lay where they were killed; crumpled planes lay like broken toys which a stupid child has tossed away and gone to play somewhere else. This was a part of the Battle of the 

Bulge and so was a nameless little town which lay in the middle of an empty battlefield, hammered to ruin by artillery as well as bombs. Later we saw more in the same shocking shape, the dead communities of Europe. American infantry men remember them painfully; they were harder to take than "populated places."

We went far off course to trace a sec tion of the Battle of the Bridges. Along every canal and river the bridges lay broken in the water. The air travelers wondered how people down there could get along without them; then remembered that most of them had nowhere to go to be much better of than where they were. 

So to Cologne, which was once a beau tiful city and may be again, fifty years from now. But in the wake of the storm most of Cologne was a shambles of roofless ruins, its great bridges tangled wrecks in the Rhine. We circled the cathedral, still in one piece, though badly shaken. 

Also three hospitals stood aloof from the desolation of Cologne, nearly unharmed. 

One near the city was badly wrecked, and somebody deplored its bombing.

Then spoke up one of our airplane's crew. "I did that job, and don't think I'm sorry. The ack ack hit our bombardier and the bombs let go too soon. He died later and he was my buddy. We weren't after their hospital, but a 500-pounder went right through the center of it. I

We flew through the long valley of the Ruhr, sometimes below the tops of tall factory chimneys. For fifty miles our pilot, who had formerly flown a fighting plane, chose to fly no more than 200 feet from the ground, which is a nice way to see the country if you do not scare easily.

But some people do. In this fashion we went hastily through Essen, where Mr. 

Krupp's cannon works were not much to look at, though they might arouse the admiration of an ambitious junkman. 

Pilots of bombers say a chimney can stand more bombing than anything else built by man, with the possible exception of a tall church tower. Chimneys still stand thick in the Ruhr Valley, though there is rarely a factory at their feet. The explanation is that a chimney is a tiny tar get from four miles high. A chimney is neatly circular and takes the blast of a near miss with hardly a shiver, while thick walls go down. An engineer ar gued, in Hamburg, that homes and offices and factories will be built in the future with streamlined walls to be safe from any bombing but the hit direct. But it seemed to some of us more reasonable to cut out the bombing and let the homes and workshops of men be any shape their owners like them to be.

NO BOMB SHALL FALL 

In more ways than the obvious one, Hermann Goering was the biggest fool of the Nazis' hierarchy and Hamburg has grim reason to know it.

Early in the war he promised the Ger mans that no enemy bomb would fall on the lands guarded by his Luftwaffe. In 

October, 1942, he said again that the situation was under control. "Although 

Mr. Churchill declared a few weeks ago that he would make a little excursion with a thousand planes over Germany every night, then I can say, first of all, that he has not as yet made a single such excursion with a thousand planes, and he will never make one, either."

The first thousand-plane raid hit Co logne. There were others against Lubeck and elsewhere. But the beginning of the end for Germany was in early August, 1943, when great fleets of British bomb ers, nine nights out of ten, smashed into hopeless ruin the great city of Hamburg.

Afterwards a Danish consular official, who escaped the slaughter, said that 

200,000 persons lay dead in the city. 

Nobody will ever know certainly how many did die, but there is a plain reason why many thousands did. Hamburg was not prepared for heavy bombing. There were few underground shelters, because 

Goering had said they would not be needed. The Nazis could hardly advise a scramble for shelter while insisting that the Luftwaffe ruled the skies.

TWO-MAN SUBMARINE, HAMBURG 

Later the Germans became expert in air-raid defense. We found thick concrete shelters in other industrial cities, and nearly every house was marked with arrows pointing to fortified shelters, where rescue crews should seek survivors.

But there were few in Hamburg. When hell stopped falling from heaven, in 1943, it was too late to save the city or people of Hamburg.

There was dreadful carnage in the old city section, down by the waterfront.

There were miles of streets on the east side where human beings could live only in holes beneath the rubble. Curiosity tempted me down broken steps into a shapeless cavern beneath a mound of masonry and tangled metal. I found my self invading somebody's living room; a woman and three children sat there in the gloom. Elsewhere little children scut tled among the rubbish, playing games in the only kind of world they could remember a world of shattered buildings and the smell of death. They made faces at the Americans and ran underground for safety, like rabbits into burrows.

All this death and destruction did not finish Hamburg. But then the Americans went to work on the waterfront indus tries. So did the R. A. F., blasting seven great holes through twelve feet of concrete roof over the submarine pens. So there were submarines upside down in stinking water, submarines shattered on

Eighth Air Force, and it proved a point.

The way to win a modern war is to de stroy the weapons with which a nation makes war. 

Seven Hamburg refineries for natural oil felt the terrific impact of American air power and went out of business. Not immediately, because a refinery can be repaired by heart-breaking effort and enough manpower. But at last they were nothing but wildernesses of twisted steel, pipes, shattered concrete and scattered bricks by millions. Their storage tanks lay blown apart like tin cans exploded by giant firecrackers. 

The largest crane in Europe was hit and its giant arms hung limp on its framework. Drydocks sat half submerged in the harbor mud. A big ship stood still upright, but not afloat, its wounds gap ing black against its burned hull. This was to have been the flagship of the fleet which once sailed the Europa and Bre men. Opposite in no better shape was the gutted hulk of the Robert Ley, the cruise ship which once took deserving workers of doubtful morals on pleasant trips through the Baltic, in reward for faithful service in Hitler's factories.

There was a military man, in the latter days of the war, who declared that Ger many could not recover from the conflict for a hundred years. The figure is still extreme and may have been disproved already. But it did not seem fantastic as we tramped over the rubble of Hamburg and threaded its cluttered waterways. 

The damage seemed beyond reach of ordinary measurements or description. 

The eye wearied with looking at it. The heaps of rubbish lost their human mean ing; they were no longer homes or fac tories or office buildings or even the wrecks of them. It was hard to believe they were made patiently and well for human work and living. It was impossi ble to imagine the total task of moving so much rubbish away, and dreadful to conjecture what lay beneath it. Hamburg seemed a city inhabited only by despair. 

They had reason to despair who once believed the loud-mouthed Goering, boast ing that no bomb should ever touch the sacred and guarded soil of Germany.

We were well treated and cared for in all our journeyings in Europe under mili tary management. This was not always easy. 

There were sixteen of us and sometimes more. Housing so many may not be a simple matter in a devastated country and a badly smashed city. Feed ing them is a similar problem. Finding them transportation may be tough, espe cially when twelve of them have a dozen different ideas where they want to go.

In Hamburg we were comfortably lodged in a handsome house in the suburbs. The house was owned, in a manner of speaking, by a retired laundryman.

He had retired a long way from Hamburg when the bombs began to fall and had left his home still furnished and excellently suited to serve as a "press camp" for the

United States Strategical Air Forces, or 

U.S.S.T.A.F. A press camp is not neces sarily a tented colony equipped with all possible discomforts and inconveniences; in Europe it was more likely to be, in the latter years of the war, the best house available when the generals had taken their pick. This one was unharmed by bombs or gunfire. There were gardens around it, a few flowers and some fruit trees. 

This is what happens when you lose a war. Strangers move into your home with no warning and scant courtesy. You move where you can. Your house is yours no longer, nor any of your posses sions which you cannot carry with you. 

The strangers sit at your table, sleep in your beds, play on your piano, your radio and billiard table. 

They handle your books, help themselves to your writing paper and admire your pictures. The laundryman must have long since left the operation of washtub and iron to others; he was evidently a magnate in his field and a man of expen sive tastes. His pictures were modern, but good stuff and pleasant to live with.

A delicate landscape in the dining room and a gay scene of festivity in some southern city. Solid portraits and more landscapes in the living room and library. 

In his master bedroom and bath the laundryman let himself go a little. It is startling to encounter artistic nudes on a bathroom's walls. On the other hand, it is likely that more undressed females are to be found in bathrooms than anywhere else. The laun dryman's nudistic tendencies appeared more dramatically in his bedroom, one of its walls half covered by a painting which was promptly titled "Strength

Through Joy." Its subject was allegorical rather than historical and it seemed likely that two seated fig ures and a couple of children in the foreground, all more or less undressed, might be the laundryman and his loving family.

AVIATION WRITERS' ASSOCIATION, HAMBURG HARBOR, JULY, 1945 

This is what happens when you lose a war. You may be ordered to wait on your conquerors, serving their meals and caring for their quarters. You may be glad to do so for the better living you can salvage from their table. 

This is what happens when you lose a war. The invader helps himself to what he pleases. He may or may not pay for it. The individual soldier does not contemplate payment for souvenirs collected by a process strongly resembling shoplifting. You could even call it looting, except that it is done without violence. 

In this war the Americans called it "liberating." 

There is another sort of liberating that is part of the price to be paid for losing a war. The conqueror takes from the conquered his thoughts and dreams, as well as the work of his hands. He borrows for keeps his unfinished inventions, his military and mechanical ideas, his plans and blueprints. 

Dr. Richard Vogt is a quiet and smiling man who speaks good English. He has worked in several parts of the world, but throughout the war as aeronautical engineer of Blohm & Voss, shipbuilders of Hamburg. And to a group of postwar correspondents Dr. Vogt was politely obliged to tell all he knows about air planes. He might refuse to talk, but this is not wise when you have lost a war.

But Dr. Vogt did not talk like a badly beaten German, dead broke and out of work. He spoke with enthusiasm of the airplanes he had built and of others he will build some day, for somebody. He compared the abilities of those who fought the war and the strategies of their commanders. It was done without bitterness, calmly and critically. Germany had lost the war, so what? There will still be work in the world for scientists and engineers.

JUNIOR AND THE JEEP 

Jeepitis is a disease not mentioned in the ordinary or domestic dictionary. It is a misery in the lumbar region, but it is not lumbago. It is a stiffness and creaking in the joints, but it is not rheumatism. It is something which makes it diffi cult to sit down and painful to stand up, but it is not paralysis.

Three osteopaths worked on my jeepitis when I reached home. One then retired from practice, another went to 

Florida and the third contracted a case of jeepitis of his own. A medicine man studied my symptoms and declared they added up to traumatic sciatica. The better name for it is still jeepitis. 

One way to contract jeepitis is to ride two hundred and thirty miles in a day over war-weary roads in an Army jeep, with a soldier named Junior at the wheel. 

It is possible that Junior possessed some other name, but it was never mentioned.

It is also likely that Junior had a mother, and his mother may be proud and fond of her soldier son. She would not care to hear him spoken of as a menace.

But the reconversion and reconditioning of Junior may be a serious social problem when he does get home. When he entered the Army at the susceptible age of eighteen the military authorities, in their doubtful wisdom, appointed him to drive a jeep. He had been driving it for two years when we met at Munich.

Will he drive a car in the manner of a jeep when he returns to civilian life? If so, he will be impatient with crowded highways and thread his way along sidewalks. He will take short cuts through playgrounds, golf courses and cemeteries. 

And he will drive at fifty-five miles an hour over any kind of road, unless the car is willing and able to go faster.

A disturbing detail of Junior's driving technique was his willingness to look back along the road, waving one hand and whistling, when a good-looking German girl went by on a bicycle. The jeep, meanwhile, careened uncertainly along the road, leaping from crag to crag like a mountain goat, while its semicivilian passengers held tight to stanchions and hawsers and prayed out loud. 

To make matters worse on this fearsome journey, we rode all day through bright mountain sunshine which blistered our noses and lips and bleached our eyebrows. This was merely a touch of sun poisoning, but it was some time be fore we compared countenances and agreed it was nothing worse. In postwar 

Europe there was constant fear of infec tion, and memories of Fallersleben and Dachau haunted our imaginations. 

Junior and the jeep were slowed down effectively when we entered the foothills of Bavaria. Suddenly, the wide autobahn stopped in midair. Down in a deep gorge lay a mighty bridge, expertly and perma nently wrecked by dynamite. Ten miles farther along we found another in the same shape.

It was no accident that made the big bridges vulnerable to dynamite at these almost impassable gorges. Beyond lay Aus tria and Berchtesgaden, where armies retreating in good order might defy five times their number. Breaking their bridges behind them, the Nazis might have taken refuge there for a long last stand, if their armies had not been bombed to a standstill and cut to ribbons before they got there. And when they gave up hope, they destroyed the bridges so that the Allies could not use them. 

Army engineers had wrought miracles in constructing detours around these breaks in the autobahn and were laboring mightily to keep them functioning under terrific traffic. Mostly it was a one-way traffic of trucks, tanks and guns lumber ing north toward a port of embarkation We were going the other way. Not only jeepitis, but eleven new gray hairs testify to the emotional strain involved in crossing a rocky canyon at the edge of the 

Alps, with Junior honking his horn impertinently at monstrous prime movers, loaded with thirty-ton tanks, bearing down upon us from above.

Under normal and peaceable circumstances Junior is probably a kindly lad, fond of children and respectfully embar rassed in the presence of ladies. But 

Junior in Germany is something else.

Junior is the gentleman whose tendency to "fraternize" with frauleins caused the military authorities no little anxiety.

Junior is the young man who thought it amusing, when the war was won, to scare 

German civilians silly by nudging them with his jeep as he passed through their towns and villages. 

But Junior tossed candy bars to little children of southern Germany with a free hand, though we wished he wouldn't when the road was rough and winding. 

Junior drove skillfully, though with apparent unconcern for the survival of his vehicle or the nervous systems of his pas sengers. After all, if the jeep broke down the Army would give him another.

THE EIGHT MARKS BROTHERS 

Everywhere in Europe, when the war was only lately ended, the man in uniform lived the life of a soldier, even though he was a civilian in disguise. 

There was no other way to live. No transient hotels were taking in travelers. 

To eat in civilian restaurants was forbidden. Traveling under military management, our party was always billeted in military hotels or requisitioned homes, sometimes well and often worse.

This monument to egotism, now a mockery of human arrogance, took three thousand men about three years to build, including the fortified highway which leads up to it. It was given to Hitler by the grateful German people, inspired or persuaded somewhat by the Nazi party.

It provided Der Fuehrer with a place to get away from it all, to plot and brood.

It is a suitable place for a man to be lonely and go mad. The view is beautiful from the chalet, the Berghof, but here there is high solitude and a far horizon of mountain peaks and a hundred miles of

Austria at your feet. There was still snow, in mid-July, on the mountain across the valley and a sort of bleakness all around.

The chalet, or what is left of it, sits amid the loveliest mountain scenery in all Austria, now badly scarred by bombing. 

The house is ruined beyond repair; so are the barracks built for Hitler's bodyguard regiment. But behind the chalet is a strange structure altogether untouched. 

Because it is there, the bombers had no chance of hitting Hitler when they swept through the valley, even if he had been there at the time of their visit. 

Through heavy concrete a doorway leads to a tunnel which dives into the mountainside by a flight of sixty-five steps.

At the foot the tunnel makes two sharp turns and is guarded by watertight and airtight doors, and by slits in the walls which were for rifles covering the en trance. Then comes a long corridor, con necting with thirty rooms or more, bur ied a hundred feet under the mountain. 

They include complete apartments, kitch ens, storerooms, a barber shop and beauty parlor. There is a small hospital, includ ing something suspiciously resembling an obstetrical ward. They thought of every thing, these ingenious Nazis.

This is guesswork, but it may well be supposed this was to be the last rathole of the Nazi high command, had they re treated safely to the Southern Redoubt. 

It would have been, beyond question, a long and difficult job to get them out of there.

LIFE IS VERY TOUGH 

When we complain that life is tough we state a scientific fact as well as a personal grievance. In respect to destroying life on this planet, the greatest attempt at mass suicide ever undertaken by civiliza tion has been a failure. Millions died in the war, but the ultimate effect on the world's population is small, almost infinitesimal. Life is very tough; it goes on everywhere though it dies somewhere. 

The stubbornness of life is more than a matter of human endurance. Trees and grass and flowers do not die out when a storm of war goes by.

There was a small forest once in the heart of Berlin, a wooded park with its trees well cared for. There was never a forest so thoroughly bombed and burned.

But a few weeks after the war ended we found its splintered and blackened branches hiding their wounds in new foliage.

There was death lurking still in the Tiergarten, for it was heavily mined and a few days earlier a jeep full of soldiers who refused to believe in signs had been blown to pieces there. But most of its trees were alive, in a fashion. Many will die this winter to keep Germans warm in 

Berlin, but the bombers could not kill them. 

Little bombs do only trifling damage to a forest. A tree bends to a small explo sion and sheds its force around its streamlined bole. A big bomb, like those used at Berchtesgaden, kicks over trees for a score of yards from where the accident occurred. But you will visit Berchtes gaden some day, when it will be one of the world's best-known playgrounds for tourists, and find no trace of where the bombs missed buildings, leaving their mark across the valley as though Paul 

Bunyan had stumbled through the woods. 

Living things grow on high mountains, as in woods and valleys. I cannot name the little flowers which grow so cheerfully on the mountaintop where Hitler did his brooding and plotting. But they were all familiar, the sort of flowers that like cool climates and are found in northern England and the higher parts of New 

York State. They were very gay in the sharp sunshine of Austria, though a little man in big boots once stamped upon them as he planned to conquer the world. The day will come when Hitler will be only the shadow of a shade on the pages of human history, but the flowers of Berchtesgaden will still be blooming. 

Even in battered London there are flowers, where the wreckage of bombing and fire has been neatly cleared away. 

Grass and young trees grow there. Grass is so healthy where a V-2 bomb fell in Hyde Park that a visitor must take somebody's word for it, plus the evidence of shattered buildings, that a bomb did fall. 

And there are bright flowers in many empty cellars of London which have no urban business there.

A reporter with more imagination than science in his system made much of these flowers some time ago, saying they sprang from seeds dormant under London since the big fire in 1666. I am obliged to differ with him. Once I lived in Woking, not far from London, and knew all these flowers, including the fireweed, to be growing there in my boyhood. It would be wise to consult the birds and bees, not a newspaper reporter, to learn how they found their way to a roofless cellar in London.

The indestructibility of life is evident everywhere in the wake of a storm of war. 

The crushed ruins of Cassino, in Italy, are hardly visible from the air at high noon, when their broken walls cast thin shad- 

OWS. But bomb craters are a violent green, because there is water there and a scum of life on the water and dreadful things beneath the water to feed new life above. The ruins are ashen gray, the color of the bare mountains surrounding them. Left alone a score of years it may be difficult to find them. Winter and weather will wear down what is left of them; life in their crevices will split them more thoroughly than bombs could do; creepers will crawl over them and trees rise above them.

Life is very tough. A continual discovery of our expedition was that modern war is an inefficient method of destroying people. This is partly because bombs and shells and bullets, as Bernard Shaw dis covered and mentioned during the First 

World War, mostly miss what they are aimed at.

THE LIBERATORS 

Throughout the war in Europe, and even more so since it ended, a formidable tonnage of trophies, souvenirs and junk has been crossing the Atlantic to clutter the homes of the American people. Much of it will end up in the trash barrel or the pawnshop.

It is a soldier's established privilege to help himself to the enemy's weapons and other minor military equipment. Of more doubtful legality is the custom of "liber ating" civilian possessions as souvenirs of war. Its excuse, in the common phrase of the Allied soldier, is that "they asked for it." The Nazis did a lot of liberating before the tide of war turned and washed them back behind their own frontiers.

In a big factory outside Berlin we heard a German tell how the plant had expanded to three times its size and four times its prewar output to supply the needs of the 

Nazi military machine. Now the factory was ruined and idle, but the German complained more bitterly because the Rus sians had stripped every building of its machine tools and shipped them to

Moscow. 

"But where," asked one of us, "did you get the tools to increase your own production?"

It was grudgingly admitted that the tools had been "liberated" by the Nazis. in France and Belgium. 

At an airfield near Riem, in Germany, we came upon hundreds of airplanes gathered for repair and salvage. There was a sound of heavy hammering and I wandered away from our aeronautical experts, studying the fine points of German equip ment, to discover what the noise was about. Two American soldiers stopped hammering as I drew near; then began again when they discovered I was nothing more than a correspondent. 

Literally, they were taking airplanes. apart with a sledge hammer. Object of their destructive energy was the leather covering of gas tanks. "I've sent home enough so far to upholster the livingroom furniture," explained the man with the hammer. "Now I'm working on the dining room."

There were lonelier people plodding the roads all over Germany. They were soldiers out of work, heading for home on the other side of Germany, sleeping where they could at night and stealing or borrowing what food they could find. They were prisoners escaped from bondage, or displaced persons finding their way back to where they came from.

All this had nothing to do with the military occupation of central Europe. 

The elaborate organization of occupying armies is a civilization functioning within and apart from its surroundings. It feeds and supplies itself. It moves about by trucks and jeeps and planes. It has its own mail service, its own telephone system. All around it in Germany and much of France and Belgium and Italy there was, when the war ended, a silence of communications, a paralysis of transportation of every kind, a return to peasant ways and hand-to-mouth existence.

WATCHING THE FIORDS GO BY 

In more ways than one it is unhealthy to live in a thoroughly bombed and burned city like Berlin, even for a few days. There is an unpleasant disease called dysentery; there were a million cases in the Berlin area during our visit and we had our share.

Also the sense of values suffers from keeping company with ruins and wretched people. Cigarettes become precious; money is of no consequence. The man riding a car feels himself the overlord of those who must walk.

Bombed buildings become too commonplace and familiar. It is somehow. shocking to see a building standing straight and square, its walls and doors and windows protecting all its privacies. 

To get away from it all we flew north to Oslo, Norway, though by a devious course. The area of Russian control ex tended nearly one hundred miles west of 

Berlin, for which reason we flew first to 

Magdeburg and then turned north. "What would happen if we flew over the Russians?" somebody wanted to know. "Nothing much," was the answer. "They'd just shoot you down." 

Primarily we went to Oslo to get away from Berlin, but also on the trail of a tall story. There were reports that the 

Germans had built an enormous airport near Oslo, from which to launch bomber attacks against New York City. The airport at which we landed was the other one, as our pilot discovered just in time to bounce out again before he ran out of runway. On the second try he made it, though with nothing much to spare.

From thence some of the party went to see the super-airport, one of the world's largest, with runways heavy and long enough to handle the biggest planes ever built. But nobody there had heard or seen anything of the super-bombers that were supposed to use it. 

A few of us found better things to do.

By courtesy of the people of Oslo, the friendliest people we met in all our travels, we took passage on the Dampskipet Sport, a small steamer which delivers passengers, mail, newspapers and freight to summertime communities scattered around Oslo's harbor. There is no better way to meet the Norwegians at their best. Mostly they are tall and straight and blond and for their playtime employments they wear as little clothing as minimum modesty permits. They were enjoying their brief summer with happy earnestness, as though to make up for long years of semibondage to the 

Nazis. 

They have summer homes and camps on all the shores of the little fiords that indent Oslo's harbor. They go about by sailboat and rowboat; they swim and fish and loaf in the sun. A bronzed boy dove off the little dock as the Dampskipet waited there; water splashed high and deluged a visiting correspondent. Every body laughed heartily, without mischief or malice. The correspondent laughed, mopping ice water out of his hair. It is easy to laugh in Norway in summertime, when the has enemy gone away.

There were few signs of war in Oslo, except where the R. A.F. once tried to hit the Gestapo headquarters and missed a mile. But there were serious scarcities of many things. My pillow for two nights was covered with a crackling paper slip, for lack of linen. Few stores were open for business, because there was so little to sell. Everywhere in the city. there was much sawing and piling of fire wood; outside apartment houses it was stacked twenty feet high. Norway has wood, but no coal; woodpiles must keep the people of Oslo warm through a long, hard winter. "We are very happy to have your peo ple here," said an English-speaking companion on the Dampskipet. "The next best place to home is Norway," said the soldier on duty in our hotel, who had seen a year's service in several parts of Europe. 

From Oslo we went to Copenhagen, in 

Denmark, for no good military reason. A sufficient reason was that Denmark had plenty to eat when the war ended, with slight exceptions. At the airport restau rant a waiter speaks fluent English, as many Danes do. He watched from the window as a dozen British Typhoons took off, circled into formation and flew away. "The R. A. F. pilots come here on any excuse they can think of," he said, “just to eat their heads off." 

For the same reason people would swarm into Denmark from all Europe, if the Danes would let them. But we were fundamen away from home and far better fed than the civilian peoples of Europe. We liked the Danish beer, too, and the schnapps which tasted no better or worse than raw gin. We were well lodged in Paris at La Francia Hotel, on Rue de la Fayette, except that the elevator stuck. It is the nature and disposition of elevators in European hotels, especially in wartime, to quit functioning between floors at the smallest excuse. 

They did it in Paris, Naples and Marseilles, and I have crawled out of better places than a bandbox elevator, somewhat resembling a bird cage, crowded with postwar correspondents and their baggage. 

The hotel in Paris had long been under military management and was feeling fed up and dispirited by the whole thing.

The most significant symptom of its war weariness was the single linen towel allotted to my bathroom. With much care and patience it had been patched in thirty-two places. 

My towel was no exception to a rule.

Other members of the party had towels so much mended that they were more patch than towel. 

The patched and cross-stitched towel was in some degree symbolic of postwar

Paris, worn threadbare by the long presence of plundering enemies. Its shabbiness was echoed a million times on the streets, crowded by people in worn and mended clothes. There were two rather extraordinary creatures among those present at the party. They could not have been seven feet tall, but seemed so in their haughtiness toward the common herd. They were girls attached to the Royal Air Force, whose business was to haul airplanes all over England to any place where planes were needed. They could fly anything on wings, it was said, and in any sort of weather. They wore smart trousered uniforms, took their cognac straight and were slightly terrifying.

The women of Paris were not quite what imagination had painted them, partly because their clothes were mostly hand-me-downs and in part because they tried too hard to do something about it. THE VALLEY OF DEATH 

None of us who saw Italy from the air will speak lightly or skeptically of the battles fought through its mountains and valleys. Probably there are worse places to fight a modern war, but not many in Europe.

The airplane permits a layman to understand in some degree the difficulties of mountain warfare and the pattern of its battles. The soldier in Italy learned the same lesson the hard way; one said confidently he could hold a mountain top, with the right weapons and sufficient ammunition, against two hundred enemies. 

And so, unfortunately, could the Germans backing up toward the Brenner Pass. 

Mountains of any kind are uncom fortable flying companions. Of mountains

I have met, I like the Apennines of Italy least of all. They have sharp edges and no place whatever for an airplane to sit down. The steepness of their sides can be measured from the air by the way in which paths twist and turn to their summits. Their valleys are narrow and there are not enough of them. 

By circling several times we gained understanding of the bitter battle for

Cassino. It is clear from the air that the mountain, topped by the poor fragments of its monastery, barred the way and had to be taken. It overlooks a great area where there are roads; there are almost none in the rocky wilderness on either side that an army on wheels could use.

There was another grim and doubtful battle in the flatlands back of the beach at Anzio, where the landing was not too difficult, but ran soon into desperate peril of being pushed back into the sea. It was touch and go at Anzio, and the advocates of air power believe that the airplane saved the day when light bombers and low-flying fighters blasted the defenders out of their well-placed bastions. 

There was not much to be seen by the untrained eye as we circled above the battlefield. A little river and a few small houses open to the sky. Black holes along the low cliffs and gray spots of concrete where guns once glared at every square yard of the beachhead. Some shallow and twisting trenches; two or three landing craft still lying where they sank. With

But back from the beach is a half circle of forbidding mountains. every mile they won the invaders came closer to murderous fire from above them on the mountain sides. And a beach has no shelter-nor farmlands and orchards and vineyards back of the beach-from artillery aimed by precise mathematics to hit any spot on a well-made map. 

As a part of this journey we enjoyed a brief aerial hang-over above Milan, where Mussolini died and dangled up side-down to prove that crime does not pay. Similarly we circled twice around 

Pisa, viewing the Leaning Tower from all angles and convincing ourselves that it does lean. Nobody in the party could recall that it ever does anything else, and not many tourists can remember much more about the historic city of Pisa. 

To right and left, as we flew the length of Italy, the shapes of towns and cities were seen, found and named on the map, then lost in the fading horizon behind us. This is not a decent way to tour a country called the "home of the arts, where glory's faded smile sheds lingering light o'er many a mouldering pile." But the aerial traveler is worse than the legendary prewar tourist who discovered he was in Florence by consulting a timetable. One of the map-readers would lock up briefly from his pencil and footrule and remark, "Leghorn coming up to the left." Leghorn would come up, whiz by and be gone.

Following the trail by map is one way to pass the tiresome time in an airplane.

It involves watching for railroad tracks, rivers and mountains, and continual argument concerning their identity. It had nothing whatever to do with our safe arrival at a determined destination.

The pilot had already chided some of us for bothering ourselves about courses and safe landings. "I'll get this end of the ship where it's supposed to go," he said. "Guess your end will come along, if you leave it alone."

But it was due to the faithfulness of the map watchers and measurers that we knew later we had flown, in all, a distance of sixteen thousand four hundred and nine miles within a month, in exactly ninety-nine hours and fifty-seven minutes.

Also they told us we had gone one thousand seven hundred and fifty miles by motor vehicle, about one hundred by boat, and enough more afoot to make up nineteen thousand miles.

Perhaps the most apprehensive moments of our air journeys, for a man with an over-working imagination, were those spent above the deadly valleys of the Italian war and the rough crags of the Apennines.

CITIES RUINED WHILE YOU WAIT 

As a teacher of Latin and Roman history I once lectured learnedly to inattentive students on the ancient city of Pompeii, with lantern slides, telling them it was a peephole into the life and culture of twenty centuries ago. It never occurred to me I would ever walk its dusty streets, to find them narrower than I thought and its buildings more crowded.

This may prove the importance of paying attention to what teacher says.

Life is a long lane with many turnings which may lead to surprising places.

Among them are many mentioned in school books. It is by no means a waste of time to learn about them, for some day you may walk into them and find your self groping for knowledge forgotten or never learned.

Pompeii was preserved, as every school boy ought to know, by one of the most extraordinary accidents of all time. We passed the villain of the story on our way, the 4000-foot cinder pile which dumped twenty feet of steaming ashes on the city in A. D. 79. Vesuvius was behaving itself during our visit, though it blew its top in a big way on March 20, 1944. Now and then the giant beneath the mountain puffed lightly on a mild cigar. But a wisp of smoke in a bright blue sky is nothing to worry aboutthough Vesuvius may blow up again tomorrow.

Little gray lizards scramble over the ruins of Pompeii, or bask in the hot sunshine, and no doubt they did so two thousand years ago, when this was a pleasure resort for sailors in search of wine, women and song. The lizards waste no energy worrying about the restless mountain a few miles away. Nor do the people who still live close to the lava beds. Vesuvius erupted last year; it may do so next year. But not this year.

There are disreputable things to be seen in the roofless houses of Pompeii. 

But the strangest sight is of small children crowding the entrance and following tourists through the streets to sell obscene souvenirs and pornographic pictures. They swarm like flies and scream their wares: "Feelthy peectures, signor, only five cigarette!" Among them, at the gate way, move a few quiet nuns with sacred trinkets for sale on behalf of a nearby children's hospital. 

A wandering pilot of the 15th Air

Force sniffed a little contemptuously at the roofless ruins. "A few loads of bombs could do a better job," he said. Somebody tried to tell him of a blazing volcano showering hot stones and steaming ashes on a city of twenty thousand people, sending them headlong down to the sea to escape doomsday. Reference was made to Pliny's story of the catastrophe. The pilot had never read Pliny, nor heard of him. "Bombs are quicker," he insisted. "Cities ruined while you wait that's our motto."

It was somewhat the same in ancient 

Rome. The driver of a jeep looked unconcernedly on the Roman Forum, taking it for granted that this was more bomb damage, of which he had seen too much.

He listened with warming interest to talk of Julius and Augustus Caesar, of 

Nero and Trajan and other characters of long ago. "Guess I'll go back to Indiana and read up about this stuff," he said. "Never heard much about it before." 

Mostly they do not know about it, these young Americans who are collectively the most traveled generation the world has known. Some do not care. In Capri, a while later, we were told that the military authorities provided a competent and scholarly guide to show the place to visiting airmen and explain its long history. 

How many take advan tage of their opportunity? Less than two per cent of those who visit Capri. 

It is not altogether fair to ancient civiliza tion to take Pompeii as its pattern. Which of the ruins made by war might be kept as a memorial of our own? Should it be the hollow shells of 

Hamburg's waterfront, a little town of northern France, a bleak area of emptiness in Hiroshima? Might it be the cathedral at Coventry, or an underground factory at Ebensee or Muhldorf? 

Perhaps the choice should be left to an unpredictable volcano, not the bomber command. What has happened before may happen again. Vesuvius cannot be trusted to stay quiet. But human beings grow used to volcanoes and quit bothering about them. In the crowded streets of 

Naples ragged children play and beg and their elders haggle and quarrel and steal, knowing and caring nothing about Pompeii and not much about Vesuvius.

Now there is atomic energy at large to worry about, but the worrying will not be universal, nor last long. Creatures who can live comfortably on the slopes of an active volcano, in sight of the city it ruined and buried nineteen centuries ago, will not worry about atomic bombs until the next one goes off.

ART IS LONG 

The late Alice Duer Miller wrote that "art is sacrifice and self-control, and who loves beauty must be stern of soul." In 

Florence, probably the greatest artistic treasure house of all Italy, he must also have good walking feet. We stared at great paintings in Florence until they all looked very much alike, and trod its great galleries until we would have swapped the entire Renaissance period for a wheel chair on Atlantic City boardwalk.

In the Pitti Palace was pointed out a brighter patch on the faded tapestry, showing where once hung a painting which the Nazis had borrowed or stolen. when they left Italy. It seemed more remarkable that they had taken only one, leaving acres of precious canvas behind. 

They were careful in Italy, these would-be world conquerors, to do minimum damage to treasures of art essentially owned by all peoples. They could have committed the greatest artistic mayhem of history by fighting for Florence, or insisting that the Allies fight for it. Instead they moved out, destroying every bridge but one across the Arno River, and firing only a few shells into the city when the Americans and British moved in. 

The exception to the destruction of bridges is the famous Ponte Vecchio, the bridge of shops. It was not pure altruism which persuaded the Germans to leave it standing. Heavy military vehicles were not permitted to cross the Ponte Vecchio when we were there, for the good reason that a medium tank or an overloaded truck might have toppled the whole works into the river. 

Florence was emerging cautiously from the shadow of war in the midsummer of 

1945. Sandbag defenses and brickwork around famous statues and monuments were coming down. They could never have saved them from a direct bomb hit; only from the shattering concussion of "near misses." For similar reasons of safety the bronze doors were still missing from the Baptistery, and the Campanile was overlaid with light masonry to a height of twenty feet or more from the ground.

But the Nazis and Fascists considered it legitimate and even respectable to defend them with anti-aircraft guns, so that an Allied plane flying over them might be unpleasantly peppered on the way. The ultimate effect was that Allied bombers and fighters went a long way off course to avoid them.

It is good that they have survived, and their paintings and carvings and statues, to be happy hunting grounds for tourists in a peaceful time to come. It is regrettable, on the other hand, that their most frequent and numerous visitors in recent years are likely to remember their flat feet and fallen arches more vividly than the splendid galleries through which they tramped. Mostly it was not for lack of good intentions. It was a penalty paid for lack of knowledge, which may be an indictment of the theory and practice of democratic education.

Perhaps it doesn't matter much. Art is long and patient, beauty bides its time. There has been ghastly destruction done in Europe in the name of war, but in astonishing degree the finest and most reverent handiwork of men has survived the storm. There is one painting missing from the Pitti Palace, and maybe more.

The Nazis are said to have stolen it, but it is an extraordinary museum which contains no stolen goods. There are great buildings ruined, but many more that have stood up, battered but not broken, while workshops and homes went down. 

And from the wreckage and despair of war may arise new craftsmanship, born of bitter necessity, to give the world new art and beauty. Men may work again with their hands, lacking machines to do their labor for them. Art is long, and very patient with the blunders of mankind.

PLUSH-LINED FOXHOLE 

As we flew offshore from Venice a column of water sprang suddenly from the sea, seemed to hang lazily in midair and fall slowly back. It might have been a spouting whale, if any whale could throw water 300 feet into the air. The pilot banked the plane to take a look and then thought better of it. Somebody down there had detonated a big mine beneath the sea. Later, on the beach of the Lido, we found signs warning all bathers to go no farther than 50 yards offshore, since a lot of mines were still waiting their turn to be put out of business. 

We landed on an undersized airport, missing by inches a low wall intended to keep the Adriatic off the runways. Cars were waiting to take us to the Villa Regina, on the Lido, where we lived in style during our stay in Venice. 

The 31 hotels on the 10-mile length of the Lido were all rest camps for Allied military organizations of many nations. 

Biggest of all was the Casino, costing more than $2,000,000 before the war and once the playground of millionaires, but now a club for enlisted men, entertaining about 1,200 a day. Across the water, in 

Venice itself, men of many nations lived in hotels and private villas, taking holiday from the tiresome work of war and the poor peace that followed war in Italy. 

A full moon was provided for our visit to Venice. Our party made merry

On a hot morning I was up at 6 o'clock to sit outside the Villa Regina and enjoy a quart can of orange juice, chilled all night in the ice box of the bar. There started about 6 o'clock an amazing pro cession which puzzled us at first. By thousands the people of Venice, mostly women and children, came by ferry across the water from the crowded alleys of Venice, strolled across the Lido and sat all day on the beach. At twilight they went back again. 

It was simple to call them lazy and shiftless, as many visitors did. But where would they work and what would they do if they should turn industrious? A man or woman may be lazy by nature, or because of the climate, but also because there is nothing to do. 

We did not do a great deal ourselves, for that matter, except enjoy ourselves in the unaccustomed luxury of our plush-lined foxhole.

The hospitality of the rest camps, which were first invented and set up in 1943 at Agadir, French Morocco, will be among the lasting and pleasant memories. of our travels. They were designed to provide a change of scene and subject for men bored by the business of war. 

An American soldier, officer or enlisted man, can count on one day of rest in every ten and may ac cumulate his days off until he has a week or more to spend in one of Europe's playgrounds. While he is their guest he gets the best there is-soft beds and clean sheets, good food and service, a wide choice of entertainment. the Villa Regina, for a symbolic example, a seven-piece orchestra played all evening long for our amusement, and cost us nothing except an occasional compliment to the musicians and a few cigarettes. Working On The Railroad 

A man may live and learn if he keeps moving. By flying through it twice we found out that the Brenner Pass is not a neat notch in a mountain wall, like the Delaware Water Gap, nor a tunnel to be bottlenecked by dropping a bomb on it now and then. The Pass is a hundred miles of valley winding through the Alps, and sixty of these miles the fighting and bombing pilots considered the worst of it.

The job done by the Army Air Force in Italy was so big and complex that there is some unfairness in speaking particularly of any part of it. In 1944, for a few statistics, the 15th Air Force alone flew 159,028 sorties, dropped 213,185 tons of bombs, hit marshalling yards 65,113 times and knocked out 3,557 enemy aircraft. The job done in the Brenner Pass was some of this story, but only a little of it.

We saw less than a little of it, mainly because our pilot was a sensible fellow and did not turn the airplane upside down to allow us a better view. Most of the time the vital railroad through the Pass was beneath our plane, though now and then the pilot tipped his wings so that we might look down on one of the toughest aerial targets of the war. This was on a pleasant midsummer day, but it was men tioned to us that much of the Battle of Brenner Pass was fought in winter, with the long valley roaring with turbulent winds and deceiving shadows patching the snow-covered mountains on either side. And on the tops of many of them, to make matters more unpleasant, the Germans had a lot of guns and their concrete emplacements were still there to see as we went by. A medium-bomber pilot, while working on the railroad, might find himself under gunfire from overhead as he threaded his perilous pathway through the mountains. 

It was difficult to knock out the rail roads in the Brenner Pass, as it was every where. Also the Germans were very ingenious in mending tracks and bridges when the bombs broke them. One smart trick was to leave a steel bridge lying where it was smashed and start a show of building another at some distance away. 

But new tracks were laid beneath the wreckage of the old bridge and traffic

The story was told of a flier who thought he knew better than the map. About midway, going northward, the valley divides and the branch to the left is wide and green; that to the right is narrow and forbidding. The road to the right is the right road, and the map said so. But the pilot believed the left valley would be the easier way. Too late he found himself hopelessly hemmed in, unable to climb out or turn around, and crashed his bomber into the cliff that barred his way. 

Great honor is due to those who flew and fought to close the Brenner Pass. 

The battle was won, finally, when not a train could crawl through the valley for days. Then the Germans gave up in northern Italy, starved out of food and weapons.

THE GENERAL'S BED 

"Which of you is the senior officer?" inquired Colonel Carl Woodward, perfect host and head man of the A. A. F. rest camp at Capri.

Some of us may have remotely resembled officers, but none could claim the title honestly. "I mean," explained the Colonel, "who is the oldest member of your party?" 

With a single voice and the finger of scorn I was awarded this doubtful distinction. "Very good," said the Colonel. "You will sleep in the General's bed."

It appears that General Henry H. 

Arnold slept well and comfortably while he stayed in the Villa Vismara, perched high on the cliffs of Capri above the 

Lighthouse Rocks. He had a six-room. apartment to call his own, and so did I. 

There was a mosquito net to thwart ravenous insects who dined all night on other members of the party. There was a little breeze from the sea and a cooling shower bath on the terrace beyond the bedroom. 

Many notables have been to Capri since the war began. In the guest book at Villa Vismara there are the names of dozens of Generals and of Winston 

Churchill. There are high compliments and tender sentiments. My own contribution was a phrase of doubtful Latin: "O insula beata, ave atque vale." Meaning to say that I hope to see Capri again before I die, for there are few lovelier places in all the world for travelers, unless they be the kind that cannot live without golf.

This was the Island of the Sirens, where Ulysses avoided shipwreck and a fate worse than death by resisting the song of the sirens. We saw no sirens except Jinx Falkenberg, who admits she cannot sing. Wearing a bathing suit that was in the main a coat of tan, she had no need to sing.

We went to swim from the beach of the Marina Piccola, a beach of crippling pebbles and no sand whatever. But the water was incredibly clear and it was glorious fun, and later there was a rub-down by a mas seur whose sole business is to keep the uniformed customers of Capri happy and healthy. We circled the tiny island by motor boat and shouted and swam in side the Blue Grotto. 

We ate magnificently and too much. We went shopping on the steep little street and the square which is the heart of Capri, and got stuck for souvenirs and silver bells and picture postcards, like a million tourists before us. 

No damage of war has been done to Capri. A cynical explanation was that the Germans owned many properties on 

Capri and hoped to use them when they had won the war. It was admitted that the military management had its anxious. moments when the island was crowded with officers of the Army Air Force on holiday, the pick of the crop, and might therefore have been considered a military target. 

Late on our last night in Europe I smoked on the little terrace outside the bedroom where once a General slept. 

Three hundred feet below lay the calm 

Mediterranean and there were lights scattered on the sea. There were bright stars in the sky; twinkling windows on the slopes of Capri's little mountains. 

Stars on the sea, on the land, in the sky.

The lights on the water, it was explained, were signs of fishermen at work. 

It is an old Capri custom to knock the bottom from a bucket or oil can, cement a pane of glass in its place, lower it in the water beside a fishing boat and insert a light. The fish come around to learn the cause of the illumination and get caught for their curiosity. They also complete a perfect picture to end a perfect day. 

Next morning we swam briefly again in the blue Mediterranean, were hurried across the Bay of Naples and out to the airport, where we waited an hour or two for a doubtful tire to be replaced on an airplane. This was on Tuesday afternoon. By way of Algiers, Oran, Casablanca, the Azores, Newfoundland and 

Newark, N. J., I was home to dinner in 

Philadelphia, U. S. A., on Thursday evening.
