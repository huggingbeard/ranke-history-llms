B. THE REPRESSIVE ORGANIC ERA 

The United States, with their relatively short histories, have, with the passage of time, been witnesses to the partial validity of Rousseau's analysis. The government of England which administered the 

Colonies prior to the Revolution was both in theory and practice repressive. As Joad has written, "The Idealist or Absolutist theory of the State forms an integral part of the great tradition of philosophical Idealism which, until quite recent years, may be said to have been the dominating influence in English political thought."l 

Anchored in an Aristotelian and Platonic concept the state represented a self-sufficing entity identical with the whole of society and endowed with a mystical importance of its own. As Burke asserted, God had willed the state as the original and natural environment for the social and political animal, man. Any suggestion of artificiality aboutt the state, such as might follow from Social Contract Theories, having been cast aside, the state stood as an indissoluble entity with ends peouliar to itself. In the terminology of the Gestalt psychologists the state became a "configuration or Gestalten" a whole far different from the mere sum of its parts. Just as water has qualities and characteristics not to be found in its uncombined elements, hydrogen and oxygen, the state in the pre-Revolutionary concept was an entity with a raison d'etre and ends not to be found in the mere constituent elements. 

Certain definite and inevitable corallaries followed from this basic hypothesis of the state as a necessary, natural, and God-willed organism. Primarily, the state being regarded as representing and containing within itself all the aspirations of the social animal and likewise fulfilling all his social needs, whatever claims upon the individual

the state might deem necessary for its own preservation took the color of absolute authority. For following the reasoning of Burke the state became the creator of individual liberty. Restraint within the state gave birth to "real" freedom due to the fact that the state organism objectified in its General Will all that was highest and most moral in the will of the individual. In the state, to use Hegel's language, man had fully raised his outward self to the level of his inward moral self. 

This real freedom, a product of and existing in society, manifested itself primarily in the laws those externalized expressions of the General Will which was but the objectification of the best moral judgment and conscience of the reasonable social being. Thus man being assumed a reasonable being provided with a will and conscience the state became the embodiment of the common reason and conscience of its members. Each individual finds his better judgment and will, strengthened and widened, returning to him as an external ruler in the form of the state and its laws.

Being

Immediately, with such a concept, the existence of the state becomes paramount, for the state ceases to be in any sense artificial. part of one's own mental life is carried on in the life of the state. 

The state wills what each individual, assumed as he is to be "incarnately reasonable," should will for himself in his most rational moments. then a real individual endowed with the tasks of guarantor and creator of liberty and personality in the citizen the state becomes not only an end in itself but likewise possesses rights of its own which necessarily override any apparent conflict with the misguided egocentric "rights" of the individual. In view of the original premises no individual, of course, possessed any recognized rights which might conflict with those of the 

State and its laws.

Even to the most casual observer it must be clear that the practical outgrowth of such a web of theory could of necessity be only repressive. The State objectifying all that is highest in man as a rational animal, any command of that sovereign entity becomes absolute law to the individual, based as it is on his own best will. The State or Law representing in itself the keystone of the state the Common Conscience or the Highest Will must by reason of its God-given nature and purpose know no end of existence. It must be preserved whatever the cost may be to any one or small group of individuals whose actions seem contrary to its well-being. 

In the final analysis this organism that we have chosen to call the Repressive-Organic State derives its form and substance from laws-written or customary theoretically representing the highest judgment 

of each individual. As Law in language and effect must, in the last analysis, be regulative and negative, so fundamentally the Repressive Theory so long dominating in England predicated and gave form to a rule of law regulative, negative, and devoid of all individualization. 

Where all men are judged by nature to be reasonable and possessed of a moral will by birthright, there all men must suffer alike when by choice they fail to follow their common conscience.

Before observing where this repressive concept of the organic state failed in theory and practice and how the Preventive Organic State differs from it we must view our second phenomenon the Era of Individualism.

ERA OF INDIVIDUALISM 

Trends in political thought and changes of government, like all phenomena having their origin in men's minds, can never be said to have begun or ended at any specific point in time. Intellect and its ideas are fundamentally evolutionary. The most one is able to do is to recognize certain peaks or points of climax in the progress of any idea or institution, and this only after the evolutionary cycle is completed. 

Only vague and uncertain tendencies are apparent in viewing contemporary movements.

In the history of Anglo-American political theory then, we may safely say that the dominant trend of influence in the Colonies prior to the Revolution was rooted in the Repressive Organic concept. Nevertheless the evolutionary cycle which had brought the concept to the fore had already passed its zenith. The Magna Carta and the later Bills of 

Rights as well as the Revolution of 1688 revealed that Englishmen resented and distrusted domination by the repressive entity, the State, and demanded specific protection for the individual as against the encroachment of absolutism.

The citizen of the Repressive Organic state had already observed the wide gap between its theory and its practice. In theory, no action by an agent of the State could be anything but representative of the rational will and judgment of the individual. Liberty and law are under concept identified, so that every law and every punishment for its infraction but further enhanced the "freedom" of the citizen. 

That practice failed to bear out theory was evidenced by the long development of certain customary and written safe-guards clearly contradicting any notion of coincidence of interests between state and individual. 

It may be said then, that though the Repressive Organic concept of

the state was dominant in the England which ruled the Colonies, there had been throughout that Colonial period the undercurrents of Individualism, feeble though they were. Incapable as we are of specifically recognizing the beginning of any intellectual movement it can only be said that at least after 1215 the concept of individualism was taking root and developed as a minor tendency through the years with the passage of the numerous Bills of Rights in England. 

It was this smoldering undercurrent of Individualism 

the inevitable reaction against the paradox of theory and practice under the Repressive-Organic concept that burst into flame with the Revolution in the Colonies. 

Repression in government as in any other field of man's mental and physical life antagonized and warped responses. The result was, and by force of circumstance had to be, an excessive over-reaction to the stimulus unreasoning repression 

an equally unthinking individualism.

With independence gained, the prime cause for radicalism was removed and, as always, a new and more conservative group of thinkers came into prominence. Practical problems of social order, of provision for financial systems, and of stable commercial and economic development brought forth not philosophers but men of business and property. These newer leaders as a group had a lesser distrust of the old English system of class rule and strong centralized government. As men of wealth and business they had determined to reconstruct the political system in such a fashion as to make it subserve their permanent interests. 

With an eye for business the desired end became a strong government, protective of business, tempered by the dominant desires of Individualism and the complacency of laissez-faire, yet in any case a government much stronger than the radical Confederate institution.

Yet, although it is impossible in our limited space to analyze the ebbing and flowing of the tides of political thought, one must not imagine that the individualistic influence faded quickly with the passage of its point of climax. Such was not the case, for conservative aristocrats though many were even this moderate Constitutional group was to a degree jealous and distrustful of all government. Demacracy in many circles was feared perhaps most of all. Excesses and their revolting effect upon the conservatism of the moderate leaders had resulted in a hyper-distrust of mass-rule. John Adams voiced this widespread feeling when he said, "We may appeal to every page of history we have hitherto turned over for proofs irrefragable that the people when they have been unchecked, have been as unjust, tyrannical, brutal, barbarous, and cruel as any king or senate possessed of uncontrollable power: the majority has eternally and without one exception usurped the rights of the minority."

D. PREVENTIVE ORGANIC ERA 

Having observed two great trends in American political theory from the early Colonial government to the modern day, we must now turn our attention to a yet evolving type of theory which in appearance is very promising. Practical results have made it apparent that neither of the older types of political theory provide sufficient bases for satisfactory governmental activity in the modern state. Modern society must of necessity have strangled under philosophies either of repression or of negativism, for at heart both are typically non-analyzing and devoid of progress. Both theories deal with man not as a progressive being subject to definite laws of nature but rather as a static phenomenon. 

Primarily then, the Preventive Organic theory of the state differs from the Repressive theory in its attitude toward man himself. The latter upon no reliable basis predicates man to be an inherently rational being capable of recognizing his highest moral will when it is embodied in the laws of the state. Every act contrary to law is then an infraction of the higher human morality and is punishable as such. Analysis of cause for the action becomes unnecessary; an evil will is a perversion of human nature. The offender becomes something less than a man, and the stability of the state is threatened. Clearly will implies a choice and every man, being by nature endowed with a conscience and the capacity to will the good by selection, becomes responsible for all his actions.

CHAPTER II. THE POLICE 

What is the average man's conception of the policeman's job?

Most of us probably picture to ourselves that somewhat traditional officer that is seen strolling rather importantly up and down the streets, with no particular aim, swinging his club with a certain degree of men- It is known, of course, that he sometimes makes an arrest, and once in a while a brave deed he has done is read of in the paper, or some allegation of graft. It is known too that there are detectives, but the general idea of detective work and methods is a bit vague, and is apt to have been formed more from absorbing books or films than from the actual performances of flesh-and-blood detectives. Actually our opinion of the police and their work is uncommonly low. The popular conception of the entire police administration gives emphasis to its brutishness, its bungling ignorance, and its entangling alliance with political dishonesty.

No one hesitates to find fault, and certainly every one is ready to believe anything bad about the "cops."

In June of 1931, a Crime Prevention Bureau was established in the police department of the city of New York by an amendment to the Greater 1 New York Charter. A truly correctional penal system must be greatly handicapped unless adequate ability and skill be available for its operation. The same personnel problem exists if we are adequately to solve the all important problems of scientific classification and intelligent follow-up work through capable parole units. 

The greatest danger with which we are faced in the development of a preventive penology is that of extremism. This tendency, as we have seen, is a common heritage of all political theories. Indeed the problem of crime, complex in the highest degree, demands that we proceed with caution. We must experiment widely. Happily such experimentation is appearing. We cannot afford to move blindly or dogmatically nor must we follow doctrinaire enthusiasts before subjecting their every proposal to searching analysis and even trial. 

Massachusetts apparently is beginning to see the folly of undertaking large scale developments in penology on a basis of enthusiasm rather than upon one of experimentally proven methodology. Her latest peno-correctional innovation at Norfolk Prison is undoubtedly too unwieldy for experimental purposes and too large for a successful program of inmate reclamation.1

CHAPTER IV. EPILOGUE

CHAPTER IV. 

EPILOGUE 

It is probably a sound generalization to observe that the continued activities of sane men are anchored at some point in a general theory. None of our functions even the most momentary are devoid of aim, and the aim itself necessitates some theory of activity in the thinking animal. Often, to be sure, the aim as well as the theory evolves so slowly that the element of activity itself comes to appear Ny as causative af aim and theory. This is true as an appearance only however, for all activity of a voluntary and continuous nature is but derivative. Too often indeed, diverted by the apparency of mechanical schemes and motions we as a nation of citizens and legislators fail to take stock of our purposes. In that common failure to discriminate intelligently between aims and activities lies one of the greatest dangers of any government. Action undertaken without a systematic prior definition of theoretical purpose can never lead to anything but confusion, misunderstanding, and waste. 

We have attempted in these pages somewhat to reconcile and explain in terms of governmental theory the seemingly impossible tangle of conflicting practice and activity in our American police departments and prison systems. It has been difficult in so short a bit of writing to keep within restrictive limits and yet give some indication of the tendencies which we believe to be present and at work. The problem of crime and the very necessary analysis of punishment are of too vast importance today to be treated so cursorily. It is in the minds of many of us the greatest problem of the modern state. An immediate and thorough reinvestigation of the aims of the state and the adaptation of the means which the state is now utilizing to those ends is imperative.

The unfortunate taint of repressive tradition clinging to the state must of necessity be long a difficult barrier in any attempt to remold the offender's attitudes.

In a nation necessity demands the solution of the crime problem. 

The attack is being carried on from many fronts and an abundant literature has arisen seeking reform of our legal machinery and our bar, reenforcement and strengthening of our police units, universal finger printing and myriads of other scattered panaceas. Zealous and sincere though these movements may be, the utter lack of any definition of aim is rendering them hopelessly ineffective. We cannot at the same time institute measures of increased social revenge and realize the fundamental fact of reformation. Treatment cannot be both vengeful and preventive.

It cannot be denied that suppressive activity by the state must be continued and perhaps be made even more effective in dealing with certain types of irreclaimable offender. The point which we are making is that a preventive program necessarily must utilize suppressive measures only as a final resort after curative attempts have failed.

The end which we seek is the safety of society and suppressive measures must be incidental to that end only and not colored, as they too frequently are, by the shades of unthinking social hysteria. 

Indeed it is discouraging in a degree to observe how slowly the repressive ideology is yielding to the entering wedges of national preventive concepts. Yet it is undeniable that despite the momentary feebleness of any sort of intelligent preventive activity the time is not far off when a whole-hearted redefinition of our theories of the state and of man will be undertaken. Already the evidences are at hand which must eventually force the correlation and redirection of our efforts.
