CALMNESS, CONFIDENCE AND COMMON SENSE 

Why do officials keep repeating “Be Calm”?

Because when you keep calm, your mind functions, you remember to do the right thing, to use common sense. When you are excited your mind is dizzy, and you either forget what to do, or you do crazy, dangerous things.

Set an example to your family and neighbors of strength, sympathy, good morale. 

Toward that end you must have confidence and knowledge. 

Have confidence in our leaders: in our Army, our Navy, our Air forces. We have airplanes on constant watch, detectors constantly listening, anti-aircraft guns on constant duty. We have the best minds in the country constantly devising the best means of defense.

However, air raiders may break through, just as in England. Therefore, be prepared against air raids.

Have knowledge: what to do; how to do it. Certain precautions and protections can be prepared in advance - now. This book tells you, simply and clearly, what you need to know in order to save life and protect property — as much as is humanly possible.

The Ultimate Protection lies with God.

DETECTORS, SIGNALS AND ALARMS 

There is a comprehensive twenty-four-hour detector service on duty on our seacoasts.

As soon as enemy airplanes are detected, a signal (in New York City, a yellow light) is flashed to Police Headquarters. However, no alarm is sounded over the City at this first signal. Why not? Because the planes are still distant, maybe a hundred miles or more, and our air-force may succeed in dispersing them. 

If the enemy planes continue to approach, — when they are within ten minutes' distance, a Red Signal is flashed to the Police, and the Alarm Sirens are sounded over the City.

The signal in New York is a long blast followed by a short one; this combination is continued as long as there is danger. When the danger is past, the 

All-Clear signal is given, consisting of a succession of short whistles. Radios also give warning.

The same system of signals will eventually be used throughout the country. In the meantime, acquaint yourself with the signals in your community. They are subject to change.

Note Well: the Alarm signal means imminent danger. It means that your mind must begin to function calmly, quickly, correctly. • ៤៩

WHY YOU MUST KEEP OFF THE STREETS AFTER AN ALARM 

You MUST keep off the streets. Why?

In the streets you have no protection against bombs, nor against shell splinters. 

Splinters are exploding pieces, from bombs or from our own anti-aircraft.

You may be struck by falling walls or by shattered glass. 

Streets Must be kept clear for police cars, fire engines, rescue squads, and for our own Troops.

It has been England's experience that the greatest percentage of injuries in air raids is among people in the streets. 

Don't be so foolish as to stand in the street or at a window gaping up into the sky, looking for enemy planes. You won't be able to see them, because daylight raider-planes usually fly about twenty thousand feet high, to keep out of easy range of anti-aircraft guns. If an enemy flier should swoop low enough for you to see him, it would probably be his suicide attempt at a definite objective, and it probably would be the last bit of seeing you'd ever do.

Keep away from windows because glass shatters easily, flies in all directions, and causes serious injuries. 

Keep off the streets to avoid danger from poison gas attacks. 

In short - keep off the streets! -6-

WHERE TO GO AND WHAT TO DO 

When the Air Raid Alarm sounds, STAY HOME. If you are within five minutes of home, walk - do not run - home. If you are too far from home, walk into the safest-appearing nearby building, and ask for their refuge spot. It will be a spot in the center of the building, away from windows and doors, away from elevators, away from outside walls.

If you are driving, park your car at once near the curb, and walk into the nearest suitable building.

Keep automobile headlight and tail-light masks available, made of dark cloth or rubber. Each mask should have a horizontal slit in the lower part, covered with blue cellophane. 

Do not smoke.

If you are caught in an open field lie face down, flat, in a hollow or ditch. A bomb exploding in a field will blast and throw fragments up in cone fashion; if you are face down on the ground you are protected. 

If you are at home, go to your Refuge Room.

If you are in an apartment house, or in an office building, the Superintendent or Manager or Air Raid Warden has instructed you where to go. Obey their orders. 

They have had expert advice and know better than you do. The tall steel structure buildings are safe.

If you are caught out at night, and the city has been blacked out, keep to the right, near the buildings. Do not strike matches. You will see an air raid warden on duty. He has a flashlight which he is operating beam down. Ask him to conduct you to the nearest refuge spot. Remain calm and obey his orders. He is trained for just such an emergency.

Obey his orders especially as to where to park your car. In heavy traffic areas streets must be kept open for police cars, fire engines, and rescue squads. 

If you are prepared with the knowledge that this book aims to give you, you will know what to do: for yourself and for your neighbors. Obtain this foundation of elementary knowledge about air raids — their methods and the correct methods of defense, well in advance of any need. It is too late to begin studying the technique of air raids and of civilian defense while the air raiders are bombarding your community; perhaps your own home. -7-

PREPARE A REFUGE ROOM 

If you live in a private house, make the following preparations as soon as possible: 

Choose a room on the middle floor in the central part of the house, away from windows. 

Place a big strong table in the refuge room. The purpose of this table is to protect yourself under it from falling plaster, etc. You can reinforce the table at the sides with mattresses, boxes of sand, or upholstered furniture. Place blankets on the floor for comfort.

Keep necessary supplies on the table; such as candles, candle holders, matches in a tin box, flashlights, handkerchiefs, bandages, hot water bottles, spirits of ammonia, hard candy, cans of fruit juices, toys for small children, books, ointment for burns, cans of food. A complete list of supplies will be found on the front inside cover of this book. 

Keep pails of dry sand and pails of water in the room. You will need these to fight fire and incendiary bombs.

Keep an Indian Pack pump or a Stirrup pump within reach; also a long-handled shovel, an axe, a rake, rubber gloves, and metal-rimmed goggles. The chapter on Bombs explains their uses. 

Pay special attention to your attic, because that is the most vulnerable spot when bombs fall. Remove rubbish and inflammable material from the attic. Remove heavy pieces of furniture; there is danger that they may fall through to the floor below. Place a quantity of sand in the attic; also a long handled shovel, a rake, and a hatchet. If possible, spread sand several inches thick over the floor. Do the same in the garage. The chapter on Bombs explains the reasons. Use a Make blackout curtains for those rooms where light may be necessary. heavy black material that will not allow a light to shine through. Cut it at least twenty inches longer and twenty inches wider than your window. That will give you an extra ten inches on all four sides, which is needed to prevent light from shining out. Nail the top and the bottom of the cloth to a stick; carpet tacks will do. Tie a cord to each stick, and hang the shade by the cord on a nail hammered into the wall. Remember the ten inch lap. Drive a nail below the window to hold the shade firm. See the illustration.

When the blackout shade is not in use, roll it up and stand it in a corner near its window.

Learn how to turn off your gas and electricity; and how to turn it on again. Gas pipes and electrical equipment may be damaged by bombs. To prevent asphyxiation, explosion, or fire, gas and electricity should be turned off during an air raid. -9-

Keep a portable radio, to use when the electric current is turned off. 

Have toilet facilities in the room. If the water supply fails, place sewage and rubbish into a paii and throw chloride of lime over it. 

Take down glass pictures and mirrors. Remove fragile bric-a-brac, glass and chinaware to a safe place.

Tape your windows criss-cross, over glass and frame both, to prevent glass from flying. Inside screens, shades, and venetian blinds help. Adhesive tape, bicycle tape, even strips of strong paper and cloth can be glued to the window-panes. 

One of the best protections is cheese cloth glued over the entire pane. Heavy draperies or blankets may be hung over the windows. Flying glass is responsible for much damage and injury.

The probability is that you have almost everything you need at home - don't rush to stores to buy unnecessarily. 

In certain types of small houses, where a well-constructed cellar has several exits, the cellar may be the safest Refuge Room in an air raid.

IF THE ALARM COMES AT NIGHT 

Keep calm. Don't lose your head, because then you will be running around in circles and doing the wrong thing. 

Put on warm clothing. 

Hang your blackout shades before you turn on any lights.

Escort children to the Refuge Room and make them comfortable with toys or books. Lay them on blankets under the Refuge Table. Turn on the portable radio. 

If you have pets, put them into boxes or traveling baskets, with food. 

WHEN THE BOMBARDMENT BEGINS 

Turn off the gas and electricity.

Find your way about with a flashlight, turning the beam down, toward the floor. 

Open the windows; that helps prevent concussion damage. 

Now all get under the table, lying flat on the floor, face down, with head and as much of the body as possible protected by the table and its reinforcements, as explained in the last chapter. 

Be sure to lie flat, face down. In that position you feel the blast least, and you will more easily escape glass, fragments, or splinters. 

If there are heavy bomb detonations, cup your hands over your ears, and keep your mouth open by placing a rolled handkerchief between the teeth. The concussion may deafen you. 

If you are trapped in the house, keep cool. Blow your whistle, and rescue squads will reach you. In the meantime, use your common sense. A little work with an axe will probably clear you.

This is a good place to remind you to join a Civilian Defense Class for training as Air Warden, Fire Warden, or in Rescue work. What you learn will enable you to help yourself and to help others. -11-

TYPES OF BOMBS-WHAT TO DO 

It is not the purpose of this booklet to give a technical description of bombs. Here is as much as the layman needs to know: 

There are two kinds of air raids. 

1. TOKEN RAIDS, whose purpose is to scare the wits out of people; to break their morale. 

2. AIR RAIDS with MILITARY OBJECTIVES; that is, to destroy or cripple fortifications, barracks, airports, manufacturing or supply systems, and ships.

Bombs dropped on military objectives are heavy, high explosive bombs, weighing from 100 to 2,000 pounds. Their destructive powers are twofold: 

1. A heaving, mining, blast effect, with a demolition force whose results resemble the work of an earthquake and a tornado. 

2. A splintering effect: the bomb cases splinter into fragments which fly in all directions with a speed and force several times greater than that of bullets. These fragments can penetrate and destroy houses for several blocks - if the concussion has not already done so. 

There is a smaller edition of the explosive bomb, weighing from 5 to 50 pounds each, used against trucks, airplanes on the ground, as well as against groups of people.

If you find what you think is an unexploded bomb, keep away from it. It may be a time-bomb. A time-bomb is set to explode after it drops. Notify the fire department or the police at once. An untrained person does not know what to do against such bombs.

Go to your Fire House and enlist as a Fire Warden. The training and informayou will receive are extremely valuable. tion

GAS BOMBS 

Gas masks are being manufactured in large quantities and will soon be distributed. There is no advantage to be gained by the enemy in gassing a city that is prepared and organized for gas defense.

All war gases are not true gases. They may be liquids or solids, dissolved. They do their damage through the eyes, the lungs, or the skin. 

Here are the most important gases: -13-

TEAR GASES irritate the tear glands; but the irritation ceases as soon as you leave the gassed area. A good pair of goggles will give ample protection.

There is a group of IRRITANT GASES, usually arsenic compounds, which require a good gas mask. These gases may be invisible, or of a distinct color. 

Breathed in without a mask, they cause headache, vomiting, and dysentery. Recovery, however,is complete after 24 hours. 

A very serious type is the LUNG-IRRITANT GAS. A mask gives full protection. 

The most dangerous of all war gases is MUSTARD GAS. It is really a heavy brown oily liquid, smelling like garlic. It may be dropped in bombs, or sprinkled in drops, or sprayed so fine as to be a vapor. 

Its danger is two-fold: to the lungs, and to the skin on contact. It penetrates ordinary clothing, causing severe skin blisters and wounds similar to deep burns. 

It evaporates slowly, therefore remaining on the ground for days or even weeks. 

Stay indoors until the Decontamination Squad has cleared the ground. The gas is neutralized by chloride of lime. If your skin has been exposed, wash it off immediately with soap and water; or with gasoline, followed by soap and water. 

Remove your clothing to be sure that none has penetrated.

Men working in a gassed area wear a mask, boots, clothes, gloves, of an impervious material, like rubber.

Should gas bombs fall, go at once to an inside room in your house; that is, the one with the fewest windows and doors. Stuff rags in window cracks and under doors. The upper floors are safest when the gas is of the heavy variety that settles to the ground. 

A gas mask for every man, woman and child.

INCENDIARY BOMBS 

INCENDIARY BOMBS are most frequently employed in Token Raids, because they are the most effective, and the easiest to use. They weigh from two to four pounds each, and many hundreds can be carried by one plane. 

They can penetrate the roofs of private houses. They burn with intense heat- 

3300 degrees Fahrenheit - for fifteen minutes; and if unchecked, one bomb can up a house. burn One plane can drop thousands of incendiary bombs and lay waste an entire community.

However, it is not necessary to become panic-stricken in the face of incendiary bombs, for they can be checked.

We shall tell you first what NOT to do, because your first impulse will be to throw a pail full of water, or a stream of water, on the bomb. That is a very dangerous thing to do, for the following reason:

Incendiary bombs are composed largely of magnesium, a metal which burns fiercely in water. A stream of water will explode the bomb, and instead of one fire in one spot you will have many fires spread over a wide area; and one of the fiery pieces may strike you.

The easiest way to put out an incendiary bomb is with dry sand. Smother it with sand. Pour sand on the floor, roll the bomb into the sand with a shovel or rake, and shovel the whole mass into a pail of sand. The reason for rolling the bomb in the sand is to prevent it from burning the floor. 

Your attic floor should be covered several inches deep with sand. That will minimize the danger of fire if an incendiary bomb breaks through the roof. 

Examine the floor carefully. It may be necessary to chop away the flooring with an axe to make sure that there is no fire burning underneath. 

Some incendiary bombs explode during the first two minutes. Protect your head and face with a lid of a rubbish can, if you can find nothing better. 

A second way to extinguish an incendiary bomb is to play a misty, delicate, wide spray of water on it for about two minutes. WE REPEAT, DO NOT USE A 

HEAVY STREAM OF WATER, AS THAT CAUSES THE BOMB TO FLARE UP AND EXPLODE. 

- 15 -

In the house a combination of the two methods, sand and water, will usually be necessary, especially if the bomb has exploded and started fires in several parts of the room. If the room is filled with smoke keep close to the floor where the air is clearest.

An incendiary bomb burns with intense heat and glare. Therefore it is advisable to wear metal-rimmed goggles to protect the eyes. Wet the surrounding area, to help prevent fire.

When spraying, lie prone on the floor, and stay about twenty-five feet away from the bomb. The bomb may shoot molten metal as far as twenty-five feet, especially during the first 60 to 90 seconds. After that period of time, you may approach closer.

An Indian Pack Pump, or a Stirrup Pump, can be operated with a few pails of water. These pumps can be bought at hardware stores. Be sure they have a wide spray nozzle.

On the lower floors of the house, a garden hose with a fine spray nozzle can be attached to a faucet.

Keep pails of dry sand and pails of water, a long handled shovel, a rake or hoe, and an axe, on each floor of the house. 

Heavy rubber gloves are an added protection in fighting incendiary bombs. Don't be alarmed if the spray does not put out the bomb at once. The spray makes the bomb burn faster, so that it burns itself out in two minutes instead of in twenty.

Keep the floor wet all around it so that the fire does not spread. 

If you have a soda-and-acid extinguisher (the kind you turn upside down) use it with your finger over the nozzle to make a spray. Do not use chemical extinguishers on an incendiary bomb because poison gases will be generated. They may, however, be used on ordinary fires. -16-

WHAT TO DO TO SAVE LIFE 

A little simple knowledge of First Aid may save the life of someone near and dear to you.

The greatest danger to life comes from wounds or shock, or both combined. The danger from wounds lies in bleeding or infection. 

If a person is injured do not move him, do not handle him any more than is absolutely necessary. See that he lies flat. Do not raise his head with a pillow. Many a seriously injured person has been killed by moving, raising, seating, carrying when he might have had a chance to survive if treated intelligently. 

Is he suffering from shock? Of course you send for a doctor. But you cannot wait till he comes. Shock must be treated immediately, for it often leads to death. The patient is cold, clammy, faint, with pulse feeble and fluttering. 

The first thing to do is to apply warmth. Cover with warm, light blankets. Place hot water bags, bottles, warm bricks, plates, etc., wrapped in cloth, all around him. Be careful not to burn him. If he is conscious, give stimulating drinks by the spoonful: a few drops of spirits of ammonia in a teaspoon of water; or warm tea, coffee, or milk.

If he is unconscious give no drinks for fear of choking him. Let him smell the spirits of ammonia. Try it on yourself first, to be sure it is not too strong. 

Now find out if the shock is caused by a wound that is bleeding. Bleeding must be stopped.

There is one kind of bleeding which you must not try to stop; you cannot stop it; that is, bleeding from the ears and nose, indicating a skull fracture. Do not touch the patient's head. Do not move him.

And here, perhaps, is a suitable place to add a note of warning on saving the mental health of children. Terror has injured more children in Europe than bombs have. Terror and shocked nerves, as is well known, have serious physical consequences. Help children lead normal, unexcited lives. Concentrate on nerve strengthening diets consisting of natural, whole grain foods; fruits, vegetables, milk, eggs.

BLEEDING 

There are two kinds of bleeding: from the veins; from the arteries. 

Bleeding from a cut artery is bright red, and comes out in spurts. Bleeding from an artery is more serious.

Bleeding is controlled by Pressure. If a wound is bleeding, try to stop it at once. 

The loss of three pints of blood may be fatal.

In ordinary bleeding, from the veins, place a pad of sterile gauze over the wound, and press gently but firmly with the fingers for 5 or 6 minutes. That will stop the bleeding. Do not remove the gauze, or you may start the bleeding again.

The gauze helps to coagulate the blood and form a scab. Tie a bandage over the gauze.

If the bleeding comes from an artery, much deeper pressure is needed, because the arteries lie deep; pressure on a spot above or below the wound, depending upon its location. The pressure spot is always between the wound and the heart. 

Sometimes a tourniquet is necessary, but that must be used with good judgment, lest it prove fatal.

To know the location of the pressure spots in arterial bleeding, and the correct use of a tourniquet, buy the First Aid Textbook of the American Red Cross. It is inexpensive. Better still, enroll in the First Aid Course; it is free. 

A wound that is bleeding profusely needs no antiseptic. Much injury is added to wounds by a caustic antiseptic roughly applied. If you feel you must use an antiseptic, pour on diluted peroxide, or boric acid solution. Then apply the gauze pad, and press.

Never press a wound with bare fingers. Always apply a pad first, made of folded sterile gauze or of the cleanest, softest cloth you have. Leave it on; bandage on top of it.

Never give fluids to drink to a patient who is bleeding profusely; they will increase bleeding. Stimulants are not to be given until the bleeding has stopped. 

Treat shock and bleeding together; one is as important as the other. If you have no help use your common sense as to which is of greater importance in that particular case. As a rule, check profuse bleeding first; your ingenuity will enable you to apply a little warmth at the same time. Use warm light covers; the patient cannot stand heavy weights.

There are many things that even little children can be taught to do, to be of help. - 19-

BURNS 

For small surface burns a good commercial burn ointment may be applied on sterile gauze and lightly bandaged into place. 

Deep or extensive burns are dangerous. Shock is usually present. Give plenty of water, small drinks at frequent intervals. While waiting for the doctor, the simplest home remedy is to apply clean linen or sterile gauze several layers thick, which has been soaked in lukewarm baking-soda solution. Add 2 or 3 tablespoons of baking soda to a quart of warm water. Bandage lightly. Cover the patient with soft blankets. 

An Epsom salts solution may be used in the same way. 

FRACTURES 

In case of a broken limb, lay the patient flat, keep him warm; handle him as little as possible, lest you aggravate a simple fracture into a serious compound fracture. 

Send for a doctor at once.

The Red Cross First Aid Text-Book gives full directions. Everyone should take the Red Cross First Aid Course; the instruction is free.

ARTIFICIAL RESPIRATION 

In some cases of shock, as well as in gas poisoning or in electric shock, breathing has stopped. But the victim may be revived by artificial respiration, constantly and faithfully applied. The Schaeffer prone pressure method is the best. Lay the victim flat on his belly. Turn his head and face to the side so that his nose and mouth are free for breathing. Bend his arm at the elbow and place his hand under his cheek. Now straddle his thighs, with your knees on the ground. 

Place the palms of the hands on the small of the back, with fingers on the ribs, the little finger touching the last rib. 

Holding your arms straight, swing forward slowly, pressing on the ribs. That presses the air out of the lungs, and should take about two seconds. 

Now let go and swing backward. Rest for two seconds. Then swing forward again and press again for two seconds. 

It helps to say a little rhyme. As you press down say, "Out goes the bad air”; let go, pause, and say, "In comes the good air." This rhyme sets the proper rate of speed - 12 to 15 times per minute. Too great a speed is harmful. Keep it up for hours if necessary. If you work in relays do not interrupt the rhythm when a new person takes over.

Read the American Red Cross First Aid Textbook. 

MORE PRINCIPLES IN ELEMENTARY FIRST AID: 

1. Do not give alcohol nor any alcoholic stimulant to a severely injured person except on advice of a physician. The indiscriminate use of alcohol is dangerous. 

2. An injured person should be handled as little as possible, and then with extreme care and gentleness. 

3. Make sure that an injured person is able to breathe normally. See whether the mouth and nose are filled with dust and debris. 

4. Do not remove clothing unless absolutely necessary, and then be very gentle. 

If the cloth sticks to a wound do not tear it off; cut around it. 

5. Blisters should not be broken nor punctured. 

6. Give nothing to eat or drink to a person wholly or partly unconscious, nor to anyone suspected of suffering from internal injury. - 21-

TO EVACUATE OR NOT TO EVACUATE THE CHILDREN 

In England's first terror at air raids there was much talk about the evacuation of children; and many children were evacuated from London. But before the year was over most of the children had returned to their parents, and have stayed with them ever since. It was found to be the best way for morale and happiness. 

CHILDREN IN SCHOOLS 

Keep the children in school. Let their lives go on normally, the same as usual, for the sake of their mental and physical health.

Have confidence in the school authorities. The best thought of the best minds is being expended on the problem of how best to safeguard the children. Obey the rules laid down by the school. See that the children obey them. 

If your school sends children home under the escort of the teacher, see that you are at home to receive them. If you must go out, arrange with a neighbor to receive your child in the event of an alarm.

Do not run to the school at the sound of the siren. If parents do that they cause confusion, panic, and clogging of the streets. The streets must be kept open for 

Rescue Services and for troops. Have confidence in your school.

Many cities are planning punishment by prison or fines for those who disobey the orders of the Defense Council. 

Do not telephone to the school during an alarm. The lines must be kept open for signals and emergencies.

Children who live too far away from the school, such as high school students, will be kept in the safest part of the building until the All Clear signal. Be assured that everything necessary is being done for the welfare of the children. 

Set an example of self-control for the children to follow. 

Do not spread rumors. - 23-

A FEW IMPORTANT DO'S 

Do be careful not to spread any information you may have of the location of troops, camps, forts, etc.

Do acquaint yourself with the latest Alarm and All-Clear signals. They are subject to change.

Do be prepared. Arrange in advance a safe place for your family and your pets. 

Make your blackout shades. Tape your windows. Fill pails with dry sand and with water. Attend to your attic. Collect the necessary articles recommended in a previous chapter. 

Remove all inflammable liquids and materials. 

Learn how to shut off and turn on gas and electricity. Be careful of pilot lights; when the gas is turned on again, be sure to light your pilot light. 

Keep your radio on for instructions and information. 

Do keep your eyes and ears open, on the alert against fifth columnists and traitors, against sabotage of any kind. Report to the police at once anything suspicious. 

Do join as many Volunteer Associations for Civilian Defense as you have time for. Apply to the office in your local community. If you have no local central office to co-ordinate the efforts of the various organizations, here is your opportunity to form one. 

There are seven protective services for civilians: 

Air Wardens; Fire Wardens; Aircraft Spotters; Aircraft-warning Service; 

Nurses' Aides; Disaster Canteen; Motor Corps.

A FEW IMPORTANT DON'TS DON'T GO INTO SUBWAYS in an Air Raid Alarm. 

Subways are not safe. They are not deep enough; neither is their roof strong enough to resist demolition bombs. One of the worst tragedies in London occurred when bombs struck a subway and killed eight hundred people at once.

Subways are dangerous in gas raids because heavy gases settle in low places. Over-crowding may in itself cause accidents and panic.

Do not telephone. The lines must be kept open for important messages to doctors, hospitals, rescue squads, wardens; and for signals. 

Do not believe nor spread unofficial rumors. Spreading false rumors is part of the fifth columnist's technique. Don't let him fool you. 

Don't strike a match or light a cigarette if you are outdoors at night during an alarm. It is remarkable, yet true: the light from a match or a cigarette is clearly visible from above. 

Don't be caught napping. Take all precautions in advance. 

Be prepared.

Don't attempt to tamper with street lights or any other public lighting unless you have received full instruction by the proper officials. A few careless or uninformed wardens have turned off traffic lights instead of street lights and caused traffic accidents.

Don't cut off the central gas valve in your house until you have been instructed on how to handle the pilot lights. Tragic explosions can be caused by ignorance concerning pilot lights in various gas appliances. 

Don't experiment with unknown electrical equipment. Be sure you know what you are doing. - 25-

PART II 

In the early part of this book we have given you the fundamental air-protection essentials. In this second part we shall elaborate the important points with greater detail. 

The people of England have made every phase of air raids a matter of deep study because of the sustained and repeated attacks which they have suffered. There is scarcely an undamaged house in the city of London. 

We do not expect repeated attacks in America, but we must remember that one enemy plane can drop thousands of fire bombs and inflict serious damage. It is wise to be prepared. Let us cease imitating the fool ostrich who thinks that if he does not see the enemy they are not there. We have already suffered too many casualties by believing lying tongues, by closing our eyes to the evidence of mountains of armaments being piled up. Maybe they were only "boondoggling" to keep little fingers busy? 

And we laugh at the ostrich!

The bombs dropped by enemy raiders are only half the danger. The other half is our own defending guns, which spray the city with shell fragments. Such fragments after exploding in the air with devastating power, return to earth at almost the same velocity with which they were shot from the guns. The number of falling fragments is enormous, and they fall over a wide area. 

Although a well-constructed cellar offers protection from shell fragments, it is a source of peril from broken water mains and gas mains. 

In business areas the lower floors usually are given over to large open spaces, fronted with show windows. If a bomb explodes in the street, the blast sweeps through these spaces; and fragments of bomb, pavement, and debris rise to the height of the fifth floor, pass through windows into the building. All glass is shattered inwardly to such height.

Therefore a safe refuge would be above the fifth floor. Bombs hitting the roof would not be likely to penetrate more than two floors in modern steel and concrete buildings. Therefore a refuge spot should be at least three floors below the roof.

It is impossible to prevent the shattering of glass except by such drastic measures as blocking the windows with protective walls of sandbags, bricks, or concrete. 

Movable shutters may be used, but a steel shutter capable of resisting fragments of a 500 pound bomb at fifty feet would have to be one and one-half inches thick. 

It is important to have materials on hand for immediate use on broken windows; such as tarpaulin, bituminous sheeting, asbestos-cement sheeting, timber, or corrugated sheet metal.

TREATMENT OF WINDOW GLASS 

Glass MUST be treated to prevent it from flying into the room. There are several ways of doing this. 

1. Screening of one-half-inch mesh wire may be nailed to the sash over the entire window, on the inside. Wire netting of a bigger mesh should not be used. -28-

2. White cheesecloth or similar cloth, such as curtain netting or mosquito netting, may be glued over the inside glass with paperhanger's paste or with flour paste. To make flour paste, mix 2 tablespoons of flour, ½ teaspoon of washing soda, with 3 tablespoons of water. Add 1 cup of boiling water, stir, and heat over a low flame for 10 minutes. Add ½ teaspoon of borax to the paste to prevent mildew. 

3. Another way is to varnish the window first, stick the cloth into it, and when the varnish is dry, apply a second coat of varnish on top. 

4. Large, thick sheets of transparent cellophane paper or waxpaper may be glued to the window.

MORE PRECAUTIONS 

It is safer to be near an inside wall than an outside wall of your room. Avoid being in a direct line with the door or windows. A closed door may be torn off when a bomb explodes.

Keep at all times a supply of water in bath tubs and wash tubs. In order to economize in our present water shortage, keep the used water in the tub after a bath. 

Make sand bags or sand mats of dry sand or ashes.

Let as many members of the family as possible take practical training in methods of fighting fire bombs. The local fire houses give such training. 

Protect your roof and attic by painting walls, ceilings or rafters, and floors with a genuinely fire-proof paint. However, there is no sense in fire-proofing the attic unless you remove all inflammable articles.

MORE ABOUT FIRE BOMBS 

Incendiary bombs are not hurled against military objectives. They are a direct threat to the life of a community, of a country. They must be met not by the feeble attempts of a few people, but by an army of trained fire-bomb fighters. 

In England there is a national fire prevention service which has been organized as Britain's Fire Guard. They have officers, watchers, fighters, special clothing and equipment. Streets are divided into sectors, each with its own Fire Guard. 

Only by such efficient organization can the menace be fought. 

In England the government distributes large numbers of sandbags and sandmats to householders. A sandmat is thinner, flatter, than a sandbag. The sandbag should be about half full of DRY sand, earth, or ashes. Tie the corners to form "ears" for grasping.

The sand should not be emptied out of the bag on to the bomb, but the whole bag, with its sand, should be placed squarely- not thrown on the bomb. Then get away quickly for fear of explosion. 

This method is best used when the bomb falls outdoors. The bomb continues to burn underneath. Therefore, if it falls indoors, - unless it falls on a non-inflammable surface, it must be treated with sand and water both. 

Spray it with water, either from a garden hose or from a Stirrup Pump, as explained in Part I.

The Stirrup Pump is a simple hand pump which is placed in a pail of water and held firm with the foot. The hardware dealer will instruct you in its operation. 

The pump has 30 feet of hose, with a double purpose nozzle that gives either a -29-

fine spray or a strong stream reaching about 30 feet. Use both types in fighting a fire bomb: the fine spray for the bomb itself, the heavier stream for the surrounding fire.

A Stirrup Pump needs only about 6 gallons of water to control a small fire bomb or to put out a fire in an average room. 

The pump is used most effectively by 2 or 3 people. One person holds the nozzle and sprays the bomb and the fire. The second does the pumping. The third person brings fresh supplies of water and acts as general helper. 

Go no nearer to the fire than is necessary. While spraying, protect yourself behind a door or behind furniture, such as an up-ended table. 

Four thicknesses of wet blanket hung over one arm and held in front of the face protect against fire, but not against shell splinters.

Fight inside bombs first, before you tackle those that have fallen in the street. 

TRAFFIC CONGESTION 

A great danger lies in traffic congestion. Wardens should order drivers to park cars near the curb. If streets are filled with standing cars, fire engines, salvage and rescue cars will not be able to get through. 

SHELTER GARAGES 

Contrary to general belief, the best as well as the most practical bomb shelters are above ground, not underground. In a city like New York, for example, digging underground shelters in the streets is impractical. In the suburbs, especially near the waterfront, it is impossible to dig deep enough without striking water. 

Engineers recommend a series of bomb-proof garage buildings to be built in various locations throughout the city, to shelter both people and cars. These buildingse can be used after the war to solve traffic problems. Federal aid for their construction could be obtained as a war measure. A six-story building, a city block square, can afford shelter to at least 8000 people and 1000 cars. 

BEWARE SOUVENIR HUNTING 

Be warned against collecting souvenirs from wrecked enemy aircraft. There may be unexploded bombs. The safe rule is to leave such things where they are. 

RESCUE RULES 

1. Do not use burning halls or stairs when a rescue can be made through the window. 

2. If you are searching a house for people, begin at the top and work down. 

3. Lie down and crawl to avoid smoke and heat. 

4. If you are moving an unconscious person along the floor, lay him on his back, tie his wrists together, straddle him with your knees on the floor, put your head through the loop of his arms; and taking his weight around your neck, crawl forward slowly on your knees and hands. 

5. If you want to move an unconscious person down a flight of stairs, place him face up on the top step, with his head toward the bottom. Support him under the armpits and move slowly backwards down the stairs. 

6. If your clothes have caught fire, place your hands over your mouth, lie down, and roll over and over on the floor. 

7. If someone else is on fire, hold a rug, blanket, or coat in front of yourself, - 30 -

throw the person on the floor, throw the rug, etc., over him and roll him over and over until the flames are put out. 

Doors and windows should be kept closed in a burning building to keep a supply of oxygen from the fire. If necessary to crawl in, keep close to the floor and to the walls.

Eardrums are apt to be injured by blast. Protect them by small pieces of cotton packed loosely into the ears.

If First Aid is being given to a person with a fractured limb, and proper splints are not available, your ingenuity will find common articles that may be used; for example, a broomhandle, an umbrella, even stiffly folded newspapers or magazines.

IDENTIFICATION TAGS 

School children will be supplied with identification tags. It is a good idea for civilians to write their names and addresses with indelible ink on the articles of clothing worn nearest the body. Identification tags are easily lost or misplaced. 

MORE ABOUT WAR GASES 

may

There should be a different type of warning for a gas attack, so that civilians at once put on their gas masks and keep them on until the "gas clear" signal is given. It has been proved that the ordinary gas mask gives full protection to the eyes, nose, throat, and lungs, against any known war gas except blister gas. 

During a gas attack one should go to the Refuge Room, because high explosives may be expected with gas attacks. Cracks in windows, floors, doors, etc., should be stuffed up.

There are nose irritants, or sneezing gases, which consist of very fine particles of solid arsenic compounds in the form of dust or smoke. They produce intense irritation and pain in the nose, mouth, throat, and chest, and often cause sneezing, headache, and nausea.

BLISTER GASES 

Blister gases are usually Mustard Gas or Lewisite Gas. Lewisite is easily recognized by its strong geranium-like odor. Mustard gas is far more treacherous than 

Lewisite because it is more difficult to detect. Both Mustard and Lewisite are liquids which may be sprayed in vapor form. 

DECONTAMINATION 

Upon exposure to blister gas, treatment must begin at once. 

Anyone exposed must go to the nearest house, remove his clothes, wash the body thoroughly in warm water and soap. Contaminated shoes and clothing should be left outside the building, to avoid contaminating others. 

People caught outdoors during a blister gas attack must not only put on their gas masks, but must keep all exposed parts, such as neck, hands, well covered. 

Spots of liquid gas on the skin must be treated immediately. Rub off the poison with cloth or cotton, burning or disposing of the cloth so as not to infect others. 

Anti-gas ointments should be applied to the skin. If no ointment is available, the skin should be scrubbed with soap and water.

If the eyes have been exposed to blister gas, whether vapor or liquid, they should be washed out at once with water or with a weak solution of salt or bicarbonate of soda - one teaspoonful to a pint of warm water. 

- 31 -

The person should immerse the injured eye- be careful not to infect the good eye in a glass of the solution, rolling and blinking the eye. 

People injured by explosion as well as by gas should receive necessary first aid and anti-gas aid on the spot, and then be removed by ambulance to a hospital. 

DECONTAMINATION OF CLOTHES 

Clothes contaminated with blister gas should be placed in a metal can with closely covered lid, to be collected for decontamination by the local squad. 

Light clothing, if only slightly contaminated by vapor, should be aired, then soaked and washed in warm soapy water.

Shoes are difficult to decontaminate, therefore be careful not to step into splashes of the liquid gas.

Food exposed to poison gas becomes poisonous to eat. Therefore protect food by airtight tins or jars. Drink no liquid that does not come from a covered container. Food wrapping paper should be waxed. Cardboard and wood absorb liquid gas. A refrigerator is a safe place for food. 

It should be known that gas masks are for use against war gases only! They give no protection against leaking illuminating gas. 

In order to remain effective, gas masks must be cared for according to directions. 

Putting the gas mask on should take only a couple of seconds. After a gas alarm, hold your breath while putting on the mask. Then breathe out violently to expel any gas that may have entered the mask. After that, breathe normally. 

A correctly adjusted gas mask should be comfortable, and fit accurately all around the head. The eyes should appear half way between the upper and lower edges of the transparent panel. If the eyes are well below the middle, the mask is too large; if they are much above, the mask is too small. 

To take off a gas mask, the right thumb should be placed under the buckle at the back of the head and the straps drawn downward over the top of the head, and then down. The left hand in the meantime is supporting the weight of the container. Any other method of removing the gas mask may damage the face piece. 

Everybody should practice regularly the putting on, wearing, and taking off of his gas mask both during the day and in the night. 

There are special gas masks for young children, and protective helmets for babies. 

If a mask is contaminated by blister gas it must be exchanged at once for another one.

TO MAKE ROOM GASPROOF 

A room on an upper floor is safer from gas, because war gases have a tendency to settle.

In order to make a room gasproof, one must block up all cracks and spaces through which air can enter.

Heavy brown paper can be pasted over cracks in walls and floors, over heat registers, and over cracks between windows and window frames. Felt weatherstripping may be used, or a pulp of wet newspaper, or calking material. 

Chimneys should be closed airtight with sacks filled with rags and paper. Spaces under doors should be closed.
