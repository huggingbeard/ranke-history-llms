POINT SYSTEM COMPARISON INTRODUCTION 

The 50 states and the District of Columbia all provide in some way for the suspension or revocation of the license of a person who is convicted of serious or frequent traffic law violations. However, to suspend or revoke the license of a habitual violator, he or she must first be identified as such. The "point system," the method chosen by the majority of jurisdictions as a means of identifying frequent or habitual traffic law violators, is the subject of this Commentary. 

Forty jurisdictions have laws or regulations establishing a point system. To aid in weighing traffic violations, a demerit point value is assigned to each traffic violation. As reports of convictions are received from the courts, a demerit value is charged against an individual's driving record. When a driver's record shows a specified point total, his driving privilege may be suspended.2/The 40 jurisdictions are:

THE UNIFORM VEHICLE CODE The Uniform Vehicle Code contains the following provision: 

For the purpose of identifying habitually reckless or negligent drivers and habitual or frequent violators of traffic regulations governing the movement of vehicles, the department shall adopt regulations establishing a uniform system assigning demerit points for convictions of violations of chapter 11 of this act or of ordinances adopted by local authorities regulating the operation of motor vehicles. The regulations shall include a designated level of point accumulation which so identifies drivers. 

The department may assess points for convictions in other states of offenses which, if committed in this State, would be grounds for such assessment. Notice of each assessment of points may be given, but notice is required when the point accumulation reaches percent of the number at which suspension is authorized. No points shall be assessed for violating a provision of this act or municipal ordinance regulating standing, parking, equipment, size or weight. In case of the conviction of a licensee - 2 -

of two or more traffic violations committed on a single occasion, such licensee shall be assessed points for one offense only and if the offenses involved have different point values, such licensee shall be assessed for the offense having the greater point value. The department is authorized to suspend the license of a driver when his driving record identifies him as an habitually reckless or negligent driver or as an habitual or frequent violator under this subsection. (footnotes omitted)

This provision is intended to provide a basic framework for establishment of a point system, with the details to be supplied by regulations, adopted by the department of motor vehicles in each state.1/

Assessment of Points 

The UVC does not contain a schedule of points to be assessed for particular offenses. The National Committee believes that the determination of point value assigned to particular violators should be made by the department and not by the legislature. 

However, a footnote to the Code section does recommend assigning six points for convictions of reckless driving and convictions of speeding when the licensee drove at least 20 miles per hour 

12 -

over the lawful limit; four points for convictions of relatively serious offenses; and three points for less serious offenses. 

The laws of two states (California and Virginia) do not contain a schedule of point assessment listing each offense and assigning points thereto. However, like the Code, the Virginia law provides for point assessment based on the seriousness of the offense as follows: serious traffic offenses, such as reckless driving, are assigned six points; relatively serious offenses, such as failure to yield right of way in violation of the basic right of way rule, are assigned four points; and offenses of a less serious nature, such as improper passing, are assigned three points.

The California law provides as follows: 

In determining the violation point count, any conviction of failure to stop in the event of an accident resulting in damage to property or otherwise failing to comply with the requirements of Section 20002 (Duty where property damaged), of driving a motor vehicle while under the influence of intoxicating liquor or any drug, or under the combined influence of intoxicating liquor and any drug, or of reckless driving shall be given a value of two points and any other traffic conviction involving the safe operation of a motor vehicle upon the highway shall be given a value of one point

The offenses listed, and the points assigned to each, vary among the schedules of point assessment for the remaining 38 jurisdictions. The five most commonly listed offenses have been chosen for comparison purposes: reckless driving, passing a stopped school bus, improper passing, following too closely, and failure to stop or yield right of way at a stop sign or a yield sign.

In addition to the offenses chosen for comparison, sections covering point assessment for speeding and "all other moving violations" (those not listed in the schedules) have also been included.

The Texas Entry System 

The entry system bases improvement and corrective actions on the number of convictions and/or accidents of a licensee. 

A habitual offender under this system is a person with four or more convictions arising out of different transactions in a consecutive 12-month period, or seven or more convictions 

43

arising out of different transactions within a 24-month period. 

The convictions must be for moving violations of the traffic laws of Texas, and offenses for which the law requires automatic suspension are excluded.

Drivers who accumulate a record of violations or accidents are sent advisory safety letters. The purpose of the letter is to deter unsafe driving practices and future violations by alerting the driver of his record, impressing upon him the fact that violations are a threat to his own well-being as well as that of others, and advising him that future violations or accidents will require corrective action. 

The system employs a "rescue schedule" which authorizes driver improvement actions based upon a licensee's record. The record contains, but is not limited to, reports of convictions and "reliable reports" of other offenses. A reliable report is defined as one which could be introduced as evidence in court. 

The following is a partial listing of driver improvement actions authorized under the rescue schedule: 1. 2. 3. 4. conduct investigation investigation and/or counseling interview require attendance at rehabilitation training school file petition for administrative hearing 

5. file petition for administrative hearing to suspend.

The purpose of an administrative hearing is to allow the licensee, whose driving privilege is in question, to be heard in his own behalf before action is taken, and to require an impartial referee to decide if suspension, probation or revocation is warranted under the law and to determine the length of any suspension or probation.

The department must give licensees ten days written notice prior to an administrative hearing. Appeal of suspension must be filed within 30 days of the effective date of suspension.

OBSERVATIONS 

The point system is a reliable and fair basis for authorizing license suspension. If properly established, it can provide licensees with a standard for use in improving their driving habits prior to reaching the level which would identify them as habitual or frequent violators subject to license suspension. - 44 · 

---

Laws establishing point systems should require notification to licensees when they have reached a dangerous level of point accumulation, and the Code suggestion of 50 percent is appropriate. Also, the practice of offering licensees an opportunity to reduce their point total at this level of accumulation appears to be a sound idea. Requiring successful completion of an approved course in traffic safety for point reduction is sensible, and seems to be the most common approach. 

However, drivers should not be awarded credit points (against future point assessment) for violation free driving. 

Owning a driver's license is a privilege, and drivers should not be rewarded for driving in the manner expected of them. 

And, all states should assess points for out of state convictions which are assessable in the licensing state. Otherwise, frequent interstate travelers who are habitual violators and licensed in a state without this practice, could remain licensed and a threat to other highway users 

indefinitely.

Also, all point system provisions should afford aggrieved licensees an opportunity for a hearing (if requested) before a suspension order becomes effective. And, requiring the licensee to file a court petition alleging that he can show cause why his license should not be suspended, and agreeing to pay court costs if he doesn't prove his allegation, as one must do in Ohio, is not what is intended by affording the licensee an opportunity for a hearing.

Those states without point systems should be urged to establish one, and all states should strive for uniformity of their provisions. The Uniform Vehicle Code provides a sound basic framework for this purpose, and the legislature in each state should be encouraged to adopt the Code provision. 

Finally, if details for the point system are to be left to administrative regulation, the agency responsible should be required to maintain such regulations in appropriate form. 

Licensees in many of the states discussed in this Commentary would find it difficult to obtain material which fully outlined the operation of their state's point system. 45

APPENDIX 

State statutes relating to driver licensing are readily available. Statutes are legislative enactments. In each state these are gathered together, organized according to substance, and published in a set of code books which are available in many law libraries. The state statutes studied for this Commentary include those adopted prior to January 1, 1978. 

State regulations are another matter altogether. Regulations are rules which are made by an administrative agency, exercising authority given to it by the legislature. 

Such regulations are not always given widespread publication, and are not readily available outside the state. In order to obtain them, it is necessary to contact the appropriate administrative agency in each state and request whatever regulations they have. We first attempted to obtain the regulations in this manner. 

Initial contact with the state agencies was mady by State and Federal Associates, Inc., Washington, D.C., an organization which monitors state government activity and which maintains an agent in each state capital. We were able to obtain a number of relevant regulations in this way. For some states, however, the response was inadequate.

The next step involved a visit to the Library of Congress, to search for administrative regulations for those states that did not respond, or whose response to the initial contact was inadequate. This research provided us with some, but not all, of the state regulations still needed. 

We next contacted the American Association of Motor Vehicle 

Administrators, Washington, D.C.

Washington, D.C. A study we received from AAMVA indicated that several of the states for which we were lacking regulations did not make use of a point system. 

Finally, we called the department of motor vehicles in the remaining states and asked if they made use of a point system and, if so, if they would send us a copy of the regulation. 

In response to our various requests for regulations, quite a few state agencies sent material which may not have the force of a regulation. For example, some states sent driver handbooks and/or pamphlets. These materials are designed to be given to driver license applicants to explain the laws and other 

- 46 - 

---

information needed to pass the driver licensing examination.

Nevertheless, we utilized this material if it provided relevant information about the state's point system. 

One of the biggest problems was with material which by its content seemed to be regulatory, but it lacked the appearance and some of the necessary elements of a regulation. A regulation should proclaim itself to be a regulation. It should have a title. A section number or some other means of citing the regulation might also be expected. A regulation should certainly be dated, and it should clearly identify the statutory authority under which it is promulgated. It should also identify the agency which issues it. Few of the "regulations" received met all of these qualifications, but a large number met none of them. 

We received plain typed sheets of paper from some states containing "rules" which were relevant to our study but which in no way were identified as regulations. In several cases we received material which was clearly labeled as a "Proposed Regulation." 

But when such material was dated, showing it to be many years old, we assumed it was no longer "proposed, and that it would not have been sent to us if it had not been adopted. 

In spite of the difficulties in obtaining regulations, sufficient statutory and regulatory material was available to complete this Commentary, and to get a fair picture of how the point system is being used nationwide. It must be noted, however, that where this Commentary shows that a state does not have a particular provision, this observation may only reflect the fact that we were unable to obtain adequate material from that state.
