AUTHOR'S NOTE 

The author of this pamphlet many years ago began to make notes on the career of his kinsman, Leonard Leopold Mackall, whose genius he admired and whose striking personality appealed to him. 

These notes were always submitted to Mr. 

Mackall for approval, so that it might, with propriety, be said that such portion of the pamphlet as relates to his professional career is almost in the nature of an autobiography.

LEONARD LEOPOLD MACKALL BORN IN BALTIMORE, MARYLAND JANUARY 29, 1879 DIED AT FALMOUTH, VIRGINIA-MAY 19, 1937 

To the readers of the Herald-Tribune's literary section "Books" the subject of this sketch was known as the learned editor of "Notes for Bibliophiles”: but in his special field of bibliography, both in this Country and abroad, he was a recognized authority. There are but few, if any, Americans of his profession who had as intimate or as accurate a knowledge of the bibliographical contents of the various great libraries, public and private, of the World than he had; and with many of the custodians of these storehouses of knowledge he was on familiar terms. 

Mr. Mackall was a bibliophile in the true sense of the word. He loved books for themselves and not for any commercial value they might possess. He shared with the Tibetans the belief that books are sacred; and in writing the history of some musty old volume his first consideration was to ascertain the truth: 

Is the book what it purports to be, or is it spurious?

To answer this question he with relentless energy sought for the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth; he wanted facts and must have them, and he never permitted mere imagination or guesswork to play any part in his research work. He was a terror to bibliographical fakers and plagiarists, for whom he entertained a righteous contempt. [13]

Nothing seemed to give him greater delight than the unraveling of the mystery surrounding the birth and adventures of some volume several centuries old. Let us say that the volume in question purports to have been printed by a certain printer, at such a time and at such a place. Are these facts true? 

Is the book genuine throughout and quite complete, or as nearly complete as the other copies either seen by him or described in various bibliographies and catalogues? These are the problems which he sets out to solve; and to find the correct answer a broad field of research work is spread out before him; and here the real work of the bibliographer begins, for it involves a careful study of all the indicia by which the genuineness of the book under investigation may be determined, such as the character and work of the reputed printer, the number of copies in the original edition, and for whom printed, and by whom distributed; the size of the book, its paging type, and the kind of paper upon which printed, the wording of its text; and the tracing of the book's journeyings from its place of birth to its present home, etc. These and other such indicia are important factors in determining whether a book is just what it purports to be.

In his excursions into the dreary wastes of bibliography to discover the missing links in the life and adventures of a rare book, Mr. Mackall, never for a moment, had in mind any pecuniary reward for his labors. His inspiration was derived from his unselfish love of his profession, and his compensation came from the consciousness that, by his efforts, he had been enabled to clear up a profound secret pertaining to some ancient volume of forgotten lore, which had baffled the ingenuity of others; and with Goethe's 

Harper (in "Wilhelm Meister") Mr. Mackall could exclaim:- [14]

"I sing but as the young bird sings, That warbles on the tree;

The rapture of the music brings, Its own reward to me."

Mr. Mackall's library, comprising approximately twelve thousand volumes, well selected with reference to the nature of his work, was housed in his residence at Savannah, Georgia. Originally the library occupied a medium-sized room on the first floor. Shelves extended from the floor to the ceiling on all wall space-many shelves containing two solid rows of books. At times many books were placed on chairs and tables, and even on the floorjust space enough being left for desk and typewriter, and an alleyway so-to-speak extending along the bottom shelves. In the course of time this room reached its full capacity, and it was then that the over-flow began-first to the hallway and rooms on the first floor, and then to all available space on the second floor, so that at the time of his death nearly every room in the house contained a portion of his library. To a stranger such a disposition of books gave the impression of hopeless disorder, and one was led to wonder how it was possible for anyone to make headway in his work in such surroundingsparticularly, as Mr. Mackall had never catalogued his library. But this apparent chaos was, in his case, such in appearance only, for in fact Mr. Mackall kept all of his books arranged strictly as to subject matter—that is, a particular subject with reference to which he bought each of them. And thus, strange as it may seem, at a moment's notice, and apparently without the slightest mental effort he could lay his hands on the book he needed in less time than it would have taken by consulting a catalogue. 

As illustrative of the almost uncanny accuracy of Mr. Mackall's memory, it may interest the readers [ 15 ]

of this sketch to know the method employed by him, when absent from Savannah, in getting from his library such books as might be needed by him as appertaining to the subject on which he was working. 

For example, let us say that he is in New York and is engaged in writing an article for "Books" and in the course of his research work he finds he needs certain books from his Savannah library. To accomplish this he merely writes or wires to his friend and relative, Mr. Francis S. Mackall, who has access to his residence, describing in such detail where the desired books can be found that the latter, with rare exceptions, has never failed to locate the desired volumes.

The only great literary character whose memory, in the writer's opinion, was comparable to that of the subject of this sketch, was Lord McCauleyof whom it has been said that if every copy of Milton's "Paradise Lost" in the World was destroyed he could re-write the book from beginning to end, word for word; and with equal propriety it might be observed of Mr. Mackall that if all the copies of all the books, coming within the range of his branch of bibliography, were vandalized and all records of them lost, he would have been able to give the names, locations and history of almost every volume. 

In the acquisition of this library, covering a period of upwards of a quarter of a century, Mr. 

Mackall did not work on any preconceived system,- [19]

nor did he have the slightest ambition to own a valuable collection of books. Possessed of but a very modest income he was not in a position, even if so inclined, to enter the field of competition with wealthy collectors in the purchase of rare books. His primary object in building up a library was to surround himself with the tools of his profession. Asked by a friend, on one occasion, why it was that he did not commercialize his extensive knowledge of old books, and in that way add to his income, he replied that he was a book lover and not a trader-adding that in his opinion rare books should be placed where they will do the most good, and not in a glass case under lock and key, to tickle the vanity of some wealthy collector. Acting on this theory he constantly observed and noted wherein the several libraries, in which he was especially interested, were lacking, either, it may be, in a volume to complete a set of rare books or, in a book to complete a collection on a certain subject: and from time to time, in pouring over catalogues of sales, or on his visits to old book shops not infrequently he would run across the missing volumes, and purchasing them, would place them on the shelves of the respective libraries where, in his judgment they properly belonged. For this reason the libraries of the Grolier Club, the Century Club, Johns Hopkins University, Harvard University, Yale University, and others have been the grateful beneficiaries of this generous impulse. Thus he remembered constantly, in practice as well as in theory, Sir William Osler's characteristic saying: "The true bibliophile has a keen pleasure in seeing an important document in its proper home." (Canadian Medical Association Journal, September 1912, page 834.) 

In the giving away of books the generosity of

Mr. Mackall was not, however, limited to libraries, for there was scarcely a friend, or a friendly acquaintance, who was not, at one time or another, the recipient [ 20 ]

of a book, carefully selected with reference to the taste or occupation of the donee and at Christmas and on wedding or birthday occasions he actually reveled in the pleasure of book giving. "Knowing I lov'd my books, he furnished me with volumes that I prize above my dukedom.

The Tempest. Act 1.Sc.2. 

When Mr. Mackall was not engaged in his research work, on which occasions he shut himself up in his library and, for all intents and purposes, was lost to the World, his mental temperament seemed to undergo a change and from a learned Bibliophile he became a simple, lovable and companionable man. 

Placable and generous to a fault, and with no trace of malice in his nature he made friends easily and soon became endeared to them. 

He took but little interest in the great political and social problems which were then and now are distracting the World-his theory being that every man should stick to his trade. "I have a fair knowledge of books, as the result of much experience," he was wont to say, "but with respect to political and social science I am comparatively ignorant, and therefore I am content to leave the solution of such problems to men who have made these subjects their life's study." 

It was the little things in life that appealed to him-a stroll, a chat, a jest, an act of kindness, a word of sympathy. He delighted in badinage and was remarkably quick in repartee, and anyone who undertook to chaff him was generally discomfitted by his witty comeback. In his hours of leisure he led an unpretentious life, and his equanimity was only occasionally disturbed when, conversing with some friend or acquaintance, some reference should chance to be made to some incident connected with a rare book: at such a time Mr. Mackall's attitude almost instantly [21]

changed, and straightway he was transformed into a ruthless bibliophile; and then like a hunting dog who sees a hare, his ears became alert, his eyes sparkled, and he was off with a flash on the scent of the book in question, and was not content until he brought the quarry to bay.

Mr. Mackall was of a decided gregarious temperament. He loved to be with people, and this propensity to make new acquaintances was so pronounced as at times to be amusing to his friends; for when he happened to be in a place where people congregated and if among the company he saw strange faces he was never satisfied until he learned their names and was introduced to them; and thereafter he never forgot them. He was particularly fond of young people-male and female-with a decided partiality for "the cuties" as he jestingly called young ladies, and they for their part were all crazy about him. 

For a number of years prior to his death Mr. 

Mackall did not spend as much of his time in Savannah as formerly, but divided his homelife, so-to-speak, between Savannah and Falmouth, Virginia, where he resided with his sister, the widow of the distinguished artist, the late Gari Melchers. The brother and sister were peculiarly congenial; their tastes and dispositions being in many respects very similar. 

Falmouth was more accessible than Savannah to the large cities of the North Atlantic Coast and New

England where were housed a number of the largest libraries in the World, and where resided many of his associates in the field of bibliography. His duties called him to New York more frequently than to other points and his visits there were generally of longer duration. When in New York he made the Century

Club his working headquarters, but when he was off duty and the time for relaxation arrived he could generally be found at the Coffee House Club-that [22]

It was almost impossible for any one to be long in the society of Mr. Mackall without absorbing, in some degree, his enthusiasm over books: and from an incident which happened on one occasion, when we were living under the same roof, the writer is almost persuaded that even intelligent animals, with whom he has had daily contact, seemed subconsciously to partake of this enthusiasm. The writer and Mr. 

Mackall were sitting in the drawing room of his residence, discussing old editions of Shakespeare, a subject in which Mr. Mackall was an expert, and the former remarked that among all the books that he had ever possessed the one he valued most was an edition of Shakespeare in one volume, which had been given to him in his youth and which he had preserved in his possession until a few years ago when it was lost, with his whole library, in the fire that consumed his summer residence in Virginia: and that it had been his custom, whenever he attended a performance of Shakespeare plays, to enter in the book on the first page of the play, the date, the place, and name of the principal actor, but that he could not remember just which edition it was, except that the [23]

print was quite small, but very clear, and not hard to read. Mr. Mackall suggested that the book in question might be the Globe edition, published by Macmillan, and bound in green cloth, but the writer was confident that it did not belong to that edition. Mr. 

Mackall then suggested that perhaps it was the once famous Pickering edition of 1826, and remarked, “I have not yet received a copy of this edition which I ordered sometime ago, but I have the Pickering edition of 1825 in nine very small volumes, printed in type very similar to the one-volume Pickering edition of 1826. Let me get a volume from my den and show it to you, so that you can see if the type looks at all familiar and reminds you of your lost book." lost book." While we were still conversing, a very intelligent and highly-bred Boston Bull puppy, belonging to the writer, was playing about the room, but suddenly disappeared without our observing his departure, and, just as Mr. 

Mackall started to go to his library to get one volume of the Pickering edition of 1825 to show me, "Skippy" reappeared with a tiny book in his mouth. Mr. Mackall immediately took it from him, and lo and behold! it turned out to be a volume of the identical edition of Shakespeare, which Mr. Mackall had just been describing to the writer. It seems the little rascal had gone into Mr. Mackall's library where books of all kinds were lying on the floor, tables and chairs, and by a remarkable co-incidence, selected the aforesaid volume as a demonstration of his book knowledge. Mr. 

Mackall and the writer enjoyed a hearty laugh over the incident, and the former declared that he intended to propose "Skippy" for membership in the Shakespeare Society, and he added, "I will keep this incident in mind and sometime in the future, when an appropriate opportunity offers, I will make use of it. He never forgot it, and always thereafter referred to "Skippy" as "The Bibliographical Dog."

Mr. Mackall was born in the City of Baltimore on the 29th day of January, 1879. On his father's side he was descended from an old Colonial family of Maryland, and inherited, in a marked degree, the characteristics of his ancesters-an intense modesty, a hatred of cant and hypocrisy, and a total indifference to the call of conventional ambition. He was the fourth Leonard in direct descent from his great grandfather, Captain Leonard Mackall of Georgetown, District of Columbia, whose old residence is still standing on the heights of that ancient city. His mother was Louisa F. Lawton, daughter of General 

Alexander R. Lawton, of Savannah, Georgia-a distinguished lawyer, soldier, and diplomat. At the age of four his father moved to Chestnut Hill, Philadelphia, where the family remained until his father's death in the year 1890, and then his mother and her three children removed to Savannah.

After passing through the customary common school routine Mr. Mackall entered Lawrenceville 

School to prepare for college, and finishing his course at that institution in 1896 he went to Europe for a year, and then entered the Johns Hopkins University, where he graduated A .B. in 1900. It was during his later post-graduate studies at Hopkins (190406) that he formed a friendship with the learned and brilliant Dr. William Osler, who, at that time, occupied the chair of medicine there, but who, subsequently in 1905, was called as Regius Professor of Medicine to Oxford University, and for his distinguished services was made a Baronet in 1911. After graduating at Johns Hopkins, influenced partly by his family, Mr. Mackall decided to take a course in Law at Harvard University. Finding, however, that he was not really fitted for the legal profession by temperament or by inclination, he remained at Harvard only two years, and then, yielding to the compelling urge to live and work among books, he definitely concluded to adopt literary and historical research and the science of Bibliography as his life's work; and as a step to accomplish this end he entered the University of Berlin, Germany, where he remained for two years, devoting himself to varied literary research, especially in connection with Goethe, and to extending his knowledge in the field of Bibliography. At Berlin he formed friendships with a number of distinguished men, who like himself, were interested in the subject just mentioned. 

Mr. Mackall was honored by being invited to edit for Goethe-Schiller-Archiv in Weimar that institution's official contribution to the 25th vol. of the 

Goethe-Jahrbuch, and he thus published there in 1904 for the first time, with exhaustive notes, Goethe's Correspondence with various Americans, together with an account of his sending a set of his works to Harvard University in 1819. He also contributed to the leading German, English and American scholarly periodicals, various papers publishing new and interesting discoveries by him relating to Goethe, Byron and others. In 1904 he returned to Johns

Hopkins University where he was elected a Fellow in 1906: he did not remain in Baltimore, however, [27]

but returned to Germany and settled at Jena, where the facilities for research were far more favorable, and where he was even allowed direct access to the shelves of the University Library, being the only person outside of the University Faculty to whom this unusual privilege was granted. While in Jena he was 

Co-Editor of the standard collection of all of Goethe's 

Conversations (1909-11, 5 vols.) edited by Baron von 

Biedermann. He was appointed Honorary Secretary for England and America of the Bibliophile 

Group of the Great International Book Exhibition held at Leipzig in 1914, and was awarded a diploma for his services in that connection, besides receiving a letter of thanks from the President of the Exhibition.

The World War naturally drove Mr. Mackall home; and thus he somewhat reluctantly, at the late 

Founder's (W. W. DeRenne), repeated and most urgent request, took charge in February, 1916, of the Wymberley Jones DeRenne Georgia Library at 

Wormsloe, near Savannah, and remained there until he finally finished in July, 1918, the general supervision of a completely new Catalogue of that great 

Georgia Collection (see Georgia Historical Quarterly for June 1918; and now the catalogue itself.) As an authority on books relating to the history of Georgia he had no rival, and by reason of his accurate knowledge in this respect, he was enabled to place on the shelves of this great library a number of important volumes which had escaped the vigilance of Mr. De- 

Renne himself. And to illustrate the uncanny capacity of Mr. Mackall to locate the places where missing books might be found, it is interesting to note that among the aforesaid missing books, which Mr. De- 

Renne was anxious to obtain, there were eight or ten volumes which he especially desired and to secure which he had used almost every avenue of information [ 28 ]

without avail. These books Mr. Mackall within a comparatively short time located and secured to Mr. De- 

Renne's great satisfaction.

The Armistice unexpectedly prevented Mr. 

Mackall from receiving the Army Commission as First Lieutenant in the Intelligence Corps for which he was examined and highly recommended. 

While in New York with his Mother, Mr. Mackall continued the bibliographical studies on Servetus (the unfortunate discoverer of the short Circulation of the Blood) and he contributed a paper on this subject to the Osler Anniversary Volumes, 1919. A further short paper on the same subject was published by the Royal Society of Medicine, London, in 1924, being an elaboration of remarks made before the Historical Section of that Society, October 17, 1923. 

Mr. Mackall was also Co-Editor of Sir William

Osler's Yale Lectures on "The Evolution of Modern 

Medicine" as finally published in 1921. And (as mentioned above) in accordance with Osler's recorded special wish he did much bibliographical work on the Osler Library Catalogue until it was finally published by the Oxford University Press in 1929, after his death. Osler was President of the Bibliographical 

Society, London, during the last ten years of his life; and when he died (December 1919), Mr. Mackall was selected to write the official Resolutions of the Bibliographical Society of America, which published them soon afterwards, together with Mr. Mackall's remarks on that occasion. (Most of these Remarks, under the new title "Sir William Osler as a Bibliophile", were in the Osler Memorial Volume which forms Bulletin IX of the International Association of Medical Museums, Montreal 1926). 

After the death (January 1924) of Professor

Gildersleeve (of Johns Hopkins University) the [ 29 ]

greatest of American Classical Scholars, his widow informed Mr. Mackall that her husband had often told her that, if any book about him was to be published, he particularly hoped that he (Mr. Mackall) would have charge of it. This wish could not be carried out in the form intended, but the Gildersleeve Bibliography printed in the volume of "Selections from the Brief Mention of Basil Lanneau Gildersleeve" (edited by his successor Prof. C. W. E. 

Miller; published by the Johns Hopkins Press, 1930) was very largely the work of Mr. Mackall, who also contributed other information acknowledged in the 

Editor's preface. Even after he had lost his eyesight almost entirely, and was over ninety years old, Prof. 

Gildersleeve continued to write most kind letters to Mr. Mackall, who treasured them very highly. 

When an entirely new literary supplement "BOOKS" of the New York Herald-Tribune was started by the late Stuart Sherman in 1924, Mr.

Mackall was urged to conduct the new department "Notes for Bibliophiles", and did so since the beginning, and he continued as editor of that department up to the time of his death. In preparing his weekly bibliographical notes for "Books", or his "stuff" as he humorously called them, Mr. Mackall did not make use of a typist, but wrote out his notes in long hand, and typed them himself: and on one occasion when the writer asked if he would not save time and labor by employing a typist, he laughingly replied, that that would be an expensive business for him for the reason "if I used a typist I would also be compelled to employ an expert in handwriting to decipher and read my notes to the operator of the machine; and then too, such expert would have to be familiar with bibliographic nomenclature"-and, illustrating his meaning, he pointed to a sheet of completed notes in his handwriting on the table, and the [ 30 ]

writer was compelled to confess that the characters on this paper looked very much like Egyptian hieroglyphics.

While still residing abroad Mr. Mackall was made a member of Phi Beta Kappa at Johns Hopkins University (1913), and an Honorary Member of the Georgia Historical Society (1912). In 1918 

he was elected a member of the American Antiquarian 

Society (Worcester, Mass.) a National Historical So ciety, in which the membership is very limited and all honorary, and was invited by the President to read a paper before that distinguished Society, which he did in April 1920 ("A Letter from the Virginia 

Loyalist, John Randolph, to Thomas Jefferson, written in London in 1779,") and it was printed in the Society's Proceedings. He also contributed since 1916 to the Georgia Historical Quarterly, the 

American Historical Review, American Journal of

Philology, Modern Language Notes, Johns Hop kins Hospital Bulletin (paper read by invitation at the opening of the Welch Medical Library, October 

17, 1929), the Modern Language Review (London), and, by invitation, to the anniversary volume "Essays 

Offered to Herbert Putnam by his Colleagues and

Friends on his 30th Anniversary as Librarian of Con gress", April 5, 1929. In 1930 he was appointed

Honorary Consultant in Bibliography to the New York Academy of Medicine. During the year 1933 

he was elected 2nd Vice-President of the Bibliographical Society of America, and in 1936 he was made 

President of said Society. On February 19, 1937, he was elected President of the Georgia Historical Society. Mr. Mackall was a member of the Century, Coffee House, and Grolier Clubs of New York, being a member of the Library Committee of the last mentioned, the most distinguished bibliophile organization in America; and he was selected to deliver the address at the opening of the great Byron Exhibition [31]

at the Grolier Club, December 18, 1924, the finest 

Byron Exhibition ever held anywhere.

In conclusion, as a bibliophile Leonard Leopold Mackall was pre-eminent, and as a man he was beloved by all who knew him. "Amicus usque ad aras. 

THE END.
