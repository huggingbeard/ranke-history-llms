The Cup of Joy 

Into my life has come a sweet, strange calm

Which, in the maelstrom of my years, does seem To be not life, but the untroubled gleam 

Of still, dear waters! (Oh, without a qualm— 

And conscienceless-Fate struck with heavy palm 

My ripple of contented days 'til dream

And waking were the surge and stress and scream 

Of lightning-driven storm!-And then like balm 

Fell the smooth silence!)-Into my harsh life

Comes quiet, and, true oared, with solemn hush, Comes happiness in the pure, radiant flush

Of my soul's youth, fair comforting and rife 

With all things godly! Gentleness and strife 

Alike stir not-soundless as reed and rush. ⠀⠀

II I have sought long and earnestly for thee, Spirit of Joy! And tears as bitter as

The hard seas of seven worlds-and grief that has The pain of all hearts-were the self of me 'Til now, the fairest of the thoughts that be, Thou leadest straight where never I might pass,

Lighting as with a torch and venice-glass 

My shore and sky! Always had been my plea—

To choose mine own course and, as well, allow 

Choosing to each. But when the storm did break,

Carrying me on and outward as to shake 

Me from my sight of blue heaven, it was thou— 

Not I-sweet sprite, must tell me rightly how 

Lieth the way which, blown and lost, I take!

III 

Lost Lost! In the unholy babel of

All moving tongues this is the silent word Which with the trumpet's strength-as clear-is heard, Harsh as black hate and sorrowful as love! 

Which on the blotless and bowed heart (above The spume and spittle of the common, stirred 

To strange pulse), glides with the scalpel's touch! Absurd 

As any wraith beside the plume and glove

Of mid-day pageant is this utterance, Leaving dumb lips, pallid and laughterless,

To meet the light demands and silly stress 

Of jest and gibe and what is fair, perchance: 

So it is said neither by gasp nor glance, Unspoken, aye, for its own worthiness!

IV 

And now the winds are strangely fallen. Now 

I find me on good earth, where grasses are, Where voices come from inland. The first star

Stands in the blue and looks upon my brow 

With eye of benediction. Eastward how,

Mutterous and heavy, the black mists sweep the bar,

While reaching westward from the zenith, far 

And fervent, many morrows gleam-endow 

The sky with prophecy! "Tis sweet to feel

The soil's fresh touch when blistering feet have trod The pitiless pavement; grateful 'tis to nod 

After a labour, grinding, harsh and real; 

So after funeral years 'tis good to kneel 

And know the giftful, living hours of God!

It is so fair a truth I can believe Only the moment's giving. I have seen 

Dawns that belied their dews-nights whose starscreen 

Shut me from every heaven. (Why does one grieve 

Attainment when himself does not achieve?)—

But here is happiness-like something green 

And fresh and sweet, and of the morning's sheen, Within a mid-day desert. Nor deceive

Me do the hours with their fine imaging. 

Neither do happy days betray. It is 

A sudden living of life's usual bliss, A soul poised silently on thoughful wing The skies above below it what they bring 

To earth in pure reflection when they kiss.

VI It is not much I ask of life, even less I wish than that I ask. For many a gift 

Is leaden, and without the great uplift

The simple joys bear with them. I confess 

The vanity of what is happiness 

To me and many!-Only silly drift 

Of restless hours, and sorry, senseless shift

Of scenes that press for lightness. (Is the stress 

Even of that fine fulfillment justified 

Does one not love the giving?) Less I ask,

Indeed, than that received. It were a task, I fear, to hold the gifts many have tried 

And found so wanting! Thus, though thrice denied,

I've all-fine linen scorning-and the masque!

VII 

And yet must labour tender every hand

An holding. Verily, that which we will 

We shall be! For, indeed, whether the mill 

Grind with the sweetest water of the land, Or turn with bitter flood, ever shall stand

The corn-fields, and the yellow grist shall fill 

The provident bins. Reward is labour's still!

We scan the silent stretches of sea sand, Bordered by evening's tranquil parallel 

Of ocean, knowing that by many more

Than brain computes grains form the endless shore 

And simple drops the deep. No one can tell

His doing-none can prophesy so well- "Til he hath done, 'til he hath tried him sore.

VIII 

In the far stillnesses of night, and, too, In the most solemn day, there's many a thought Comes from the past-deep and forgotten-brought Unto the musing present on the true 

And lifting wings of strong ideals! Do (I ask me earnestly) these ghosts, once wrought

From the fine and good, which leave one so distraught,

Bring aught out of the visionary blue 

That is of strength and sweetness?-fetch the gifts 

We think are theirs alone? Or do they not

Rather disturb the hour and rudely blot The fair turn of tomorrow-much as sifts The silt and snow into the careless rifts 

Betwixt the out and in? Is't that?-or what?

IX 

It was so harsh a day I cannot bring 

Sunlight, or laughter, or that bit of cheer

Which decently is life's, or aught that's dear 

To wondering hours, from its least part! We cling

At times to sorrow, for the backward swing Of the great pendulum is far, and sheer 

Into the midst of odourous things! Right clear

It is that straight and openly we sing Sesame and the syringa and as oft 

The lonesome cypress.-What is of the past

Extreme and poignant never shall be cast 

Into the utter night. Or even aloft 

And lovely do we see the stress, and soft 

We speak it, justifying all at last.

Behind the gates is memory. Behind 

Are moody shades which mock the hapless night, Ill-beckoning and ill-boding! and the sight,

Too, of a lone wraith which, of gentler kind, Sweetly persuadeth! Is't that ourselves find 

Shrewd pardon for the laggard thought? Or, quite 

And quick resigning, pleasure in the blight 

And bitter mildew of the days that wind 

So sourly back? I know not which it is, Or why the past compels. I only know 

Our thoughts in fond review to those hours go, Turning a back upon the present bliss, And, be the grief some petty that or this,

Or fall'n ambition, we would have it so!

Of mem'ries, have it so? Who not, indeed?

We are but of the earth: when voices plead 

Out of the former time we cannot hush 

Our answering selves, nor stay the clamorous crush

Of things associate. We must ever heed, It seems, rather the call incautious, bleed Our healing hearts, than listen wisely, brush With an indifferent hand the motley drift 

Clear and away from this our human flood! 

But whose is wisdom? Not in flesh and blood,

Methinks, is it to fill the desolate rift With what is poor and senseless, or to lift Us-though deluded from the warm and good.

XII 

Nothing that's fair shall perish from the earth.

It is a thought-and I would keep it near 

The heart of me. For many things are dear, 

Not of the present only, of that birth Which is the giftful prophecy's, but of mirth, And what is else than levity, a tear, A suffering, of the changeless past! How sere

The world did she have but a summer girth 

Of seasons! Every hour is richness quite,

Whether it shiver in the frosted day, Or languish sadly in the southern play 

Of moonbeams. And so memory, be its light 

A warmth or chill, shineth of its own right

Upon the hills, or in the thorny way.

Here I will bide me for a dreamy spell,

Here where a deep, forbidden thought returns 

To stir contented ashes!-thought which burns

Into the tired brain, to the utmost cell, And leaves us flush with wonder! It is well

That memory, in complete divorcement, learns 

The sweetness of disaster-that grief earns

A part-much merited-in the great swell 

Of an enlarging past! For does one dip 

Into the sorrows of his yesterdays

He finds that they are jewels-these strong, harsh rays Which once had hurt and blinded. There the drip, The wash, of tears is orient, while the lip 

Is full and dumb and tremulous for amaze!

XIV 

Lightly the cradle rocks where once the breeze Strove high and threatened with storm fury. Far, Eastward and radiant, gleams the morning star,

Pausing o'er night, bringing fair thought of these The pictures of the darkness.-For we please 

The iron conscience, the hard will, and jar No balance of contentment, when we are Poised-still-in painless retrospection. Ease, Aery and quiet, soothes the heart of us 

Who feel the sweetness of emotion, and, Listening aloft, hear the celestial band 

And know the voice of sunrise! It is thus 

The grieving may know gladness: promise plus

The real-left by a past of generous hand.

XV 

I do not know the present, nor do I 

Care aught about the future. 'Tis the past,

Unto whose mould my heart and mind are cast, Shapes all of me-though once I put it by As but of must and mockery! While a sigh 

Will now disturb, and though I am aghast 

And often angry with me, at the last 

I find it only sweetness, and deny

No virtue of its giving. For I knew 

A great completion when the troublous time Was happy sufferance, before rust and rime Gathered too thickly. So does trial imbue 

One's grieving self with meanings large-not few, As he would number them, and quite sublime!

XVI 

It is so slight a thing, indeed, can turn The suffering human! Truth we know it when, Within a quick hour's passing, our whole ken 

And range of fine emotion change them. Burn

The mourning-cote of a long grief and learn 

That grave-dust is not deader-colder then- 

Than is the ash of dead thoughts. Are they ten,

Or by a thousand more, we shall discern In each the lifeless gray which is but this- 

A past consumed of its own fire! And well

We deem it; for the melancholy spell 

Clings with the closeness of a demon's kiss, Halts in its slow speech with unnatural hiss, Whose breath, at times, is as a flame from hell!

XVII 

Consumed of its own fire! The pain was long 

And harsh, and in a crystal ball of tears

I saw the moments lengthen into years, Turning in lasting cycle! "Twas a strong 

Yet slender thread ran through it as a song 

Leads through a life-time; and my hopes and fears, Holding a course which neither clouds nor clears, Followed the doubtful line, knowing no wrong, No right, no why, or wherefore. 'Twas a time 

Troubled to sourness! But like the quick death of 

A last spark, and as if 'twere willed above, The vision vanished, and no ash or rime 

Remained of the blear past, while peace sublime 

Moved o'er the new hours like a slow-winged dove.

XVIII Indeed, when I look back I marvel much 

That briefness could be crammed unto the last 

With change unsought and so entire!-the past

Stretching to nothingness, and living such As it had just begun. Some strange, sweet touch 

Upon the bitter sands had poured a vast 

And rapid flood; and, never a bit aghast,

I plunged the cooling depths, and with no clutch, Or gesture, of the sinking. It was o'er- 

All-all of it-the worry and the pain- The many-headed hydra ne'er again 

Should show firm fangs, or loose his venom more! 

Even after many days I marvel, nor 

Shall ever feel that this high peace is vain.

XIX 

How came it, and how ever did I lose 

The infinite sorrow-the immortal grief?- 

Than which no other hurt, no mad belief, Is bitterness and burning! Not by twos, Or tens, or any thousands, shall I choose 

The answer. Simply thus it was! Yet chief,

And always chosen, is a fact so brief, So mere, I cannot say for lightness. Whose

It was to change the way of years is not Even for memory, nor is the silly joy 

That wrought it.-A fair hand, perhaps a coy 

Persuasive smile, maybe no matter! What,

Giftful and great, concerned my troubled lot Was the new thought and what it should employ.

XX 

Truly, a chapter of the past! And now 

Is closed forever! Closed the horrid gate

Which had so long admitted fear and hate 

And every goblin of the baneful brow, Clamorous and wicked! Like a murmurous bough

In the mild summer of the Indian-late In the year's life-my tired heart felt a great 

And growing song-a song it knew not how 

To sing. And though, within, the nightingale 

Felt the full ecstasy of living youth,

It was a burden tedious and uncouth The unasking world should hear-a silly tale One would set forth to be of fine avail 

Who knows the grandeur of heroic truth!

That answer straight though sourly! For who brings 

A mightier word, who has a clearer throat,

Whether of times today, or pasts remote, Than he whom fateful jibes and sudden stings 

Of circumstance goad to an utterance? Things

Themselves to the expression understood 

By him who suffers! And to him it does 

Sound sweet and well. And where a great voice was 

Of loud ambition simple speaking would 

Offer a quiet message, fair and good, With honest meaning in each stress and pause.

An autumn's wakening!-wondering how above 

A past so dull with misery and tears

Shall claim an heritage of joy: the dove

XXVII In the still triumph of the fair, new day, Where peace hath her sweet whisper and her face 

Of very earnest line, I find the grace

The culture of contentment. The long way, Where bowed the willows through a stormy gray,

Leads up the sunny highlands, and, apace, Walks the bright soul of joy. Truly, the race 

Is to the victor, stumble though he may;

Nor doth deny the seeker. Here is that 

Far water from whose draught is never thirst,

The sought of ages ever since man durst 

Believe and hope and mortally combat

With dimness seen, yet truly. In the lapse
