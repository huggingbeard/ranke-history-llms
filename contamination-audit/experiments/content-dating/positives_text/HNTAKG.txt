FOREWORD 

The development of trade associations in the United States, which has assumed peculiar importance at the present time, has large significance for any understanding of the international economic relations of Pacific countries. Whatever the fate of the proposed 

National Industrial Recovery Bill, it is probable that some form of industrial cooperation along the general lines of trade associations will continue to grow for some time to come. 

Concerned as they are with the processes of production and distribution through which the United States is linked to the raw materials and markets of other Pacific countries, trade associations are important potential factors either of conflict or control between the United States and other nations.

For these reasons, the American Council has included this paper in its series on conflict and control of economic forces in the Pacific area. It hopes that this material will help to provide the necessary background of information for effective round-table discussion at the Banff Conferencé. The American Council, of course, assumes no responsibility for statements, either of fact or of opinion, which are contained in this paper. These remain as much the personal expressions of the author as if they had been made orally.

These words, spoken at the hearings of the Senate Committee on National Economic Planning in 1931, adequately summarize the importance of the trade association to all proposals for increasing control or cooperation in American industrial life. Practically all of the score of national plans which have been most widely publicized in the last three years have been based directly on the trade associations of this country. In those trade associations which are already national in scope, there has been found a nucleus for fuller coordination and stabilization of business, and in their experience, gained largely since the years of the war, there is thought to be a basis for an extension of the same methods to industries as yet relatively unorganized.

The principal factor explaining this larger interest in industrial planning has been, of course, the depression. Increasing unemployment, falling wages, commercial inactivity, and business failures have resulted, during this depression as during previous ones, in increasing competition for contracting markets, and at the same time in increasing public demand for some kind of stabilization and control. 

This demand has come both from the public at large and from business interests. It has encountered strong opposition, and there has as yet been little agreement among the different suggestions for control which have been advanced. It is entirely possible that a

Industrial Self-Control 

The specific reason for the importance of trade associations in this discussion has been the desire, especially on the part of business interests, to work out some form of cooperation which would involve a minimum of government control. Coordination of production with consumption has been seen to be of direct value to all manufacturers, and easily defended as in the public interest. How could this be achieved, many business leaders have asked, without adding to the already burdensome control of government over industry? 

It is only natural that they should have turned to trade associations as their answer. With over two thousand manufacturers' trade groups in this country, representing more than 80 per cent of all leading factory owners and employers of industrial labor, there appeared to be already existing a network of "bridges" over which the necessary coordination might pass. Two of the most powerfully supported plans for national industrial recovery, advanced by the Chamber of Commerce of the United States and by the National Association of Manufacturers, have proposed an almost complete reliance upon the voluntary cooperation of individual producers in the various trade associations. In proposing the latter plan, President Gerard Swope of the National Electrical Manufacturers Association, one of the strongest of American trade associations, stated: "Production and consumption should be coordinated on a broader and more intelligent basis, thus tending to stabilize employment, and thereby remove fear from the minds of the workers as to continuity of employment. This should be preferably by joint participation and joint administration of management and employees... . . Since the 

War, trade associations have endeavored to collect information as to output, shipments, stocks on hand, etc., and such information is sent to each of the different members so that we have a very good idea of the relations of our business to the industry as a whole.

Now we can guide ourselves and use our intelligence as to how much to make, how much of a legitimate demand there is for that particular article. Using that information we can more nearly coordinate production to consumption."2 

Mr. Swope was asked by Senator LaFollette, Chairman of the

Senate Committee before which the hearing was being held: "You feel, then, that American trade associations, if properly functioning, can successfully cope with the problem of coordination of production and consumption?" "Well," Mr. Swope replied, "I don't know that they can successfully cope with it, but I am perfectly sure that they can more successfully do so than any other method."

Compulsory Control 

Although there was some opposition to the Swope Plan, on the part of employers, because of its statement, in Section 3, that "the public interest should be protected by the supervision of companies and trade associations by the Federal Trade Commission or by a Bureau of the Department of Commerce or by some Federal supervisory body especially constituted," this provision did not go as far in the direction of government control as some other plans. Even among industrial leaders, there was frank admission that a real degree of coercion might prove to be necessary. A former president of the Chamber of Commerce has stated: "I do not believe it is feasible to permit trades to go ahead and formulate their own plans without government sanction and approval; and whenever those plans involve restriction of production —and inevitably price-fixing-I do not believe it is good policy that they should be permitted to do that on their own account without direct supervision. . The conflict of interests in most industries is such that, I think, it is very difficult indeed to secure agreement on any programs on a voluntary basis. . . . I think that unfair and deceptive trade practices have been among the greatest contributing factors to the difficulties through which we have been passing." A great many different plans have been advanced, especially in the last year, which provide for some degree of government supervision over trade-association practices and some measure of compulsory control within each industry. These plans vary widely, and the degree of government control proposed is also by no means the same. In some of them, a relaxation of the present anti-trust legislation is considered to be sufficient inducement to meet the problem of the small minority within each industry which might continue competitive practices of price-cutting. THE DEVELOPMENT OF TRADE ASSOCIATIONS 

There are many trade associations in the United States which were founded more than fifty years ago. The New York Chamber of 

Commerce, which received its charter in 1770, was formed "to carry into execution, encourage and promote by just and lawful ways and means such measures as will tend to promote and extend just and lawful commerce,” and has served as the model for nearly all the chambers of commerce which have subsequently been established in this country. The Chamber of Commerce of the United States, founded in 1912, and the National Association of Manufacturers, formed in 1895, have for some time been recognized as the outstanding national associations of organized employers in this country, the leaders in the former being usually affiliated with power and banking groups, while those of the latter are always manufacturers. It was only during the war, however, that associations of business men according to their special interests began to grow in important num bers. The War Industries Board, under Chairman Bernard Baruch, provided the first tangible inducements to small manufacturers to cooperate in its dispensation of government assistance and licenses. 

Priorities were established governing the supply of transportation, raw materials and other needs, and recognized industrial associations were naturally given preference in their allocation. 

There is at present no single national organization which represents the many smaller groups that have grown within individual industries since the war. The American Trade Association Executives is a federation of the managers of leading business organizations, but it does not represent the entire field. There is no single publication devoted to the problem of trade associations. Several organizations, however, have from time to time compiled information on the growth of trade associations.

Number of Trade Associations 

The most complete source of information at present is the files of the Department of Commerce at Washington. The files of this 

Department listed, in September, 1931, approximately 20,000 commercial and industrial organizations in the United States. About 

3,000 of these, such as the Academy of Political Science or the American Asiatic Association, are not, strictly speaking, commercial. 

About 11,000 are local in their activities, while an additional 2,800 operate within individual state boundaries. Finally, there are roughly 2,300 which are national in membership and in operation. 

It is this final category with which most discussion of trade associations in connection with national affairs is concerned. 

The most numerous group within this last category is unquestionably that of the manufacturers. Over one-half of the strongest trade associations in the United States are in the manufacturing industries. 

Next in number are the associations of raw-material and foodstuff producers. Organization has progressed, on the whole, more rapidly and more effectively among wholesalers than among retailers. Professional groups have set up relatively few but effective associations, and the financial field, although it lists few associations, is one of the most highly organized.

The Chamber of Commerce

United States. Nearly 500 trade associations are listed among the 

24,869 members of the national Chamber, and a wide range of services are designed especially for their use. A detailed program of cooperation between the Chamber and trade associations has been worked out in recent years. Most of the associations in this country which are national in scope have their headquarters in New York, although Washington and Chicago are also important centers. General Activities 

A survey made at the end of 1932 by the Trade Association Department of the Chamber of Commerce of the United States was intended to reveal the present activities of these organizations, and the relative importance attached by them to different lines of association work. The survey took the form of a study of 500 organizations, of which 313 were national or international in scope, 59 regional, 75 state, and 53 local. Of the total, 227 were manufacturing or extractive associations; 27 were wholesale distributor associations; 

59 were made up of retailers; while 27 were financial or professional and 94 were “vertical" associations, embracing more than one type of membership.10

The most frequently listed activity, the holding of conventions and meetings, was claimed by 428 of the 500 associations reporting. 

After this, in order, were publications, engaged in by 73 per cent of the total; cooperation with other associations, listed by 70 per cent; establishment of business standards, listed by 61 per cent. Statistical service of some kind was rendered by 59 per cent, and legislative

activity was engaged in by 58 per cent of the 500. Combating unfair competition, with 53 per cent, publicity, with 48 per cent, and public education, with 40 per cent, were next in order. Cooperative buying was reported by only 34 associations, or 7 per cent, employeremployee relations by 15 per cent, and technical research by 28 per cent. The wide variety of activities engaged in by different trade associations is indicated by the fact that there were listed in all 68 separate kinds of activity, and that each association reported an average of 17 separate varieties.

Another indication of the types of activity most favored by trade associations is provided by the annual award of the American Trade Association Executives. This was first offered in 1929, when it was won by the National Automobile Chamber of Commerce. Subse quent winners were the National Association of Dyers and Cleaners and the American Paint and Varnish Manufacturers Association. 

In 1932, the award was given to the Cotton Textile Institute, for "its persistent and constructive methods in the elimination of undesirable living conditions for its thousands of employees; its introduction of modern cost accounting in cotton mills; its development and promotion of new uses for cotton and cotton products; and for the example it sets to American business of a trade association which is helpful to industry and public alike."¹¹ 

In the same year, the National Retail Dry Goods Association won honorable mention for a program of research and application in the field of department store merchandising and financing. The American Dry Milk Institute was similarly mentioned for its development of new markets for its members, the Southwestern Ice Manufacturers Association for educating the employees of ice companies in the selling of their product, and the Tanners Council of America for a program of consumer education in the various grades of leather. 

Publications 

Over 200 national trade associations in the United States publish weekly or monthly magazines of material size. Nearly all of them publish bulletins and reports, pamphlets, catalogues, trade directories or customer registers. Much of the material contained in these magazines is, of course, promotional. The editorial content is geared directly to the professional interests of the member companies, and those periodicals have been most successful in the past which have most effectively contrived to present trade information of a useful character. Statistical Service 

The activities of trade associations which are reported in these publications range over almost the entire field of management. Cost accounting, industrial and commercial research, simplification and standardization of product, credit information, traffic and transportation problems, trade ethics and promotion are all included. The most important of these activities are made available to the member companies through the statistical services which have become an increasingly important part of the functions of trade associations. The rapid growth of these statistical services was noted in the survey of American business entitled Recent Economic Changes as one of the distinguishing characteristics of present-day industry in the United States. "Perhaps the growth of trade associations and the expansion of their programs is the clearest evidence of the new attitude." 

No feature of trade association work has enlisted more complete support than this. Mr. Robert P. Lamont, former Secretary of Commerce, speaking before the 18th Annual Convention of the Chamber of Commerce of the United States in May, 1930, said: "Great as has been the progress in the development of current statistical measures, there is still much that needs to be done. It seems to me that the time is now at hand when business men should get behind a policy for the further development of needed statistics on produc- 14

tion, stocks, costs, employment, construction industry and many other activities which would make it possible to keep business informed currently as to the basic facts upon which policies must be predicated."12

Especially during the experimental period of trade association development, the collection and publication of industrial and commercial statistics have proved to be activities on which business men of different views as to the efficacy of cooperation could afford to agree. Mr. Myron W. Watkins, of the National Industrial Conference Board, wrote in 1928: "Few will contend that the unwarranted and excessive price variations under conditions of disorganized and spasmodic distribution may not be minimized by making available to all producers full information upon the basic factors in the market. Trade associations cannot compel adherence to sound marketing policies, but they can do a great deal to encourage the general adoption of such policies by making known the actual facts. Business judgment can then be trusted to do its part, as a rule.”¹³ 

The development of this activity has been considerably strengthened by judicial decision that it is not necessarily in violation of anti-trust legislation. In 1925, the Supreme Court of the United 

States, in its opinion in the Maple Flooring case, ruled: "We decide only that trade associations or combinations of persons or corporations which openly and fairly gather and disseminate information as to the cost of their products, the volume of production, the actual price which the product has brought in past transactions, stocks of merchandise on hand, approximate cost of transportation from the principal points of shipment to the points of consumption, as did these defendants, and who, as they did, meet and discuss such information and statistics without, however, reaching or attempting to reach any agreement or any concerted action with respect to prices or production or restraining competition, do not thereby engage in unlawful restraint of commerce.

Public Relations 

Grouped under the general title of education, a wide range of trade association activities have to do primarily with safeguarding the interests of the producers or manufacturers in their public relations. Embodied in the code of ethics or rules of business conduct which are adopted by most trade associations, there is always a recognition of the public responsibilities of the industry and the need to observe these responsibilities. The public-relations work of the trade association is in large degree related to this. It takes many forms in practice. It includes general promotional and advertising work aimed at the consumer, either to discover or to initiate trends and changes in consumption habits, and sometimes to promote more general understanding of the nature and possible uses of a given product. It also includes activities commonly described as "lobbying," designed to secure favorable treatment of member companies in legislation on taxation, tariffs, etc. 

Of the 500 trade associations recently surveyed by the Chamber of Commerce of the United States, only 85, or 17 per cent, actually maintained representatives at Washington.17 All of the trade associations, however, are represented in this regard through their membership directly, or indirectly through their members, in such organizations as the Chamber of Commerce of the United States or the National Association of Manufacturers. The influence of trade associations is thus brought to bear on the government itself and on public opinion in general in regard to questions both of foreign relations and of domestic policy.

In 1924, the Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce began to apply to the promotion of domestic commerce some of the experience it had already gained in the foreign field. Increasingly since that date, this work has come to be concerned closely with the work of trade associations. Much of this cooperation is in research. One trade association, for example, has used the Department of Commerce in a study of the comparative costs of different selling methods. Weaknesses of Trade Associations 

The weaknesses of trade associations are, perhaps inevitably, legion, and there is little substantial agreement as to their relative importance. The cause of failure most commonly referred to, fear of prosecution by the Government under the anti-trust laws, has not deterred some trade associations, notably those in paint, lumber, retail dry goods, hardware, automobiles, the law, banking or soap, from achieving a position of usefulness to their members. A more important cause is undoubtedly the reluctance of individual manufacturers to realize the advantages to be gained from industrial cooperation. 

Specific causes for the failure of many trade associations can for the most part be traced to this underlying reason. Too many associations have concentrated their activities to an excessive extent on lobbying, and on the less reputable features of political activity. 

Statistical work, on the other hand, has been relatively neglected, and it is only in recent years that trade associations have begun to realize the possibilities of this work. The outstanding work of the 

American Petroleum Institute, the United Typothetae, the Tanners

Council and the National Lumber Manufacturers Association in this regard has done much to strengthen the case for statistical services. 

Personnel and financial problems are still further causes for the failure of trade associations. Competent managers are still difficult to find, and trade-association funds are not as yet such as to attract outstanding individuals to this type of work. A stronger central organization of trade organizations, with its own publication, would undoubtedly help the movement, as would a better direction of association activities and reduction of internal dissensions between members.

Finally, much of the weakness as well as the strength of the trade-association movement has been drawn from the character of American business life itself. The felt need for efficiency devices

LEGAL ASPECTS OF TRADE ASSOCIATIONS 

The development of trade associations in the last two decades has been intimately connected with the history of trusts and anti-trust legislation. The merger movement itself, one writer has stated, has grown primarily "to enable competing companies to exercise as a single corporation the stabilizing influence upon prices, production, and division of territory which the law forbids them to exercise by agreements as independent units."22 At the same time as large corporations have sought stabilization through merger, however, the trade associations have provided another means to the same end. 

Professors E. F. Gay and Leo Wolman, reporting for the President's 

Research Committee on Recent Social Trends, write: "Added to the unusual number of outright consolidations, mergers, and varieties of holding companies, the period since 1920 was featured by the organization of a multitude of informal price controls and trade associations hoping to achieve by gentlemen's agreements, in part at least, the goals and benefits of actual concentration in ownership and management."23 

Anti-trust Legislation

The legislative background against which trade associations have developed begins with the Sherman Act of July 2, 1890, intended to 

22 Williams, J. H., "The Reign of Error," Atlantic Monthly, v. 147, June, 1931, p. 791. 23 

Recent Social Trends in the United States, New York, 1933, v. 1, p. 250. 21

protect trade and commerce against unlawful restraints and monopolies. Real enforcement of this act, beginning in 1903, coincided in large part with the beginning of the movement for consolidation of industrial enterprise in this country. The "rule of reason" interpretation in 1911 in the Standard Oil case is generally regarded as the first step in the gradual relaxation of the more stringent provisions of this legislation, but it was followed in 1914 by additional laws directed toward more effective control of consolidations and combinations. The Federal Trade Commission Act of September 

26, 1914, "to create a Federal Trade Commission, to define its powers and duties, etc.," set up a quasi-judicial and administrative body whose chief functions are to prevent unfair competition, to find facts in aid of legislation, and to report facts in regard to alleged violations of the anti-trust laws. Finally, the Clayton Act of October 15, 1914, "to supplement existing laws against unlawful restraints and monopolies," completed the legislative defense against the danger of monopoly.

The history of anti-trust legislation since 1914 has been one of judicial interpretation of these laws rather than one of additional legislation. Mergers and trade associations have both been used to accomplish some of the goals ordinarily achieved through largescale consolidation, but this has not prevented a recurrent movement of protest against the restrictions of these laws. This movement has reached its heights in times of depression, during the depression of 1921 and at the present time, and has relatively subsided during prosperous years. The increasing tendency toward concentration of control in industry, however, does not appear to have been arrested, and a continuation of protest against the anti-trust laws is to be expected in the future.

Judicial Interpretation 

The advantages of cooperation through trade associations were quickly apparent to some business concerns, and in several industries, under the guise of codes of business ethics and statistical interchange between companies, fairly complete market and price stabilization programs were worked out. In 1921, the American Hardwood 

Manufacturers Association, which had been a leader in the new movement, was declared illegal by the United States Supreme Court. 22

"This is not the conduct of competitors ," the Court ruled, "but is clearly that of men united in an agreement to act together to restrict production and increase prices. 

Four years later, in the Maple Flooring Manufacturers Association and the Cement Manufacturers Protective Association cases, the Supreme Court issued rulings which indicated that the collection and distribution of price and production statistics, so long as no agreement was reached or sought, was not illegal. "It is not, we think, open to question," the opinion stated, "that the dissemination of pertinent information concerning any trade or business tends to stabilize that trade or business and to produce uniformity of price and trade practice. Exchange of price quotations of market commodities tends to produce uniformity of prices in the markets of the world. Knowledge of the supplies of available merchandise tends to prevent overproduction and to avoid the economic disturbances produced by business crises resulting from overproduction. But the natural effect of the acquisition of wider and more scientific knowledge of business conditions on the minds of individuals engaged in commerce and its consequent effect in stabilizing production and price can hardly be deemed a restraint of commerce or if so it cannot, we think, be said to be an unreasonable restraint or in any respect unlawful. "It is the consensus of opinion of economists and of many of the most important agencies of government that the public interest is served by the gathering and dissemination in the widest possible manner of information with respect to the production and distribution, cost and prices in actual sales, of market commodities because the making available of such information tends to stabilize trade and industry, to produce fairer price levels and to avoid the waste which inevitably attends the unintelligent conduct of business enterprise.' Putting an end to injurious practices and the consequent improvement of the competitive position of a group of producers is not a less worthy aim and may be entirely consonant with the public interest, where the group must still meet effective competition in a fair market and neither seeks nor is able to effect a domination of prices.'

NATIONAL PLANNING 

It has been suggested that the trade association movement has in the past thirty years been closely connected with periods of prosperity and depression. It has perhaps been inevitable that business men and manufacturers should turn to cooperation in times of falling profits, and that a renewal of active industrial expansion should bring with it a reassertion of individual competition. Much of the organization, as well as the activity, of trade associations has been vague, and it could hardly be said that the movement as a whole has been developed with any real understanding of its public interest. When an industry, or a group of concerns within an industry, has found it profitable to cooperate for certain limited objectives, a trade association has developed, and its subsequent growth has been determined principally by the degree of profit realized by its members and the advertising skill of its promoters. During two major periods of crisis, however, the movement has taken on, at least temporarily, a more definite character. During the war, trade

Coordination within Each Industry

The major achievements of the past few years in trade association development have been within individual industries. The opposition to association activity has notably weakened, and although many organizations have suffered from reduced funds, cooperation has on the whole been strengthened. A special committee of the Chamber of Commerce of the United States reported on February 6, 1933, that "the activities now carried on by many trade associations aid management in promoting company planning, and can, by enlargement and adaptation, constitute a fundamental basis upon which an industry-planning program can be built. While modification of the anti-trust laws has been recognized as needed to enable joint action in some directions, it is not necessary to delay the adoption of many constructive activities pending such modification."'80

Coordination between Industries 

At the very start of the present depression in the United States, there was an appreciation of the need for some sort of Federal assistance in bringing about better self-regulation of business. The issue was placed squarely before industry when President Hoover, at the initiative of the Chamber of Commerce of the United States, called together the National Business Survey Conference in December, 1929. This conference, with all of the 400 largest trade associations in this country as active participating members, made monthly surveys of the national business situation throughout the early part of 1930.

Although this conference was hailed at the time as the greatest voluntary effort of American business without Federal direction in decades, perhaps the comment of a former president of the Chamber made a year later was not undeserved: "The difficulty has always been to secure some leadership for such a movement, which would command general confidence. In general, the men who could be elected to have such leadership were already so over-burdened or engaged in so many enterprises of a public character that they were unwilling to take on anything more. . . . The conference was called by the Chamber of Commerce of the United States in the fall of 1929, after the market collapse. . .. Steps were taken toward the appointment of a general guiding committee . . . but it never did work out, and the organization was not carried through."32 

National Industrial Recovery Bill 

The most recent development in the history of trade associations in the United States is embodied in the National Industrial Recovery Bill introduced this year into the 73rd Congress by Senator Wagner and supported by the Administration. Before the bill is passed, it is too early to appraise its real significance. The direction in which it points, however, is clearly indicated by its first section: "It is hereby declared to be the policy of Congress to remove obstructions to the free flow of interstate commerce which tend to diminish the amount thereof, and to promote the organization of industry for the purpose of cooperative action among trade groups, Establishment of National Economic Council, op. cit., p. 485. 29

to induce and maintain united action of labor and management under adequate governmental sanctions and supervision, to eliminate unfair competitive practices, to reduce and relieve unemployment, to improve standards of labor, and otherwise to rehabilitate labor and to conserve natural resources."33

A trade association within any industry is authorized under the proposed bill within two years to prepare a code of fair competition which sets forth the standards covering trade and competitive practices within that industry. Such a code is without effect until approved by the President, who may impose a code of fair competition upon any industry which is unable or refuses to cooperate, and who in extraordinary circumstances may subject the industry to Federal licensing. The President is further empowered to establish an industrial planning and research agency. After any code has been approved, it shall become the standard of fair competition for the industry to which it applies, and concerns refusing to submit to the code will be prosecuted under the anti-trust legislation as engaging in methods of unfair competition. The bill contains safeguards of the rights of labor to organize. 

Even before the bill has been passed, numerous trade associations have proceeded to draft codes for submission to the Government. The public statements of trade association officials have for the most part been favorable to the proposed legislation, and there is little doubt but that its provisions will be used by the majority of organized industries in this country if the bill is passed. 

At the same time, opposition has been voiced by manufacturers, particularly to the labor and the licensing clauses of the bill. The 

National Association of Manufacturers has expressed disapproval of the collective bargaining provision, claiming that this will result in "closed shop" practice and penalize American manufacturers in foreign markets.34

Those trade associations which are engaged solely in export trade were exempted from many of the provisions of the anti-trust laws in 1918. How far their experience can be used as a prediction of

what may be the immediate developments for domestic trade associations is uncertain. Their ability to fix and control prices, to agree on production schedules, to cooperate in sales efforts, and to enter into international agreements has not protected them from the effects of the depression. On the other hand, several of them have established precedents and machinery which may prove to be helpful if the present trend of association development continues. 

Many of the domestic trade associations, moreover, represent industries which secure their raw materials or sell their products in foreign markets. Just so far as trade associations in the future widen their cooperative and statistical services into some form of planned industrial control, their importance in international affairs will rise. 

In the exchange of commodities which links the nations of the Pacific, the interests of private and competing merchants or concerns will tend to be replaced by the planned operation of associated industries under some form of government control. In place of price wars and competitive bidding between American concerns abroad, one may expect, if this trend continues, an allocation of markets, production quotas, price schedules, both in export trade and in domestic manufacture, which cannot fail to have large importance for international economic relations. 

This trend toward industrial cooperation is by no means confined to the United States. Similar organizations have developed very rapidly abroad in recent years. Few examples of such cooperation abroad have grown without some measure of government assistance and control, and their influence, not only on economic relations between nations but also on international politics, has steadily grown. 

The development of trade associations in the United States has taken place up to the present time solely in domestic terms. The present situation, however, provides an opportunity for testing the strength of such industrial organizations in problems of an international character. The economic interdependence of Pacific nations has brought the Silk Association of America into direct contact with the agricultural problems of Japan, and American manufacturers' associations into close relations with Canadian markets. With any increase in the importance of trade associations in the national life of the United States, there will come inevitably an increase in their significance to international affairs. 31
