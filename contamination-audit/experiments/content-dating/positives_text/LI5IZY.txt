Section I Development of Our Highway Network 

The inadequacy of our present network of highways is a matter of common knowledge. Traffic jams, insufficient parking space, frequent detours, and worn-out surfaces serve the motoring public as indices of the situation, just as traffic counts, sufficiency analyses, accident rates, transportation costs and other technical indices serve the expert. In spite of record expenditures for highways, the situation has reached a critical stage. The degree of inadequacy of our highway plant raises three general questions: first, how did the situation develop; second, how serious is it; and third, what should be done to remedy it?

The origin of much of our highway plant can be traced to our earliest history. Following Indian trails, meandering streams and avoiding natural obstacles, rudimentary roads were built to accommodate horse-drawn vehicles between widely scattered communities. The development of railroads and canals took place when our population was increasing rapidly and the "industrial revolution" overtook America. Development of these forms of transportation to meet long haul requirements and the absence of other impetus combined to restrict road construction and design for at least half a century. 

By 1890, lands away from the railroad had largely been settled, and farmers began to demand all-weather roads. At about the same time, bicycling became a popular fad. Between them, farmers and cyclists succeeded in launching the "good roads movement" which was expanded to vast proportions because of the development of the automobile. 

Until 1891, support of roads was purely a local responsibility. In that year, New Jersey began to provide state aid to counties for road building, and

by 1917 all other states had similar laws. Parallel with this development came the establishment of state highway departments responsible for constructing and maintaining state highway systems. 

Congress initiated the policy of federalaid in the Highway Act of 1916. In 1921 the states were requested to select a system of principal rural interstate and intercounty highways which became the rural portion of the federal-aid primary system. mud.

These developments coincided with the campaign to get the farmer out of the In the two decades 1920-1940, a million miles of hard-surfaced highways were constructed on roadbeds which, for the most part, had been selected to accommodate traffic of earlier eras. Until the '30s little attention was paid to such factors as width, grades and curves in the improvement of highways. 

Only then, as the number, speed, weight and width of vehicles continued to increase, were curves rounded, steep grades cut down and sight distances improved.

Despite the increasing strain on the highway plant, the United States entered World War II with a highway system substantially adequate to the needs of the day. All this changed in a few short years. While total vehicular mileage declined during the war, vehicle miles of travel of the heaviest vehicles increased, and their heavy loads made wear and tear on the roads even worse than normal. Yet materials and manpower shortages severely limited the amount of highway work that could be done. Only the most necessary construction could be undertaken; roads scheduled for replacement had to be kept in service; even normal maintenance was severely curtailed. Transportation, including highway transportation, played a major role in winning the war, but the damage to roads was heavy.

The end of the war served as a signal to open the floodgates to a tremendous increase in highway use. Total vehicle miles of travel--333 billion in 1941 and

Section III Steps Being Taken to Meet the Situation 

The states and their subdivisions and the federal governement were not unprepared to deal with an increased highway use demand following World War 

II. 

Plans and surveys were drawn and large amounts of money were available to undertake a big road-building program. Unfortunately, shortages of men, machinery and materials delayed the start of the program and inflation reduced it in size.

Despite these handicaps, the several levels of government were able to devote almost 50 per cent of their combined direct highway expenditures to construction in 1947--an increase from 25.6 per cent in 1945. Since then the proportion of the highway dollar devoted to construction has been raised each year, to the 1954 estimate 61.8 per cent. In current dollars, capital outlay expenditures rose from $368 million in 1945 to an estimated $3,729 million in 1954. Total expenditures during the same period rose from $1,434 million to $6,037 million. (See Appendix, Figure 7.)

State and local governments have added greatly to their total highway debt since 1945. Proceeds of state and local bond issues rose from $62 million in 1945 to $1,922 million in 1954. In both 1953 and 1954, it is estimated, the proceeds of bond issues have represented more than 25 per cent of the total revenues for highway and street purposes--or about one-third as much as represented by the total of highway-user and other taxes, tolls, federal-aid and miscellaneous receipts. On the other side of the ledger, despite an increase in obligations retired, total direct expenditures have -9-

been raised even more rapidly, so that the former represented in 1953 and 1954 less than 6 per cent of total disbursements. (See Appendix, Figure 8.)

Of the volume of bonds issued, the several states issued by far the larger part. State bond proceeds totaled only $11 million in 1945 but were $1,539 million in 1953 and are expected to reach $1,602 million in 1954. 

Over the 1945-1953 period, total state obligations for highways outstanding rose from $1,417 million to $4,560 million. 4/ (See Appendix, Figure 9.)

The major reason for the rapid acceleration in state highway debt has been the construction of toll roads. With the exception of the original section of the Pennsylvania Turnpike--160 miles--virtually all of the mileage of toll roads now in operation--1,152.5 miles--has been built since the war. 

The total mileage of toll roads under construction is 1,439.3. These are superior facilities and on the average their building cost or projected cost exceeds $1.5 million per mile. With relatively unimportant exceptions, all toll roads built or under construction have been financed by the issuance of state bonds.

The cost of building toll roads authorized but not yet under construction-a total of 3,708 miles--is estimated to be more than $3.5 billion. In addition, a total of 2,640 miles is projected or is being investigated; although the cost of building this mileage cannot now be estimated, it probably will be about $1 million per mile. Given these data, it is apparent that the states do not feel that they have as yet exhausted toll road possibilities. To pay for their expanded highway efforts, the states have increased user tax rates. The trend has been in the direction of increasing state motor fuels levies. In 1945, among all states and the District of Columbia, the range in gasoline tax rates was from 2 to 7.5 cents per gallon, the average about 4.51 cents. Other highway-user tax rates have also increased. Registration fees for automobiles, trucks and busses were raised in many jurisdictions. Gross receipts, weight-distance and other special taxes on heavier vehicles were levied by many states. In still other states the rates of these special taxes were increased. 

At the federal level, the authorization for federal grants-in-aid of highways was set at $500 million for each of the first three post-war fiscal years, 1946, 1947 and 1948. No authorization was made for fiscal 1949. For the fiscal years 1950 and 1951 the amount authorized was $450 million; for fiscal 1952 and 1953, $500 million. For fiscal 1954 and for the current fiscal year, $575 million is the annual authorization, including the first specific authorizations for the interstate system--$25 million annually. 

The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1954 (Public Law 350, 83rd Congress) authorizes an annual federal grant program for fiscal years 1956 and 1957 of $875 million, including $175 million for the interstate system. (See Appendix, Figure 12.)

Federal responsibility for highway construction is not limited to grants to states. Public Law 350, for example, authorizes annual expenditures of $57 million for fiscal years 1956 and 1957 for construction of forest highways, forest development roads and trails, parkways, Indian roads, and public lands highways.

The large amounts of money made available for highway construction from federal, state and local sources have achieved substantial results. For instance, in Michigan, the legislature in 1950 authorized the issuance of $200 million in Limited-Access Highway Revenue Bonds. In November 1951, $80 million of these bonds were issued to obtain funds for construction of two expressways, the total estimated cost of which was $134 million, with most of the difference--$54 million--expected to be made up by federal funds. To meet debt service requirements, the state pledged a minimum of $2.5 million annually, and Wayne County and the city of Detroit $1.25 million annually. 

It was anticipated that highway-user funds and federal aid payments would amortize the debt over a 25 year period. The 23 miles of expressway will be finished in five years, a job that could not have been done on a pay-asyou-go basis in less than 15.

In Texas, in five major cities, large urban expressways are being build as cooperative ventures of the cities, the counties, the state and the federal government. Generally speaking, the design and construction costs are being paid for from state and federal funds. In all of these projects, the cities are paying all right-of-way costs.

It is estimated that there are 3,348,000 miles of roads and streets in the United States. This most stable of highway statistics has increased about 

1 per cent since before the war. In rural areas there are 2,994,000 miles of roads; the remaining 354,000 miles constitute the urban networks. When the roads are categorized according to level of government with controlling authority and primary financial responsibility, the following breakdown ensues: state systems including local roads under state control encompass about 641,000 miles, of which 601,000 are rural and 40,000 urban. Local systems including county, town and township networks total about 2,315,000 miles; local systems of urban places extend 314,000 miles. The remaining 

78,000 miles--roads and highways on United States lands--are the complete responsibility of the federal government. (See Appendix, Figure 19.) 

About 670,000 miles of our highways are included in one of the several federal-aid systems. These roads are eligible for federal assistance in the form of grants to the states to be used in support of road construction only. The federal grants for highway construction are matched, normally

on a fifty-fifty basis, and administered by the states. 

About 70 per cent of the state systems, more than 440,000 miles of the 641,000 total, are included in one of the federal-aid networks. Most of the remaining state mileage consists of local roads for which the states have assumed responsibility. About 216,000 of the 2,315,000 miles of roads in the local-rural or county, town or township networks are included among the federal-aid systems. Of the 314,000 miles of urban roads under local control, less than 5,000 are eligible for federal aid. (See Appendix, Figure 20.)

The more important of the federal-aid systems, the primary network, includes 228,000 miles of highways, almost all in the state systems, More than 211,000 miles of federal-aid primary miles are designated rural and more than 16,000 urban. From the 228,000 miles of federal-aid primary roads, a total not to exceed 40,000 miles (of which nearly 38,000 already have been designated) constitutes the National System of Interstate Highways. As far as can be determined, all interstate roads are on state systems. The interstate network connects our major population centers and is especially significant for our over-all economy and national defense. Although constituting little more than 1 per cent of all roads and streets, the interstate system carries 19 per cent of all rural and 9 per cent of all urban travel. 

The federal-aid secondary system, some 436,000 miles of roads in January, 1953, constitutes the remainder of the federal-aid network. Included therein are the more important of our farm-to-market roads. 

Section 13 of the Federal Aid Highway Act of 1954 directs that a study be made of the cost of completing the several state and local road networks, according to standards prescribed for each system. When completed, results of the study are to be Presented in the form of a ten-year improvement program, 1955 to 1964, supplemented by cost estimates for the next twenty years.

The study contemplates an interstate system in which all parts would be more adequate for 1974 traffic needs by 1964. All new construction would be of high type pavement with geometric life expectancy of 30 to 40 years. 

Rights-of-way for additional lanes also would be reserved or acquired for all sections requiring additional lanes by 1984, and in all cases sufficient rights-of-way would be reserved for four-lane developments. Toll roads existing or under construction that meet these standards are to be regarded as interchangeable with the interstate system for purposes of this computation. 

The ten-year cost of bringing the interstate system to the prescribed standard is estimated to be almost $24 billion, of which about $13 billion would be expended on rural portions and more than $10.9 billion on urban sections.

For the rest of the federal-aid primary system, roads which are now or shall become critically deficient for 1964 traffic are to be rebuilt by 1964 to accommodate traffic needs ten years beyond the date of construction. 

Such construction, also of high type pavement, would have a thirty-year geometric life expectancy. Rights-of-way for any construction required by 

1974 would be reserved or secured by 1964. Capital outlay requirements for the federal-aid primary system, excluding the interstate system, are estimated to be $29.9 billion at current cost levels, about two-thirds to be expended on the rural segments and one-third on the urban parts. (See 

Appendix, Figure 21.)

The more important segments of the remaining systems are to be rebuilt and rights-of-way procured according to standards prescribed for the federalaid primary system, with two principal modifications. First, wherever justified, suitable lower type surfaces may be substituted for high type pavements; and second, new construction may be geometrically adequate for a

period as low as twenty years. The remaining roads, presumably those with an average daily traffic of fewer than 400 vehicles in rural areas and up to 5,000 vehicles in urban places--depending on the size of the cities--would be rebuilt by 1964 only to the extent required for acceptable local service. 

The ten-year capital outlay needs of such a program for the remaining systems would be: federal-aid secondary $14.8 billion; other rural roads $17.1 billion; and other city streets $15.6 billion. 

Given current revenue sources, prevailing tax rates, and expected proceeds from bond issues already authorized, federal estimates indicate that probable construction for the years 1955-1964 is $54.6 billion short of the more than $101 billion tabulation of need. It is expected that $46.8 billion for capital outlay would be derived in the following amounts from the sources indicated: $27.5 billion from state road-user revenue, $8.3 billion from federal funds, $6.3 billion from bond proceeds, and $4.7 billion from local property taxes and general revenues. Probably more than 80 per cent of the bond proceeds would represent state issues, largely for toll roads. 

Federally financed construction presumes highway grants to states for ten years at an annual level approximating the $875 million already prescribed by Congress for each of the fiscal years 1956 and 1957. (See Appendix, Figure 22.)

Section V Current Cost Factors 

It is not enough to review the statistics relative to motor vehicle ownership and use, estimate future highway use and, on the basis of the statistical record, project arithmetically the financial size of the current highway problem. As the previous section indicates, the deficiencies will not be remedied without expenditure of sums far greater than any arithmetical projection would indicate.

The first and most obvious cause for the great difference in cost between current and proposed construction on the one hand and completed construction on the other is inflation. Calculated on a base 1940 = 100, the composite mile index was 211.0 in the second quarter of 1954. This would indicate that to build a mile of highway in 1954 to the same construction and design standards as a similar mile of highway built in 1940 would require more than double the expenditure of the earlier year. (See Appendix, Figures 24 27.)

Inflation accounts for much of the difference in cost, but not all. 

Design standards adequate for many miles of highway in 1940 are not adequate for highways built in the same areas today. Curves must be rounded, grades cut down, traffic lanes widened and new lanes added if more vehicles, including many more heavy vehicles, are to use about the same number of miles of highway and travel at greater average speeds. These and other factors have forced the revision upward of design standards.

Roads constructed to accommodate the truck and bus traffic of prewar years would be inadequate today. In prewar years roads could be "paved" over existing roadbeds in many cases. This happy solution to the problem of building roads serves as a solution no longer. In 1940, 4,663,027 trucks and busses were -20-

registered. The 1954 estimate is over 10 million. 

Vehicle miles of travel of trucks and busses were estimated to be less than 53 billion in 1940. The 1954 estimate is double that. In 1940, trucks moved 57.8 billion ton-miles of goods on all rural roads; in 1953, 173.3 billion ton-miles. The average of carried loads was just under three tons in 1940; just over five tons in 1953. Trucks with axle weights under 18,000 pounds travelled less than 1.5 billion miles in an average prewar year (1936-1940) as against about 13.5 billion miles in 1953 • 

Trucks with more than 18,000 pounds axle weight in the average prewar year travelled about 375 million miles and in 1953 about 3.2 billion miles. 

Roadbeds must be stronger, shoulders wider, and more yards of concrete poured to handle today's and tomorrow's traffic. (See Appendix, Figures 28 and 29.)

One effect of the relative stability of tax rates has been to encourage state borrowing for large scale projects. In recent years, a device much used by the states has been the "authority" which issues bonds to finance toll road or bridge construction, such bonds not being backed by the state's "full faith and credit." Many miles of highway and many bridges have been constructed through this method of financing, but the costs have been greater than they might have been had the state's credit been pledged. 

An advantage claimed for this method of financing is that large projects can be undertaken with the assurance that sufficient funds will be available to complete their construction in the shortest possible time. This explaine in part the popularity of toll roads. On the other hand, to determine the distribution of available highway funds on a geographical basis may delay or prevent the construction of major projects.

Another obstacle in the way of an accelerated highway program is the debt limitation of state and local governments. In only five states are there no constitutional restrictions on borrowing; in three states minor procedural restrictions are imposed; in 20 states popular referenda are required; and in 

20 states the constitutions limit the borrowing permitted or prohibit borrowing. 

Constitutional limitations upon the authority of state legislatures to borrow take the form of specific dollar amounts in many cases. 

These range from $50,000 to $2 million and, in a few cases, up to $3.5 million for specific purposes. Debt limitations are tied to assessed valuation in three states and to the year's total appropriation in one. Seven state constitutions prohibit borrowing. In brief, given the large sums required for highway construction programs, the several types of constituticnal debt restrictions may limit or delay the start of any major program. At the local level, debt limitations defy simple cataloguing for easy explanation, but their net effect is similar to that of constitutional prohibitions imposed upon states. 6/ 

A combination of restrictive factors, among them rigid geographical allocation of available highway funds and local debt limitations, have contributed to a relative decline in local support of highway construction, Presumably the causes for the decline in local support vary in intensity from state to state, but the overall result has been to lower the local contribution from 32.2 per cent of the total in 1945 to 28 per cent in 1947, a more nearly normal postwar year, and to 24.5 per cent in 1954. Section VI A Cooperative Program for Highway Construction 

The highway problem confronting the several levels of governmens has been outlined. The data presented have shown the obsolescence of much of our present highway system and the great need for extensive rehabilitation. It is clear that expenditure of billions of dollars will be needed in the next decade in order to overcome existing highway deficiencies and build roads capable of meeting the demands of an increasing number of automobiles, trucks and busses. 

The National System of Interstate Highways, limited by law to 40,000 miles, includes only the most important of our many miles of roads and streets. 

Designated by Congress "to serve the national defense," the system includes our major road arteries of interstate commerce. In event of war, these highways would serve as possible routes of evacuation from our larger cities. The interstate system, in brief, represents that portion of our highway network most important to the federal government in meeting its responsibilities under the Constitution.

Closely related to the interstate system and the needs it is designed to fulfill is the problem of traffic congestion in urban areas. The movement of commerce is seriously impeded by the lack of what might be called "urban access roads"--arterial and radial routes which permit easy and rapid ingress and egress.

Grievous as this lack may be from the standpoint of commerce, it is far more serious for civil defense. Little imagination is required to picture the chaos that would result if it were necessary to evacuate one or several of our major centers of population on a few hours' notice. Adequate protection against an atomic or hydrogen holocaust requires not only an early warning net and strong air and ground defenses, but also the means to evacuate target cities. -25-

Construction of essential urban access roads will assist materially in meeting the last requirement. For the purposes of this report, therefore, the term "interstate system" includes essential urban access roads whether or not these roads are now on any federal-aid system and whether or not they are in existence.

It is proposed that the federal government assume primary responsibility for financing an accelerated construction program, and through the Bureau of Public Roads, for the establishment of general design and construction standards for the interstate system. State participation in financing construction of the system should be continued at about the amounts suggested in the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1954, i.e. about $140 million per year or $1.4 billion during the decade. It is further proposed that, in the allocation of federal funds, due credit be given for the funds expended by those states that, either from public or toll road revenues, have constructed satisfactory sections of the interstate system. The states or their political subdivisions, as each state may determine, will be responsible for actual construction, maintenance, administration and policing of the interstate system. Relative to the latter functions, no change in the present federalstate relationship is recommended.

In order to protect the investment in the roads, avoid protracted delay and permit immediate possession for highway right-of-way purposes; federal and state officials should confer relative to what sections of highways should be constructed as limited-access facilities or for which immediate possession of rights-of-way is desired. An agreement having been reached, state procedures should be employed in those states having effective statutes permitting immediate possession and limitation of access. In those which do not have effective laws for that purpose, the Bureau of Public

Roads should be given the power to acquire necessary real estate by condemnation. (This would require federal legislation.) Such real estate could be leased to the state for a nominal consideration. 

The federal interest in our network of highways is not limited to the interstate system--the first federal-aid highway act having been enacted in 1916, twenty-eight years before Congress directed that the interstate system be designated. No objective criteria exist to determine what financial responsibility the federal government should assume with respect to all the miles of roads and streets not a part of the interstate system. It is felt, however, that the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1916, as amended, reflects a reasonable approximation of the federal interest expressed in dollar terms and, therefore, that the federal government should continue to make grants to states for highways in sums about equal to those authorized by the Federal- 

Aid Highway Act of 1952, Public Law 413, 82nd Congress $550 million per year ($575 million less $25 million authorized for the interstate system). 

It is suggested further that existing apportionment formulas and sliding scale provisions for state matching be retained and that present federal-aid procedures as they pertain to federal supervision of the planning, construction and maintenance of projects be continued.

The general scope of the requirements is clear. Explanatory Note 

Figure 18 The solid line traces the growth of total vehicle mileage from year to year expressed as percentages of the 1940 total. Gross national product is shown by a solid line with dots. The points plotted for each year were derived from yearly totals of gross national product corrected for variations from 1940 price levels and then converted to a percentage of 1940 gross national product. The dashed line represents the depreciated value of the highway plant in each year, the net resulting from cumulative investment less cumulative depreciation. These yearly estimates of highway value are also reduced to 1940 dollars and then expressed as percentages of the value of the highway plant in 1940. 

The vehicle mileage curve, starting low in 1920, soon caught up with the curve of gross national product and continued in close parallel with it through the decade of the thirties. By that time, the use of motor vehicles was closely geared to the general economy. The curve of depreciated investment, also rising from low values, approached the two other curves in the early thirties and ran with them throughout the decade. During this decade our highways were most nearly adequate to existing demands.

With the onset of the war the three curves diverged. While gross national product rose sharply, vehicle miles dropped under the compulsion of tire and gas rationing; and the depreciated highway investment, affected by abrupt curtailment of construction, began a gradual decline. In this latter decline began the accumulation of deficiencies--compounded by the growing obsolescence of many of our best constructed roads, few of which were designed to accommodate the revolutionary and continuing increase in traffic since the war.

The close juxtaposition of the three index curves which existed during most of the thirties suggests a condition which must probably subsist in any period of appropriate balance between road usage and the facility provided. If this is true, the highway problem for the years ahead is to achieve a rate of new highway investment adequate to raise the trend of our depreciated highway investment to the close parallel with vehicle growth existing in the thirties.
