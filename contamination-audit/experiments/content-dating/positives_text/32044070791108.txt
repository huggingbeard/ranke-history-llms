FOREWORD 

n understanding of our trade relations with other countries should be a part of every post-war plan. ning program.

It is impossible in a book this size to give all details of the trade agreements between the United States and various countries, but an attempt has been made to bring to your attention-reasons for such agreements; the phraseology of the Act which amended the Tariff Act of 1930, thereby giving authority to the Executive Branch of our Government to negotiate trade agreements; the dates of the signing of such agreements and day they became effective; the type of trade carried on between the United States and other countries; and concessions made by various countries and the United 

States. 

This material can be used in many forms-for round table groups, forums, study groups, and for individual study. 1

RECIPROCAL TRADE AGREEMENTS What is the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Program? 

An act by Congress, passed in 1934 and renewed by Congress in 

1937 and in 1940 for three-year periods.

Did this act authorize the President to enter into trade agree. ments with other countries? Yes.

With how many countries have we entered into agreement? 

Twenty-six-sixteen in the Western Hemisphere, nine in Europe, and one in the Near East. In the order of signature, the countries are as follows: Cuba, Brazil, Belgium, Luxembourg, Haiti, Sweden, Co lombia, Canada, Honduras, The Netherlands, Switzerland, Nicaragua, Guatemala, France, Finland, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Czechoslovakia,

Ecuador, the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland, Turkey, Venezuela, Argentina, Peru, Uruguay, and Mexico. Negotiations with Bolivia, Iceland, and Iran are under way. 

How much of the total world trade would be carried on in normal times among these twenty-six countries?

About three-fifths of the total world trade and more than that proportion of United States foreign trade is carried on with trade agreement countries.

Why was the Trade Agreements Act of 1934 passed? 

Because, under restrictive trade policies followed by the United States and nearly all other countries after World War I, international commerce had been strangled, obstructed and almost destroyed. 

What is the purpose of the agreements negotiated under the Act? 

To expand foreign markets for United States agricultural and industrial products and thus help to lift employment, production and buying power in the United States.

How do the agreements expand foreign markets for United 

States goods? 

1. By removing and reducing foreign tariff and other barriers which keep our goods out of foreign markets.

2. By lowering excessive United States barriers against imports from other countries, facilitating the sale of those products in the United States, and thus helping other countries to increase their purchasing power for Ünited States products. 

How and by whom are these agreements negotiated? 

Proposals in regard to an agreement are worked out after thorough study and public hearings, by an interdepartmental organization of some sixty committees and sub-committees representing Government agencies concerned with our foreign trade. They are then recommended to the President, through the Secretary of State, for his approval. The law provides that before concluding an agreement the President shall obtain the advice of the Departments of State, Agriculture, and Commerce, the Tariff Commission, and such other Government agencies as he may see fit to consult. After approval of the trade agreement proposals by the President, actual negotiations with representatives of the other country are carried on by the State Department. When the provisions of a trade agreement have been agreed upon by both countries, the agreement is signed, and after the necessary formalities have been complied with, the new tariff rates and other provisions of the agreement are proclaimed by the President.

Is the President limited in his authority relative to the provisions of these agreements? 

Yes, he is not authorized to— 

1. Reduce any United States import charge by more than 50%, nor 2. Shift any article from the dutiable to the free list or vice versa, nor 3. Conclude agreements without advice of the specific Govern ment agencies, nor 

4. Reduce United States tariffs within the 50% limit unless corresponding advantages for American export trade are obtained from the foreign country concerned, nor 5. Conclude any agreement without adequate public notice and full opportunity for any interested person to present views and information concerning the proposed agreement both at a public hearing and in writing.

What proportion of the United States foreign trade is carried on with trade agreement countries?

About 68%, on the basis of 1939 figures. About three-fifths of the international commerce of the world is carried on among countries, including the United States, that have signed trade agreements.

What has been the trend of our trade with agreement countries as compared to trade with non-agreement countries? 

Only one agreement was in effect throughout 1935. The period, 1938-1939, was the last two-year period before the present war. The 

United States exported that year, to the sixteen countries with which we had agreements in effect, 63% more than in 1934-1935. In the same period, exports to all non-agreement countries averaged only 31% higher than 1934-1935, or less than half the gain in exports to trade agreement countries. 

Does the United States gain, through a trade agreement, any tariff benefits for products other than those specified in the agreement? 66 Yes, many benefits. There is a mutual agreement that all United 

States products, whether enumerated in the agreement or not, receives as favorable treatment as is given the same or like product coming from any country other than the United States. Under this most-favored-nation" principle, hundreds of United States products not enumerated in trade agreements have been enabled to enter foreign markets on the basis of customs equality with the products of other countries which otherwise would have been admitted at a preferential rate with a consequent competitive advantage over United 

States goods. 

Are there any exceptions to this practice?

Yes. Because of the special economic and historical relations between the United States and Cuba, this country reserves, in agreements with countries other than Cuba, the right to accord preferential tariff treatment to Cuban products. Reciprocal tariff preference be tween the United States and Cuba have been in effect since 1902. On the other hand, the United States has agreed to certain recognized exceptions, of limited scope, requested by other countries. These exceptions affect only a relatively small part of world trade. 

Do reciprocal trade agreements protect United States exports to non-trade agreement countries against discrimination by those countries?

Yes. The desire of non-agreement countries to benefit to the extent of their natural ability from the trade agreement concessions made by the United States and applied to products of all countries that do not discriminate against United States trade, is a powerful incentive to them to refrain from such discrimination against Amer. ican exports. As a result, the United States exports have had valuable protection against inequality of tariff treatment in most countries of the world.

Do imports at reduced tariffs cause United States producers and workmen to lose their markets and their jobs to low cost foreign competition?

Although this is a highly controversial question, many specialists and economists say "No." "No." Concessions generally are made on products which either are not produced at all in the United States or are not produced in sufficient quantity to supply the American demand for them. Only a relatively small segment of American industry comes into direct competition with foreign goods in any case. The biggest and most profitable American industries-those paying the highest average wage scales-as well as the major branches of United States agriculture, are either on an exporting basis already meeting foreign competition in the world market, or are secure in their ability to supply all, or almost all, of the needs of the home market. 

These great branches of American agriculture and industry have nothing to gain, but much to lose, from high tariffs that throttle our export as well as our import trade. On the other hand, they benefit from the enlarged foreign markets which are made possible through the trade agreements. Then, greater activity, by increasing the purchasing power in the hands of American consumers, benefits, in turn, those American producers who are interested solely in the home market.

What facilities other than the Reciprocal Trade Agreements 

Program does the United States have for improving commercial relations with other countries?

There is no other mechanism established by law to perform the particular function of cooperative removal of trade barriers by reciprocal bargaining.

If Congress wanted to reduce United States barriers to imports, why did it not simply amend or repeal the tariff law instead of granting authority to the President to bargain, item by item, and make reductions piecemeal?

The selective reduction of our tariff rates through negotiations with foreign countries is a practical method of reducing trade barriers on both sides. No responsible person, not even the most academic economist unaware or unmindful of the power of specialinterest pressure groups, would advocate the sudden wiping out of all tariffs.

Doesn't the Constitution require all treaties with foreign coun. tries to be ratified by the Senate?

Yes, but the reciprocal trade agreements are not treaties. The 

Supreme Court has upheld the validity of executive agreements with

foreign governments covering matters not required to be handled under formal treaties. From the beginning of our history, the 

Executive has made such agreements. 

Do the trade agreements serve any useful purpose during war time?

Yes. A considerable amount of trade goes on under the influence of economic, rather than military factors, and such trade continues to benefit from the provisions of the agreements. 

Why not wait until after the war before renewing trade agreements?

It is obvious that conditions for international trade must be more favorable than in the past and no matter what new circumstances may arise, we should try to reduce trade barriers now so that there will be no obstacles after the war when foreign markets will be greatly needed.

Will the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Program alone solve the problems of post-war international commercial relations? 

No, but it is the keystone and the first practical step toward the solution of such problems.

Will the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Program help to raise the standards of living? 

Yes. Living standards depend on two things: 

1. Available supply of goods and services. 

2. The extent to which that supply is equitably distributed. It is believed by many that the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Program is a tested and effective mechanism for reducing or removing these obstructions as between nations, thus making possible higher living standards here and elsewhere. In short, it helps to increase available supplies and the purchasing power whereby consumers can share them.

Has the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Program any contribution to offer in the formulation of lasting peace plans? 

Yes. Many wars have had their origins largely in economic causes, as had this present war. No country has within its borders all of the natural resources and all the productive abilities it needs, therefore, it must trade for the things it needs, do without them, or fight for them. If denied this privilege, unrest, resentment and depression ensues.

It is believed that the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Program is an effective agency for removing obstacles and discrimination in trade which make such a situation possible. 

Can the United States terminate the agreements when and if it wishes to do so?

Yes. The law provides that each agreement must be subject to termination, on due notice to the other country concerned, not more than three years after it goes into effect. In addition, the agree ments themselves contain provisions permitting termination under special and unusual circumstances before the expiration of the initial period. If not terminated before the end of the initial period, the agreements remain in effect indefinitely, subject to termination by either government on six months' written notice or on shorter notice in special or unusual circumstances. 10

What general provisions are provided in the agreements? 

1. Reduction of import charges on selected products. 

2. Guarantees against increase of such charges on other products. 

3. Continued duty-free entry of certain products already on the free list. 

4. General assurances against discriminatory treatment in the regulation and control of imports through tariffs, quotas, exchange control, or otherwise.

Have the agreements increased trade with the Latin American countries?

Yes. In 1939, before high tariffs and other obstructions to trade between the United States and the Latin American republics had reached their peaks, the total trade between this country and the fifteen countries listed above was valued at $1,690,500,000. By 1932, less than two years after this country had erected a high tariff wall (Tariff Act of 1930), the value of trade between the United States 11

and these Latin American republics had shrunk to only $472,100,000, a drop of over 70% from the level of 1929. 

On what products have tariff rates been reduced?

Canned corned beef, cattle (in the agreement with Mexico, which is the only American republic from which cattle can be imported under existing sanitary laws and regulations), hides and skins, coarse wool, sugar, winter fruits and vegetables, certain ores and metals (including manganese, lead, zinc, and molybdenum), and petroleum.

Among those assured of continued duty-free entry are: coffee, cocoa, bananas, cinchona bark, certain dyeing and tanning materials, rub. ber sisal, henequen, and various other tropical products. 

How does low tariff or tariff free products benefit our country, as well as Latin America?

These concessions facilitate and stabilize the sale in this country of many products of the Latin American countries and enhance their buying power for products of the United States.

Commercial Convention of 1902: 

Under the Commercial Convention of 1902, dutiable imports from either country enter the other at tariff rates at least 20% lower than the rates on the same or like products imported from any other country. These preferential rates, as modified by the trade agreement, apply exclusively to trade between the United States and Čuba and are not generalized to any other country. This exception to the unconditional most-favored-nation policy adhered to by the United States has been recognized in the provisions of all the other reciprocal agreements concluded with countries other than Cuba, as well as in many commercial treaties of the United States. 

The general provisions of the agreement with Cuba not only guarantee the continuance of the tariff concessions included in the agreement but also cover such other questions as quotas, exchange control, internal taxation and various customs and administrative regu lations and formalities.

United States Concessions to Cuba 

Tariff concessions made by the United States on imports from Cuba, while applying to a relatively small number of individual products, nevertheless affected commodities which make up about 90 per cent, by value, of total imports into this country from Cuba. 

Prior to the war imports of sugar from Cuba into the United States were limited, under the provisions of the United States sugar program, to specified percentages of the estimated total requirements of this country. Although the quota provisions of the sugar program have been suspended, imports from Cuba are limited under present conditions by a shortage of shipping. Under the agreement, the reduced rates of duty on cigar tobacco applied to an annual quantity of imports of 22,000,000 pounds. 

The 2-cent per pound duty on 96-degree Cuban sugar, imposed by. the Tariff Act of 1930, was reduced to 1.5 cents per pound by Presidential proclamation effective June 8, 1934, and was further reduced to 0.9 cent per pound in the original trade agreement with Cuba. 

In the second supplementary agreement, effective January 5, 1942, the rate was still further reduced to 0.75 cent per pound. 

United States tariff reductions on grapefruit, lima beans, white or Irish potatoes, fresh tomatoes, cucumbers, eggplant, okra, peppers, and squash applied only during specified seasons other than those when the chief domestic crops of these products were moving to market.

Through the agreement, each country bound duty-free those products of the other which entered duty-free on the date of the signature of the convention in 1902, and the tariff preferences in the convention on dutiable imports from Cuba were continued in the trade agreement, having been increased in the case of some items. 

Cuban Concessions to the United States

Cuban concessions on imports from the United States take various forms. In some cases there were direct reductions of tariff rates. In others, the tariff preference given to imports from the Untied States was increased, and in some cases the two methods were combined. 

Certain of the concessions made by Cuba reduced or removed internal taxes on products of interest to the United States; others reduced certain customs charges other than ordinary tariffs. 

An important Cuban concession was the reduction of both the tariff and the consumption tax on lard. These charges were reduced, progressively, from a total of 10.18 cents per pound to 1.45 cents per pound. Cuban tariffs on cottonseed, corn, and soya-bean oils were sharply reduced and tariff preferences increased. On wheat flour the Cuban consumption tax was removed and increased tariff preference was accorded to flour milled from United States wheat. Duties on a large number of pork products were reduced. 18

United States Concessions to Mexico 

The United States grants lower duties on products of which this country's imports from Mexico in 1939 were valued at $8,900,000, or 16.4 per cent of the total value of imports from that country in that year.

The existing tariff treatment is bound on products of which imports from Mexico were valued in 1939, at $26,300,000, or 48.3 per cent of the total in that year. 

Existing duty-free treatment is bound on products of which United States imports from Mexico in 1939 were valued at $22,600,000. 

The reductions in the existing rates of duty granted to Mexico apply to such important products as petroleum; lead; zinc; mediumweight cattle; and tomatoes and certain other fresh winter vegetables. 

Lead, zinc, tomatoes, and cattle are subject to "emergency" duty reductions. The agreement provides that after the national emergency period the rates on zinc will return to the pre-agreement rates, and those on lead will be increased to points somewhat below preagreement levels. After the termination of the national emergency and the abnormal situation in respect of tomatoes, the rate of duty thereon will be increased to a level somewhat below the 1930 rate of duty. In the case of light and heavy cattle, the agreement removes, until after the emergency period, the limitations on the number permitted entry at reduced rates of duty. After the termination of the national emergency and the abnormal situation in respect to cattle and meats, these limitations will be reestablished and a limitation on reduced-duty imports of medium-weight cattle will be established; excess-quota imports of light and medium-weight cattle will be duti able at the 1930 rate of duty, and of heavy cattle at slightly less than the 1930 rate.

The agreement also reduces customs restrictions on imports of petroleum and fuel oil by extending to all imports of these products the reduced rate of import tax which formerly applied to only a limited quantity.

Among the products on which existing United States tariff rates are bound against increase in the agreement with Mexico, are vanilla beans, harvest hats, and hard-fiber cords and twines. 

Products bound on the free list in the Mexican agreement of which United States imports from Mexico were valued at more than $1,000,000 in 1939, are bananas, coffee, henequen, and crude chicle. 

The agreement also provides for reductions in United States duties on certain products of which countries other than Mexico have normally been the principal or important suppliers. Under the existing emergency situation imports of such products from Mexico will be facilitated. The pre-agreement rates on these products may be restored by the United States, in whole or in part, on six months' 24

written notice to the Mexican Government at any time after termination of the national emergency but may not be increased during the life of the agreement.

The agreement, therefore, gives the fullest recognition to the requirements of the United States for imports of essential and strategic materials and at the same time leaves the way open for proper ad. justments if and when such adjustments are required.

Nicaraguan Concessions to the United States 

Concessions made by Nicaragua applied to over 100 commodities or about 25 per cent of United States total exports to Nicaragua. 

Duty reductions applied to imports from the United States of hog lard, prepared milk, canned and dried fruits, canned vegetables, varnishes and ready-mixed paints, and medicinal preparations and proprietary medicines. Partial Termination of the Agreement 

Certain provisions of the trade agreement, including the reciprocal tariff concessions, ceased to be in force on March 10, 1938. This action was taken, after friendly discussions initiated by the Nicaraguan Government, in order to permit an increase in the conversion rate between the paper and gold cordoba of Nicaragua for customs collection purposes. The Nicaraguan Government was thus enabled to increase its revenue from imports at a time when a difficult financial situation was confronting the country. 

However, Nicaragua did not increase its basic tariff rates on the products of the United States that are enumerated in the agreement, and since all rates and customs treatment granted by the United States to Nicaragua had also been included in other trade agreements, trade between the two countries continued on about the same basis as when the agreement was fully in effect. The general provisions in regard to non-discriminatory treatment of each country's trade by the other remain in force.

AN ACT 

To amend the Tariff Act of 1930

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That the Tariff Act of 1930 is amended by adding at the end of title III the following: 

"PART III-PROMOTION OF FOREIGN TRADE 

"SEC. 350. (a) For the purpose of expanding foreign markets for the products of the United States (as a means of assisting in the present emergency in restoring the American standard of living, in overcoming domestic unemployment and the present economic depression, in increasing the purchasing power of the American pub. lic, and in establishing and maintaining a better relationship among various branches of American agriculture, industry, mining, and commerce) by regulating the admission of foreign goods into the United States in accordance with the characteristics and needs of various branches of American production so that foreign markets will be made available to those branches of American production which require and are capable of developing such outlets by affording corresponding market opportunities for foreign products in the United States, the President, whenever he finds as a fact that any existing duties or other import restrictions of the United States or any foreign country are unduly burdening and restricting the foreign trade of the United States and that the purpose above declared will be promoted by the means hereinafter specified, is authorized from time to time- "(1) To enter into foreign trade agreements with foreign governments or instrumentalities thereof; and "(2) To proclaim such modifications of existing duties and other import restrictions, or such additional import restrictions, or such continuance, and for such minimum periods, of existing customs or excise treatment of any article covered by foreign trade agreements, as are required or appropriate to carry out any foreign trade agreement that the President has entered into hereunder. No proclamation shall be made increasing or decreasing by more than 50 per centum any existing rate of duty or transferring any article between the dutiable and free lists. The proclaimed duties and other import restrictions shall apply to articles the growth, produce, or manufac ture of all foreign countries, whether imported directly, or indirectly: 

Provided, That the President may suspend the application to articles the growth, produce, or manufacture of any country because of its discriminatory treatment of American commerce or because of other acts or policies which in his opinion tend to defeat the purposes set forth in this section; and the proclaimed duties and other import restrictions shall be in effect from and after such time as is specified in the proclamation. The President may at any time terminate any such proclamation in whole or in part. 30

"(b) Nothing in this section shall be construed to prevent the application, with respect to rates of duty established under this section pursuant to agreements with countries other than Cuba, of the provisions of the treaty of commercial reciprocity concluded between the United States and the Republic of Cuba on December 11, 1902, or to preclude giving effect to an exclusive agreement with Cuba concluded under this section, modifying the existing preferential customs treatment of any article the growth, produce, or manufacture of Cuba: Provided, That the duties payable on such an article shall in no case be increased or decreased by more than 50 per centum of the duties now payable thereon. "(c) As used in this section, the term 'duties and other import restrictions' includes (1) rate and form of import duties and classification of articles, and (2) limitations, prohibitions, charges, and exactions other than duties, imposed on importation or imposed for the regulation of imports."

SEC. 2. (a) Subparagraph (d) of paragraph 369, the last sen tence of paragraph 1402, and the provisos to paragraph 371, 401, 1650, 1687, and 1803 (1) of the Tariff Act of 1930 are repealed.

The provisions of sections 336 and 516 (b) of the Tariff Act of 1930 shall not apply to any article with respect to the importation of which into the United States a foreign trade agreement has been con. cluded pursuant to this Act, or to any provision of any such agreement. The third paragraph of section 311 of the Tariff Act of 1930 shall apply to any agreement concluded pursuant to this Act to the extent only that such agreement assures to the United States a rate of duty on wheat flour, produced in the United States which is preferential in respect to the lowest rate of duty imposed by the country with which such agreement has been concluded on like flour produced in any other country; and upon the withdrawal of wheat flour from bonded manufacturing warehouses for exportation to the coun try with which such agreement has been concluded, there shall be levied, collected, and paid on the imported wheat used, a duty equal to the amount of such assured preference. (b) Every foreign trade agreement concluded pursuant to this Act shall be subject to termination, upon due notice to the foreign government concerned, at the end of not more than three years from the date on which the agreement comes into force, and, if not then termi. nated, shall be subject to termination thereafter upon not more than six months' notice. (c) The authority of the President to enter into foreign trade agreements under section 1 of this Act shall terminate on the expiration of three years from the date of the enactment of this Act. 

SEC. 3. Nothing in this Act shall be construed to give any authority to cancel or reduce, in any manner, any of the indebtedness of any foreign country to the United States. 31

SEC. 4. Before any foreign trade agreement is concluded with any foreign government or instrumentality thereof under the provisions of this Act, reasonable public notice of the intention to negotiate an agreement with such government or instrumentality shall be given in order that any interested person may have an opportunity to present his views to the President, or to such agency as the President may designate, under such rules and regulations as the President may prescribe; and before concluding such agreement the President shall seek information and advice with respect thereto from the United States Tariff Commission, the Departments of State, Agriculture, and Commerce and from such other sources as he may deem appropriate. 

Approved, June 12, 1934.
