PRE-TRIAL PROCEDURE 

This pamphlet has been prepared for the benefit of newly appointed United States District judges who may be interested in making use of pre-trial procedure in their courts. 

What is Pre-Trial? 

Pre-trial procedure is a common sense method of sifting the issues and reducing the delays and expense of trials so that a suit will go to trial only on questions as to which there is an honest dispute of fact or law. When efficiently conducted, it offers benefits to litigants, courts, attorneys and witnesses, and contributes to the efficiency of the administration of justice. It saves time and expense. It eliminates unnecessary attendance and long waits of witnesses. It accelerates trials. 

Rule 16 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure makes provision for pre-trial as follows: 

"Rule 16. Pre-Trial Procedure; Formulating Issues 

In any action, the court may in its discretion direct the attorneys for the parties to appear before it for a conference to consider (1) The simplification of the issues; (2) The necessity or desirability of amendments to the pleadings; (3) The possibility of obtaining admissions of fact and of documents which will avoid unnecessary proof; (4) The limitation of the number of expert witnesses; 

(5) The advisability of a preliminary reference of

issues to a master for findings to be used as evidence when the trial is to be by jury; (6) Such other matters as may aid in the disposition of the action.

The court shall make an order which recites the action taken at the conference, the amendments allowed to the pleadings, and the agreements made by the parties as to any of the matters considered, and which limits the issues for trial to those not disposed of by admissions or agreements of counsel; and such order when entered controls the subsequent course of the action, unless modified at the trial to prevent manifest injustice. The court in its discretion may establish by rule a pre-trial calendar on which actions may be placed for consideration as above provided and may either confine the calendar to jury actions or to non-jury actions or extend it to all actions." 

1. The simplification of the issues.

One of the basic objectives of pre-trial procedure is to take the trial of cases out of the realm of surprise and maneuvering. Apart from mere considerations of efficiency, it is vitally important to just decisions that parties should not be required to proceed to trial upon a welter of issues, some genuine and some frivolous, some material and some immaterial. 

Full discussion of the case by counsel under the guidance of the court goes far toward bringing out the real questions of fact on which the parties differ. It is highly desirable for the court to ascertain what needs to be tried and the points actually in dispute as distinguished from the formal and often general allegations in the pleadings. For this purpose not only the contentions of the parties but sometimes also

the evidence at their disposal must be submitted for preliminary examination by the court. Often in the course of a discussion of the case, counsel become aware that issues honestly believed to be material are not in the case at all, and that issues over-w looked during preparation are key points in the case. Moreover, the open, candid, give-and-take atmosphere of the pre-trial conference is a strong deterrent to insistence by counsel upondo points known to be without merit. In some courts the attorneys are asked to meet in advance of the pre-trial conference and stipulate agreed facts. In other courts they are asked to come into court with stipulations prepared on which they seek agreement. In still other courts, stipulations are drawn at the conference. 

Documents needed in the preparation of the case, if not obtained by discovery procedures under Rule 34 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, may be ordered produced at the pre-trial conference.

It is of utmost importance that the exact scope and terms of admissions and stipulations be reduced to writing before the pre-trial is ended. Documents whose genuineness is admitted should be promptly initialed by the pre-trial judge, the clerk, or by counsel, so that they may be introduced at trial without question as to genuineness. If these matters are not promptly and clearly recorded, misunderstandings and lapses of memory may undo all that has been accomplished at pre-trial. 

4. The limitation of expert witnesses.

Many experts are being used in trials of cases today. 

They should be limited in advance of trial. Counsel at pretrial may also agree to limit the number of witnesses other than experts.

5. Preliminary reference to a master.

Where accounts are complex and intricate, or the documents and other evidence voluminous, or where extensive computations are to be made, it is sometimes better practice to refer the matter to a special master or commissioner than for the judge to perform the task himself, and there is inherent power in the courts to compel a reference. In the Matter of

6. Such other matters as may aid in the disposition of the 

This part of the rule affords opportunity for a broad and flexible exercise of discretion. The pre-trial judge may determine whether the court has jurisdiction of the action. He may decide whether the case is moot, or not brought in good faith. 

All issues of fact may be resolved, and the case go to summary judgment. Motions filed both before and after pre-trial conference may be disposed of by the pre-trial judge. Issues may be separated and separate trials ordered as to each of them, or cases may be consolidated for trial, or third parties brought in. The pre-trial judge may rule on depositions, interro-

gatories, restraining orders, preliminary injunctions, bills of particulars, witnesses, disclosure of evidence, and motions for jury trial. He may require the submission of briefs on controlling points at a fixed date prior to trial to enable the trial judge to be better prepared to rule when the points arise. The utility and flexibility of this section is limited only by the imagination of the pre-trial judge. 

Settlement 

The settlement of cases is not a primary objective of pretrial conferences, but, when properly presented, it is an important by-product and often the logical result of pre-trial. 

If the judge has the technique and personality for it, if he is understanding and does not coerce settlement but constantly reminds the lawyers that they are entitled to their day in court, the proper atmosphere will be created to encourage compromise and settlement. When the impartial judge has discussed the issues and stated them in his own language, counsel may begin to have some misgivings for the first time as to the force of his case.

Counsel may be reluctant to initiate discussions of settlement because this may be considered an admission of weakness and lack of confidence in his case, but when there has been a full hearing of the case, the judge may raise the question

by asking whether settlement has been considered and may even go further and make discreet suggestions as to the possible outcome of a trial. If he has the impression that the figures of one side or the other are greatly out of line, he may ask counsel if they would consider some other amount or settle on a different basis. By narrowing the area of disagreement and pointing out the pitfalls of going to trial, the pre-trial judge can thus attempt to clear the way for a settlement advantageous to the interests of both parties.

Techniques of Pre-Trial Practice 

1. The scheduling of pre-trial conferences.

A calendar of cases ready for pre-trial should be prepared by the clerk of the court. The setting up of effective administrative rules for the holding of pre-trial conferences includes informing the bar, either by court rule or by a pre-trial notice, of what preparation is expected of the lawyers who attend the pre-trial conferences. Due notification must be given to the attorneys in each case of the time of their pre-trial hearing. 

Effort should be made to conserve the time of the bar by spacing pre-trials during the day and attendance, except on reasonable excuse, seasonably presented, must be insisted upon.

Sanctions may be used to enforce this where necessary. The conference must be attended by the lawyers who expect to try

the case and they should have authority to make stiuplations as to facts and issues and to agree to the form of the pre-trial order. 

Sample notices of pre-trial conferences and some pre-trial orders are attached. 

2. When should the pre-trial conference be held?

Many believe the best time for holding the conference is not long before trial, generally from one to three weeks in advance of the actual trial date. Counsel will then be fully prepared, knowing what they are going to prove, what the issues are, what documents are to be introduced, and what witnesses will be called. At the same time, it may be possible to secure stipulations which may obviate the calling of certain witnesses, some of whom may live at distant points. On the other hand, in certain types of complicated cases, if pre-trial is postponed until the eve of trial, the possibilities of eliminating unnecessary preparation and proof are largely destroyed. In these cases pre-trials may be required shortly after issue is joined.

In this way the court participates in preparing the course of the trial. 

3. Where should the conference be held?

Whether the conference will be held in open court or in chambers varies, and is largely a matter for each judge to deeide, depending upon local conditions, the nature of the case,

and the personality of the individual judge. The majority of federal judges questioned on this point favor pre-trial in chambers because of the informality of the atmosphere. There is, however, strong opinion among some of the leading advocates of pretrial in favor of hearings in open court, thus emphasizing that pre-trial is a court proceeding, lending stature to the procedure, omitting personal matters and discussions having no bearing upon the case, and avoiding cursory, unprepared, and indefinite discussions accomplishing nothing because of failure to deal objectively with problems of the case. Conferences in open court are also beneficial in educating the bar in the proper handling of pre-trial. 

4. In what types of cases is it most helpful? 

The calling of a pre-trial conference in any case lies within the complete discretion of the court. A number of federal courts require a pre-trial conference in all civil cases. The 

Pre-Trial Committee of the Judicial Conference of the United 

States has recommended establishment of a pre-trial calendar and the pre-trying of every civil case, but no attempt has been made to force the use of pre-trial in any district. 

There is general agreement that in complicated cases where there is voluminous documentary evidence, and especially in antitrust cases, pre-trial is not only helpful, but indis-

pensable, as noted in the reports of the Judicial Conference 

Committee on Procedure in Antitrust and Other Protracted

Cases. In the federal courts it has been widely used in negligence cases of all kinds, in federal rent control cases, and in a variety of contract actions and other types of litigation. The nature of the evidence to be produced and the experience of the individual judge will form the best criteria of the extent to which pre-trial will be helpful.

There is hardly a single federal judge who has not used the pre-trial conference to some extent. Information of the 

Administrative Office is that in 40 percent of all district courts, pre-trial is used regularly and in another 20 percent it is used fairly often. This includes every type of district, metropolitan, urban, and rural, districts with congested dockets and those fully up-to-date. The federal courts are well in advance of the state courts in this respect, as there are not over a dozen states where pre-trial is used extensively in the state courts, and New Jersey is the only state which requires pre-trial in practically every case in its trial courts of general jurisdiction. 

5. Preparation.

Pre-trial is a full-dress procedure which cannot progress

in an orderly and scientific manner unless the judge and the attorneys are fully prepared and have their objectives fixed firmly in their minds. The judge should prepare himself by carefully examining the pleadings and such papers as may be in the file and by familiarizing himself with the issues in the case. The lawyer who comes to the conference should be the one who expects to try the case and the attorneys should carefully review their cases, before attending the hearing. They should be especially certain respecting the exact issues of fact and of law involved so that they may declare their positions in the course of simplifying the issues. They ought to be aware how far, with their client's approval, they may go in the admission of facts and of documents and whether there is authority to make a settlement. 6. Procedure.

At the pre-trial conference the usual procedure is for counsel for the plaintiff to make a careful, concise, and factual statement of his case. The court may clarify any matters not set forth to its satisfaction by questions. Counsel for the defendant is then called on to fully state his case, pointing out the facts which will not be in dispute and the area of agreement and responding to any questions from the court. A general discussion follows under the judge's guidance with

the view of clarifying and narrowing the issues. Admissions and stipulations are made, the statements compared to the pleadings, documents and exhibits are marked, other matters may be considered, and the possibility of settlement explored. 

7. Entry of the pre-trial order. 

Rule 16 provides that the pre-trial order is binding upon the parties unless modified at the trial to prevent manifest injustice. This is indispensable if pre-trial is not to be an empty form.* At trial the judge, who may or may not have presided at pre-trial, will have a ready reference to ascertain the real matters in dispute, the stipulations entered, the admissions made and the questions of relevancy and materiality reserved for him.

A pre-trial order should be drafted after every conference where anything has been accomplished or any agreement reached in order to prevent subsequent disagreement as to what happened at the conference. The procedure followed in some jurisdictions is for the presiding judge, as the definitive issues become apparent, to dictate the pre-trial order directly to a typist during the course of the conference or immediately before its conclusion. The dictation takes place in the presence of the attorneys who pay close attention, since they will be required to initial the order before the conference adjourns, after which it is signed by the court. This procedure expedites the court's business, and gives an added guarantee of accuracy to the content of the order. 

It also results in a much more succinct statement of the case than is usually possible where a transcript of the conference is used as the pre-trial order, or where counsel attempt to draft the order after the conference. Ability to dictate a good pre-trial order "on the spot" requires concentration by the presiding judge. The benefits obtained, however, are ample rewards for the effort expended.

Pre-Trial Demonstrations 

A large number of institutes and demonstrations of pre-trial procedure have been held in various parts of the

country, at bar association meetings, judicial conferences, and law school functions. In this way pre-trial has been introduced in a practical form to present and future members of the bar and to the bench. Its merits appear more fully from actual experience with it.

Judicial Conference Recommendations 

The following recommendations in reference to pre-trial procedure were submitted by the Pre-trial Committee of the Judicial Conference of the United States and approved by the 

Conference itself in 1944: "(1) The committee is of the opinion that every civil case in which issue has been joined should be pre-tried before it is assigned for trial, unless there are special circumstances which render such a conference unnecessary or inexpedient, or unless the case is one of such a nature that it appears that pre-trial would be of no advantage. "(2) The committee believes the use of pre-trial procedure in criminal cases should be left to the discretion of the individual judge. "(3) The committee considers that settlement is a byproduct of good pre-trial procedure rather than a primary objective to be actively pursued by the judge. "(4) The committee believes that the question of whether the pre-trial conference should be held in chambers or in open court is a matter which each judge should decide for himself. But in every case, wherever the conference is held, the Committee recommends that an order be entered recording the results of the proceedings. "(5) The committee approves of the practice of the great majority of the judges using pre-trial procedure regularly, of

holding the pre-trial conference shortly before the actual trial is had, the preferable interval before the trial being not less than one week or more than three weeks. In exceptional cases it may also be advantageous to call a pre-trial conference immediately after issue is joined. "(6) The committee is impressed with the desirability of drawing up a pre-trial order while the parties are present, setting forth what has been agreed to or accomplished at the hearing. "(7) The committee recommends that the Conference request the Attorney General to call to the attention of all United States attorneys the benefits to be gained through pre-trial conferences and the desirability of their cooperation in securing the advantages of this procedure in government cases. "(8) The committee desires to furnish assistance to district judges wishing to inaugurate pre-trial procedure in their courts or desiring to improve their technique of holding pre-trial conferences, and requests approval by the conference of a plan for giving such aid, either by sending a judge experienced in pre-trial practice to the district of the judge requesting aid, or by permitting the latter, upon his request, with the approval of his senior circuit judge, to be temporarily assigned to a district where pre-trial procedure is being effectively used."

Pre-Trial Proceedings Statement of Nature of Case: 

Suit for personal injuries claimed to have been sustained by plaintiff as a result of flame and lightning coming through the telephone receiver which was part of the telephone equipment installed by defendant at the U. S. Naval Observatory. 

Plaintiff is Superintendent of the Naval Observatory, and during an electrical storm received injuries while using the telephone at the Observatory. Plaintiff claims that defendant was guilty of negligence in installing and maintaining the telephone equipment, that it failed to ground and sufficiently insulate telephone wires, and failed to install necessary protector blocks, failed to install adequate tube insulation and to install adequate shock coils.

UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT FOR THE DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA BROWN 

Plaintiff. VS.

MUNICIPAL TRANSIT COMPANY 

Defendant.

Calendar No. 

Civil Action No. 500-50 PRETRIAL PROCEEDINGS STATEMENT OF NATURE OF CASE: 

Action for personal injuries and property damage.

Plaintiff No. I was the owner and driver of an automobile in which Plaintiff No. 2, his wife, was a passenger. 

Plaintiffs claim that the automobile was proceeding westerly on Nebraska Avenue and was struck by defendant's bus proceeding northerly on Connecticut Avenue, the collision taking place in the intersection between Nebraska and Connecticut Avenues.

Plaintiffs claim that the automobile was crossing on a green light and that the bus ran against a red light. 

Defendant admits that the collision took place in the intersection.

Defendant claims that the automobile ran through a red light; that the bus was proceeding on a green light and that the collision was due to the sole negligence of plaintiff No. 1, or, in the alternative, that plaintiff No. 1 was guilty of contributory negligence.

Defendant claims that plaintiff No. 1 had imbibed intoxicating liquor shortly prior to the accident and that plaintiff No. 2 was aware of that fact. 

Defendant claims that plaintiff No. 2 assumed the risk

of her husband's negligence.

Defendant further claims that the car was being driven on a mission for plaintiff No. 2 and that, therefore, the negligence of plaintiff No. 1 should be imputed to her. 

Defendant further claims that the bus driver made every effort to avoid the collision. 

Defendant counterclaims against plaintiff No. 1 for damages to the bus.

Plaintiff No. 2 claims permanent injuries to her left femur and pelvis. 

Plaintiff No. 1 is granted leave to amend the complaint by asserting a claim for loss of consortium. 

Defendant objects to this.

Plaintiffs are granted leave to amend their complaint so as to increase the demand for damages to $20, 000. 

Defendant is granted the right to a medical examination of the plaintiffs on this additional issue. 

Plaintiff No. 1 claims permanent injuries to his spine and an aggravation of his arthritic condition. 

Defendant will have the right to a medical examination and to take the deposition of plaintiff No. 1 on the claim of aggravation of arthritic condition. 

STIPULATIONS: 

By agreement of counsel for the respective parties, present in Court, it is ordered that the subsequent course of this action shall be governed by the following stipulations unless modified by the Court to prevent manifest injustice: 

It is stipulated that plaintiff No. 1 was the owner and driver of the automobile and that plaintiff No. 2 was his wife and a passenger in the automobile. 

It is stipulated that the intersection was controlled by traffic lights and that the traffic lights were in operation at the time of the collision.

It is stipulated that the following evidence may be admitted without formal proof subject to objections as to relevancy and competency: (1) Traffic regulations. (2) Hos pital records, insofar as they are otherwise admissible. 

It is stipulated that plaintiff's Government leave records may be admitted in evidence without formal proof. 

X-ray photographs which are being marked may be admitted in evidence without formal proof. 

It is stipulated that the photograph number 1, so marked, may be admitted in evidence without formal proof, merely for the purpose of showing the locale.

It is stipulated that photograph number 2, which will be marked, may be admitted in evidence without formal proof, merely for the purpose of showing the point of impact. 

It is stipulated that the Weather Bureau report which is being marked may be admitted in evidence without formal proof.

It is stipulated that the medical and hospital bills which are being marked may be introduced in evidence without formal proof.

It is stipulated that the cost of repairs to the automobile was $457. 25 and that this was a reasonable cost. 

It is further stipulated that the reasonable cost of repairs to the bus was $295. 25.

It is stipulated that the plat which is being marked as Defendant's Exhibit 1 may be admitted in evidence without formal proof. Dated

PRE-TRIAL ORDER NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS 

This is an action by plaintiff to recover damages for personal injuries which plaintiff claims she sustained by reason of the negligence of the defendant, growing out of an accident when a truck owned and operated by defendant struck plaintiff while she was crossing the street in the vicinity of S. E. 33rd Avenue and S. E. Kelly Street, in the city of Corpus Christi, Texas, County of Nueces.

ADMITTED FACTS 

The following facts have been agreed upon by the parties and require no proof: 

1. That the defendant at all times herein mentioned was and now is a corporation, duly organized and existing under and by virtue of the laws of the state of and engaged in the delivery of freight and merchandise in and about the city of Corpus Christi, Texas, and maintains a principal office and place of business in the city of Corpus Christi, Texas. 

2. That the plaintiff was a citizen and resident of the state of Texas at the time of the commencement of this action, and

ever since and is now such a citizen; that the amount in controversy in this cause exceeds the sum of $3000.00, exclusive of costs and interest, and there is a diversity of citizenship between plaintiff and defendant. 

3. That in the furtherance of defendant's business, it owns and uses certain trucks, one of which was involved in the accident herein described. 

4. That the driver of the truck at the time of and during the accident described was acting within the scope of his employment and in the furtherance of defendant's business. 

5. That at all times hereinafter mentioned S. E. 33rd

Avenue and S. E. Kelly Street were and now are public streets and highways in the city of Corpus Christi, and intersect each other at approximately right angles; that said S. E. 33rd Avenue runs in a general northerly and southerly direction and S. E. 

Kelly Street runs in a general easterly and westerly direction.

Books Nims, Pre-Trial, published in 1950 by Baker, Voorhis & Co., Inc.

The most comprehensive work on pre-trial in existence. 

Contains an account of methods used in the pre-trial conference by a number of federal and state judges, a complete review of court decisions on pre-trial and a verbatim transcript of four pre-trial demonstrations.
