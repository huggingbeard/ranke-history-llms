PREFACE 

Trials by summary conviction are of small importance if only the amount of the penalty or the possible imprisonment are considered. The number of such cases, however, must be very great and each case may be of large importance to the individual citizen who may happen to be involved. The proceeding comes into direct contact with the liberty and rights of the individual citizen and the misuse or abuse of this proceeding might greatly endanger this liberty and these rights. This fact explains the protection of the right of appeal from summary convictions contained in the constitution of Pennsylvania. It also explains the apparently disproportionate attention devoted by the appellate courts to questions arising out of these proceedings where each case on its face involves only a small fine or a few days in jail.

The need for some of this attention might be eliminated if the legislature would codify the procedure in cases of summary convictions and separately codify the procedure in suits for penalties and in subsequent legislation, when new offenses are created, prescribed trial under one of these codes. One learned justice remarked that "there are few subjects of procedural law upon which so much learning has been expended (and perhaps wasted, considering the amounts involved and the results obtained) as that distinguishing a summary conviction from a suit for a penalty and determining the proceedings in each." This burden upon the courts grows out of the failure of legislative draftsmen to recognize the clearly defined distinctions between the two procedures.

1. DEFINITION. 

Summary conviction is a mode of procedure for the punishment of a public crime or offense, which is directed by statute, for the conviction of offenders without the intervention of a jury. It is a criminal proceeding, commenced in the name of the Commonwealth, and founded upon information or complaint which must be in writing. All its characteristics indicate its criminal nature. The acts providing for its application are strictly construed, and the parties to it are given the right of appeal to the court of quarter sessions. "Summary conviction" by long usage has come to be a technical legal term designating this form of procedure. In Commonwealth v. Messinger¹ it was pointed out that the words "in all cases of summary convictions" in the Act of 1876 2 mean "in all summary

3. CONSTITUTIONAL STATUS. 

Trial in a summary proceeding is before a single justice of the peace or other magistrate, without a jury. The question arises whether or not such a proceeding can be reconciled with the constitutional guar-

The Constitution of Pennsylvania, Art. 1. Sec. 6 

provides that "Trial by jury shall be as heretofore, and the right thereof remain inviolate." This clause does not extend the right of trial by jury but only makes inviolate the right as it existed prior to the adoption of the constitution. Since the trial of certain classes of petty offenses by summary proceedings long antedates the constitution, such trials in these classes of cases are not forbidden by this clause. Neither does this clause prevent the legislature from creating a new criminal offense and directing that it shall be tried summarily. The disposition of all minor offenses by formal jury trial would make the administration of criminal justice too cumbersome, costly and burdensome. Practical common sense and experience require some shorter and simpler and more expeditious form of procedure for disposing of the large mass of petty offenses the importance of which would not warrant the formal and elaborate trial by jury. The welfare of the accused himself demands a simpler and more prompt disposal of the charges against him than can be reached through the more formal procedure. The procedure by summary conviction grew up at an early date in response to this need, and in England, gradually crystallized into the Summary Conviction Acts. 

No such formal codification has been undertaken in Pennsylvania but the Pennsylvania practice embodies substantially the same principles upon which the English acts are based. 5 

In Von Swartow v. Commonwealth, after pointing out that this clause of the constitution only makes inviolate the right of trial by jury as it existed when the constitution was adopted, the opinion continues: "But the trial by jury is as it was at the formation of the constitution, and the right as it then

There is nothing to forbid the legislature from creating a new offense and prescribing what mode they please of ascertaining the guilt of those who are charged with it. Many tribunals, unknown to the framers of the constitution, and not at all resembling a jury, have been erected and charged with the determination of grave and weighty matters, for instance, commissioners, viewers, and appraisers of damages, county and township auditors, and those officers. of the state government whose duty it is to settle the public accounts. All of these functionaries have, at different times in our history, been empowered to decide the most important controversies without appeal. In some of them the right of an ultimate trial by jury has been given; but this was not done because the laws were believed to be unconstitutional without it. The purpose of the constitution undoubtedly was to preserve the jury trial wherever the common law gave it, and in all other cases to let the legislature and the people do as their wisdom and experience might dictate. Summary convictions were well known before the formation of the constitution, and they are not expressly or impliedly prohibited by that instrument except insofar as they are not to be substituted for a jury where the latter mode of trial had been previously established."

The rights secured to the accused in a criminal pros ecution by Art. 1 Sec. 9 of the constitution are not infringed by a statute making a petty offense triable by summary proceedings. He can be heard by himself and his counsel, may demand the nature and cause of the accusation against him, may meet the witnesses

A summary conviction is not a "prosecution by indictment or information" in which the clause gives the right to "a speedy public trial by an impartial jury of the vicinage." The constitutional guarantee that the defendant "cannot be compelled to give evidence against himself" applies to trial by summary proceeding just as to other criminal trials.

When a statute enacted by the legislature within its constitutional authority provided a summary proceeding for the trial and punishment of petty offenses, and a person accused under such statute has been arrested under a warrant based on proper written information or complaint, and convicted and sentenced after fair and open and impartial trial before the justice of the peace, this regular and open and orderly procedure is "due process of law" and is "the law of the land.”·

The "due process of law" clause in the constitution of the United States does not require all trials in state courts to be by jury. What it does require is that a person shall not be deprived of his liberty or his property otherwise than according to the settled and orderly course of judicial proceedings.

4. FORM OF THE PENAL CLAUSE IN THE STATUTE. 

A typical form of properly drafted penal clause is as follows: 

Any person violating any of the provisions of this act "shall, upon conviction in a summary proceeding before any alderman, magistrate or justice of the peace, be sentenced to pay a fine not exceeding twenty-five dollars, or, in default of the payment of such fine, to undergo imprisonment in the county jail for a period of one day for each dollar of fine imposed."

This clause provides for (a) a conviction; (b) a summary proceeding; (c) before any alderman, magistrate or justice of the peace; (d) sentence to pay a definitely limited fine; (e) in default of payment of the fine, a clearly described imprisonment; (f) and elsewhere in the act, for a disposition of the fine. The acts sometimes specify a "conviction" before designated magistrate without describing it as a "summary" "" conviction, as in the Act of April 9, 1760, 1 Sm.

Laws 229. It will be noticed that no technical designation of the grade of the offense appears in the typical clause. When the legislature declares an offense to be a misdemeanor and makes it punishable by

Another form of incomplete penal clause provides that the person violating any of the provisions of the act shall forfeit and pay a designated sum, without specifying a method of recovery. A penal clause of this kind may form a basis for an action to recover the penalty but could not, under the rule of strict construction, be the basis of a summary conviction. 

Good draftsmanship requires that the act authorizing summary conviction should set forth clearly all the elements contained in this typical form. Absence of any of these elements leads to obscurity and difficulty in the practical application. Only gross carelessness can explain acts confusing summary convictions with suits for penalties. It is true that petty offenses, not essentially criminal in their nature (mala in se) may be made punishable by either method at the discretion of the legislature, but there is no excuse for failure to clearly and definitely designate the method intended. 5. SUITS FOR PENALTIES DISTINGUISHED. 

A summary conviction is a criminal proceeding for the trial and punishment of a minor criminal offense under a statute. It is brought in the name of the Commonwealth and its result is a fine or imprisonment, if defendant is convicted, or an acquittal if the offense is not proven. The fine goes to the county or to the Commonwealth as directed by the act under which the prosecution is brought, or, if the act contains no disposition of the fine, it will be paid to the county. Appeal from summary convictions is to the court of quarter sessions. The distinguishing feature of the summary conviction is its entirely criminal character. 

A suit for a penalty is essentially a civil proceeding. 

Recovery under a city or borough ordinance imposing a fine is by suit in the name of the city or borough before a justice of the peace or alderman, in the same manner as debts not exceeding $300 are recoverable. 

The result is a judgment for the penalty and costs, from which a transcript can be filed in the common pleas. The suit will be brought in the name of the borough or city and the proceeds will go into the city or borough treasury. Appeal from this judgment will be to the court of common pleas. While these proceedings are penal in their character, yet they are essentially

6. QUI TAM ACTIONS DISTINGUISHED. 

A qui tam action is an action of debt under a statute providing a penalty part or all of which goes to the informer. An action under a statute providing for a conviction is not a qui tam action even when a moiety of the penalty is given to the informer." The qui tam action will be in assumpsit in the name of the informer or to the use of the informer, while the summary conviction is always in the name of the Commonwealth.

9. CHARACTER OF OFFENSES TRIABLE BY SUMMARY CONVICTION. 

This form of procedure is necessarily confined to petty offenses below the lowest grade of misdemeanors triable in the first instance in the court of quarter sessions. As has already been indicated, this class of offenses has usually no technical term indicating its grade. The substance of game, fish, forestry, pure food laws and their like is in a constant process of change; the methods of trial, however, vary only in the increasing definiteness and clarity of the clauses defining and prescribing the form of procedure. As Paley has stated, "There is seldom any difficulty in ascertaining the offenses liable to summary convictions, since each must be the subject of a special law.” The magistrate can be certain, therefore, that a given criminal act is not triable by summary proceeding unless specifically made so by statute. Where the act describes an offense and directs that when complaint is made to the magistrate, he shall issue his warrant and cause the offender to be arrested and brought before him, whereupon he shall hear and determine the guilt or innocence of the person so charged and if convicted such person shall be sentenced to pay the fine fixed by the act, this proceeding constitutes a summary conviction.25

1. NECESSITY FOR INFORMATION OR COM- PLAINT. 

The first step in a summary proceeding is the information or complaint. The two terms are used interchangeably and are used to designate the pleadings by which a criminal action is instituted in the court of a justice of the peace or other magistrate. The information or complaint is the basis and foundation upon which the whole proceeding rests, and no such proceeding can be sustained in the absence of a formal information or complaint. When a police officer arrests an offender upon view, he must at once take his prisoner before a magistrate and make formal complaint. 

It can safely be stated that a summary conviction must be based on an information or complaint which should set forth "every material element of the offense and facts necessary to establish the jurisdiction of the justice." 2

The information is necessary for the performance of two functions. It must show all the facts and circumstances necessary to give the magistrate

2. JURISDICTIONAL FACTS. 

The proceeding is always based on a statute and the statute itself specifies the elements which must be present to give jurisdiction to the magistrate. The presence of all the facts necessary to give the magistrate jurisdiction must appear affirmatively on the record. 

The proper place for these elements is in the information or complaint. The information upon which the proceedings before the justice of the peace is founded must set forth every element of the offense and facts necessary to establish the jurisdiction." "The complaint is the foundation of the proceeding," says Audenreid, J., "and the jurisdictional facts must appear on its face by a plain statement of them. Neither the warrant nor the docket entries can supply them nor dispense with their presence there." This requirement that the jurisdiction of the magistrate must appear is fundamental In the absence of "a properly stated complaint, fulfilling the requirements of the law as to precision and particularity" the magistrate would have no jurisdiction and the entire proceedings would be vitiated. 2. TIME OF HEARING. 

When the accused is brought into court, the next step is for the justice to fix a time for hearing. Since the purpose of the summary procedure is to insure prompt justice to the defendant and speedy disposition of charges of petty offenses, there must be no delay in bringing defendant to trial. The hearing may be held immediately, or with only such delay as may be necessary to enable the defendant to secure counsel or summon his witnesses; or defendant may be permitted to enter bail for his appearance on some reasonable future date better to enable him to prepare his defense.

The justice may, on application of either party, adjourn or postpone the hearing from time to time as the necessities of the parties and the ends of justice may require. Adjournments are also frequently arranged by agreement of counsel when the parties are represented by counsel. The magistrate has a wide discretion in this regard and it will not be disturbed so long as it is reasonably exercised and the legal rights of the parties are not prejudiced.

When the defendant was discharged in the former proceeding, without trial, for want of prosecution, such a proceeding is not available upon trial in a second prosecution for the same offense under a plea of autrefois acquit; it was held to have no more effect than "would attach to a nol. pros. after indictment.'' 26

When a plea of former conviction or former acquittal is sustained, the result is the acquittal of the defendant and the end of the hearing.

7. WITNESSES. 

The accused has a right to compulsory process to secure the attendance of necessary witnesses and is entitled to present their testimony before the magistrate. Summary proceedings differ in no way in this respect from other criminal trials.

The swearing and examination of witnesses proceeds just as is usual in trials in the court of quarter sessions except that the proceeding is a little less formal. 

The principles of the law of evidence are applicable and the same forms of examination and cross-examination of witnesses are in use. A strict technical application of the rules of evidence to trials in summary convictions is neither practicable or necessary. The magistrate is not presumed to be learned in the law. and since the examination is to inform the magistrate himself, the same nicety in excluding irrelevant or incompetent testimony need not be exercised. 

In a trial in a summary proceeding the justice must take the testimony of the witnesses in writing. In 

England, according to Paley, "it is the duty of the justice to take the examination of the witnesses formally in writing; mere memoranda, or such minutes as might satisfy the judgment of the justice at the moment, were held not to be sufficient when by 3 Geo.

At the conclusion of the testimony, the magistrate must render a decision on the facts presented to him. 

He must find that the defendant did an act which is prohibited by the statute alleged to be violated, or that he did not do such an act. If the record does not disclose a formal conviction of a criminal act, any sentence imposed will be void.82 The correct procedure would be for the magistrate to find defendant "Guilty" or "Not guilty."

9. SENTENCE. 

The sentence should be within the limits specified by the act of Assembly under which the prosecution is instituted. If the act fixes a specific fine or imprisonment, the sentence must sentence the defendant to the exact fine or imprisonment specified. The authority of the magistrate rests on the specific act of Assembly.

If he levies a fine either larger or smaller than the act prescribes, the sentence will be void. If the act allows him to use his discretion within certain limits, the sentence must keep within the prescribed limits. 

The exact amount of the fine must be specified and to whom the fine is payable. If the particular act of 

Assembly states to whom the fine shall be paid, the sentence should contain the same specification. If the magistrate is in doubt as to whom the fine is properly payable, he can make it payable "to the Commonwealth." The fine can then be distributed to the proper parties. The alternative jail sentence should be stated with equal clarity.

5. PETITION FOR ALLOWANCE OF WRIT OF CERTIORARI. 

The petition to the court of common pleas for writ of certiorari to a magistrate in a summary conviction should contain, as a minimum requirement, facts sufficient to give the court jurisdiction to act in the premises. The constitution grants the power to issue the writ; to cause the proceedings to be brought before them and right and justice to be done. The exercise of the power to issue the writ is within the sound discretion of the court, and, if the petition does not disclose any irregularity, abuse or omission in the proceedings in derogation of the rights of the petitioner, or causing injustice to the petitioner, there would be no proper cause for issuing the writ.

3. SUGGESTIONS AS TO RECORDING TESTI- MONY. 

The testimony should be entered in the magistrate's docket in its order in the course of the proceedings. 

The following is a suggestion as to form: "John Smith, sworn, says (give substance of his testimony in full). "Frank Jones, sworn, testifies (substance of testimony) (and so continue until all witnesses for prosecution have been examined). "The defendant was then given an opportunity to answer the charges, and being sworn, stated (give substance of testimony). "Defendant called C. D. who being sworn, says (and so continue until all of defendant's witnesses have been examined)."

If papers or other objects are offered in evidence and received or refused, note the fact in the docket. 

If defendant confesses, the substance of the confession should be entered in the docket in place of the evidence.

NOTE: The record must show the substance of the testimony of both prosecution and defendant. It must be sufficient to sustain the conviction. It must be part of the record; a copy of the evidence attached to the transcript is not sufficient.
