The Commonwealth of Massachusetts GROSS WEIGHT OF CERTAIN MOTOR VEHICLES. 

SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

This report is based on a directive of the General Court to study (1) a proposal to reduce by 10 per cent the present statutory maximum gross weights of certain motor vehicles (House, No. 1521 of 1959); and (2) another proposal to require two-axle trucks to limit their gross loads to three times their empty weight, within the statutory maximum of 23 tons 

(House, No. 1918 of 1959). 

To determine the need and justification of these proposals, this report discusses (a) the economics of vehicle sizes and weights, (b) the distinction between gross weights and axle weights and their respective effects on road surfaces and bridges, (c) efforts to establish a more scientific basis for future regulation, through various road tests, and (d) statutory controls here and elsewhere.

COMMERCIAL MOTOR VEHICLES AND HIGHWAY DESIGN. 

Highway Design. 

Highway design is generally classified as either geometric or structural. The former or geometric approach is concerned with traffic lanes, widths, curvature, grades and the like, and the latter structural aspects with pavement thicknesses, bridge design and supporting capacity. 

This report deals only with structural aspects, including the closely related questions of gross weights and axle weights of vehicles. In doing so, it will consider the following three classifications of commercial vehicles: (1) single unit trucks, (2) combinations of tractors and semitrailers, and (3) combinations of trucks and full trailers. These types of vehicles present varying considerations of (a) the number of axles concerned and the weight they carry, (b) the length of the vehicle and distribution of its load, and (c) the gross weight of the vehicle.

8 [Mar. HOUSE ---- 

Axle Weights in Structural Highway Design. 

Axle weights are very important in pavement construction because they largely determine the strength required of highway surfaces and foundations, and thus, their cost of construction. Every road has a maximum axle-load which it will support in indefinite repetition; greater axle loads can be carried but they cause fatigue and eventual failure of the road surface if repeated sufficiently. 

In Massachusetts and seven other eastern States, vehicles are permitted to operate with a load of 22,400 pounds on a single axle. Massachusetts is one of 10, mostly eastern, States which permits 36,000 pounds on a tandem group of two closely spaced axles. Most other States allow maximum loads of only 18,000 pounds on a single axle and 32,000 pounds on a tandem group, as recommended after extensive testing by the United States Bureau of Public Roads and the Ameri can Association of State Highway Officials (AASHO). 

Gross Weight in Bridge Design.

Bridge design is controlled by both the grouping and spacing of axles and the length and the gross weight of the vehicle, particularly the latter. While, as indicated, Massachusetts allows heavier axle loads than are recommended by the above authorities, it authorizes a gross weight maximum which is somewhat less than they favor. 

Road Tests. 

The report summarizes various key road tests over the years to develop factual bases for road design and construction, to increase road serviceability, to establish proper maximum axle loads, and to allocate costs among the different weights of vehicles using the highways. It emphasizes an unfinished current test occurring in Illinois, which is the most ambitious that has ever been undertaken, and which will cost more than $22 million before completion in 1961. 

STATUTORY REGULATION OF VEHICLE SIZES AND WEIGHTS. 

Legislative regulation of the sizes and weights of motor vehicles of a small group of eastern States differs markedly

1960.] 

from those applied in the majority of western States. In the east, higher axle loads are the rule while the west favors higher gross weights and longer vehicles. 

Western truckers can, as a result, generally move freight in fewer vehicles and larger trailers. Authoritative studies demonstrate that heavier loads can be moved without damage to roads and bridges and without sacrificing economy of operation simply by increasing the horsepower of the vehicle and the number of its axles. 

Oregon's highway tax program assesses vehicles in proportion to their responsibility for the cost of road building and maintenance and is basically a graduated mileage tax which is leveled at a specific rate per mile for various weight groups. 

Other jurisdictions use the same general approach in modified form.

MASSACHUSETTS LAW GOVERNING MAXIMUM WEIGHTS OF VEHICLES. 

Background and Features. 

The original 1946 Massachusetts law regulating gross weights of motor vehicles and its frequent subsequent amendments are discussed at length. A major point raised is the inconsistency of a statutory maximum gross weight level which vitiates other provisions of the law. Axle weight limits are also out of line with statutory maxima in most jurisdictions, though in line with a contiguous group of eastern States. The latter fact, however, does reduce confusion of interstate truck movement in large degree. 

Considerations related to Proposed Changes in Law. 

In general, most of the 1,700 state-controlled bridges in this Commonwealth will adequately support the maximum authorized legal loads. About 100 of the older bridges are limited to a supporting capacity of from one-half to twothirds of the present legal maximum without being overstressed.

In view of the foregoing, no savings on bridge costs and maintenance can be anticipated from enactment of House, No. 1521. Likewise, pavement costs would not be reduced because the proposal wholly omits reference to the vital factor of axle weights.

[Mar. 

The bill is unusual in that in most jurisdictions, Legislatures are faced with rather steady pressures to increase the permissible gross weights of vehicles. If a 10 per cent increase were proposed here rather than the 10 per cent decrease, the Commonwealth would have to spend about $5 million to reinforce certain bridges. 

With maximum weights at their present level, limited enforcement personnel is faced with increasing frequency of violations of the weight limit of vehicles, coupled with an upward trend in the degree of overloadings. More than 1,000 known violations of the maximum gross weight law occurred in 1959 as contrasted with only 365 two years previously; moreover, it is certain that these known violators are only a portion of the actual total. The real violation figure is somewhere between six and 20 times the number of violations actually detected, according to Registry personnel. 

The struggle of only 10 men being required to police 281,000 commercial vehicles operating in an area of nearly 8,000 square miles is bad enough, but is made worse by the statutory requirement that a suspected violator's truck must be weighed within three miles of the point of stopping scales which are not always adequate to do the job. CONSTRUCTION VEHICLES AND WEIGHT LIMITATIONS. 

House, No. 1918, as indicated above, would restrict the gross loads of two-axle trucks to not more than three times their empty weight within a maximum gross weight of 23 tons. 

This bill would further restrict light construction vehicles now operating with larger payloads than heavy-duty vehicles. 

The larger payload is possible because the light trucks weigh about one-fourth the maximum gross weight when empty while the empty weight of heavier trucks is about 40 per cent of the maximum gross weight.

Many owners of light vehicles in the construction business are registering them above their maximum gross weights; some as high as 46,000 pounds, which is the legal maximum

1960.] 

for two-axle trucks. Completely aside from controlling potential damage from gross loads, House, No. 1918 would, it is argued, limit the payload to a more rational figure consistent with the carrying capacity of the truck and the consequent 5 effects on the road. More restricted loads on these light vehicles would also be beneficial from a safety viewpoint. 

House, No. 1521 would also seriously affect the livelihood of owners of vehicles by reducing payloads and thus, earnings. 

CONCLUSIONS. 

Many segments of the economy are affected by our laws regulating weights of commercial motor vehicles. Thus, any change warrants serious legislative consideration. 

The evidence seems adequate to conclude that enactment of House, No. 1521 would lead to considerable adverse results while no balancing benefits have been uncovered. 

The enforcement of statutory weight maxima is now handicapped by (a) insufficient personnel, (b) insufficient and inadequate scales, and (c) statutory exemption of the most frequent violators.

Among vehicles engaged in construction work, heavy-duty units operate at a disadvantage with competing light units because of registration practices of light vehicle owners and the absence of statutory restrictions to control such practices.

An informed solution to these problems will be greatly assisted by the findings of the current AASHO Road Test in Illinois, which it is estimated will be completed during next year.

Chapter II. Commercial Motor Vehicles and Highway Design. 

General. 

Highway design falls into two broad classifications: (1) geometric, and (2) structural. Each class is specifically related to the type and volume of vehicular traffic being served, which in turn controls statutory limitations on the maximum size and weight of motor vehicles. 

Geometric design is concerned with the number of lanes, their width, their degree of curvature and gradient, the height of overpasses, and the like. Closely related to such design are the widths and lengths of vehicles and the various combinations in practice. 

Structural design is concerned with the thickness of pavement, the type and capacity of bridges, and the like. Closely related to structural design are gross weights of vehicles, both single and tandem axle weights and vehicle heights.

Engineers responsible for the structural design of highways have developed certain specifications under which our highways ideally should carry loads appropriate to such specifications. The imposition of loads on the highways which are heavier than the maxima specified will consequently quicken highway deterioration and increase highway maintenance costs. The results are adverse to the public both as highway users and as taxpayers. 

To control and prevent such damaging physical and financial effects, legislative policy has been to specify maximum gross weights and axle weights, in most instances based on considerable scientific testing as well as practical engineering experience. So many factors enter into road and bridge design that no pat answer or solution to several structural engineering problems is easily obtainable. As will be seen in Chapter III the search for answers to many perplexing problems incident to structural highway design continues today with greater effort then ever before. This can only be beneficial, for many regulations today are unrealistic in view of the rapid and major changes in the design of highways and of motor vehicle transport equipment since World War II. 

Meanwhile the General Court and its highway agency, the Department of Public Works, will want to act with caution when faced

Trends of Gross Weights and Axle Weights. 

With the increased number of larger and heavier vehicles, which have made possible larger and heavier payloads, there has developed an increase in the gross loads of trucks and combinations. Moreover, there is increasing frequency of the occasions when it is observed that certain numbers of trucks and truck combinations exceed desirable weight maxima. On the latter score, the New England States were reported to have had a low frequency of gross loads of 50,000 pounds or more when compared to other regions, according to the U. S. Bureau of Public Roads in a definitive study made a few years ago. This showing is attributable to various circumstances, including the economy of the region as contrasted with other areas, its geographical characteristics and the nature of its regulatory statutes relative to weight limits. 1 On the other hand, the same study revealed that substantially greater frequencies of heavier axle loads occurred in New England and Middle Atlantic regions. The large proportion of loads which exceeded 22,000 pounds can only be explained in terms of violations of the law, according to the Bureau of Public Roads. This study also established the interesting fact that high frequency of heavy axle loading is not necessarily associated with high frequency of heavy gross vehicle weight. Thus, the Pacific coast region has the highest frequencies of heavy gross loading, yet its frequencies of heavy axle loading are lowest. The explanation lies in regulatory laws of the Pacific Coast States which encourage the distribution of larger permissible gross weight to a larger number of axles, by permitting longer vehicles. The result is an axle weight concentration closer to the smaller legal limits prescribed in those States.

Distinction between Axle Weights and Gross Weights. 

To facilitate understanding of this distinction, there follows a brief discussion of the relationship between highways and bridges on the one hand and gross weights and axle weights on the other. 

Axle Weights in Structural Highway Design. -The axle load of vehicles largely determines the necessary strength required of highway surfaces and foundations. This weight is defined as "the total load transmitted to the road by all wheels whose centers may be included between two parallel transverse vertical planes 40 inches apart, extending across the full width of the vehicle." All roads have maximum axle loads which they will support in indefinite repetition. They will also carry greater axle loads but when these are sufficiently repeated they cause a fatigue of the road structure and eventual failure. The number and frequency of application of such excess loads required to produce failure depend upon the size of that excess.

Investigation has also shown that the load transmitted to the road surface through the tires of a vehicle in motion is not constant but varies with several factors. While most vehicles in motion register close to their static weight, there can be differences in transmission of loads to road surfaces by a moving vehicle. This is because a moving vehicle must transmit its load to the surface of the road differently under acceleration or deceleration and under dynamic changes of the load-force induced by variation in the road profile.

In light of these facts, what may be deemed a reasonable maximum axle load in order to protect the roads? The General Court of Massachusetts enacted a statute in 1946 providing that weights up to a limit of 22,400 pounds on a single axle would be permissible on road surfaces of the Commonwealth. (G. L. c. 90, § 19A, as inserted by Acts of 1946, c. 397.) This limit, still in effect, has placed 

Massachusetts among the eight northeastern States which allow maximum axle loads of 22,400 pounds on a single axle, the highest in effect in the continental United States.¹

Gross Weights in Bridge Design. - Unlike road surfaces, bridges are affected not only by the axle weight of vehicles but also by the gross weights. In the case of multiple-axle vehicles they are also affected by the load on any group of axles. 

Every bridge spans some opening and must have sufficient strength to support not only its own weight, but all loads it must carry. If the bridge is longer than the length of a vehicle which it is supporting, the bridge must be strong enough not only to support the gross weight of the vehicle but to transmit such weight to its two abutments. If, however, the span of the bridge is shorter than that vehicle it need only have the strength to support the loads on the number of axles of the vehicle which can lie upon the span at one time. Before the 1920's bridges were designed to support the weight of "critical" vehicles, i.e., the heaviest vehicles which were then of some 10 tons. The statutes usually established a general limit on the weight of the usual two-axle vehicle for bridge design. Where bridges were deemed unsafe to carry this maximum, they were prominently posted for a lesser weight. 

During the "twenties" the character of motor vehicles changed markedly. They became longer, they acquired more axles and they became heavier.

As a result, the maximum legal weight limits were raised. The 

I old 10-ton bridge was replaced by a standard design capable of supporting a 15-ton vehicle. For purposes of bridge design, the latter vehicle was assumed to have two axles and a 14-foot wheel ད་ base, with loads of three tons being carried on the forward or steering axle and 12 tons on the rear, or drive axle. The bridge designed to support this heavier vehicle came to be known as the H-15 bridge. 

A bridge designed for a 10-ton vehicle of two axles, with the same 14-foot wheel base, with two tons carried up forward and eight tons on the rear, was described as an H-10 bridge. Later, as even heavier and longer vehicles became prevalent, a stronger, H20-S16 bridge became standard on the interstate system throughout the nation. This bridge, is designed to carry a two-axle tractor of 14-foot wheel base, with loadings of 4 tons on one axle and 16 tons on the other axle, which is followed at another interval of 14 feet by a trailer axle carrying 16 tons. These same factors of greater weight and length have motivated statutory increases in maximum loadings.

In 1931, the AASHO Bridge Committee brought forward a proposal radically changing the form of vehicular weight control. This proposal adjusted for the fact that differing stresses and strains will be caused in a bridge by vehicles of equal weight but of different length. If two vehicles of the same weight but of different length use a given bridge, the shorter vehicle will generate the greater stress. 

The AASHO therefore proposed that the limit on the gross weight of vehicles should vary with the length of the wheelbase, using the formula W = C(L+40), where W equals the permissible gross weight in pounds, L the distance in feet between the first and last. axles of the vehicle, and C is a coefficient for each State reflecting the general strength and condition of its bridges.

Chapter III. Road Tests. 

Road tests have been conducted for many years in various sections of the country to establish factual bases for road design and construction, to increase the serviceability of existing roads, to establish reasonable axle-weight limitations, and to allocate cost responsibility to different weights of vehicles. This chapter will deal with the most significant of these tests. 

Bates Tests. 

These tests were conducted by the Illinois Division of Highways in 1922-23. A special test road was constructed which became widely known as the Bates Road. Its objective was to prepare the way for proper design of primary roads to be built under a $100 million highway bond issue.

1960.] 

The Bates Road was about two and a half miles long and 18 feet wide with 68 experimental sections varying from 100 to 350 feet in length. Each section had a different material composition or design with respect to its base and surface construction. Test vehicles were Liberty trucks equipped with solid rubber tires. They were operated at speeds varying from 12 to 15 miles per hour, night and day, with regulated numbers of trips at successively larger cargo weights. During the first year of the test more than 50,000 trips were made over the road; during the second year, another 28,000 trips were added. From the behavior of several of the test sections which showed failure after relatively few increases in cargo weight, there quickly developed in other States two responses: (1) an adoption of a thickened-edge design; and (2) legal prescription of axleload limits in conformity with different pavement designs adopted in various States. At first, only four States - Illinois, Iowa, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin - had specific axle-load limits, but two-thirds of the States had axle-load or wheel-load limits on their statute books soon after the tests.

Impact Tests of U. S. Bureau of Public Roads. 

At the same time the Bureau of Public Roads began a series of tests, the purpose of which was to measure the impact to rcad surfaces from vehicles in motion. One test finding was that the magnitude of the impact force is significantly affected by the kind and condition of tires and by the speed at which vehicles are operated. From the test results, especially with respect to balloon tires, the Bureau recommended a maximum axle load of 18,000 pounds which it still maintains is reasonable.

Chapter IV. Statutory Regulation of Vehicle Sizes and Weights. 

GENERAL DISCUSSION. 

Legislative Approaches to Regulation. 

Two types of state legislation limit size and weight of motor vehicles throughout the States of the nation. Each type prohibits the operation of vehicles which exceed certain specified maximum weight levels. Beyond this, however, they differ not only in their basic concepts of the relative need for load limitation on highways and bridges but in their evaluation of the influence of heavy vehicles upon highway traffic. To demonstrate these differences a comparison is made below of the size and weight laws of the New England and Middle Atlantic States on one hand, and those of the far Western States on the other.

One of the chief characteristics of size and weight legislation in the eastern group of States is the permissible use of relatively heavy axle loads, e.g., 22,000 pounds or more on a single axle in ten jurisdictions (Conn., Maine, Md., Mass., N. H., N. J., N. Y., Pa., R. I., D. C.). The benefits flowing from heavier axle weights allowable are, however, restricted to a comparatively small number of vehicle types because of low flat-rate limits in gross weights of vehicles. 

Moreover, by omitting reference to group axle load on other than tandem axles, the gross weight differential between two-axle and three-axle trucks is narrowed by the heavier single axle load permitted, to such an extent that it greatly reduces the payload. And because the permissible gross weights can be readily realized by the use of a limited number of heavier axle loads, the advantages of multiple-axle combinations are in many cases largely nullified.

[Mar. 

HOUSE - 

No. 2880.

Just the opposite condition prevails out west. Axle loads are limited to a maximum of 18,000 pounds but relatively heavy gross vehicle weights are allowed provided the spacing of group axle loads conform to bridge formula requirements. The gross vehicle weights permitted vary with the arrangement and spacing of axles and are generally much higher than those permitted in the eastern States. 

Within the greater vehicle lengths generally allowed, there is improved opportunity for a better load distribution to take advantage of tandem axle vehicles and multiple axle combinations. It is a mistaken notion that large size vehicles go hand in hand with excessive weight as demonstrated by various state highway surveyteams. Thus, large moving vans and large trailers built to transport automobiles almost never violate weight laws. The surveys indicated that transportation of certain commodities, such as liquids, sand, gravel, stone, coal and logs accounted for most of the reported excessive loads. Mining products figured heavily in the violations detected; they were chiefly moved in large single unit trucks rather than the large combination vehicles. 

Motor Carrier Operation. 

Many elements contribute to the cost of heavy motor vehicle operation. Typical factors are the type, standard and condition of the highway; the volume and composition of traffic; the type and condition of heavy vehicle; the commodity hauled; and the class of service, etc.

Among these elements, the extent of dead weight of the vehicles themselves is considered of major importance. With respect to these considerations, it is argued that limitations of vehicle size and weight in the eastern States restrict freedom of choice in selecting the vehicles and combinations thereof which are best adapted to transport the different commodities.

In the western States, the limitations are such as to enable transporters to utilize fewer vehicles and larger container units, in moving a comparable amount of freight.

Primarily, the effect of restricted vehicle lengths in this region has been to compress the heavy vehicle operation into vehicles of shorter wheelbases which accommodate fewer axles. Fewer axles, in turn, mean heavy axle loads which must be restricted if they are not to go beyond the design capacity of road surfaces. Eastern States have been raising vehicle length limits to come up to AASHO recom-

1960.] 

mendations. Massachusetts extended the maximum length for semitrailers from 45 to 50 feet in 1959. Length limits in western

States range up to 65 feet on certain roads for semitrailers. 

It has been argued that only a small minority of truckers overload their vehicles or improperly distribute their loads so as to constitute an axle-weight violation. However, this claim can be challenged on the basis of a recent Canadian study which showed that when an operator loads to the permitted gross weight, one or more axles tend to be in violation of the maximum weight permitted. This was true without exception among those vehicles checked by Canadian authorities.¹ Nevertheless, spokesmen for the trucking industry vigorously question the restrictive policy governing the weight limitations which are particularly burdensome to interstate carriers. 

A trucker moving from State to State is often confronted with several standards, of which he must conform to the most restrictive. 

Statutory limitations on axle weights or combinations thereof pose a frequent problem for haulers of non-liquid freight which may shift in transit so as to make an otherwise legally loaded truck illegal by concentrating too much weight on one axle. Carriers of fuel oil, gasoline, milk and other fluids are not bothered so much by this problem. Except in multicompartment tanks, the load distribution is always the same on all load-carrying axles. 

To avoid such statutory inconsistencies, the effect of AASHO's policy is to stimulate transport of heavy tonnage in multipleaxle vehicles of supportable axle load. To this end, permissible lengths of vehicles are increased to make possible more axles, spaced properly to bring their combined loads within the safe capacity of bridges. The latter approach is illustrated in the accompanying figure which compares the operating economy of two different multipleaxle semitrailer combinations. The comparison indicates that a bigger 5-axle combination can carry a payload of 17.5 tons compared to the 3-axle combination payload of 12.5 tons, yet the axle loads of the larger vehicle (13,750 pounds on the tractor drive axles and 12,550 pounds on the trailer axles) are actually less than those of the smaller vehicle (18,000 pounds each on the tractor drive axle and the trailer axle). Highway and Bridge Costs and Taxation.

As indicated above, the trucking industry argues for higher legal axle loads. Highway officials recognize that operating economies undoubtedly flow from such heavier axle concentrations, but they contend that any such advantages to the east are nullified in whole or in part by the increased original cost of highway and bridge construction required for their accommodation. Moreover, frequent applications of excessively heavy axle loads certainly are a large factor in increased maintenance costs. 

In connection with the effects of vehicular loads on the economics of highway and bridge construction, some highway officials have supported the adoption of tax policies which call for taxation of vehicle owners in proportion to their use thereof. 

Such a highway taxation policy was the basis of a bitter dispute in 

Oregon, culminating in two referendum issues: (1) sustaining the

Legislature's increase in the rate of fuel taxation paid by the heavy vehicles, and (2) amending the state constitution so as to limit taxation of heavy vehicles, (a) to registration fees based on weight, and (b) to fuel taxes based on gallonage. The proposed amendment was initiated by the trucking industry. The big truckers suffered a severe defeat when the first carried by nearly 2:1, and the second was defeated by nearly 4:1.

The Oregon highway tax program, which has been copied in modified form by several States, is financed by user taxes. Each class of vehicle pays in proportion to its responsibility for the cost of road construction and maintenance. Other methods used to tax heavy vehicles include a ton-mile tax equal to a flat fee multiplied by the tonnage of the vehicle. Abundant literature and statistical data are available to weigh the benefits of alternative policies of weight-mile or ton-mile taxation. 

MASSACHUSETTS LAW GOVERNING MAXIMUM WEIGHTS OF VEHICLES. 

Statutory History. 

The present Massachusetts law regulating gross weights of certain motor vehicles was first added to the General Laws in 1946, as follows: 

"Any provision of section thirty of chapter eighty-five to the contrary notwithstanding, a motor vehicle having two axles, which vehicle with its load weighs not more than eighteen tons, and a semi-trailer unit or a motor vehicle having three axles, which unit or vehicle with its load weighs not more than twenty-five tons, may travel on a public way without a permit as required by said section thirty; provided, that no such motor vehicle or semi-trailer unit, the weight on any axle of which, measured at the ground, exceeds twenty-two thousand four hundred pounds, or, in case of axles spaced less than six feet apart, eighteen thousand pounds, shall so travel without such a permit." (G. L. c. 90, § 19A as inserted by Acts. of 1946, c. 397.) 

This act was based on House, No. 1891 of 1946, which was substituted for the original petition of the Massachusetts Motor Trucks Association which proposed the single-axle load limitation of 22,400 pounds and also called for a table of weights according to distance between axles. Maximum gross weights of up to 73,278 pounds would have been allowable under the original truckers' bill. 

Between 1946 and 1956 the law was amended several times so that today it bears no resemblance to the original section. Of three amendments enacted in 1951, the first authorized law enforcement officers to compel suspected violators of the maximum weight provision to proceed to the nearest weighing scales within a three-mile

Incongruity of Massachusetts Law. 

The application of G. L. c. 90, § 19A to the commercial world of highway operations contains several inconsistent parts. Using the statutory method for determining the maximum gross weight of a vehicle, a figure is achieved which is not attainable because the law limits the gross weight of vehicles of three axles or more to a smaller figure. The following example, under assumed conditions illustrates this statement:

Assume a 40 ft. wheelbase (L) and apply the statutory formula W = 1000 (L+25). The maximum gross weight (W) would be 65,000 lbs. But the statute specifically limits the gross weight of vehicles of three axles or more to a gross weight of 60,000 lbs. The statutory table also places a ceiling of 60,000 lbs. gross weight for vehicles with a wheelbase greater than 35 ft. 

In other words, a 1959 statutory amendment authorized an increase in the length of vehicles from 45 feet to 50 feet, thereby indirectly allowing heavier and larger payloads, yet, at the same time, the effect of increase is voided because of the existing ceiling on gross weights.

Secondly, the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, the AASHO 

policy statement and the model Uniform Vehicle Code of the National Committee on Uniform Traffic Laws and Ordinances, all favor the 18,000-pound maximum load on a single axle. Nevertheless, Massachusetts is among the small minority of contiguous States which adheres to the much heavier maximum of 22,400 pounds (except that the maximum in Massachusetts is 18,000 pounds if the axels are spaced less than six feet apart).

As pointed out elsewhere in this report there appears to be no experimental basis for the 22,400-pound maximum, whereas the contrary is true for the 18,000-pound figure.

The owners and operators of the large trucks cite their design and the job they are built to do, as factors that should not be overlooked in considering this legislative proposal. The big truck must be of sturdy construction to sustain the heavy impact of loads being dropped from power shovels. Chapter V. Conclusions. 

Existing laws which regulate the weights of commercial motor vehicles using state bridges and highways affect (1) the equipment and operation costs of motor carriers, (2) the manufacturers who build their equipment, (3) the design and construction of state bridges and highways, and (4) the taxpayers who must pay for these roads and bridges. Obviously, therefore, any substantial amendment of the existing laws warrants serious consideration by the 

General Court. 

Control of motor vehicle weights is made difficult by the interlocking relationship among gross weights of vehicles, and their payloads, axle weights and lengths. The close correlation between gross weight of a vehicle and the payload which it carries naturally prompts an operator to load his vehicle up to its legal maximum weight. In doing so, it is not uncommon to have maximum axle loads exceeded. Overloaded axles, in turn, are a prime factor in damage to road pavements. Along with road design, soil and climate, they ultimately govern both original and maintenance costs of pavements.

The report has summarized existing evidence relative to the widely held opinion that, as between axle loads and gross vehicle weights, the former is the principal factor in pavement costs, where as gross loads of vehicles are the important factor in bridge design and costs. 

The report has also discussed the considerable results which would flow from enactment of the pending proposal (House, No. 1521) to reduce gross weights by 10 per cent, while no benefits have been uncovered. Since truckers would have their payloads cut by more than 10 per cent they would undoubtedly agitate successfully for higher carrying charges and the public would be the ultimate loser. 

The Commonwealth would be heavily affected, if carrying charges were raised by the owners of construction vehicles, because the State is the principal road builder, and hence the biggest customer for the services of such vehicles.

House, No. 1521 proposes the forced reduction in gross weights of vehicles. Even if enacted it would not achieve any savings in bridge construction or maintenance costs because (a) existing bridges have been designed to support heavier gross weights than those allowed by statute, and (b) those bridges on federal aid arteries must meet federal standards which require a considerably greater capacity to support gross weights than is proposed by House, No. 1521. Because the bill omits any reference to axle loads, savings could not be achieved on pavements.

The report has also discussed the benefits to be obtained by the State by enforcement of sound statutory maxima for axle loading. 

Such enforcement should also prevent distributing maximum vehicle loads solely for convenience in unloading and thereby overdoing the vital factor of axle weights, with much harm ensuing to the roads. 

Certainly the evidence demonstrates that when operators do load to permitted maximum weight, one or more axles frequently show a loading in violation of statutory limits. Similarly, the total combination load of a tractor and semitrailer may be below the gross weight limit, yet it frequently happens that either the tractor or semitrailer is overloaded.

From all the evidence, the axle weight limit in Massachusetts and its enforcement merits careful study by the General Court. Its axle load limits are considerably higher than those recommended by the distinguished highway authorities cited in the report.

The record of known violations indicates inadequate enforcement of present limits in axle weights. The reasons given for this showing are ascribed partly to limited personnel assigned to enforcement, partly to lack of adequate scale and other facilities to enforce the limits, and partly to the fact that the more frequent offenders are exempted by statute from the weight limit. 

Heavy duty vehicles also raise special problems because they compete with lighter trucks which carry gross loads of such weight as (1) to constitute an inequitable operation under the maximum weight statute and (2) to be a safety hazard.

An informed solution for all of these problems will be greatly assisted by the results of the very comprehensive $22 million dollar road test now being conducted in Illinois by outstanding experts.

Weighing of Motor Vehicles and Trailers. Every operator of a motor vehicle operated on a way shall, upon request or direction of any officer authorized to enforce the provisions of this chapter, operate such motor vehicle with any trailer which may be attached thereto to and upon the nearest scales within a distance of not more than three miles and permit the weighing thereof together with its load. Whenever such vehicle has been so weighed and is found to weigh in excess of the limits provided by this chapter, no person shall further operate it upon a way unless the gross weight shall have been reduced to comply with said limits by removal of the excess weight. (Acts of 1951, c. 344.)

Operation of Overloaded Motor Vehicles, Trailers, etc., Prohibited. No motor vehicle or trailer or semi-trailer unit shall be operated, nor shall the owner or bailee thereof require or permit such operation, when the gross weight of such motor vehicle or trailer or semi-trailer unit exceeds either that provided by this chapter or the gross weight for which such vehicle is registered, nor shall any person load or cause to be loaded such vehicle in excess of such weights. 

(Acts of 1951, c. 617, as amended by Acts of 1956, c. 389, § 1.) Travel upon Public Ways by Certain Types of Motor Vehicles Authorized. Not withstanding the foregoing provisions of this section, nor any contrary provision of section thirty of chapter eighty-five, a construction type motor vehicle having two axles, which vehicle with its load weighs not more than twenty-three tons, or a construction type semi-trailer unit or motor vehicle having three axles, or a semitrailer unit or motor vehicle having three axles while carrying liquid petroleum products, which vehicle with its load weighs not more than thirty tons, may travel on a way while engaged in hauling construction materials or liquid petroleum products without a permit as required by said section thirty of chapter eightyfive; provided, that the gross weight of such vehicle as operated does not exceed the gross vehicle weight rating as established by the original manufacturer of the chassis; and provided, further, that the vehicle is duly registered in this commonwealth for such weight. The operator of such a vehicle shall at all times comply with the speed limitations as provided by section seventeen. [HOUSE, No. 1918 of 1959.] The Commonwealth of Massachusetts 

In the Year One Thousand Nine Hundred and Fifty-nine.

AN ACT RELATIVE TO THE GROSS WEIGHT LOAD ON CERTAIN CONSTRUCTION TYPE MOTOR VEHICLES. 

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives in General Court assembled, and by the authority of the same, as follows:

Section 19A of chapter 90 of the General Laws is hereby amended by striking out the last paragraph, as inserted by chapter 782 of the acts of 1951, and inserting in place thereof the following paragraph:- 

Notwithstanding the foregoing provisions of this section, nor any contrary provision of section thirty of chapter eighty-five, a construction type motor vehicle having two axles, which vehicle with its load weighs not more than three times the tare or empty weight of said vehicle, or a construction type semi-trailer unit or motor vehicle having three axles, which vehicle with its load weighs not more than thirty tons, which is directly engaged in highway or bridge construction work or in supplying or transporting materials for any public works project which is being constructed under contract with the commonwealth or any of its political subdivisions or agencies or with any department or agency of the United States government, may travel on a public way while actually engaged in such work as previously defined within a radius not exceeding twenty-five miles from the site of such a construction project without a permit, as required by said section thirty of chapter eighty-five; provided, that the gross weight of such vehicle as operated does not exceed the gross vehicle weight rating as established by the original manufacturer of the chassis; and provided, further, that the vehicle is duly registered in the commonwealth for such weight; and provided, further, that the hauling of liquid bituminous road materials from a Massachusetts refinery be excluded from a mileage limitation. The operator of such a vehicle shall at all times comply with the speed limitations as provided by section seventeen, subject to the further provision that any such vehicle whose gross weight, including its load, exceeds the maximum limits authorized by the first paragraph of this section shall not be operated at a speed in excess of thirty miles per hour on any way when carrying such weight.
