Gift of the Orient 

WHEN you walk into a room for the first time, you frequently receive an immediate impression of friendliness, or warmth, or dignity, or coldness. It happens every day because rooms, just like people, can and do radiate personality; and the kind of personality they radiate depends on their component elements and how they are blended. 

The most important factor in achieving decorative harmony is to first select your keynote, the dominant element around which the room is planned. It may be an unusual piece of furniture, a painting or tapestry, but most often it is the decorative treatment of the floor. This is easy to understand because the rug or carpet is physically and actually the base of the room; it is the first thing you come in contact with as your feet cross the threshold; it makes a definite impression upon you through its feel as well as its appearance. Properly chosen, it sets off the room furnishings to advantage, yet has a distinct character of its own. It harmonizes with them in feeling and in color, but in a soft gracious manner. It provides subtle overtones for the color scheme which can be repeated here and there in the general plan of decoration. 

How often, upon entering a well-appointed room, have you made the mental note, "It's that beautiful rug that 'makes' the room. 99 To a person of discernment it becomes readily apparent that the choosing of the rugs and carpets for the home is a matter of paramount importance, especially when you want to create an atmosphere of beauty surroundings that express your own personality and good taste.

The purpose of this booklet is to help you make that choice in the most completely satisfactory manner. 

For a rug is something you do not buy often-at least not for the 

Page Three

same room. It will last ten years, twenty years, or longer. 

It is something you are to live with day after day.

If a rug is uninteresting, it has a depressing effect on you, whether you are aware of it or not. If it is a thing of beauty, it enriches your home, provides a suitable background for your personality, and is a constant source of pleasure. 

Since the selection of a rug is so very important, it deserves consideration from many angles before a decision to purchase is made. While you are interested in learning the best type of rug to choose, the one which will give you the fullest measure of beauty and enjoyment, you naturally will seek the one offering the best possibilities for lasting service and a definite degree of economy. 

You know that true economy has very little to do with the initial price you pay for things. It consists rather of the length of use and amount of enjoyment you get. These are the real measure of value.

The important factors that constitute value in Oriental rugs and carpets are pointed out briefly in the chapters that follow.

IT T IS important to remember that the term "Oriental 

Rugs" can be rightfully and truthfully applied only to rugs and carpets that are hand-woven in the Orient and near- Orient by the same traditional methods that have prevailed for hundreds of years. Only in Oriental rugs can be found that rare combination sought by the wise purchaser-distinction, beauty, utility, economy, and investment value.

The following pages tell you how Oriental rugs are woven, the relative merits of the various types, how to distinguish one from another, the meaning attached to the distinctive designs, and many other interesting facts about them. 

In order to get the most out of this information, we suggest that you visit your local Oriental rug dealer and there see and feel for yourself the various types mentioned here. The dealer, too, from his vast and. deep knowledge of the subject, will help you to recognize the differences between a Sarouk, a Kirman, and other types of Oriental rugs. You will find it highly worthwhile to possess this interesting knowledge and, in turn, to impart it to others on occasions.

The Changeless Art of 

Rug Weaving

THERE are two main types of hand-woven rugs; the knotted rug, and the flat or tapestry woven rug. The latter type was known from the early days of civilization. The "knot" was known in Turkestan as early as the third century and from there it was carried by travelers into other 

Oriental lands.

The earliest known knotted rugs in existence today are some 15th century Hispano-Mooresque pieces woven by Moors. There are also some exquisite 16th century rugs that were woven in Turkey and in Persia (now known as Iran, its ancient name).

Included among the flat-woven rugs of the Orient are the 

Sumak (often called Cashmere), which come from Caucasus, and Kilim rugs made in Turkey, Caucasus and Turkestan. 

In most industries modern machines have replaced handwork, but Oriental rugs are hand-woven today exactly as they were in olden times. There has been no change. 

What a tribute to the art! And this, undoubtedly, accounts for the availability today of Oriental rugs with the same high, enduring quality as of old. 

Hurry and bustle are unknown quantities to the rug weaver. With characteristic patience he still carefully ties each knot by hand. The dyer of the wool still makes his dyes from the dried bodies of insects or the roots of plants, using a secret formula handed down from generation to generation. The designer of the rug still follows age-old patterns which, to him, have a special significance. 

The weaver is the real producer of the rug. His tools are few and simple-only a knife, a comb, and a pair of scissors. 

He sets up his loom by placing two upright timbers into the earth or against a wall, spaced as far apart as the rug is to be wide-three, six, nine or more feet. Oriental rugs are woven as wide as 30 feet, so the distance between the timbers sometimes is quite great. The weaver then joins the up-

Spinning Yarn rights at top and bottom with crossbars and the loom is ready.

Warp strings are the foundation of the Oriental rug. The number used varies greatly, depending upon the size and the fineness of the weave. These strings are tied on the loom running parallel between the uprights. Following a pattern that has been laid out, or even making up a pattern as he goes along, the weaver knots by hand around the warp strings the wool strands that form the pile. 

Knots are tied in rows, one to each pair of warp strings.

The weaver's fingers work with extraordinary dexterity. 

Using a hooked knife to lift the threads, the weaver passes a strand of wool between the two strings, twists it around one and under the other, pulls the resulting loop into place in the row, and then cuts off the strand-all in less time than it takes to describe. Expert weavers repeat this operation as many as 8,000 to 12,000 times a day.

A nine by twelve rug may contain as many as four million separate, individual, hand-made knots. This accounts for the fine detail in design, and the amazing compactness that makes for unusually long life. 

After the weaver has finished his rug, he takes it to one of the many market-places to be sold to representatives of American and European importers. While most 

Oriental rugs conform to a high standard of quality, some weavers are naturally more talented and gifted than others, as is the case with artists and sculptors. 

When a well-known weaver appears with a rug of fine quality, there frequently is spirited bidding for the purchase of the exceptional fabric. 

Until recently the rugs were carried to the port of shipment by the ancient and colorful camel caravan, but today they are speeded on their way by motor trucks over improved roads.

After months of patient, skillful hand-weaving, and a journey of six to seven thousand miles, these luxurious masterpieces of Oriental art are placed upon your dealer's floor, where you, at your leisure, may delight in their beauty and choose the one that best suits your purse, purpose, and personality.

Buying Oriental Rugs 

ORIENTAL rugs are bought either by collectors as genuine works of art or by home-makers as the most suitable background for a distinctive home.

It is not the purpose of this booklet to deal with 16th and 17th century masterpieces for they are very rare and are found chiefly in our museums and in the homes of prominent collectors. Yet, many of these charming old pieces are not always unavailable and prohibitive in price. Fre quently the writer has witnessed the sale of a number of these gems at extremely low prices. One exquisite large 

Turkish rug woven in the 16th century was sold recently for $1400.00 (the only mate of this rug is now in a European museum and cannot be purchased at any price). The buyer, in this instance, procured a real bargain. A large Ushak, also a 16th century piece, was purchased for $1100.00.

This buyer also made a most fortunate purchase for one hour after the sale was consummated another buyer offered him twice the amount for the rug. These purchasers not only secured Oriental rugs that were collectors' pieces but invested their money wisely. Indeed, for one who has the means and likes to invest money in an object that is a delight to own as well as handle, Oriental rugs offer a most desirable opportunity. 

18th and 19th century rugs are also obtainable occasionally today. Although not made on royal looms (except the old Hereke rugs of Turkey) they are charming examples of native art. Such rare pieces as an 18th century Joshagan, Shiraz, Tekke-Turcoman, Ghiordes, Kula, Chien-Lung (Chin ese), or the so-called Armenian Dragon carpets of the Caucasus, varying from subdued tones to almost barbaric but fascinating interplay of design and color, are priceless to a collector. For, after all, an antique rug is of no value unless it is made with good wool, good dyes and with sound foundation (warp and weft). It must have well coordinated designs and suitable colors. No intelligent collector would buy today a tattered rug void of character, no matter how old it was. 

And a character is that intangible "it" that all Orientals must have. 

If you are buying Oriental rugs for the first time ask yourself the following questions: 

1. Is this rug made of good materials? Does it have a good strong foundation and a pile made of quality wool or silk? 

2.—Are the dyes reasonably fast? 

3. Do the designs and colors have character?

It is not necessary that your rug be closely woven if the pile is sufficiently high; only when the pile is short should you insist on a closely woven rug.

Most of us know that all dyes will fade somewhat if exposed to the elements or if subjected to a certain amount of washing. Even vegetable dyes are no exception to this rule. The important thing to know is that the dyes used in dyeing the fabric of which the rug is made are the best vegetable or chemical dyes obtainable. A slight amount of fading mellows the colors and tones down the sharpness in design of some Oriental rugs; it brings out the natural lustre of good wool and makes the rug more attractive. This does not mean that a good Oriental rug is one in which the colors are pale and almost white!

A reliable Oriental rug dealer will help make these points clear to you and volunteer some tests.

Regarding designs, Persian rugs as a group are floral and the same applies to many Turkish and large Indian rugs. 

Caucasian rugs are generally geometric in pattern and many of them are woven in small narrow sizes. Rugs from Turkestan (often sold as Bokhara and Samarkand) are also geometric in design with octagons, or "Guls", which are like tribal seals.

A red color predominates in the so-called Bokharas; and 

Chinese greys, blues, yellows and reds abound in the socalled Samarkands. Last, but not least, it is interesting to note that Chinese rugs are usually plain or made with a little design on their borders or center employing green, rust, red, apricot, gold, blue and other delicate shades which are masterpieces of plain carpeting. Some exquisite pieces featuring sculptured designs have been introduced recently. 

They have been most favorably received. 

As to the selection of design and colors that is something which your individual taste should dictate. When choosing your rug bear in mind, however, that an Oriental rug is a thing to be used on the floor and not as a painting or similar works of art. 

When thinking of colors one must also consider that all famous Oriental rugs were originally woven in rich hues. Old rugs of Persia abound in wine-red, indigo, light and medium-blue, gold, brown, green and related shades of color; because the colors were used moderately these rugs are a delight to the eyes of those who appreciate real art.

It is really not important that you know a lot about the origin of your Oriental rug. It may have come from 

Persia (Iran), China, Russia (Caucasus or Turkestan),

Turkey or India. It does not matter whether your rug has a "Sehna" knot or Ghiordes knot; whether it has webbings or tassels, overcast sides or salvaged ones. The important points to bear in mind are—it must be well made, within your means in price, and possessing colors and designs of distinctive character that blend with your decorative scheme.

It is the Oriental Rug that "Makes" the Room

Your rug may have been made by nomads such as Kurds, Kashgai, Turcomans or Yuruks. If such is the case it prob ably has good materials and original designs. It may have been woven in a village, like most rugs made in the Caucasus are. But most likely your Oriental was woven under skilled supervision to insure you good materials, well harmonized patterns and a lifetime of service. Your rug may even increase in value many times its original cost if care is used in its selection. Many fine rugs after giving years of hard service frequently are sold at a handsome profit. 

History relates that Oriental rugs have always graced the floors of famous mansions. The charming atmospheres of our fine old 18th and 19th century homes would not have been possible without the aid of Oriental rugs. Many hooked rugs were also woven and used in the early days of America and the same colors that abound in Oriental rugs were em ployed in their making.

You are apt to hear much loose talk today about Oriental rugs being out of style. This argument, usually employed by those with axes to grind, fails to impress any one who is alert-because when it is considered that many of the largest manufacturers of domestic rugs and carpets are competing with one another for supremacy in the production of machinemade imitations of genuine hand-woven Oriental rugs, and take pride in advertising their ability to do so, it just does not ring true.

PERSIAN 

Persian Rugs

ERSIAN rugs due to their individuality, excellent workmanship and distinctive designs are among the most popular of Oriental rugs both in the United States and Europe.

In distinguishing modern Oriental rugs it is best to remember that there are two classes; nomadic rugs, and townmade rugs. Also, names of rugs often mean types. For example, a Kashgai rug is named after the nomad tribe of the same name; a Sarouk, after the town in which it is woven-although the best Sarouks are produced around 

Sultanabad. 

The nomadic rugs are unsupervised, independent weav ings; while the town-made rugs are supervised rugs woven on order and under the supervision of "Ustads," or masters, who contract to produce them for American and European firms.

Each kind of rug is distinguished by certain definite characteristics which are here briefly outlined. 

KIRMAN-Rugs of this name are among the richest of Persian weavings. They are produced in the city of Kirman and in the nearby villages of Rawar, Mahun, and Zarand. 

The patterns are usually composed of floral forms, frequently roses, bound together with roaming tendrils and sprays that make an exquisitely graceful and elaborate decoration. The colors are one of the outstanding achievements in Kirman. rugs. Fine highland Persian wool permits of pastel shades in green, soft rose, red, rich gold, ivory, and blue. This unusual, different kind of wool is endowed by nature with qualities that make it ideal for this purpose, combining silky softness with remarkable durability. The knotting is generally compact and firm. Kirmans are particularly appropri ate with French furniture, and are also ideal complements for other graceful types of decoration. Marco Polo, famous world traveler, wrote in glowing praise of Kerman rugs in the 13th century.

KASHAN-The weavings known as Kashans have often been called the most "heavenly" of rugs. They are chiefly 

Page Twelve

woven in the city of Kashan. It would seem that the weavers incorporate in their rugs their dreams of a fair and lovely place in which to live.

The patterns usually lean toward foliage motifs. This motif is repeated in a dignified all-over arrangement. The background color is commonly a deep, rich rose or blue. It is not uncommon for a good Kashan to have as many as 

40,000 to 50,000 knots in every square foot.

In Kashan lived the most famous of all Persian rug weav ers, a man named Maksoud, who went to his final reward 

400 years ago. His fame still lives, however, because of one carpet he wove the "Ardebil Carpet", in the weaving of which Maksoud spent almost his entire life. This rug now reposes in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London where thousands go each year to admire its perfection. In a corner of the rug, its creator wove in Arabic the words, "Maksoud of Kashan". These words have become his badge of immortality. Succeeding generations of weavers, proud of his achievement, have been striving to emulate this supreme example of the rug-weaver's art. 

SAROUK-The rugs known as "Sarouk" are likewise named after the town in which they originated. Saruk is a tiny hamlet in a western part of Iran. Here came into being the weave which has become the most popular of Persian rugs in the United States. After having introduced this popular type of rug, the weavers in Saruk were unable to keep up with the demand for their products. Conse quently, the production of Sarouks spread to dozens of nearby cities and villages. The largest of these is the city of Sultanabad, the rug producing center of Iran. 

Thick-napped and solidly woven, the modern Sarouk is characterized by floral designs placed on a deep rose or blue background. The rugs are heavy and durable; many of them weigh more than 100 pounds in the nine-by-twelve-foot size. 

Two of the main design motifs in Sarouks are the Cypress Tree and the Vase. The former represents to Persians the 

Tree of Life, which represents eternal life. The Vase is a relic of the love of the old Persian Shahs for Chinese vases. 

There is a difference in design between modern Sarouks and those woven 50 years ago and more.

KAZVIN-Another type of rug which takes its name from the place in which it is woven is the Kazvin, woven in

LILIHAN-Lilihan is the name of a small Persian town whose inhabitants are largely of Armenian descent. The 

Armenians were brought to Persia more than 300 years ago by a famous Shah who was much impressed with their artistic ability. Present-day Lilihan rugs are woven not only in the town itself but also in surrounding villages. The floral designs, and the rose and blue backgrounds of Lilihans are similar to Sarouk patterns and colors; the weave is somewhat looser and softer. Other rugs are also woven in these communities. Among them are the ARAK which equals the Lilihan in quality, the MAHAL which is somewhat loosely woven and not as fine, and the MUSHKABAD which is inferior to the others in compactness of weave and in quality. These rugs are available at reasonable prices. 

FERAGHAN-This famous name means to Persian rugs what Chippendale does to English furniture. These very fine rugs are woven in many of the villages strung across the plain that stretches between Sultanabad and the city of Hamadan. Usually they are woven in all-over designs and colors which range from soft reds to midnight blue, with apple green in the border. Antique and semi-antique 

Feraghans are highly prized by collectors.

KIRMANSHAH-Kirmanshah is the name of a city in the northwestern part of Iran, hundreds of miles from the city of Kirman. It is a wool centre and only a few rugs are woven here by Kurds. These rugs have geometrical designs abounding in eight-pointed stars and large undulating leaves. They are the true Kirmanshah rugs and are today obtainable only as semi-antique pieces that is, rugs about 50 years old or older. Now the city markets few rugs and

HAMADAN-Old Hamadan rugs are well known for their natural colored, tawny brown wool. Today the city is a market place where many rugs woven in the surrounding villages are sold. The majority of Mosul rugs are marketed here. Hamadan is the old city of Ecbatana, and the tomb of Esther, of biblical story, is reputed to be located here.

HEREZ, SERAPI, GOREVAN-These three weaves come from the northwestern district of Iran. They have strong, imposing patterns, usually large medallions and rectilinear corner decorations. The designs are geometric, bold yet graceful. They are very durable.

TABRIZ The city of Tabriz in northwestern Iran is the home of this famous weave, specimens of which include both medallion and all-over designs. It is the site of the famous blue Mosque and in the 16th century was the Iranian capital and a prominent rug weaving center. 

MESHED Magenta and blue colorings are most popular in these rugs which originate in and around Meshed in Khorassan, East Iran. Thousands of devout Mohammedans come each year to Meshed to pray at the shrine of Ali Riza, regarded as a saint.

TEHERAN-Woven in the city of Teheran, the pres ent capital of Iran. The city is in the shadow of 18,000foot Mount Demavend, the place where Iranians believe 

Noah's Ark came to rest. Teheran rugs are compactly woven with pile clipped short to give clear definition to intricate patterns. 

ISPAHAN is one of the most sumptuous cities of Iran.

Here are to be found gorgeous mosques and famous palaces. 

Across the river, and connected by a bridge is the Armenian city of Julfa, inhabited by descendants of Armenian artisans brought here forcibly by Shah-Abbas in the sixteenth century from the Caucasus. Ispahan is the market place of several types of rugs woven in the surrounding country.

Rugs from China 

ROM China also come beautiful pieces of handwoven rugs. Unlike Iran, however, where rug-weaving takes place in almost every city, large and small, the Chinese rug. weaving industry is concentrated in three centers on the Eastern coast of the country-Tientsin, Peiping (formerly 

Pekin), and Kalgan.

There is a great difference between the production methods of the two countries. In Iran, in most instances, the individual weaver produces his own rug, brings it to the market-place and offers it for sale to the highest bidder. 

In China, on the other hand, almost all the weaving now takes place in large modern work-rooms, the majority of which are owned and operated by American firms. Hundreds of skilled Chinese weavers are employed. They work on rugs for which the wool, the dyes, and the designs are supplied to them.

Most of the production, however, follows the age-old tradition of the Chinese rug weaving art. That there is such a tradition is proved by records which show the Chinese were weaving famous rugs as long ago as the year 1400. 

As a result of this improved, yet highly individual, method of production, Chinese hand-woven rugs are available today in modern designs that are very popular. The patterns are created by eminent designers, forwarded to China, and rugs are produced which blend harmoniously with modern rooms. 

Other types of rugs woven in China today are repro- 

Page Eighteen

ductions of the most famous French rugs of the Louis XIV and XV period. These rugs duplicate exactly the most fam ous Aubusson and Savonnerie patterns and enjoy a wide vogue in the United States.

In their weaving, the Chinese use exactly the same type of knot employed in many Persian rugs, the "Sehna knot".

This is because, in ages gone by, traveling Chinese merchants brought back to their native land Persian rugs which were then copied as to weave; also, Persians went to China and taught the Chinese how to produce this type of fabric. 

While the weaves are similar, the designs are very different -so different in fact that Chinese rugs can easily be distinguished from all other types of Oriental rugs. 

Where the Persian weaver uses a dozen colors in a single rug, the Chinese is content with four or five. The shades he uses, moreover, are often unlike any seen in other rugssoft, lustrous shades of gold, tan, copper, rust, green, and rose. Full primary red is almost never seen. When a red is used, it is in the soft tints that are found on fine Chinese porcelains. Peach, persimmon, apricot, and pomegranate hues are frequently employed.

Design is another unique characteristic of Chinese rugs, unique because of its sparseness. Where the Persian rug weaver covers the background of his rug with a maze of pattern, the Chinese usually weaves a plain background of one single color. Sprays of design in harmonizing colors come out diagonally from opposite corners and there is occasionally a touch of design in the center of the rug. 

This method of weaving makes a lovely rug which is as charmingly exotic in its way as the sing-song of the Chinese language. Many modern specimens are woven without borders of any kind and the effect is one of simplicity and spaciousness which is so often an asset in decoration. The number and variety of Chinese symbols is almost endless, as can be seen when we consider that there are three great religions in China-Confucianism, Taoism, and Buddhism. Following is an explanation of a few of the more important symbols.

A canopy is the emblem of sovereignty. A vase or urn represents a receptacle for sacred relics. Purity is pictured by a lotus flower, and a conch shell means victory for the soldier and safe voyage for the mariner. 

A dragon symbolizes the universe and Emperor while a lion is the guardian of the law and protector of sacred temples. Animals signify the month of the year, from 

January to December, in the following order: horse, dog, bullock, monkey, serpent, dragon, rabbit, rat, tiger, hare, fowl, and boar.

A sword represents supernatural power and a fan is the symbol for rebirth of the souls of the dead. A phoenix stands for both the Chinese Empress and for brides. 

There are many other insignia and symbols, all of which have deep meaning to the Chinese and historic interest to us of the Occident. It adds considerably to the pride of possession to understand and be able to interpret the symbols in one's own Chinese rug.

The most important reason why Chinese rugs have met with such favor in Western countries is their superb quality. 

A Chinese rug will endure in all its beauty for years. 

Their soft pastel colorings and spacious patterns make them ideal rugs for many types of rooms. In bedrooms, for instance, Chinese rugs are supreme.

In living rooms of Queen Anne and Chinese Chippendale style (to mention only two), Chinese rugs enhance the furnishings and bring out all their latent beauty. Modern rooms, and rooms in the French periods find their ideal complements, of course, in the rugs that are being woven particularly for these styles in far-off China.

ALTHOUGH 

LTHOUGH Iran and China are the main sources of supply for hand-woven Oriental rugs, there are other Eastern and Near-Eastern countries which also produce rugs and carpets of real merit.

Foremost among them are Turkey, Russia, and India. In these countries, rugs are woven in exactly the same manner as in Iran and China.

In matters of design, compactness of weave, use of colors, and religious symbolism, however, the rugs of each country differ. Therein lies their individuality. 

The most important centers of rug weaving in Turkey today are Smyrna, Hereke, Istanbul (Constantinople), Sivas.

Kaisarieh and Ladik. The names of the most famous types of Turkish rugs are: Ghiordes, Kula, Bergamo, Ladik, Anatolian, Melez, Kir-Sher and Konieh. 

These are antiqes and semi-antiques, however, and many of them are not woven today bcause the Armenians and Greeks, who formerly made most of them, were persecuted and had to flee their homes. Millions were actually driven from the towns and cities and massacred. 

Rugs called Anatolians, done in bold colors, are woven in Turkey today. In the town of Hereke a closely woven rug called "Hereke" is also produced. In Sivas, Kaisarieh and the district of Smyrna a few rugs are still woven by native workmen.

Many weavers of the Sparta rugs, who formerly wove this quality in Turkey, now produce them in Greece where they fled to escape persecution. In Balkan lands, too, Turkish-type rugs are now produced by refugee Armenians and Greeks.

The Turkish government has long since realized the fatal mistake of permitting to go unchecked the atrocious massacres that wiped out millions of its useful Christian citizens. 

In spite of the government's sincere offers of peaceful settlement the refugees and their families refuse to go back. 

Only a shadow of the former production of Oriental rugs is carried on in Turkey today.

Rugs from Russia

UGS from Russia have been enjoying renewed popu larity in the United States during the past five years. From 

1918, the year of the Russian revolution, until 1932, no Caucasian or Turkestan rugs were exported to the United States. These rugs had always been popular here among those who knew them, but by 1932, due to their scarcity, they had achieved a sort of legendary fame based on their unique designs, bold colors, and fine quality. In recent years, the Russian Government again began the manufacture and exportation of these rugs, employing the weavers who had made them in the past and who had never forgotten their hereditary art.

The sections of Russia in which the rugs are produced are north and east of Iran. The country is as wild and primitive today as it was 700 years ago when Gengis Khan ruled all the known world from his headquarters there.

The Caucasus (where Caucasian rugs are woven) is a region of towering mountains. It contains twelve peaks higher than Mont Blanc, Europe's highest. The land is peopled by seventy distinct primitive tribes, most of whom do not speak the language of neighboring tribes. These tribes weave rugs that are as wild and colorful, as bold and sturdy as themselves. There are many Armenians and 

Georgians weaving rugs here.

From the Shirvan district in Eastern Caucasus come such rugs as Shirvan, Kabistan (a variation of Kuba), Daghestan and Hila (sometimes called Baku). Nearby, are woven the shaggy but rustic Kazak and Guenge, the well-known 

Chichi and Lesghian. In the south the beautiful Karabagh rugs are woven mainly by Armenian mountaineers. A tribute to the cleverness of these people is the fact that in the nineteenth century they copied successfully by hand the French Aubusson patterns. There is also adequate proof that the so called Dragon carpets woven in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries were woven by Armenians. Reports now coming from Erivan (capital of Armenia) one of the important rug producing centres of Soviet Russia, Page Twenty-two

state that Armenians stand high in the ranks of rug weavers. 

They are designing and weaving patterns of the famous Armenian manuscript illuminations of eleventh to fifteenth centuries. Except for Karabaghs, Caucasian rugs are woven in scatter sizes and runner lengths.

Three or four of these small rugs placed in a room will lend sufficient warmth and color to give a highly individualized and lovely appearance. This is an economical method of creating the effect of one large Oriental rug. 

CENTRAL 

AL Asia is a region of great sandy deserts extending to the borders of China proper-it includes Turkestan. Here are produced such rugs as the Tekke, Yomud, Saryk, Salor and other famous Turcoman rugs, including the Bokhara as well as the so called Smarkand rugs woven in Chinese Turkestan.

Turcoman rugs are the easiest of all Oriental rugs to distinguish because, except for minor differences, all of them are similar in appearance. This is very rare among hand-woven fabrics but the beauty of Turcomans is so great that, regard less of their similarity, they are highly prized. They are erroneously called "Bokhara".

They are woven with a pattern of octagons evenly and symmetrically spaced over the entire field. The color is the famous Turanian red or plum blended with natural white, black, and yellow. The octagons, called "Guls", are tribal seals. These have been passed on from generation to generation.

In the weaving of Tekke rugs the "Sehna knot" is used, as in Chinese and Persian rugs, but the wool used is finely spun and woven more compactly. There are as many as 30,000 individual knots in one square foot of a Tekke rug—making it durable beyond belief. These are the "Royal Bokharas" of the market.

An interesting fact about Turcomans is that they harmonize unusually well with modern furniture. It is really remarkable that these primitive Asiatic tribes weave rugs that match perfectly the modern furniture which American designers have produced to express our streamlined civilization. Rugs from India

N INDIA too, the art of rug weaving is hereditary and traditional, extending as far back as the year 1550. 

At that time, one of India's great emperors, Akbar by name, imported hundreds of Persia's most expert weavers, set them up in his various palaces, gave them food, lodging, and salaries and ordered them to produce rugs as fine as those of their native land. Soon the various princes, nobles, and wealthy men of India, following the example of their Emperor, also imported weavers, and a thriving rug-making art was established.

Some of the original rugs woven by these Persians, their sons and grandsons, are still in existence. One is on view at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York City where it may be inspected by the public. This particular rug, woven in silk instead of wool, is said to have 2,552 knots in every square inch!

The rug weaving industry of the country assumed a more commercial aspect as the fame of Indian rugs spread throughout the world. Many foreign companies, American and 

British, maintain work-rooms in the cities of Amritsar, Srinigar, Agra, and Balala. Other rug weaving cities are 

Lahore and Allahbad. 

Of all these cities, Amritsar is the most important. The story of how rug weaving came to Amritsar is very interesting. Up to 1840 Kashmir was world-famous as a shawlproducing center, but in that year the city suffered a famine. 

Many inhabitants fled the city, going to Amritsar. Later the vogue for shawls began to decline and the former shawlmakers turned to rugs instead. Today, America obtains many rugs from Amritsar, and the chances are that the Indian rug you see at your dealer's store was woven by a descendant of the weavers who produced the Kashmir shawl your grand. mother wore.

Odd and Interesting

Facts About Oriental Rugs 

In the Orient, rugs are used not only on floors, but Aladdin and His Lamp also as beds, pillows, door hangings, and saddle-bags for horses and camels. No Oriental would think of walking on a rug while wearing his shoes. Upon entering a house, he removes his shoes and dons slippers. It is the constant tread, over many years, of smooth clean slippers that gives Oriental rugs a natural, soft sheen which is so highly valued. 

The majority of recently woven rugs are given a similar sheen by means of a special process that produces in a few days the effect of many years. This process also mothproofs the rugs. The organic dyes used in Oriental rugs are obtained from insects, roots, plant leaves, and other vegetable matter. To obtain "cochineal crimson", one of the loveliest shades, the dyer must go to the woods and search for cochineal bugs found in the bark of oak trees. Only female cochineal bugs are of value since the male bugs do not contain the coloring extract. Fifty thousand female bugs are boiled in special vats, then dried in the sun. The result is one pound of dye! 

Dyers cannot obtain the same color twice because after dipping the wool in the dye they permit the excess dye to drop back into the vat, thus changing its intensity. Occasionally, a weaver uses up all his yarn of one color before the rug is completed. To reproduce the exact color is impossible. 

The weaver therefore continues the rug with wool of a slightly different shade. This causes bands of varying colors in the same rug, known as "abrash". Sometimes the rug becomes more valuable and interesting because of the effects produced in this way.

RUGS 

UGS of all kinds are subjected to hard treatment, especially in homes where there are children. But 

Oriental rugs are so well made that they can stand lots of abuse. An occasional cleaning adds years to their beauty and usefulness.

Natives of the Oriental countries, to whom we look for enlightenment on the subject of caring for rugs, remove their shoes and put on slippers when they enter a house. They do this because they know that the element which is most destructive to the rug is not the rubbing action of smoothsoled shoes-it is grit!

Grit, composed of the rough hard particles of dirt which we track into the home from outside, sifts down into the roots of the wool nap. When the rug is walked on, the grit cuts against the strands of wool and saws off small pieces of the pile.

It is against this grit that primary attention should be directed. The best weapons we have to counteract the damage done by grit are the carpet sweeper and vacuum cleaner, suction type only. Rugs should be vacuumed or swept at least once a week. Neither the sweeper nor vacuum cleaner can do any damage to an

Oriental rug because the hand-tied knots are so firmly placed that you cannot pull them out even with a tweezer 

Rugs should also be beaten once a month, but not in the old-fashioned way of hanging them over a line and striking them with all one's might. Instead, the rug should be placed upon the grass and beaten gently but firmly with a carpet beater, the face of which is wide enough to distribute the force of the blow. Both sides should be beaten.

Some bits of grit become imbedded so deeply that they are beyond the reach of household implements. These are the most dangerous of all. It is for this reason that an Oriental rug should be cleaned once every year by an expert carpet

Oriental rug repairers are so skillful that their mending cannot be detected.

The most frequent types of spots and stains and the best methods of removing them are as follows:

Ink-stains: When ink is spilled, take up as much of it as possible with a blotter or sponge. Apply luke-warm water a few drops at a time, working the water well into the pile, then absorb the mixture with a fresh blotter, being careful not to rub or spread the stain. Continue this operation until all the excess ink has been removed. An ink eradicator may then be applied but this is not always satisfactory for it may bleach the wool. If this treatment does not remove the stain entirely, it is best to call in a professional rug-repairer. 

Rust-stains: A patented solution, known as "Erusticator", available at drug stores, is recommended for stains of this type. 

Coffee, mercurochrome, and iodine stains: These are diffi cult for the layman to remove. The best that can be done is to follow instructions for ink-stains. That part of the stain which remains can only be completely eradicated by a qualified rug cleaner or repairer.

Food, grease and oil stains: Carbon-tetrachloride, obtainable at drug stores under either its own name or tradenames, will remove stains of these types. Dampen a cloth and rub lightly until the stain disappears. Do it at once, because food and grease stains attract moth larvae. 

Animal-stains: Should be treated at once by neutralizing the area with a solution containing one part of household ammonia with 20 parts of water. Apply only enough to dilute the spot, absorbing the excess with a cloth or blotter. 

If traces remain, a rug repairer should be called.

About Decoration INTERIOR 

NTERIOR decoration is, of course, a study in itself, requiring many years of serious application. It cannot be covered in a booklet of this type, yet a discussion of Oriental rugs would be incomplete if a few salient facts about decoration were not mentioned.

In the first place, there is an Oriental rug for every type of decoration. The variety of weaves, colors, and patterns makes it possible to select Orientals that are appropriate for any room in your home.

For instance, Jacobean furniture finds its best complement in a correctly chosen Sarouk rug with rich rose background. 

William and Mary furniture is perfectly harmonized with a pastel Kirman. Chinese Chippendale and the correct

Chinese rug make an ideal blend. A Kazak or Kabistan sets off Colonial furniture to perfection.

And so it goes through every period of decoration. Even with Modern rooms, there is a correct Oriental rug, as you will see when you place a Bokhara or Fereghan in a setting of this type.

In rooms which do not happen to be done entirely in a particular period, there are usually two or more pieces of furniture which are in the same or kindred styles. It is these pieces which set the decorative keynote of the room. 

The correct Oriental rug will not only go with them, but will blend all the furnishings in the room into one harmonious whole.
