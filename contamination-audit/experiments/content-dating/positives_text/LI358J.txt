THE HONORABLE ROBERT B. MEYNER GOVERNOR OF NEW JERSEY CONCERNING A STUDY OF THE FISCAL METHODS, PRACTICES AND PROCEDURES OF THE NEW JERSEY STATE LEGISLATURE

The Honorable Robert B. Meyner Governor, State of New Jersey State House 

Trenton, New Jersey

Dear Governor: 

In the late Spring of this year, you discussed with me the repeated accusations made by various members of the Legislature with respect to alleged improper transfers by various Executive 

Departments. You informed me that you had been advised that the legislative financial transactions were subject to far more criticism and attack than those of the Executive Department and accordingly instructed me to make a study of the financial practices of the Legislature. 

The legislative concern with Executive spending--in fact, with all phases of administrative functions--has found expression in countless inquiries and investigations into the Executive branch by the Legislature. However, I have not been able to find one single instance in the history of New Jersey where any comprehensive study or examination has been made of the operation and management of the Legislature's fiscal affairs. I am not aware of any inquiry or investigations of the legislative fiscal process in its entirety as ever having been made in any of the States.

The weapons in the arsenal of the Executive Department, and particularly in my office, are limited where an inquiry into the Legislature's affairs is involved. The power of subpoena and the right to interrogate witnesses under oath, so essential to a successful and thorough inquiry, are not available in this respect.

The right to study and examine public documents and to ferret out the facts is available to every citizen and to the Attorney General's office as well. This right I have fully exercised, and a large quantity of evidence has been completely -1-

i.

explored and evaluated by members of my staff under the supervision of John G. Thevos, Administrative Director of the Department of Law and Public Safety and Deputy Attorney General. 

On the basis of the factual findings produced so far, I am prepared to show that: 

1) The leaders of the Legislature have applied a double standard, in the moral as well as legal sense, to govern the fiscal affairs of the 

Executive Department and their own Legislative branch.

On the one hand, they have assiduously invoked rules of strict budgetary practices upon the Executive agencies, and on the other hand, completely ignored those rules in the loose, bewildering maze that has characterized their own spending. (2) In the past five years, the Legislature expended in all its budgetary accounts the sum total of $7,383,427.51. A large portion of this amount was spent without regard for sound fiscal principles and without regard for the rules it itself has laid down. Ignoring the restrictions it has imposed upon all Executive Departments, the Legislature has spent public funds by giving itself what amounts to a "blank check" on the Treasury of the State. Respectfully, Grover C. Richman, Jr.

LEGISLATIVE "CONTROL" OVER STATE FUNDS 

The Legislature has always exercised control over spending by the Executive. This control was greatly strengthened by a law adopted in 1954 (effective July 1, 1955), by which the Legislature created the office of the Legislative Budget and Finance Director for the purpose of establishing tighter control over the fiscal affairs of the entire State Government, on a day-to-day basis. The effect of this law and its administration by the Legislative Budget and Finance Director was to give complete continuing control to the Legislature over spending by the Executive. 

This supervisory function embraces the fourteen.

Executive Departments as well as the various "Committees, Joint Committees, Legislative Commissions and the Agencies and Commissions established in the Legislative Branch of the 

State Government." -5-

Specific power was granted to the Legislative 

Budget and Finance Director to "keep and maintain at all times such books of accounts and other accounting records as may be necessary to enable him to insure against the over-expenditure of any appropriation made to the Legislature" and its various subdivisions as outlined above. (emphasis supplied). 

In addition to these broad powers, the Legislature, its Committees, Joint Committees and Legislative Commissions and each Agency and Commission established in the Legislative branch of the State Government, are charged with the responsibility by this legislation to "specify all expenditures to be made by them during the next ensuing fiscal year, 11 before

October 1st of each year and the filing of the same with the 

Legislative Budget and Finance Director. 

The act further provides that the Director "shall receive and examine the same and shall certify them, with the requests for appropriations for his office, to the Governor and shall report thereon to the Joint Appropriations Committee of the Legislature. These provisions are to be found in 11 

R.S. 52:11-32 to 42, inclusive.

This statute also provides that the Law Revision and Legislative Services Commission shall designate the 

Legislative Budget and Finance Director. Pursuant to this authority, G. Clifford Thomas, former Speaker of the General

Assembly and Assemblyman of Union County, was appointed as Director. He is also vice-chairman of the Law Revision and 

Legislative Services Commission. J. Lyman Brown was designated as Deputy Director of the Legislative Budget and Finance office in July of 1956. -6-

Under the terms of this law, these officials have been charged with the duty of examining Executive expenditures during the year and requests for appropriations as well. As far as the Legislature and its various budgets are concerned, these officials are charged with the duty of approving, auditing and certifying the expenditures of their own Division and maintaining a careful, day-by-day check of all the expenditures of the various Legislative budget, accounts. 

In theory, the Legislative Budget and Finance

Director's office, as a vital arm of the Legislature, was to assist the Joint Appropriations Committee in the budgetary allocation and subsequent expenditure of public funds for all branches of State Government. In practice, the Legislative 

Budget and Finance Director's office did nothing to change the Legislature's system of appropriating and disbursing state moneys for its own purposes without proper budgetary allocation. 

LOADING THE LEGISLATIVE BUDGETS 

The prevalent practice of the Legislature up to 1955 was to vote original appropriations annually for legislative purposes far below the anticipated expenditures.

This practice continued after the creation of the Legislative 

Budget and Finance Director's office in 1955. this process of understatement has been followed in order to minimize the cost of operating the legislative branch of the State Government at the time its original budget is adopted. Apparently -7-

CONCEALED EXPENDITURES BROUGHT TO LIGHT IN RECAST BUDGETS 

In order to determine the true pattern of Legislative spending, we have examined the records of the State Treasurer for the fiscal years 1955-1956 and 1956-1957 and checked these expenditures against the A-10 Legislative budgets for those two years as they appear in the Governor's 

Budget Messages dated January 31, 1956 and January 31, 1957.

The ensuing pages of this report contain photostatic reproductions of the A-10 Legislative budgets for the fiscal years 1956 and 1957. On the opposite page of each reproduction appears a recast of actual expenditures made by the Legislature for those two years. These actual expenditures made by the Legislature are analyzed and itemized, on the basis of the Manual for the Preparation of Budget Requests issued by the Budget Bureau in the Department of the Treasury. This manual is the official guide of the Legislature in considering the budgets for all Executive departments. The classification of accounts as it appears in the 1956 and 1957 Recast Budgets has been substantially followed for many years by the Legislature with respect to Executive budgets. 

The classification of accounts is used not only for appropriations but also for legislative control over expenditures by the Executive Department. This is one key by which the Legislature exercises its power over the public purse. The Executive and Judicial branches of State 

-12-

government have adhered to the methods and procedures set forth in this manual. However, in plotting their own budget and spending, it appears that the fiscal leaders of the Legislature have not adhered to the manual. 

Legislative fiscal officers have authorized the payment of many bills running into the thousands of dollars in one fiscal year and charged these expenditures to the previous fiscal year. By this device, the Legislature defeats the very purpose of a budget. For moneys are appropriated for specific purposes for a definite period of time, i.e. from July 1 in one year to June 30 of the following year. At the end of the fiscal year, unexpended funds in any budget must revert to the General Treasury unless reappropriated or encumbered. The Legislature strictly adheres to these principles insofar as they concern Executive Departments and forces compliance, but completely ignores these principles in its own budgets. 

Money appropriated in a prior year is spent for expenses of a subsequent year. -13-

THE CONSTITUTIONAL PROHIBITION Article IV, Section 4, Paragraph 7 of the 1947 

Constitution provides as follows: "Members of the Senate and General

Assembly shall receive annually, during the term for which they shall have been elected and while they shall hold their office, such compensation as shall, from time to time, be fixed by law and no other allowance or emolument, directly or indirectly, for any purpose whatever.

President of the Senate and the Speaker of the General Assembly, each by virtue of his office, shall receive an additional allowance, equal to one-third of his compensation as a member." (emphasis supplied)

The In 1948, the impropriety of providing meals for members of the Legislature at State expense, and the conflict of that practice with the Constitutional provision above quoted, was given wide publicity. -6-

On September 21, 1948, A. R. Everson, Executive

Vice-President of the New Jersey Taxpayers' Association informed the then Attorney General of New Jersey of the expenditure of more than $10,000 in State tax funds for legislators' food bills and other expenses. Mr. Evers on urged "proper action by the Attorney General to forestall payment of any illegal bills or to recover any illegal expenditure of tax funds. 11 At the same time, the New Jersey Taxpayers' Association charged that the expenditures in question were "clearly in violation of the new State Constitution." It was further charged that the "Use of tax funds for personal expenses of legislators and their clerks is an innovation which started only recently ***Presumably it is the duty of the State's

Attorney General to take proper action to forestall payment of illegal bills or to bring action to recover any illegal expenditure of tax funds." 

On October 7, 1948, Attorney General Van Riper replied to Mr. Everson in a letter confirming that the expenditures in question had been made by the Legislature and that "the meals were served to members of the Legislature and employees thereof.

The opinion by the Attorney General on October 7, 1948 stated further that such expenditures "for members of the Legislature being for personal rather than legislative purposes, were -7-

in contravention of Article IV, Section 4, paragraph 7 of our 

State Constitution, which provides that members of the Legislature shall receive 'such compensation as shall, from time to time, be fixed by law and no other allowance or emolument, directly or indirectly, for any purpose whatever.'"

Attorney General Van Riper urged the discontinuance of the practice and concluded that, although he did not feel justified in advising the State's fiscal officer to start proceedings for the recovery of the money improperly expended, he thought it "fair to assume that the views expressed here in will be adhered to in the future." Dear Mr. Everson: 

Under date of the 21st ultimo, you wrote me calling attention to an article appearing in the Newark Sunday News of September 12 concerning expenditures alleged to have been made by the State for "legislators' food and other bills during the past session" and asking me to investigate this allegation and take action for the recovery of any illegal expenditures. -8-

In acknowledging receipt of your letter, I called attention to the fact that

I was without any knowledge of the subject matter of your communication except that which came to me from a reading of the News article, but that I would investigate the same and communicate with you further with reference thereto. 

I have investigated the allegations contained in your letter and in the News article by examining the official vouchers covering the expenditures in question and by conferring with the State's fiscal officers and with some members of the Legislature. I find that expenditures were incurred in an amount approximating that mentioned in your letter, which expenditures are evidenced by vouchers made out to the State of New Jersey by various hotels and restaurants, and that these vouchers were approved in due form, by the 

Chairman of the Senate and House Committees on incidental expenses, and subsequently processed by the State's fiscal officers charged with that responsibility, and then paid out of the Legislature's Appropriation, entitled "Incidental and Contingent Ex penses".

With the exception of a comparatively small amount, which was for hotel rooms, the the expenditures seem to have been for "lunches and dinners". The vouchers do not identify any individual legislator to whom these meals were served, and they are not itemized further than detailing a certain number of meals. It is to be presumed, however, from the fact that the expenditure covering these vouchers was paid out of the legislative account that the meals were served to members of the Legislature and employees thereof. Assuming this to be a fact, it is my opinion that expenditures for hotel accommodations and meals for members of the -G-

Legislature being for personal rather than legislative purposes, were in contravention of Article IV, Section 4, paragraph 7 of our 

State Constitution, which provides that members of the Legislature shall receive " such compensation as shall, from time to time, be fixed by law and no other allowances or emolument, directly or indirectly, for any purpose whatever" 

Further investigation by me discloses that expenditures of this kind appear to have been the practice for many years, although the Old Constitution contained the same inhibition against "other allowance or emolument" as is in the new Constitution:

The facts seem to indicate that the expenditures this year were larger due to the extraordinary nature of the session which imposed more than ordinary demands upon the members. 

Based upon the fact that the expenditure of money for this purpose has been a practice for so long a period of time and upon my conversation with members of the Legislature and State fiscal officers with whom I have discussed the subject, I am satisfied that the expenditures were incurred, approved and paid in absolute good faith without any suspicion on the part of those concerned that they in any way conflicted with the constitutional provision above referred to.

It is a fact furthermore that, so far as I can ascertain, the legality of such expenditures has never heretofore been officially questioned in this State and no court of this State has ever passed upon their validity. In addition to this, an examination of our files shows that the Attorney General, who is the legal advisor of the Legislature and the State's fiscal officers has never advised either the Legislativé body or the fiscal officers concerned that there was any question about the right of the Legislature to incur, or of the fiscal officers of the State to approve, these expenditures, -10-

In view of all the circumstances, therefore, I do not feel justified in advising the State's fiscal officer whose responsibility it would be to authorize the institution of proceedings for the recovery of money which has been improperly expended, to take any further action concerning the expenditures in question. Now that the law has been clarified concerning expenditures of this type, I think it fair to assume that the views expressed herein will be adhered to in the future.

LEGISLATORS and committee attaches who have become accustomed to dining sumptuously at the expense of the taxpayer must pick up their own dinner checks hereafter. 

The attorney general has informed them they have no constitutional right to personal expense accounts, Individually or collectively.

The little item of $10,000 or so which was piled up during the last session for food and refreshment at Trenton's pleasant inns will stand as paid. The violation was unintentional, and anyway it would be impracticable, rules Mr. Van Riper, to recover from individual legislators. 

But, he warns, in effect, don't let it happen again. 

The issue was raised by the New 

Jersey Taxpayers' Association, which was outraged by The Newark 

News's disclosure that many of the legislative branch were dining. and dining on the taxpayer, during legislative sessions. For one night the bill was $425.05. 

The Constitution permits members of the Legislature to fix their own salaries, but denies them any "other allowance or emolument, directly or 

Indirectly, for any purpose whateve- There is no doubt that this specific language precludes personal expense allowances.. 

There was a measure of justification for having the state pick up dinner checks back in the days when assemblymen and senators toiled through the Winter and much of the 

Spring for a mere $500. But the new salary of $3,000 per annum which they voted themselves at the last session would seem sufficient to take care of a large number of steaks, chops and martinis, with something left over. 

Granting Mr. Van Riper's premise that the absence of personal identification on the expense vouchers makes a recovery suit impracticable, this anonymity should not preclude action if the offense is repeated.

ILLEGAL PRACTICE CONTINUED TO DATE 

Due to the magnitude of the task involved in examining the voluminous records of the State Treasurer's office for the entire nine-year period, it was impossible to determine whether this practice continued immediately after Attorney General Van Riper's opinion. However, a spot check of the State Treasurer's records for the fiscal years 1950-1951 and 1951-1952 disclosed that the Legislature did expend State funds for meals. The records prior to 

July 1, 1950, are microfilmed and, as indicated above, the job of examining them was not undertaken because of the lack of sufficient manpower. 

This Legislature did continue this illegal practice from July 1, 1952 through June 10, 1957 despite the

Constitutional prohibition and the ruling of former Attorney 

General Walter D. Van Riper.

LEGISLATORS' SALARIES INCREASED FROM $500.00 TO $5,000.00 DURING PERIOD 1948 TO 1956 The 1948 opinion of Attorney General Van Riper was written six months after a salary increase from $500.00 to $3,000.00 a year went into effect for members of the Legislature. 

This was on April 9, 1948. The Legislators had been receiving $500.00 annually prior to April 9, 1948.

But the practice of having the State pay for its food, meals and in some instances, its hotel accommodations, continued despite the 500 per cent salary increase; and with the start of the 1956 legislative year on January 10, 1956, the Legislature gave itself another salary increase of 66-2/3 per cent, from $3,000.00 to $5,000.00 a year. In other words, members of both Houses now receive 900 per cent more than they did prior to April 9, 1948.

METHODS USED IN OBTAINING STATE PAYMENT FOR MEALS 

Except for the office of the State Auditor, the

Law Enforcement Council (whose administrative affairs are conducted by assigned members of the State Police) and the Mosquito Control Commission, the Legislature and its other agencies utilized voucher Form 100 (a copy of which is annexed hereto) in obtaining payment for meals. This form is expressly designed for use by vendors of materials and supplies and independent contractors who have claims against the State. 

The three agencies first mentioned above used

Form 105 (a copy of which is annexed). Form 105 is expressly designed as a travel and expense voucher and is the correct form to be used in this instance. It properly requires an itemization of all expenses incurred while in the service of -20-

j.

State, and is uniformly and mandatorily used by employees and officers of the Executive branch. Form 100 requires no such itemization.

The almost exclusive use of Form 100 to obtain payment for legislative sustenance has evolved from the adoption and use by the Legislative branch of the "meal ticket system."

The meal ticket system provides for the issuance by the Legislature of meal tickets to members and other officers, employees, clerks and attaches of the Legislature. The meal tickets are in two colors, a blue one for luncheons at $1.00 and a white ticket for dinner at $3.00. These persons then use the tickets to purchase luncheons and dinners at the various eating establishments honoring them. These tickets are supposed to be signed by the persons using them and handed in lieu of payment to the restaurant management. 

In turn, the restaurant owners forward these meal tickets together with the Form 100 vouchers to the legislative fiscal officials. Only a handful of the hundreds of Form 100 vouchers submitted by the Legislature to the State Treasurer lists the names of the members of the Legislature or its employees who incurred the meal expense. 

No vouchers have been discovered for payment of meals to which any meal tickets were attached to support the expenditure and, more important, to identify the individuals who incurred the expense. The tickets were apparently separated from the vouchers before those vouchers were forwarded to the State Treasurer for payment. As a result, there is no record in the State Treasurer's office of the identity of those legislators and employees and attaches for whom the State provided food and drink. -21-

The regular use of these meal tickets is confirmed by the fact that the General Assembly ordered the printing of a large quantity of these tickets from an Elizabeth printer during the period beginning April 8, 1953 to January 25, 1957.

Executive Order No. 24, dated July 1, 1950 provide in part as follows: 

"Section 22. NO ALLOWANCE AT OFFICIAL STATION. 

Under ordinary circumstances no subsistence expenses will be allowed an employee at his official station, at the place where he resides, or within a radius of 10 miles from such station. if ' "Section 23(c). Dinners. If employees work overtime or are in travel status for periods of three hours or more, charges for dinners will be authorized at a rate not to exceed $2.00 per person, including tip."

No effort has been made by the Legislature to conform to these and other regulations, so that, once more, a double standard has been imposed which, even if it is contended that it can be justified legally, no moral justification can be spelled out. The practice of anonymity in submitting vouchers runs counter to any accepted efficient business practice, and lends itself to obvious opportunities for fraud and deception. 

I cannot recommend to you that action be taken to recover sums improperly paid in this situation, because the identity of the persons who charged the State for food and drink is not known. Their anonymity has been preserved because: (1) The great part of the Form 100 vouchers were vague and indefinite, and failed to disclose the names of the persons who incurred the meal expense. -31-
