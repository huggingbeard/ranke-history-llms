HIS PAMPHLET is intended as a practical guide for the use of consular officers. It owes its origin to the fact that the writer, while associated with the firm of Schnitzler, Thorn & Dayton, attorneys for the German Consulate General in

New York City, has found a need for a concise statement of the rights and duties of consular officers to aid those unfamiliar with their legal status and the extent of the powers conferred upon them by our law. 

Although this subject has been heretofore treated in detail by learned authorities of international reputation, it would seem that recent decisions of the courts, and in particular the changes caused by the Treaty of Friendship, Commerce and Consular Rights of 1923 with Germany, would make a new presentation welcome.

CHAPTER I CONSULAR OFFICERS 

HERE ARE four classes of consular officers: (a) Consuls 

General, (b) Consuls, (c) Vice Consuls, (d) Consular Agents.

There is no distinction as to privileges and immunities between any of the four above named officers. A Vice Consul is classed as a principal consular officer, even though he may be vested with no power when the chief consular officer is present and acting.¹

Hereafter the word Consul, unless expressly stated to the contrary, will be used in its generie sense to describe all the above consular officers.

Eligibility, tenure of office, rank and manner of appointment are, of course, determined solely by the laws and regulations of the foreign power by which the consular officers are commissioned, and with these we are not concerned. 

There is no rule prescribing the nationality or domicile of a consular officer. He may be a citizen of the appointing country, a citizen of the country to which he is accredited, or a citizen of a third country.2

There is, however, a distinction between consular officers who are citizens and subjects of their own country and engaged exclusively in their consular functions and those who reside in the country to which they are appointed and who are engaged in other pursuits there, as to the grant of many privileges and immunities. No differences whatsoever exist between these two classes in the eyes of the law of nations however. The distinctions which do obtain have been created by treaties. 

The appointment as consular officers of residents engaged in business in the country or district to which they are appointed is a recognized practice, which has been dictated and sustained by expediency, in localities where the consular office is relatively inactive.3

Consuls, while resident within their consular district, may retain domicile in their home state.4

THE NATURE OF THE CONSULAR OFFICE 

A Consul is an officer or agent accredited by his government to reside in a foreign country for multifarious purposes, but primarily to represent, promote and protect its commercial interests and those of its citizens and subjects. He is clothed with authority for commercial purposes only.¹ 2 A Consul General or other consular officer is not a public minister or diplomatic officer, but he seems to be more than a mere commercial agent. Authorities have differed in the past on the standing of consular officers. A very few have given to them a diplomatic standing, and a number have held them. merely mercantile agents, not entitled to any consideration or protection other than that of a private alien. The view which has been adopted by practically all nations now is, that while not public officers entitled to the protection of the law of nations as representatives of their state, consuls are under international comity, and out of respect for their office given a limited international status and entitled to certain privileges and immunities."

The position of the United States Government has been variable and somewhat obscure in the past. Secretary of State 

Adams, in a letter to the President, wrote as follows: "Consuls are indeed received by the government from acknowledged sovereign powers with whom they have no treaty. After taking a series of conflicting positions, the United States now seems definitely committed to the position that a consul has certain privileges, although not the immunities of a diplomatic officer.

The attitude of the government is best expressed in the consular regulations of 1881, which read in part as follows: "Although consuls have no right to claim the privileges and immunities of diplomatic representatives, they are under the special protection of international law and are regarded as the officers both of the state which appoints them and the state which receives them.” 

The regulations of 1896 and the revision of 1923 made no change in the above.

A consul does not generally have the power to communicate directly with the receiving government. He may communicate. with the local authorities, and a failure by them to grant the desired redress or satisfaction will justify resort to diplomatic channels. In the absence of a diplomatic representative, communication may then be had directly with the government. 

This right is accorded in most of the treaties. 

International law and comity dictate that proper respect shall be shown to the consular office, and legislation has evidenced this duty and protected it. The Act of Congress of

SOURCES OF CONSULAR AUTHORITY 

The sources of consular powers, privileges and immunities are: (a) treaties, (b) international law, (c) international comity, (d) regulations and legislation of the country appointing the consul, (e) regulations and legislation of the country receiving the consul, (f) the exequatur.

In view of the multiplicity of these sources and the attendant conflict between them, there has been a wide disparity in the opinions of various well known writers as to some phases of the consular office and its functions. 

It naturally also follows that there have been and still are many distinctions and differences between the rights, powers and privileges granted to consuls of different countries. Happily, through treaty provisions, and the application of the "most favored nation" clause, the consuls of the various powers are gradually being placed upon an equal plane. 

Of the above sources of consular authority, the regulations and legislation of the appointing country need not be considered here, as they operate only as a limitation upon the consular power and are best known to the individual consuls. 

Treaties are the Supreme Law of the land and supercede all other sources of authority. Article VI, Sec. 2, of the Constitu tion of the United States, expressly states: "This constitution and the laws of the United States which shall be made in pursuance thereof, and all treaties made or which shall be made under the authority of the United States, shall be the supreme law of the land; and the judges in every state shall be bound thereby, anything in the constitution or laws of any state to the contrary notwithstanding."

Chief Justice Marshall held "there can be no mistaking the significance or the effect of these plain, concise and emphatic provisions. The states have surrendered the treaty-making power to the general government, and invested it in the Presi dent and Senate; and when duly exercised the treaty resulting is the Supreme Law of the land, to which not only state laws, but state constitutions, are in express terms subordinated."

THE EXEQUATUR 

The government of the United States is not compelled by international law to receive foreign consuls. It might exclude consuls from all portions of its territories or it might admit them conditionally, providing that such exclusion or conditional admission apply equally to all countries and providing further, of course, that such exclusion or limitation be not in violation of treaty right.

The right to maintain consuls arises solely through treaty or convention or depends upon permission from the country of residence.¹

The appointment of a consul is evidenced by a commission or patent forwarded through diplomatic channels to the Secretary of State for transmission to the President. This patent is submitted to the President and, if there is no objection to the reception of the individual, recorded in the Department of State. An exequatur may then be issued to the appointee. 

The exequatur is signed by the President and countersigned by the Secretary of State, and bears the great seal of the United States. This formal exequatur, however, is not always issued to consuls and is rarely issued to subordinate consular officers, who receive in its place an authorization signed by the Secretary of State. The exequatur is, in effect, an executive order recognizing the official character of a consul and declaring him free to exercise the powers and enjoy the privileges conferred upon his office by international law, treaties or local legislation. 

A consul must receive the exequatur or equivalent authorization before acting. However, pending the issuance of the exequatur, the consul may be authorized to act provisionally, and if So, his official acts are binding and effective. 

1. Arnold v. United Ins. Co., 1 N. Y. Comm. Law, 354 [13]

"An exequatur will not be issued to a consul sent by a foreign government unless he presents a formal commission." 

In the absence of express stipulation, a consul granted an exequatur is entitled to the same privileges as his predecessors. 

But an exequatur may expressly limit or restrict his powers. 

If such an exequatur is accepted, the limitations contained therein will become effective pro tanto.³ 

The grant of an exequatur necessarily means the recognition of the foreign government. Individuals or other agencies may, however, without formal appointment and without the grant of an exequatur, exercise in fact the consular functions with the permission of the local government without recognition of any sovereign power resulting therefrom. 

While a consul has no official status until the issue of the exequatur, it has been held that the grant of an exequatur after the commencement of an action or suit in court by a consul in his official capacity, and before the dismissal of the same as improperly commenced, cured the irregularity ab initio." 

Since the granting of an exequatur is a recognition of the consul's official status by the government of the receiving state, a change in the political complexion of that government will not affect the status of such consul unless and until his exequatur be withdrawn."

Nor does the overthrow of the sending government invalidate a consul's powers if the exequatur is still in existence." As recognition of the official status of the consul is purely an executive function, the courts may not go behind the act of the Federal Executive Department to determine whether or not such recognition is proper.

CHAPTER II PRIVILEGES AND IMMUNITIES HE EXTENT of consular privileges and immunities under international law would seem to be the amount or degree necessary for the proper performance of consular duties.¹ 2 No diplomatic immunity is granted under international law to consular officers, nor as to privileges is there any well established international rule except as to certain privileges which are recognized by rules of international comity and the law of nations as necessary for the efficient performance of the consular functions. An example of such a privilege is that rendering the consular archives inviolable. Nearly all of the other privileges and immunities now obtaining have been granted by treaties or by the Constitution of the United States, federal legislation or local state legislation.³ 

The privileges granted are not personal to the consul but are regarded as those of the government appointing him, and hence cannot be waived by the consul. In this respect there is a distinction between privileges granted consuls and those granted to their families, suites or servants, in that those granted to the latter may be waived by the individuals. Furthermore, these privileges are only applicable to consular officers recognized by the executive department as possessing consular character. A chancellor of a consulate, even though his duties be identical with those of a consul and even though in fact he is exercising consular powers, cannot claim them.5

INVIOLABILITY OF CONSULAR ARCHIVES 

The inviolability of the consular archives is now universally recognized as a matter of right under international law. It has properly been considered an essential to the proper and efficient performance of the consular functions. The protection granted by international law applies, however, only to official documents. It does not cover private papers, and any attempt to obtain protection for private papers by placing them in the consular files is considered an abuse of the consular authority. 

Official papers include all papers in the possession of the consul used in the exercise of his function, such as communications with his government, with the diplomatic and other consular officers of his country, and with diplomatic and consular officers of other countries, or communications with any person having to do with his official business. They include records, statistics compiled by the consul, and information obtained by him; in short, all papers that have come into the consul's possession by virtue of his office as distinguished from those which he may have obtained in a personal capacity. 

The consul must be and is the proper and only judge as to whether or not a particular paper in his possession forms a part of the consular archives. If this were not so, local authorities might entirely destroy the privilege. An appeal to the government from the decision of a local authority holding that the document was not an official paper and directing its submission. to examination, even though it might result in a decision affirm- [18]

ing the consul's claim, would be merely an idle gesture, as the damage would have been already done if the inspection had been allowed.

In the event of the refusal by a consul to allow an inspection of any papers which are thought to be subject to such examination, it seems that the proper course is for the government of the receiving country to apply to the government which appointed the consul. Such government will direct the consul to turn over the paper if such action is deemed proper. The receiving government has, of course, a further remedy in the event of an abuse of this privilege, namely, the revocation of the consul's exequatur.

This privilege of inviolability is inherent in the consular office¹ and not in the person of the consul, and hence can only be waived by the government of the appointing state. The consul cannot and should not waive the privilege, and any request to inspect consular records should therefore be made through the proper diplomatic channels to the foreign government, and not to the consul.

In order to render this immunity effective, it is extended to testimony. A consul cannot be compelled by subpoena duces tecum or otherwise, to produce or testify as to the consular archives. He is permitted to testify only as to facts within his knowledge in an individual capacity, and not as to those of which he is aware by virtue of his office.² 

Immunity is further extended as a matter of practical necessity to the fixtures necessary for the proper safe-keeping of the archives.

As to any further immunity in this regard, the individual treaties must be consulted. The consular regulations of the various countries almost invariably prescribe the inviolability of the archives, and instruct the consuls to maintain such status.

Virtually all of the American treaties affecting consuls have expressly stipulated such inviolability. The treaties, while not identical in phraseology, are practically identical in purport. 

The clear and unmistakable object of all of them is absolute immunity from search or seizure. Such treaties as do not specifically make provision for inviolability contain a "most favored nation" clause. The Treaty of Friendship, Commerce and Consular Rights with Germany of 1923, furnishes a good example of

EXEMPTION FROM TAXATION A. GENERAL TAXATION 

Neither international law nor international comity recognizes any right of exemption from taxation of consuls or their property. Such exemptions as exist are strictly a matter of local legislation or treaty and vary considerably among the different countries. Where exemptions are granted they cover, in most cases, only direct taxes, rarely indirect or property [20]

taxes, and in nearly all cases are based upon reciprocity. Great 

Britain, which has always refused to enter into consular treaties, does not recognize any exemption from taxation as a matter of right. The United States' consular regulations recognize the consul's liability to taxation.¹

Nearly all of the treaties of the United States affecting consuls provide some exemption from taxation, but such exemptions are not uniform.

There are now in effect nine general types of treaty provisions exempting consular officers from taxation, though under most favored nation clauses some of the countries may claim privileges accorded to others.

C. INCOME TAX 

In the absence of treaty provisions, there is no rule of international law requiring a state to exempt foreign consular officers from the payment of income tax. The amendment of 

March 7, 1864, to the income tax law, exempted consuls from federal income tax on their official salary, as well as on salary from sources outside the United States, on a basis of reciprocity. 

The 16th amendment to the Constitution and the income tax laws enacted thereunder omitted this provision however. 

After a period of considerable doubt as to whether the United States should take the position that a consul is a nonresident alien and hence subject only to tax on income derived from sources within the United States, or the position that although the income be derived from sources here, exemption from taxation thereon should be granted as a matter of international comity, the Treasury Department ruled in 1925 that all foreign consuls and employees of consulates who are nationals of the state appointing them, on a basis of reciprocity, are exempt from the payment of federal income taxes on salaries, fees and wages received as compensation for consular services.

The present policy of the United States seems to be that where reciprocity can be secured, the official income is exempt. 

Income on property of any kind situated or belonging within the territory of the state in which the consul is functioning is taxable, but income from sources outside the country in which the consul is resident may not be taxed.

It must be noted that while the exemption of official income from income tax is granted to all consular officers and employees. who are nationals of the appointing state, all other tax exemptions may be obtained only by consular officers and employees who, in addition to being nationals of their country, are not engaged in business for gain within the state in which they function.

D. INHERITANCE TAX 

There is no provision for exemption from federal estate or state inheritance or transfer taxes. The Secretary of the 

Treasury reported on April 22, 1921, that a federal estate tax was payable on the estate of a deceased British consul. On 

April 25, 1921, the inheritance tax department of Oregon held that there was no exemption from state inheritance tax, as this was a tax on the privilege of taking by inheritance and not [25]

a tax against the consul. In 1923 the Secretary of the Treasury ruled that the estate of a Norwegian consul was subject to inheritance tax, and there being no provision in the law allowing a waiver, that the Treasury Department had no option except to assess the tax. The State Tax Commission of New York also ruled in the same way.

Under international law a consul may be called as a witness just as any private individual,¹ with the exception, as previously stated, that he cannot be called upon to testify as to the contents of the consular archives, nor be compelled to testify as to information which came to him in his official capacity.2

Such immunity from compulsory attendance as witnesses as is granted to foreign consuls is regulated entirely by treaty or local legislation.

The Consular Convention with France in 1853 was the first to include any provision on this point. This treaty granted absolute immunity from attendance in court and provided that if the testimony of a consular officer should be desired, he should be notified to appear in court, and if unable to do so his testimony should be taken in writing or orally at the consular dwelling.

This provision immediately caused complications. In 1854 the French consul in California was served with a subpoena duces tecum in a criminal case pending in the United States District Court. Upon his failure to appear he was brought before the court by body attachment. The defendant in the criminal action contended that the sixth amendment to the Constitution of the United States gave the defendant the right to compel the personal attendance of the witness. Suits against a consular officer might be divided into two classes: a. Those against him in his official capacity. b. Those against him as an individual.

The latter class might be again subdivided into actions arising out of a. Acts performed in his official capacity.

b. Private obligations, acts or omissions. 

In general, it may be said that under international law no immunity is granted to consular officers from either civil or criminal action.¹

Under our system of law the sovereign is immune from suit. 

This immunity is extended by comity to foreign governments.2 

Consequently a suit will not lie against a consular officer in his official capacity, as such an action would in effect be an action against the foreign government. Acts done on behalf of his government may furnish grounds for diplomatic discussion, but they do not properly come within the jurisdiction of local courts.3

It must be borne in mind, however, that a foreign consul may be individually liable for an unlawful use of his official authority or for an act apparently official, but actually outside the scope of his authority. A suit based upon such an act is in fact an action against the consul as an individual, although the basis for the suit may be an act done in an official capacity.

IMMUNITY FROM ARREST AND CRIMINAL PROSECUTION 

In the absence of treaty provisions, there is no rule of international law or comity securing immunity from arrest to foreign consuls. They are, as Attorney General Butler stated, “entitled to no immunities beyond those enjoyed by persons coming to this country in a private capacity from foreign nations, except that of being sued or prosecuted exclusively in the United States Courts."... They must look for protection to the laws of the state where they reside and are answerable to the penal laws of such place.2

However, a foreign state has the right to demand that its officers be accorded full protection in the discharge of their duties and any unwarranted arrest which of necessity interferes with the consular functions and affronts the dignity of his office, would furnish the basis for a diplomatic protest and in some cases a claim for reparation.

Absolute immunity from military service and military billeting as to all consular officers who are citizens or subjects of the country by which they are appointed, is so well recognized by all nations that it is unnecessary to discuss the subject further in detail. CHAPTER III RIGHTS AND POWERS 

HE common law on this point has been almost entirely by treaty. duties are two sources: first, from the federal government, under the constitution, federal laws and treaties, and secondly, from state governments by local law. The tendency of the federal government has been to expand the consular powers, but the state governments have shown but little interest in this regard, and grants by them have been almost negligible. In fact, the tendency of the state courts seems to have been to limit and restrict consular powers whenever possible. The powers and duties of consular officers are concerned principally with commerce and navigation, and with the protection of property of their nationals in their consular districts. In connection with commerce and navigation, in addition to the jurisdiction which they exercise over shipping and disputes between seamen, masters of vessels, etc., which is discussed below, they are authorized to vise passports of foreigners, issue passports to their nationals, to authenticate records and contracts, certify signatures, and to translate and legalize documents. These various functions will not be discussed in detail herein, as they are all covered by the laws and regulations of the sending country.

The second broad field of authority is that of protecting, preserving and safeguarding the property of deceased nationals of their country and the protection of the property and interests of their countrymen. Considerable confusion seems to exist as to the scope of the consuls authority in these regards. 

They will be taken up in detail.

This construction is confirmed when we consider that it harmonizes the function of consuls under the treaty with the function of consuls under established international practice. 

What that practice is has been stated in decisions and confirmed in declaratory statutes and regulations. The function of consuls is to preserve derelict estates. When their countrymen die in foreign lands it is their duty to step in and guard the stranded property from waste. This right belongs to them, irrespective of express statute or treaty, by virtue of their office. (Rocca v. Thompson, 223 U. S. 317, 331; Carpigiani v. Hall, 172 Ala. 287;

If there are no such representatives, it is his duty, so far as he is able, to administer the estate to the extent of gathering it in and transmitting it to the jurisdiction of the domicile. This much he should do, though the title of administrator be withheld from him. If the title were to be given him, its purpose presumably would be rather to authenticate his powers than to enlarge the occasion for their exercise... The convention with Sweden was intended to confirm the powers thus established by international comity, and to facilitate their exercise. Consuls are to have the right, where the estate is in peril, to intervene at once, and if other representatives are lacking, they are, moreover, to have the right to be appointed administrators themselves. 

They are to have this right, not to displace others competent under local law, but the better to fulfill their inherent function as provisional conservators. If there is no qualified relative within the jurisdiction and no one else whom our law gives the right of administration, the consular representative under this treaty may come forward and demand the grant of letters. 

This view is in harmony with the English practice as established by 24 and 25 Vict. (c.121, s. 4). It is in accord with the decision of the Supreme Court of Minnesota. (Austro- 

Hungarian Consul v. Westphal, supra.) It is not inconsistent with a decision of the United 

States District Court for California where the court, while holding the Swedish consul eligible as administrator, found it unnecessary to determine that all others were excluded. (Matter of Holmberg's Estate, 193 Fed. Rep. 260.) It makes the phrase "so far as the laws of each country will permit" equivalent to the phrase "conformably with the laws of the country," construed in Rocca v. Thompson (supra). And, finally, it maintains the continuity of the purpose, revealed repeatedly in conventions and treaties throughout our history, to subject the rights of consuls to the requirements of local law. If the convention means more than this, if it was intended to confer upon foreign consuls an exclusive and paramount right, strange consequences must follow. It is not restricted in its operation to the estates of aliens who have died in the United States while temporarily sojourning here, but who have retained a domicile in their native lands. It applies equally to the estates of aliens who have their domicile in the United States. If an alien, settling in New York, were to build up a business, and, marrying, rear a family among us, the respondent would have us hold that his children, citizens of this state, would have to yield the right of administration to a foreign consul. In that view the consul is not merely eligible as administrator, but eligible to the exclusion of every one else, even the closest relatives of the dead man, and that, too, though the entire estate is located here, and is here to be distributed. We are unwilling to believe that such results were contemplated in concluding this convention. We hold it to be incredible that there has been attached to the consular office a right that exceeds so greatly the occasion and the needs of the consular function." [42]

It can therefore now be stated as settled law that no consul, under any of the present treaties, has a right to administer in contravention of state laws or in preference to persons designated by state statutes.12

It should be noted that in New York a Public Administrator or County Treasurer, both of whom have priority over the consul, may not renounce but may be excused by order of the surrogate.15

A consul cannot delegate any priority which he may be given by statute to any one else, nor is he relieved from the obligations of giving a bond as any other administrator might be compelled to do.

C. REPRESENTATION OF NATIONALS IN ESTATE MATTERS 

Consular officers have, however, a further function, namely, that of representing their non-resident nationals in the administration and settlement of estates here. 

In the Bello Corrunes the court held ".... An authorization given by alien beneficiaries to a consul to represent them and recover their legacies or intestate shares impliedly authorizes the retention of a lawyer and the payment of proper counsel fees.16

Care should therefore be taken to avoid conflict, and a consul should not interfere in any case where his national is already represented unless his action is justified by the circumstances, and then only upon receipt of a power of attorney revoking that previously executed.

The power inherent in the consul's office which has been confirmed by treaty and recognized by the Surrogates' Courts, to protect the interests of his nationals and to receive and receipt for the payment of their distributive shares in estates, would indeed be an illusory one were not the correlative right of petitioning or initiating proceedings in the Surrogates' Courts also recognized. A right to represent and collect monies is of practically no value if the power of enforcing such right is denied. It was surely never intended that the position of the consul must be a purely passive one, standing ready to receive and acquit, but powerless to demand payment. 

Although the writer has not found any case where the consul's right to petition on behalf of his nationals for a compulsory accounting has been opposed and upheld, he can state from his own experience that such petitions have been entertained as 

16. Kosciuski v. White, 286 Fed. 211 [49]

a matter of course by many of the Surrogates. Similar petitions by a consul for orders directing payment to beneficiaries by the City Chamberlain of New York, County Treasurer, and others, have been granted without question. 

The broad powers conceded by the courts in The Bello 

Corrunes, Matter of D'Adamo, Rocca vs. Thompson, Oscan yan vs. Winchester Arms Co., and many others, all of which have been discussed above, clearly seem to include such a right. 

In Matter of Szabo," the court held that a consul may stand in the shoes of a "person interested" in an estate who is a national of his country, in order to petition for revocation of letters or for a compulsory accounting.

In Carpigiana vs. Hall,18 it was stated a "consul had a right to be heard on his petition, independently of treaty provisions, for the purpose of procuring the removal of these administrators... and that the prerogative attaches by law to his consular office without any special authority from those entitled to the estate." This case, however, seems to be bad law, insofar as it controvenes the decision in Rocca vs. Thompson as to the consul's right to be appointed administrator. 

The only authority which the writer has been able to find denying the right to petition for a compulsory accounting is the decision of Surrogate Slater of Westchester County, New

York, in Matter of Tripodi.19 It does not seem that this decision will be followed.

It is the writer's opinion that, irrespective of the nationality of the decedent, under the German treaty, a consul acting for his nationals has a right to petition for a compulsory accounting. 

There is no provision in any of the treaties specifically authorizing appearance by the consul in proceedings to prove a will, and as the basis of the consul's rights is the protection of property which is not directly involved in such a proceeding, there would seem to be no specific authority for such a power. 

As a matter of fact, the Surrogate's Court of New York County has held that a consul cannot, in his official capacity, appear for, and waive the service of citation upon, his nationals in probate proceedings.

Since obviously no country can impose its laws upon another, it follows that a consul, as such, is powerless to create any legal relationship or status by any act of his, unless he is empowered by the receiving country to do so.¹

C. NOTARIAL FUNCTIONS 

International law does not concede to a consul any notarial powers. Such as do exist are not inherent in the consular office but are created by the laws of the consuls country. 

As acts performed by virtue of the authority of the sending country solely could not have any legal effect outside of that country, a great many of the treaties carry provisions authoriz ing certain notarial acts. 

In the absence of statute in his own country or treaty stipulation, a consul has no notarial authority.¹ The power to administer an oath² and to take acknowledgments of deeds, powers of attorney and other papers are beyond the scope of his official authority unless authorized by Statute or Treaty.3

MARITIME JURISDICTION 

The consul of a foreign power resident in the United States exercises a limited maritime jurisdiction. The power originates with the government that the consul represents. It is usually stated, prescribed and limited by the treaties to which the consul may look for guidance. It is an established principle of international law, that this maritime jurisdiction of the consul exists only by consent of the local government in which the consul is a resident. (Denise vs. Hale, 91 U. S. 13; Elwin Kriplan 

9, Blatch 438; the Aubrey 26 Fed. 848.)

Prior to the passage and enforcement of the Seamen's Act of March 4th, 1915, the maritime jurisdiction of consuls of foreign powers, resident in the United States, was greater in scope as to affairs relating to the internal regime of merchant vessels while in the harbors of the United States, than since that Act went into effect. Now, such consuls retain general jurisdiction. to decide disputes between officers and crew; to arbitrate and determine disorders arising on the vessel not affecting the peace of the port. It is one of their functions to provide for shipwrecked or destitute seamen of vessels of their country; to issue passports, visas, make port inspections of vessels of the United States under such treaty provisions, when in the interest of their nationals; to enforce regulations incidental to the trade and commerce of the nations represented. The duty of arbitrating and maintaining destitute mariners of the consul's nationals devolves upon the consul as one of his maritime functions.

Most treaties give the consul a right to take such steps as he may deem necessary to protect the rights of seamen of the nation he represents, whose injury or death occurs in the United [58]

States, or on vessels of the United States, or on other vessels engaged in commerce with the United States. In the absence of dependent relatives of the decedent, the consul is entitled to letters of administration, and it is his duty to retain counsel or take other steps to enforce laws for the benefit of dependents, or obtain relief by virtue of existing statutes, (such as Sec. 688 

U. S. Code-Vol. 46) or Workmen's Compensation Acts. 

If, however, a seaman of a foreign vessel that has entered a harbor of the United States for the purpose of trade, alleges that he has made a demand for half wages upon the master, he may apply to the courts of the United States for adjudication of his rights, and it is mandatory on the courts of the United States to accept jurisdiction, and administer justice in accordance with the local law. See Dillon vs. Strathern, 252 U. S. page 348; U. S. Revised Stat., Vol. 46, Sec. 597; as to waiting time-see Section 596. 

It may be said, however, that there is no disposition on the part of the courts of the United States, to take jurisdiction of matters relating to the internal regime of foreign vessels while in her harbors, unless it appears that the laws of the United States with regard to wages, or maintenance of sanitary conditions, have been violated, or unless injury has been suffered by complaining seaman through the assault by their officers; or there has been neglect of their duty to furnish proper medical care and treatment. Klassen vs. Pearson, 1925 A. M. C., page 1309; Anderson vs. Seirstad, 1926 A. M. C., page 726.

Ordinarily, matters involving nationals of foreign governments, or growing out of the navigation of foreign vessels, while within the territorial waters of the United States, are left on the basis of international comity or etiquette to the jurisdiction of consuls of such nations. Whether a foreign seaman may invoke the protection of Section 588 U. S. Compiled Statutes 

(an amendment to the Seamen's Act of March 4th, 1915) for injuries occurring on board foreign vessels while in the waters of the United States, has not yet been determined, although it does apply in some instances where longshoremen are employed on such vessels. Uravic vs. Jarka, 282 U. S. 234.

With the exception of consuls of the United States and other countries, seated in non-christian jurisdictions, foreign consuls have never enjoyed any judicial power, Per Se in maritime or non-maritime matters. Under those instances where judicial power has been conferred upon foreign consuls, of which the [59]

lipen consular courts, created by Section 141 U. S. Compiled Stat utes, Vol. 22, are typical, the consular court exercises judicial functions in civil and criminal matters between their own nationals only.

In China, incidentally, the American consular courts have been superceded by more formally constituted courts, from whose decisions appeals may be taken to the United States 

Court of Appeals, 9th Circuit (California). See Section 194 U. S. Comp. Statutes, Vol. 22. 

We observe, however, that with the exception of the practice above noted in non-christian countries, and in the arbitrations of wage disputes, of which a review may be had or from which an appeal may be taken, no civilized power permits by treaty or otherwise, the establishment and exercise of judicial power as such within its own territory by consuls.
