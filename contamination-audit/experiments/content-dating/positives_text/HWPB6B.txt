Readers who hailed R. L. Goldman's previous books will find in this mystery novel further justification for their enthusiasm. The same qualities swift action, inexorable logic, lifelike characterization - that made his previous novels such outstanding hits, are found again in "Out On Bail."

A young doctor, Robert Jason, enmeshed in a net of circumstantial evidence, is charged with the murder of a colleague. Out on bail and awaiting trial, Jason sets about solving the crime, with the establishment of his innocence as a powerful incentive to action. The trail is elusive, sometimes completely baffling and always beset with excitement and adventure.

With "Out On Bail," R. L. Goldman secures his place among the leading writers of the mystery novel.

I broke through and got out into the corridor through the door that Jim had used as exit. I ran down the corridor and at the head of the stairway I flung myself at Jim. I grabbed his arms from behind and pinioned them to his sides. "Jim," I said, "the game's up. Come back and take your medicine."

He did not struggle. I let him go from behind and took hold of one arm, turning him around. He said: "I'll go, Bob. I know when I'm licked. I guess you know I didn't plan to get you into this. It was just fate, that's all. I found that handkerchief and used it. After that I had to go through with it." 

I heard running feet around the turn of the corridor. I spoke fast. "You noticed we left out a lot? Is that what you want?” "Good God, yes!" "Then come back and make a confession. I'm going to help you, Jim. I'm prepared to help you, God forgive me. You'll get either the electric chair or life in the pen." "I'd rather have the chair. I can't live without dope."

The men turned the corner of the corridor and ran toward us. 

I took hold of Jim's hand. "Get me, Jim? Now make your confession." 

Half a dozen court officers surrounded us. Masters came puffing up a moment later as we started back to the courtroom. Jim and the officers walked ahead; Masters and I followed. "Well, Captain," I said breathlessly. "This is the pay-off. I'll be grateful to you for the rest of my life." "Forget it," he said gruffly. "I'll never forget it. When this is over I'll give the reporters my first interview. I'll see to it that you're on the sunny side of the fence, Captain."

When we entered the courtroom order had been restored. We walked into a silence that quivered. We passed through the swinging gate. I dropped weakly into my chair at the table. Jim, with an officer on either side of him, stood midway between the Defense and Prosecution tables in front of the bench. He just stood there. 

Judge Morgan said: "Proceed with direct examination of the witness, Counsellor." 

Nat faced Clancy on the stand. "Can you point to the man you call Chief?" 

Clancy said, "Yes." "Point to him!" 

Clancy directed a thick finger at Jim. "That's the man. Dr. Jim Lark."

Jim, his head erect, his shoulders back, addressed the Judge. "Your Honor, let me take the stand." 

Judge Morgan said to Nat, "Do you wish to call this State's witness for re-cross-examination?” "I do, Your Honor." "Does the district attorney offer any objection?" "No," said Wilson, shrunken in his chair.

Clancy was excused and Jim stepped up on the stand. He did not seat himself nor wait until Nat questioned him. He said at once: "Your Honor, I want to confess to the murder of Dr. Amberson. 

I am ready to place myself under arrest. I believe that's all that's necessary."

He stood, erect, waiting. 

Nat walked over to Wilson. They talked in low tones and I did not hear what they said. After a few minutes, Wilson rose and addressed the Court. "May it please Your Honor, in view of the startling development of this case, which no man could foresee, the State is satisfied as to the innocence of the defendant, Robert Jason. We move, therefore, that the Court order a directed verdict of acquittal." 

The spectators took that as a show of sportsmanship. Several hysterical persons began to applaud. The bailiff squelched them with his gavel. 

Judge Morgan turned to the jurors. "You have heard the motion of the district attorney for the State. 

The Court requests you to consider a verdict of acquittal for the defendant. So say you all, Gentlemen?"

The jurors, some of them appearing dazed, some smiling happily, nodded their heads vigorously. 

I was a free man. At last Mary and I were alone. She sat beside me in my car and 

I drove slowly up Lincoln Boulevard toward her house. The sun hung low in the west and the glow of a gentle fire bathed the city. 

Mary had slid down with her legs stretched out under the dash and her head resting on the cushioned top of the seat. Her face was pale and her eyes were closed. But she was not asleep. I spoke to her while she rested. "Just lie there and relax," I said. "Your nerves can stand just so much and no more. I know what a terrible shock it was for you when

you learned that it was Jim." 

Without opening her eyes, she said: "I can't seem to be as happy about it-about you and me—as I ought to be. Jim Lark! Our friend!" 

I said heavily: "Out there in the corridor, he told me that it was fate. He found my handkerchief there on the desk and he used it. I doubt that he knew it belonged to me, though he may have. But he used it. It was the instinct of self-preservation. After that, he couldn't turn back. 

He had to try to sacrifice me to save himself." 

She opened her eyes and looked at me. "I can't understand much of this. I know what happened, I heard it all in the courtroom, and yet I don't grasp anything of it. It's like a crazy dream. I don't know what led you to Jim. Was it Clancy?" "No. I knew it was Jim before I went to the jail to see Clancy. 

That's what I want to tell you, Mary. You didn't hear the 'whole story in court. There's something more that no one will ever know except Nat and me-and you. Now close your eyes, darling, and rest while I talk to you. "I didn't suspect Jim until about two and a half weeks ago. On that Saturday night when I went to Mitchel's office, I made my escape by way of the window. I went down the fire escape, as I told you; but when I got to Jim's office window there was a light in there and I saw Jim using a hypodermic. That was the first time 

I discovered that he used dope. "Even then I didn't connect him with the murder. The next morning I almost forgot that revelation when I read about the deaths of Mitchel and Claudia. That knocked me out and I didn't think of anything except that I was done for. Then you came over to see me and bucked me up, and before you left you gave me the letter that Howard Amberson, Junior, had written to his father in France. 

After you left I read that letter. I hadn't seen it since that night you showed it to me in the study. That was before I went to Gainsport. "But in the meanwhile, I had received a letter from Jim. I showed it to you, do you remember?"

She was sitting up now, and her eyes were upon me. "You mean the letter he wrote when he notified you of your suspension?" "Yes; the handwritten one. When I read young Amberson's letter, I was struck by a strange familiarity of handwriting. I compared that letter with the one that Jim had written."

"Bob! What are you saying?" "Wait! I compared the two letters. There were a number of words and phrases present in both letters. I'll point them out to you some time. I was convinced, Mary, that the handwriting in both letters was the same! "Later I took those letters to a handwriting expert and he said the handwriting was absolutely identical. But that was a day or two later. That morning when I realized that the handwriting was the same, the whole truth rushed over me. I remembered many things that I hadn't given any thought to before. Some were just trivial things that you wouldn't believe a man would retain in his mind. But there they were, all rushing out at once. I remembered how uncannily the gang seemed to anticipate everything I did. It seemed almost spooky how they always knew what I knew and what I was going to do next. They knew things that I mentioned to no one but you and Nat and Jim. "But that isn't all that rushed upon me that morning. I remembered talking to Jim in his office one day and getting up to stand behind his chair. In a kidding way, I tousled his hair. And I remembered something that I hadn't paid much attention to at the time. I had noticed that the roots of his hair were darker-very much darker-than the surface! I suddenly realized that that isn't natural. The hair of a blond is likely to be somewhat darker on the outside and lighter at the roots. It is the surface that fades and grows darker. But Jim's hair was darker at the roots. "In connection with that, I remembered that when I looked at him through the window from the fire escape, he had a towel wrapped around his head. When I went into the office after he was gone, I noticed that the washbowl was sort of stained, because he hadn't washed it out well, and there was hair in it, as if he had been washing his hair. Later I had another opportunity of ruffling his hair and I saw again that the roots were dark." 

I paused a moment. Mary did not speak. "Howard Amberson was never drowned. He made his escape by swimming down the river or letting the tide carry him down to a point where he could safely go to shore. He went to another city in another state, and when he got into trouble there, he moved on to another town. For more than a year he led a life of crime and managed to dodge the police. He was addicted to the use of morphine, and he broke into drug stores or held them up in order to get the stuff. "You can read between the lines of the letter he wrote to his father

how he became an easy victim of the dope habit. In that letter he mentioned his overwork at college, his nervous condition. Clancy initiated him into the use of morphine, and as we know, Clancy showed him a way to get it. "When his father returned from France and began to practice again in Gainsport, he showed up one day at his father's office. He had bleached his hair, but otherwise he was unchanged. The police of a dozen towns and cities were looking for him and the Federal agents were hot on his trail. He knew that he couldn't dodge them much longer. They didn't know it was young Amberson they were after, but they had a full description of him from different people he had robbed. "He didn't tell his father that he used dope, but he confessed everything else, and he begged him to operate on him and change his appearance. He promised that if his father would do that for him, if he would save him from the penitentiary, he would lead an honest life. He would take another name, continue his studies, make an honorable place for himself in the world. "Dr. Amberson did it. He couldn't refuse and let the boy walk out into the hands of the police. He wanted to give the boy a chance to remake his life and he knew that there wasn't any hope of mercy from the law. If there had been only that first crime with Clancy, something might have been done to get him out of it; but with the record of that desperate year there wasn't a chance. So he remodeled the boy's nose and set back the outstanding ears. Using his great skill he made Jim Lark out of Howard Amberson. "Mary, now we can understand his attitude toward plastic surgery. Now we can understand why he was so abnormally bitter against it. He must have vowed that never again would he perform another such operation. "He took the newly born Jim Lark under his wing and sent him to school. Partly through kindness, partly because it made things look better, he did the same for me and several other fellows. When Jim was a doctor he took him into his office. He had his son and his good name was secure. He didn't know that Jim used dope. He didn't know that Jim became the Chief of the dope ring. He only found that out when he heard about Mitchel from Cain, and Mitchel tried to hush him up by telling him that if he went further into the matter, he would involve his own son. You see, Jim, Mitchel and Clancy got together shortly after Jim's graduation, while Jim was interning at Mulvihill; and they started the organization of dope runners. Jim was the logical leader. He had the brains and the powerful personality."

Mary passed her hand over her eyes. "Bob, I don't know whether I'm awake or sleeping."

I had turned off the Boulevard into Lincoln Park, and as I talked I had been driving slowly along the winding roads. The sun had dropped out of sight; the dusk had deepened. The city lights had come on and shone wanly through the twilight. I headed the car back to the Boulevard.

When we came out of the park we heard the shrill voice of a newsboy, shouting, "Extry! Extry!" "Let's get one," said Mary. "No, not now," I said, putting on a little speed. "It's just about the verdict."

I didn't want her to see that headline now. I knew pretty well what it would be. I knew that Jim hadn't waited long after he was taken from the courtroom. That was something I have never told 

Mary. It is something that I have never told a living soul. Perhaps she guessed it later when she read the paper, but if she did, she never said a word to me. No one might understand. No one might understand that Jim had always been my friend until fate, in the form of that forgotten handkerchief, had come between us. No one might understand that for all that he had made me suffer, I did not want to see him suffer. He had once been my friend, he was the son of my benefactor, the step-brother of the girl I was to marry.

I helped Jim. I gave him a way out. Out there in the corridor of the courthouse, when I took hold of his hand, I left a capsule of potassium cyanide in his palm. "Murder, Murder, Murder..." That's what Chief of Police Alan Miller heard every time he picked up his telephone. "Murder, Murder, Murder ." repeated over and over again in a weird, chilling voice. Those words began an unexpected train of circumstances which will keep the reader guessing right up to the end.
