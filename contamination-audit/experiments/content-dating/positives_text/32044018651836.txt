MR. MODSTONE'S offices were in a street off the Strand, in a tall terrace that provincial admirers of Mr. Shaw sometimes mistook for the Adelphi. 

There were three rooms. The first was a waiting-room of sorts, divided into two parts by a wooden counter. The caller sat on one side, a small and thin and uncommonly shrewd-looking clerk on the other. The door from the passage was on the clerk's side, and to open it enough for an entry he had to be persuaded to move his obstructing stool. 

There was a certain purpose in this: it ensured an interval in which a waiting client could be passed on to the second office before the new-comer entered. It was a principle of 

Mr. Modstone's that visitors should not be brought face to face. 

The second office was occupied by another small and thin and shrewd-looking clerk some ten years older than his colleague. His name was Goldman, and he seemed to be concerned mostly with the immense filing and index cases that lined a whole side of the room. Almost always he had a drawer on the desk before him, and his manner of por-

ing with a heavy leer over the cards astonished and impressed the more innocent of the visitors. 

The third room was Mr. Modstone's private office; his den, as he called it, with tribute to our English pleasantries and no thought of a connotation obvious to some of his clients. It was a very pleasant room, with panels from an ancient mansion, a table from a Norman château, and two great chairs from a Scotch church. Mr. Modstone had an eye for "pieces." He could distinguish anything of value in a shop window, even from the top of a bus; and bedrock prices took on a strange fluidity when he chose to bargain. 

He had the reputation among dealers of being a “hard man"; indeed, they often told him so, for he took an ingenuous pleasure in his celebrated toughness. Who would deprive him of that? No one so human as an antiques dealer. 

Mr. Modstone was a pleasant fellow: a little, round, wrinkly-faced, clipped-headed, bull-necked man: kindly and honest except in the way of business and old furniture, and confining his business to its proper sphere of rich men's vices; never glancing at crime save in the Sunday papers and the better sort of literature. 

He was a Jew of some kind—his father had sprung from one of the ghettos of Eastern Europe-and much of his business came to him from the circles that, having long controlled the credits, have now taken a notion for the homes and titles of the English. His brains were good, his dis-

cretion admirable. At the time of which I write, he was more attracted by his hobby than his business, and he was contemplating a tranquil retirement in which he would enjoy the society of his chairs and his dealers and his old wife Rebecca, and grow used to the idea that he must inevitably part with them all.

At the hour when the story begins he was sitting in one of his great Presbyterian chairs, smoking a cigar. He did not greatly care for cigars, but it had been impressed upon him in youth (and Mr. Modstone was a conservative), that their odour was a desirable addition to the more tangible evidences of a flourishing concern. He had a paper before him to which he was giving some attention, though considerably distracted by a new catalogue from the firm of Antiente & Moderne, Ltd., which also faced him. 

There was a knock at the door, and a young man came in. 

He was a short and smartly dressed fellow, and although he was growing fat he gave the impression that he was very active. Certainly his expression was alert, and his large, dark eyes, well-fleshed jowl and heavy nose suggested considerable intelligence of the special sort necessary to people of ambition in our society. 

Mr. Modstone nodded and smiled. "Good morning, Edward. I was expecting you to be in." "How're you feeling, Uncle? And how is Aunt Bec?" "She is very well, thank you, Edward," said Mr. Modstone who had the sometimes disconcerting trick of taking the lit-

tle conventions of speech in their literal sense. “And I am well, too. This is a big account for expense you have put in, this one, Mr. Steyn." He flipped the paper in his hand. “The 

Barbary case; is it not becoming extended, eh?” 

Steyn frowned. "It is. I haven't been able to wrap it upfinally. Something's always coming unstuck. But I'm getting to the end, I think. I'm sorry the exes. have been soaring, but there you are; you can't run with a crowd like this without lunches, cocktails, dinners, theatres, cabarets, night clubs, and a girl friend. You can't hunt on your own, you know. 

I've been having Sadie Kahn around with me." 

Mr. Modstone nodded. “Yes. A nice girl, Sadie. She knows what you're after, Edward?"

The young man smiled. "Ra-ather. She's my second pair of eyes." "Is that so?" Mr. Modstone chuckled. "Her father's as sharp as a razor. Nice fellow, too. How did you come to let her in? Mind you, I know you like her, and nothing pleases me better than that. But you must be on a very good footing, eh?" “We are. "They certainly-well—” "Can you prove that?" "From the basement..." “Well, what more do you want? The case is finished so far as we are concerned. We can give Lord Barbary the facts, the evidence, the proofs. That is what he wants. Our hands are washed of it." "M-m-m-" Steyn sighed. "No. No, not yet. I'm on to something here, Uncle. Something big. I'm going ahead.” 

The old man turned to him sharply. "What is it? What is it? Is it any business of ours? We are paid to do one thing. 

You say that is done. What more can interest us?" 

Steyn waved his hand airily. “There's a devil of a lot to interest me." "Are you going to tell me?"

"No, not just yet," said Steyn coolly. "What! You will not tell me? But I must know, or you must drop the case." "Sorry, Uncle, I don't want to hide anything from you; but just give me another day or two, please. To-day's 

Wednesday. Let me have till Saturday. It's really not that I don't want to tell you; I'd like to clear it up first. Put a polish on it." "I don't like it," grumbled the old man. “I don't like not to know our own business. Is it our business, Steyn?" he added. "Oh, it's business all right, Uncle. Good business. You leave it to me. I won't let us in for anything." "You are very confident, young man. Very well, then. But you will tell me on Saturday. You come here at eleven o'clock on Saturday and then-tell me or drop the case." "Oh, I'll tell you. Now I must be getting along. I lunch at the Ritz at 1.15. I shall be sorry to drop this case, I can tell you." "Take a bus there," said Mr. Modstone. 

The old man watched the door close; and then he rose and went to the window and watched Steyn go out and walk briskly toward the Strand. "He's my own blood," thought Mr. Modstone. "The nearest I've got now that Mirah is gone. I can trust him. But I wonder what he's at. I wonder what he's at."

BOSTALL WOOD is down in the south-eastern corner of London, at the edge of the stone wilderness, a tug's whistle from the river. It is one of those pleasant parks that save the grim face of the city, and it is a favourite resort of the cooing young from Woolwich and Greenwich and Deptford. You may listen to a band there on Sunday evenings in the summer; or you may slip away into the dusk with, if you are lucky, your favourite girl on your arm; and no one will heed you unless you brush one of the thousand other couples in the wood.

On Sunday afternoon Constable Adrian Brown was pacing the Bostall paths. Constable Brown was young and he had a favourite girl; he remembered his duty, but he envied the pleasure around him. He saw the manly flannels and the dainty skirts go by, and he thought of his own white flannels in the press at home and of Bella's new green dress. 

A gentle melancholy stole over him and he stopped to stare idly at a bird singing on a bough. 

There was a shout from the trees behind him. He jumped and spun about on his heels. A boy and a girl were running II

from the wood. They cried out to him. He began to run too, until he remembered his training and fell into a proper walk. 

The boy and the girl threw themselves at him, and each caught him by an arm. “Oh, oh, oh!” cried the girl. "It's—it's—it's—" the boy stammered. "Now then. Nower then," said Constable Brown, severely. "What's all this?" "There's there's somebody-a body-somebody dead in there!"

MR. STEPHEN CRAWLEY had rooms in Westminster: very elegant and very expensive rooms, with a view towards St. 

James's Park and a glimpse of the sober grandeurs of 

Whitehall; rooms admirably expressive of Mr. Crawley's personality, for he was himself an elegant and expensive young man, and if he mostly regarded the world as a park of pleasure and privilege, he was not unmindful of the prizes that await a measured industry and an accommodating temper. 

He was short and plump and sleek. His hands were soft and moist, and his chin had already acquired that comfortable double fold that looks so well on the Front Benches and at civic banquets, and his fingers customarily rested at his lapel as if they were posed for the inevitable reproduction in stone or bronze. His voice was high-pitched and shrill, an ever-ready vehicle for the distribution of Mr. 

Crawley's pearls.

He came of a wealthy family of iron-masters in the north. 

For three generations his people had grown richer and richer, and their factories larger and larger, and the slums 217

about them more and more extensive. Mr. Crawley had realized, when he was quite a boy and at the very threshold of his career, that those slums were a sad and bad facet of the otherwise splendid jewel of Crawley greatness. Not, of course, that one could put the blame for that on the Crawleys: declare, as some rabid fools did, that the slums were a result of an insensate lust for money and power in the Crawley blood. No sensible man looked at things like that: they were just a product of the System, the vast, inhuman System of Industry, necessary tributes to Progress. They were evils, but they were evils that accompanied and emphasized, by very contrast, the good, the many good results of Scientific Advance: such, for instance, as the aggrandizement of the Crawleys, the giving of the Crawleys to the larger world, the giving of the larger world to the Crawleys and their kind.

These, of course, were benefits of a large and undeniable kind: nevertheless, the latest of the Crawleys had to admit that they hardly extended to the mass of his employees, save in the most diffused and unsatisfying forms. He remembered a visit, taken during his first year at Cambridge, to the seat and root of the family fortunes. He had never seen the place before; the Crawleys had resided in London and the southern shires for a long lifetime, now. But he felt that he ought to see it. His conscience, which was always a sensitive one, urged him to go; it insisted that if the conditions in the 

Crawley neighbourhood were really as bad as they had 218

been painted (there had lately been a Commission of some kind which had been vulgarly brutal in its descriptions-to compensate perhaps for the mildness of its recommendations), he ought, at the least, to know the worst. 

He had spent two whole days in the town. He had inspected the factories and walked in the streets. He had shaken hands with a specially washed group of senior employees. He had even selected a tie-just for fun, of course, and not to wear-from the Crawley Drapery Stores. The price of it was docked from the clerk's wages, but he felt it an honour, thinking often of Mr. Stephen walking down 

Regent Street in a tie he had sold him; and the books balanced, so nobody minded.

He made a charming speech at a complimentary dinner tendered by the leading officers and townspeople. He looked in, for a cheery word or two, at several of the Crawley owned pubs; and he even visited a number of tenement houses and actually spoke to some of the people who lived in them.

The general impression left on his mind was that the Commission had been decidedly unfair to the property owners and employers. The tenements were inhabitable, for people inhabited them; the life was livable, for people lived it. But, nevertheless, he realized that the lot of the poor was not in every way a happy one.
