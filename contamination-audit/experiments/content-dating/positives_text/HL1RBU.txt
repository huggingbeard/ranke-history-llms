INTRODUCTION 

This Commentary presents an annual rating of state traffic laws to show how they compare with the rules of the road part of the Uniform Vehicle Code as of January 1, 1977. Prior ratings were based on state traffic laws as they existed in 1968, 1971, 1972, 1973, 1974 and 1975.1/ This rating covers 1976 state traffic laws but does not include changes adopted by state legislatures in 1977.

Traffic Laws Annotated2/ shows how state traffic laws compare with provisions in the "Rules of the Road" Chapter of the 

Uniform Vehicle Code³/ in over 150 "Statutory Annotations."

Reading these Annotations would provide a general picture of the traffic laws in any one state. Any law that differs from a section in the Uniform Vehicle Code could also be detected by reading all the Annotations. This Commentary attempts to provide a more precise picture of the status of state traffic laws as of January 1, 1977 that will facilitate rapid identification of particular areas of a state's laws that may not be in substantial conformity with the Code, indicate areas of the statutory law where uniformity may be markedly poor on a nationwide basis, and identify states whose traffic laws may need substantial attention on a priority basis as part of contemporary efforts to improve highway safety. 

This document, when used with Traffic Laws Annotated, is particularly useful in determining which laws in any one state differ from provisions in the Uniform Vehicle Code (and from laws in other states).

Uniform rules of the highest quality are important for highway safety and substantial improvement in such uniformity, and the termination of laws that are archaic or that cannot be reasonably expected to improve the safe and efficient use of our highways, are imperative and should occur as soon as possible.4/

CAVEAT VIATOR/ 

The above rating system and its application is not perfect and could be misleading for many reasons.

Reducing the law to numbers is a process of questionable value.

The rating system also fails to make distinctions based on relative importance among rules of the road. That is, a law duplicating the Code's rule against riding in house trailers and a law duplicating the Code provision on the duty of a driver making a left turn to yield to oncoming vehicles each receive equal treatment -- plus 5 points. Thus, if a state duplicates - 2 

---

one rule but not the other, its total score would be 5. Though all rules of the road are deemed necessary for highway safety by the National Committee, some contend that certain rules contribute more than others. Certainly, some rules do cover situations encountered more frequently by many people and, for that reason alone, may make a more significant contribution to highway safety particularly when they are reasonably uniform. 

The rating is unfair to some states because it does not reflect changes made by state legislatures during 1977. It is also unfair because it assesses state laws in comparison with the UVC as it was revised in July of 1975 and there has been little opportunity for state legislatures to adopt these revisions in 1975 or 1976 because a supplement containing the changes was not available until the first week of December, 1975.

NUMBER OF SUBSTANTIAL DIFFERENCES 

The 13 tables in Appendix B permit rapid identification of areas where state traffic laws differ from a rule in the Uniform Vehicle Code. These differences are shown by using minus numbers. 

One of the reasons why this rating was originally published (despite its many imperfections) was to facilitate the location, consideration of and, hopefully, the elimination of such differences. 

All one has to do is locate the minus numbers in the 13 tables in 

Appendix B and consult the Annotation in Traffic Laws Annotated indicated at the top of the column. Reading the Annotation in 

TLA will indicate how the laws with a minus score differ from a provision in the Uniform Vehicle Code. If one follows this procedure for a particular state, it does not take long to determine how the traffic laws in that state differ substantially from the 

Uniform Vehicle Code.

SUMMARY OF 1976 CHANGES IN STATE TRAFFIC LAWS 

The traffic laws of Colorado, Delaware and Pennsylvania were extensively revised in 1974. There are no substantial differences between the Uniform Vehicle Code and the traffic laws of five states: Georgia, Kansas, North Dakota, Pennsylvania and washington. Pennsylvania, which was ranked 50th and which had eight substantial differences, now stands fifth and has no substantial differences. The improvement in Pennsylvania's traffic laws is the most significant and dramatic one in this decade. 

The number of substantial differences between the UVC and state laws increased in three jurisdictions: Arizona, South 

Carolina and Vermont. By comparison, the number of substantial differences decreased in 12 states and remained the same in 36 states.

OBSERVATIONS 

One of the principal reasons for publication of the previous ratings was to stimulate consideration and elimination of major differences between state laws and the Code. Though many states made significant advances toward uniformity during 1969-1976, 213 substantial differences still remain. Particularly in view of the fact that almost 50% of these differences involve traffic control devices and right of way, this number is too high. All substantial differences should be eliminated on a priority basis by each state as soon as possible unless the difference can be clearly justified as improving safe and efficient movement of traffic.

Another disappointment is the failure of 14 states with total scores of less than 426 to make more significant progress toward uniformity.7/

Not only are the total scores in these 14 states low, but these states account for 44% of all substantial differences. Seven encouraging developments in this regard were significant changes in the laws of Maryland, Nebraska, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, South Dakota and Vermont resulting in their moving up from the last 14 states in the rankings during 1969-1976. 

Most states with low scores are located in the eastern half of the United States. For instance, Massachusetts has 12 substantial variances and, as noted in the 1970 Commentary, is the only state whose traffic laws have never been closely patterned after any edition of the Code published between 1926 and 1975. 

8 -

Legislators in stat with scores of less than 426 should be informed that their laws appear to be nonuniform, obsolete and in need of modernization based upon the most recent edition of the Uniform Vehicle Code. Legislators in these states probably do not know about the condition of their traffic laws and cannot be expected to take action without knowledge of the problem.
