137 90 The monetary conference of 44 nations which met in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in the summer of 1944, recommended the establishment of two international organizations to increase international trade, rebuild the destroyed industries of Europe and Asia, and develop new industries in many parts of the world. 

The Bretton Woods Plans are links in a chain we have to forge to make a better world.

They are necessary if we want sixty million jobs in the United States after the war.

Labor's Stake in Bretton Woods 

While we are fighting this war we are also trying to build the peace.

The kind of peace we want is one that will last. 

How can we get it?

In the first place, we must learn from our mistakes. It was our mistakes which made this war, and if we repeat them, there will be another war for our children to fight. 

If we look back, we can remember how this war came about. 

There were rough and tough guys with a swastika beating up Jews in Germany.

There were little Jap soldiers burning down the houses of poor Chinese workers.

But do you remember some of the pictures in the magazines back in the thirties? There was one of a Chinese coolie carrying the guns of a Japanese soldier. 

What made the coolie do it? Hunger.

There was another picture of American stevedores loading scrap iron for Japan on San Francisco docks. 

What made labor help the killers of labor? The threat of being out of a job. Hunger again. 

The War in the Making 

But it wasn't in the thirties that this war was in the making. 

It was earlier, much earlier.

When the firemen on Argentine railroads used corn on the cob for fuel instead of coal, why did they do it?

Because it was cheaper to burn food instead of coal. Argentina had more corn to sell than Argentina could eat. There were people enough in Europe who needed the Argentine corn to eat. But they couldn't buy it, not enough of it, in any case. 590167

They couldn't buy coffee, either, so coffee was dumped into the sea in Brazil.

They couldn't buy enough cloth, so our government paid farmers in the South for not growing cotton, instead of paying them for growing it.

All this was the one great chance for the wreckers, the Fascists, the Nazis, the Jap officer-gangs, to turn decent but desperate people into trained beasts. 

We know the rest of the story. We're in it now. 

Our boys are in it up to their necks, in the Pacific, in 

Europe, in China.

We at home are in it because of our anxiety for them, as we stand at machines, working overtime to produce for wara war that may go on for years.

Peace-And How to Get It 

We know what kind of a peace we want after this war. 

We want jobs for all at living wages-sixty million of them in the United States. We want better housing, cheaper cars. 

But, above all, we want peace. We want no more rabble rousers at home trying to pit white against Negro, Gentile against Jew, veterans against labor; no more goose-stepping armies abroad dragging us into war. 

But what about the job of really getting such a peace? It's not just one job; it's a number of jobs. Beautiful words won't build a better world. Building a better world is like building a house. You have to know what it's to be used for, what it's to look like. You also have to know how to make it that way. 

There are two different kinds of jobs to be done. 

One is to stop gangster governments in the future the moment they goose-step over some frontier. Work on this job was started at Dumbarton Oaks and continued in San Francisco. 

But even more important is to prevent gangster parties from gaining control of a country's government. 

The best insurance against that is full employment in peacetime. And full employment will be greatly helped by freeing and sustaining international trade.

The proposals made at Bretton Woods for helping all countries get on their feet and exchange their goods may seem complicated. But what they mean to the American worker, if they succeed, is a valuable contribution to his goal of steady employment and income. 

In addition, they can mean an important contribution to peace that the workers will not be taken out of the production line and put into the battle line. Countries with steady work and good wages aren't likely to resort to war. 

Unemployment Leads to War

Countries must be prosperous if their people are to eat more Argentine corn, drink more coffee from Brazil, wear more cloth from American and English mills, drive more automobiles made in Detroit or England.

In other words, we must find a way for the people of one country to buy more goods, and buy them from all over the world, because that will spread employment over all countries. 

For we know by now that unemployment anywhere threatens employment everywhere, and that sooner or later mass unemployment may lead to war. 

International Monetary Co-operation

Forty-four governments sent delegations to the Bretton Woods conference. The delegations were composed of experts. 

They discussed plans which American, British and Canadian government experts had drawn up before the conference began. 

At the end of the meeting the delegates agreed unanimously to recommend the plan they had worked out during the conference. 

There were two problems to be solved at the conference. 

One was how international payments can be made more easily. 

The other was how to make poor countries richer without making rich countries poorer.

To take care of these two problems, the delegates proposed two international organizations, to be run by a staff from the forty-four countries. The funds to run them would be paid by these countries—that is, by their governments. The two organizations would be the Bank for Reconstruction and Development and the International Monetary Fund.

Suppose that everything we need here in the United States could be grown and processed here everything from the rubber tree we can't grow to the tires we can make better than anyone else. In that case we could, if we wanted, build a wall around our country, and tell the rest of the world to look after its own troubles.

But we can't do that. We have to buy some things from abroad, though not as much as some other countries. Countries like England, Germany, Belgium and Italy are still more dependent on trade with other countries. English mills, for instance, must have American cotton. And the cotton must be paid for in dollars.

Between the two wars, trade was often disrupted because it was hard to say what the money of one country was worth in terms of the money of other countries. Inflation upset the international money exchanges. Besides, inflation caused ruin in the countries which carried it far enough. On the other hand, countries which continued to measure their money in gold were obliged to force their prices down, and this caused distress. 

Much of the misery and bitterness which played into the hands of fascist rabble-rousers, and led to the present war, was rooted in international money and trade disturbances. 

The Fund would permit countries to pay each other without unforeseeable ups and downs in the value of their monies. 

It is provided that each nation which joins the Bretton Woods plan will pay a certain sum of money to the Fund. Most of it will be paid in the money of the country, with a small portion in gold. The Fund, therefore, has the monies of all its members in its vaults and a gold reserve in addition. 

If we assume that all the countries which are members trade with each other in such a way that each country buys as much as it sells, debts and credits would cancel each other out. But the exchanges have to go on for quite a while before we can expect this result.

After the war the liberated countries will have to buy more from abroad than they will be able to export. Therefore the seller nations, America, Canada, the Argentine, will sell more than they buy, either because they don't need the goods the liberated countries can produce or because the shops to make

such goods must first be rebuilt. Therefore, if troubles are to be avoided, there will have to be a referee who can intervene on certain occasions.

The Fund a Referee 

According to the Bretton Woods agreements, the International Monetary Fund is to be the referee. 

There will be many occasions on which the referee will have to blow his whistle. Two situations are especially important. In the first, one country's currency becomes scarce in the Fund's vaults because that country sells more than it buys; in the second, the country's currency becomes abundant in the Fund because it buys more than it sells. 

In the first case the Fund may make efforts to bring more of the country's currency into the fund. Or the Fund may ration sales of the seller country's currency. 

But what about the case of the member which for the time being cannot sell enough abroad to balance its buying from abroad? This so-called "deficit" country will want to make its goods more attractive to buyers in other countries. One way it could do so would be by lowering the value of its currency in terms of the currencies of other countries. 

Yet devaluation, which makes the products of the devaluing country cheap in foreign markets, does not always help. And even if it helps it sometimes does harm to other countries, because it restricts the competitive power of their producers. Or if the different countries started a devaluation race, it might cause much international confusion. 

The "deficit" country may also make its goods cheaper to foreign buyers by lowering wages and prices at home. But this might cause business failures and unemployment. This solution is therefore even worse than devaluation. 

It may use still another method to restore the balance between its imports and its exports. It may put restrictions on imports, either by raising its tariffs, or by saying it will buy only so much abroad, or in other ways equally harmful to world trade. 

The Bretton Woods agreement on the International Monetary Fund adopted principles of international monetary cooperation which would help solve these difficulties.

Help to One, No Harm to Others 

The International Monetary Fund will help countries which have gotten into trouble because they have no foreign money. 

It will help them to pay for their excess imports or settle other foreign debts.

It will help them in a way which will not harm the other members of the international community. 

Whenever the trouble is only the consequence of a temporary difficulty, the Fund will enable the "deficit" country to avoid an unnecessary devaluation of its currency. The support given by the Fund to the "deficit" country will permit it to make economic adjustments which will restore its position on the world markets and bring it once again the foreign money it needs. Such adjustment may take many years. 

But if the disease lies deeper, the "deficit" country will be allowed to devalue its currency. 

This is necessary if the country isn't to be forced to resort to the very dangerous measure of reducing wages and prices. 

A stable ratio between the values of different currencies is a worthy aim. But it should never be attained at the expense of full employment and social security. For the "deficit" country and also for the world economy as a whole, it is better to devalue a currency than to drive the "deficit" country into unemployment.

But countries should not be allowed to devalue their currency for the purpose of gaining competitive advantages over other countries. Plans for changing the value of the currency in terms of other countries' currencies should therefore be subjected to international supervision. In this way, unneighborly and unnecessary devaluations can be avoided.

Working on these lines, the International Monetary Fund will render important services to all countries. Economic warfare, at least in the monetary field, will be avoided. Member countries will be able to pursue effective policies for full employment and social security without fear of international bankruptcy. They will not be driven into deflation and unemployment because of temporary lack of foreign means of payment. 10

They will be given a breathing spell to adjust their economies gradually to changed world conditions. And they will be spared much unemployment.

The Job of the Bank The Argentinians used corn for fuel because they couldn't sell it profitably at home or abroad. We in the United States did the same sort of thing with cotton. Others did it with rubber, coffee and all kinds of goods. 

But there was never enough of anything really to satisfy the needs of the poor. They didn't have enough money to buy what they needed.

If the Brazilians and the Argentinians had had more industries and different branches of farming, instead of a few specialized ones working for the foreign buyer rather than for home consumption, coffee might never have been so abundant that it had to be dumped into the sea. Other countries would have found bigger and better markets in Argentina and Brazil, for more production in these countries would have required more machinery, tools and clothing from abroad. 

After the war the job will be to get more industries, more and better farming, in countries which are either not developed or have in the past produced just one or two lines of goods, betting on the chance of selling them abroad and in exchange getting all the other products they needed. 

But there will also be another job ahead, and that will be to rebuild the countries the war has devastated. 

Enough Money to Build and Rebuild here is money enough in the world to build and rebuild. 

The money is in those countries which can afford to increase their exports without a simultaneous and equally high increase in their imports. The United States, Sweden, Switzerland and some other countries are in this situation. 

It would be a good thing to get these other countries to invest abroad. In the past this was done by private banks, which made loans to foreign governments or private enterprises abroad. It was also done to some extent by industry. 11

The investments which are most needed abroad are for electrification, road building, railroads, housing, and, in Europe, rebuilding destroyed industries. Some of these investments will be necessary to increase the standards of living of many countries and make them good customers of the developed countries —and, what is more, satisfied with the conditions of peace. 

On the other hand, investments abroad which would prove harmful in the long run should be avoided. An example of such investments might be creating industries which would perpetuate substandard wages in countries of low living standards. 

Reasonable investments will promise sufficient profit. But private investors aren't likely to assume very heavy risks after the war, except at intolerably high interest rates. If, however, an international public institution guarantees the loans, they will be prepared in most cases to invest abroad at interest rates which the borrowers are able to pay.

The Bretton Woods Plan for Foreign Investment 

The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development proposed at Bretton Woods is an attempt to direct international investments into the right channels.

All the countries participating in the Bank will subscribe to its capital-a total of ten billion dollars. The greater a country's share, the more voting power and influence it will have in the conduct of the Bank.

The Bank—and this is its most more important functionmay guarantee loans made by private capitalists. 

If, however, private capital is unavailable for carrying out important development or reconstruction plans, the Bank may also make direct loans. Both kinds of loans may be granted to member governments. They may also be granted to cities, provinces, states and private corporations, on condition that the governments of the countries concerned guarantee such loans. 

These powers of the Bank are to be used under two sets of conditions.

In the first place, a loan made, or guaranteed, has to be for repair of the destruction caused by the war, or for developments, such as electrification, railroads, flood control, the crea- 12

tion or extension of extracting or manufacturing industries, improving agriculture, etc.

In the second place, a project can be aided only if it promises to yield interest rates and repayment of principal. Repayment may be spread over a very long period of years, but unprofitable ventures are not the Bank's business. 

TVAs All Over the World 

What the Bank would do on a world-wide scale the United States government has done at home by financing projects like the Tennessee Valley Authority, or public housing programs to clear slums and create work.

The results of the TVA speak for themselves. The TVA has been studied by economists and government experts from all over the world. It has been praised by both liberals and conservatives abroad. We will see it copied after the war in many foreign countries.

What is the secret of the TVA's success? The government had the money to build power plants and flood control works in a region which didn't have enough electricity and was always in danger of floods. The TVA made power cheap, farms safe, fields more fertile. It also brought more jobs for people of the region. 

What were the consequences? A large number of brand new industries and shops sprang up. There was more money to buy goods and services, and more hope in the future, which made people more inclined to spend their money or risk investing it in new businesses.

The International Bank will not only help to rebuild the destroyed industries of Europe but will also help to establish 

TVAS all over the world. 

Other Links in the Chain

The Bank and the Fund are links in a chain we must forge if we want a better world and lasting peace. 

We shall need other links just as badly. The ILO (International Labor Organization) is one. It's the machinery with 13

which wages and hours-depressed in many parts of the worldcan be lifted to standard levels. 

Besides the ILO, many other links will have to be forged.

Take just one. Foreign trade may have suffered between the two wars because of chaotic changes in the value of money. 

But it has suffered even more because each nation large and small built high tariff walls around itself. These tariffs were defended in the beginning on the grounds that they would help build up the industries each country badly needed. But protective tariffs remained long after any possible need for protection had gone.

We should lower tariffs in America and all the other countries as well. Bretton Woods wasn't a conference on tariffs and how to reduce them. But every expert there knew that this will be necessary sooner or later. 

Can labor support tariff reduction?

The answer is simple. It can and should if we in America complete our unfinished social security system; if our government and private industry share the responsibility for maintaining full employment at living wages; and if we help build up other countries so they can buy from us. 

We shall need a good deal of legislation and much good will to get that far. The International Bank can't do more than a small part of the foreign investing that will be necessary. We in this country must extend the powers of the Export-Import Bank, and probably create other public agencies for the purpose. 

Foreign investment will increase foreign trade, but, even more important, it can reduce poverty in many parts of the world by making devastated and backward countries more productive. And, by reducing their poverty, it can make them able and willing to be good neighbors.

The Role of the United States 

The participation of the United States in the two agencies of international monetary co-operation proposed at Bretton Woods will serve the economic interests of this country in at least two ways. 14

The International Monetary Fund and the International 

Bank for Reconstruction and Development will give valuable support to sound employment policies in the United States, first, by providing jobs in the export industries, and so in other industries as well, and, second, by sparing this country the troubles which can't fail to arise whenever other countries' foreign trade is disturbed.

But our country's participation in the two agencies is of still greater significance for world peace. It is vital for political security.

If the United States should refuse to take part, the reconstruction and development of the poor countries would be slowed down. Worse, they might be driven into aggressive economic policies which would bring about economic warfare. It is hardly necessary to call attention to the bad effects of such warfare on world peace.

The Bretton Woods plans are recommendations only. Congress and the legislatures of the other forty-four countries which participated in the conference will have to approve the plans before the Bank and the Fund begin to work. 

But what Congress does may decide the question. The 

United States today is the richest country in the world. It will have most to say in the management of the Fund and the Bank. 

Legislatures abroad are likely to do what Congress does. 

The Bretton Woods recommendations will soon be discussed on the floor of the House and Senate. It will be an uphill fight for the Administration and its supporters. 

Should labor help congressmen who fight for Bretton Woods? 

The answer is yes.

It is clear that other links in the chain of progress are just as necessary. But labor must help forge each link when it is on the anvil. 15

CLASSES OF MEMBERSHIP 

There are seven classes of membership.

STUDENT MEMBERSHIP is open to registered students of recognized schools or colleges, who must present their registration cards or other proof of their status, such as letters from deans, registrars, or principals, with their applications. It includes subscriptions to the 

A.L.C. News Letter (fortnightly) and International Postwar Problems (quarterly), reductions on the prices of the Conference's other publications, and reduced admission fees to all the public meetings of the Conference. 

REGULAR MEMBERSHIP includes the same subscriptions and privileges as student membership and, in addition, the Conference's information service, which consists of copies of the texts of all important international documents, such as the Dumbarton Oaks Proposals and the Bretton Woods Agreements, as they become available. 

ASSOCIATE MEMBERSHIP includes all the subscriptions and privileges of regular membership and, in addition, subscriptions to the Occasional Papers and the Studies in Postwar Reconstruction.

CONTRIBUTING, SUPPORTING, SUSTAINING AND DONOR MEMBERSHIPS include all the subscriptions and privileges of associate membership and, in addition, copies of all books published by the Conference.
