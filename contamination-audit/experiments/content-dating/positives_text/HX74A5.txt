INTRODUCTION 

'HE Author of this little volume, wherein he relates episodes, personal experiences and makes references to historical facts, seeks the applause of neither the reader nor the literary world. He is far more concerned about the effect on his reputation for veracity. Conscious of his own shortcomings as a raconteur, he accepts cheerfully in advance the raillery that may come his way. 

For twenty years his friends have urged him to write something of his experiences in the eastern hemisphere and having been persuaded to do so, he feels the futility of relating tales that may have already been told by other narrators, who, though they may have drawn largely on imagination, have, through their qualifications for writing, produced articles of merit. Under the circumstances he approaches the task expecting both criticism and skepticism and only asks that his readers take comfort in the old proverb: "Blessed is he that expecteth nothing for he shall not be disappointed." If, in the ensuing tale the works of others are drawn on without too much credit having been given, bear in mind that Kipling explained such matters with: "When 'Omer smote 'is blooming lyre, 'E'd 'eard men sing by land and sea;

An' what 'e thought 'e might require, 'E went and took-the same as we."

Family Origin and Childhood 

|T WAS in a little city of southern Ohio that the narrator of these tales first saw the light of day. 

His ancestors were Quakers and under the leadership of Thomas L-, left England too late to be passengers on the good ship, Mayflower. (We have often thought that if that vessel's passenger list on her first voyage was as great as present-day claims would indicate, she would probably have sunk at her dock before starting on her memorable voyage.) 

Landing at what is now Newport, Rhode Island, these Quaker forebears pioneered: worshiped in some degree of peace, became prominent in the Revolutionary War and in the Union of Colonies which followed that war. Later on, some moved to newer frontiers to the west and south and, ultimately, great grandfather trekked by oxcart with his little family to southern Ohio, near the river by that name, where he procured a tract of land which is still in the family in this year of 1941. It is still the home of several members of the "clan" representing the seventh and eighth generations in this country. (3)

From the old home and farm, father, with his little family, moved farther west in Ohio, and purchased sufficient land to provide a home with fruit trees and a garden which more than met the family's wants. Recollection of that garden is that it seemed more like a farm, in as much as it prevented me from playing in many a baseball game and any time the best girl took it into her head to walk our way, she invariably caught me picking potato bugs from the plants. That garden was the pet abomination winter or summer, particularly when, as the last of seven, I was the only one left to do the chores. 

Would that some of the younger generation of today could have gone to school with us in those days. You got your "Three Rs" and little else. The little old "Red

School House" was three stories high and you walked up "sans" elevator or escalator. No bus hauled you to school and mother did not come for you in the family limousine. At recess you played in a yard largely paved with brick and a railing separated you from your girl friends.

Your record for attendance and deportment was kept on a slate hung on the front blackboard and memories recall the tough boy of the class who, for the first time in his school life, got his name on that slate, when along came a fire alarm from the school-bell which, by-the-by, turned out to be a practice drill. The tough boy rushed up shouting: "Save the roll of honor!" Throwing the slate down three floors below through the open window, he then climbed out to a convenient lightning rod and shinnied down to safety and a much broken slate. (4)

Father was not only a farmer of rare ability, but an engineer and a mechanic who never, to our recollection, was balked by any mechanical problem but once. A lady who had heard his praises sung in that respect once too often, undertook to break his record. She brought him a needle from which the eye had been broken, and ironically asked him to put a new eye in it. He remarked that it might take a little time and suggested that she come in another two hours and pick it up; which she did, and, much to her amazement, found the job well done. She did not know that he got a good needle from our mother's stock to replace it.

On our property was an old blacksmith shop where he often taught us tricks of many trades. Among his accomplishments was his knowledge of gunsmithing and locks. I recall with pride the day father let me start helping him make a breech-loading rifle which I was in later years to inherit. Father started with a well-seasoned piece of walnut for the stock. He then selected a piece of round steel rod for the barrel which he planed octagon with an ordinary carpenter's wood plane, and bored it on a pedal-operated lathe and rifled it with a hand-made equipment of his own design, which made the spiral grooves perfectly. His breech block action was his own, and different from any then in use. He made the lock, trigger guard and hammer, together with all other necessary parts with his own hands. It turned out to be a beautiful sporting model and a wonderfully accurate weapon. Helping to make that gun was an experience never to be forgotten. (5)

Early Water Works Experience 

At this time, father was superintendent of the City

Water Department and I haunted the plant on every possible occasion. My early experiences were in the ditch, boiler-room, on the pumps and in the office, so it was but natural to later accept a position with a large 

Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, company, which owned and then operated over fifty water, gas, and electric power companies, scattered over many States. At the age of twenty-two, I became the superintendent of the first plant at Wellsville, New York. Not long after taking over there, the company was offered a franchise by that little town for its first electric lighting. Persuading the Pittsburgh officials to accept the offer, opportunity was given for first real experience outside of the water works field and confession must be made to frosting the windows of the plant the first night it started operating. No audience was wanted that night; particularly as it turned out that there were problems which proved both numerous and harrowing. There were no automatic devices in those days and you were alone with God.

Recollections of the gas engine that drove the generators linger in memory. It was devoid of any self-starting device and it was by main strength, awkwardness and hand-turning of the flywheel that you might hope to get it going. That thing would back-fire and throw me into the brick wall several times nightly and to this day there are bodily scars from combats with that infernal engine. 

Hearing of a mill outside of the town where there was a (6)

similar, but larger, engine operating, I called on the owner and asked him how he liked his engine. 

He replied, "Fine." "Don't you have any trouble starting it?" "Hell, no," he said. "I never stop it."

Those were the good old days when natural gas was plentiful and you used all you wanted on a flat rate without being metered.

Another experience with the operation of our water works in that same town, was in connection with the three kinds of pipe used in the distribution system, cast iron, steel and wood. A new pipe man was hired one day and was sent out to fix his first leak. In a short while he came in, slammed his bucket of tools on the floor and asked for "his time." Being somewhat annoyed, he was reminded that he claimed to be an experienced pipe man, and was asked why he could not fix a simple leak. 

His reply was, "I am a pipe man but I am no damned cooper!" He had tackled his first wood pipe and was made to like it.

The last job with this Pittsburg company was at Huntington, West Virginia, constructing a modern filter plant and the manufacturer of the equipment seemed so well satisfied with the assembly thereof, that they offered a better job. I became superintendent of construction for that particular company, a job that was ultimately to lead far afield. A little more than a year later, the company and myself joined a large combine of the three then existing manufacturers of filter equipment. Some of the (7)

largest contracts in the country were completed under our charge, including the famous Little Falls, New Jersey plant, then the largest in existence. 

It was in connection with such work and celebrated experimental filtration work and law-suits, that it was made possible to know intimately some of the famous engineers of the country, and frindships thus made lasted down through the years. 

These included Clemens 

Herschel, John M. Diven, William R. Hill, George W. 

Fuller, George A. Johnson, Allan Hazen, Edmund B. 

Weston, and many others long since gone to their reward. Milwaukee

Tiring of this constant travel, we let a very large engine and pump manufacturing company of Milwaukee tempt us to join their shop staff as assistant to the shop manager. While wonderful training, it was an unhappy experience, as the factory was months behind on orders and it soon became apparent that success was largely dependent on how good a liar we could be as to delivery promises. The camel's back finally broke when a customer called us up from Cincinnati and asked why he had not received his engine. When told it was not finished, he wanted to know how far along it was. Consulting with the Chief, he was reminded that we both knew work had not even been started on that particular engine and that lying about such matters was becoming unbearable. The Chief was long on profanity and short on ethics and after being told by him that we were to (8)

advise the customer his engine was seventy-five percent completed or else, we so phoned that so phoned that message to Cincinnati.

Two days later the customer appeared at our desk and asked to see his machine. Consulting again with the 

Chief, we were instructed to take him down in the factory and show him engine number twenty-seven on the erecting floor. The customer looked it over and then stepped up to the engine and picking up a pair of outside calipers, turned and said, "Mister, if that is my engine you have made a hell of a mistake for that shaft is two inches under the size specified."

Some of my previous experiences may have been humiliating, but that one probably changed the whole course of my life. 

The reply was: "Mister, your engine has not even been started and as I have lied for this firm for the last time, come upstairs and see me get fired." 

I took him to the Chief and watched the "fur fly," after which I went to my desk to clean it out, knowing that my chances of remaining on were akin to that of a certain proverbial snowball. On the desk was a second letter from an old New York employer of filter building days, again asking if they could interest me in going abroad with a new company then being formed. Manna from Heaven!

Farewell Dinner Party and Departure for England 

In due course, I presented myself at the New York office and was greeted with the query, "How are you, Boy, (9)

have you got thawed out yet after a Milwaukee winter"? "No," I replied, "and it will take a lot of hot weather to do the trick." 

The next query: "How would you like to go to India"? "Fine, when do I start"?

In the laugh that followed, we discovered that there was a third party present who remarked in unmistakable 

English accent, "My word, but you Americans work fast." 

The stranger turned out to be a captain in the royal engineers of the British army and the personal representative of the Maharajah of Mysore in South India. He was authorized to purchase complete equipment for a modern water works and filtration plant, the entire project estimated to cost one million dollars. And that is how the author commenced a jaunt that was to take him. around the world; north and south of the equator and into many lands. That meeting was destined to cement a friendship with Captain De Lothbinere that proved both enduring and profitable until his departure as a colonel many years later, in a contingent of His Majesty's forces bound for the battle of Gallipoli. He was killed in the first World War. Through him we had entree to the State ruler and his officials, a privilege that was exceptional and lucrative in the years to follow. One of the rare privileges was being a guest with many others of the Maharajah on his birthday anniversary. Such celebrations often lasted two weeks and you are entertained as only the third richest Indian prince can (10)

entertain. That ruler, Sri Krishnarajendra Wadiyar 

Bahadur, died only last year.

The new duties took us first to England where we were authorized to contact British manufacturers with a view to having our equipment made in that country. 

This was ultimately done at a saving of twenty-seven percent over American costs, and with more prompt deliveries. Our chief, Samuel Morrison, a fine gentleman and a royal host, was a judge of good wines, and it was his hobby to delve around in the monasteries of Spain in search of rare wines for his cellar in New York: He gave us a farewell dinner party to which he invited New York backers of his new company, so they might look us over. That was ordeal enough, particularly as I was the only one in a business suit, not owning a "soup and fish" outfit. Our host ordered cobweb-covered bottles of wine brought in, over which he made quite a ceremony in opening; all of which was lost on us, our education on wine and kindred stimulants having been sadly neglected. Not daring to refuse it, I remember looking at the glass as containing so much medicine. Making up my mind to go through with it, I drained it at one gulp. 

What a laugh! Our host looked at me as though I had kicked his favorite bull-pup and asked in a strained voice: "Did you like it"? 

My embarrassed reply was: "Yes, Sir." 

To which he replied: "Well, you ought to for that wine stood me eight dollars a bottle." 

Certainly a brutal lesson, but a good one, for I learned much that night that was to serve me well in (11)

future years. I also acquired about four introductory cards to London tailors and apparently passed inspection with my new bosses who never failed to "kid" me in later years about that "coming out" party.

Came the day of our departure for England, and what a trip that turned out to be! The liner "Etruia" of the Cunard Line, then the greyhound of the Atlantic, wallowed and twisted her way across the big pond, much to my undoing. That first experience in seasickness stands out in memory as being less bearable by reason of having a cabin companion who persisted in keeping disgracefully well. He tortured me by naming the various dinner courses that I was missing and the only time I really felt even with him a little bit was when he was persuaded to lay aside his old razor that he claimed had been in his family since Britain started ruling the waves, and try my safety razor, then a new thing. When he cut himself thoroughly, I felt partially repaid. passenger ever stepped ashore with less regret that I did that day in Liverpool. Learning that the train for York would not go for a few hours, my cabin companion put aside his reservation for London and set about to entertain me. As the train pulled out, he slipped his card in my hand. He was a member of Parliament. Not once had he alluded to it and our paths never crossed again. 

A Little Bit About York 

No 

Our destination was York, from New York to Old

York-what a contrast! Arriving at the hotel, we (12)

inquired of the "graven image" in livery, who by-the-by, seemed unable to see us at all, where one could register. 

Not being able to get anything out of him, and finally seeing a sign reading, "Enquiring Department," we modestly solicited the pleasure of affixing our signature to the hotel register. But even this was denied us, for the lady clerk curtly asked our name and entered it, minus address. Our room was spotless and beautifully furnished. Even the grate was polished, but it did not take long to learn that the English, and particularly the Scotch, make ornaments of their grates, for it would take a lot of argument to ever get a real honest-to-goodness fire built in one of them. The view from our window overlooked the city walls for which York is famous. 

Anyone visiting England should see York, if Hitler lets any part of it remain intact. History is not clear as to when it was founded, but historians seem to agree that this was around A.D. 80, and that the founder was 

Agricola. Adrian, a Roman emperor, came in A.D. 

125, and left his Sixth Legion on his departure. Next came Emperor Severus who died A.D. 211, and his body was burned on a hill specially erected for the incineration, which hill is now occupied by a city water reservoir. 

The Romans finally withdrew from York in A.D. 450, and later it became the capital of England and was famous as the home of kings.

About one-third of the present-day city is enclosed within walls built by Romans. These walls are probably among the best specimens in all England. At the time of our first visit, the four gates were all intact and were (13)

fitted with a portcullis consisting of a heavy oak frame, fringed with a row of metal spikes at the bottom. These were so arranged as to be dropped on the unwelcome visitor or on the approach of an enemy. These particular gates are famous as being the place where the heads of many luckless victims were displayed on spikes. One gate was once so decorated (in 1460) with the heads of the Duke of Rutland and the Duke of York. Both men were prominent in early English history. Originally each gate was protected by an outer wall and a second gate, but these have all been removed but one. All the remaining gates are decorated with many coats of arms. and traces of the gilding can still be seen. 

York is rich in relics of the past. The River Ouse flows through the city and the gaps in the walls end in towers at the water's edge, and one can still see the worn places on those towers where the chains that once blocked the stream were suspended. Near them have been found many Roman coffins which are always of stone, and in these coffins are usually found a coin supposed to have been put into the mouth of the deceased to pay for ferrying him over the River "Styx." Many of these coins may be seen in the York Museum. Arms, plastercasts of bodies, pottery, mosaics, and even coils of human hair adorned with crude hair-pins are also found in these old Roman coffins.

Among the ancient structures in the city is the York Minster Cathedral. The first church built on the present site, dates back to A.D. 627, at which time, Edwin, the Saxon king, was there baptized. Ruinous wars and (14)

fires have twice practically destroyed the structures. The present building is built over the old foundations and was started sometime in the eleventh century and was not completed until about 1426. Similar to many other cathedrals of the period, it is built in the form of a cross and covers about three acres with about one-half acre of glass in its windows. Cromwell once used it to stable his horses.

The old building shows the marks of time, its outside being a mass of images, statues, niches, and gargoyles. These are somewhat shapeless. The windows are warped and have lost much of their beauty, but are rich in old glass that can never be replaced. The building is stone within and without and is cold and cheerless. 

The different sections show the development in architecture from the early Saxon on down through early Norman or semi-Norman. The interior is rich in monuments, tablets and figures, dating back to the early days of the church. In the center is one of the world's finest organs with two smaller organs in the nave and choir loft. 

To hear a service there and the choir of male voices, is worth going to York for. We did not appreciate to the full our first visit to the cathedral. On that occasion we arrived just as the congregation was leaving. Joining a crowd which watched from a roped off area, and asking questions, we were informed that this was the annual military service and very important. Working our way to the ropes and the side of a policeman, our attention was directed to the wigged man leading the procession from the church. He was preceded by strange (15)

looking emblems of office being carried by attendants. 

Our curiosity getting the better of us, we asked, "Bobby, who is the fellow in the false hair and robe"?

After an annihilating glance, the policeman answered, "Tom Clark," or so we understood.

Not to be stopped, we again asked, "Well, who is 

Tom Clark"?

The policeman did not explode but as he gave a good imitation of an apoplectic attack, it dawned on us that it would be wise to pursue our inquiries elsewhere under safer conditions. We returned to the hotel, and at lunch, having picked out an Englishman who looked human, inquired if he knew a local celebrity by the name of Tom 

Clark. But that name did not register with him. Telling him I was just an ignorant American whose curiosity was insatiable and of the experience of the morning at the cathedral, he said it was the town clerk we had seennot "Tom Clark"! 

While in York we were asked: "Did you ever see a king," and remarked that there were painful memories of once seeing four kings in a poker game. However, this time it was the real thing and it was our privilege to go onto the railway station platform to see the royal train go through on its way to Scotland. Promptly on the minute, came the pilot engine followed shortly by the train, and in the front were the footmen, the coachmen and other servants, all dressed in red livery. In the center of the train, in the royal coach, was seated King Edward on one side and Queen Alexandra on the other. Both were reading and, although the train came (16)

to a brief halt, they did not deign to notice us, and the crowd made no demonstration. By that time your author had begun to calm down a little and keep his enthusiasm under control. We saw them later on their return journey and on that occasion the crowd did raise a weak little cheer. In later years it was again our privilege to see that same queen feeding pigeons in Venice in the plaza of St. Marks.

At the time of this first visit, England was the quaint old England that is no more. There were few if any automobiles and you "biked" if you wanted to see the country leisurely. It was our good fortune to tour much of the country with a friend who certainly knew his England. Our best recollections are of old ruins, cathedrals and inns that were rich in historical associations and feather-beds. Those were places where, when you ordered a six-penny tea, you were served cold mutton, scones, marmalade, thin slices of bread spread with unsalted butter, and tea and your host would be disappointed if you failed to eat it all.

Vienna, Trieste and Some Construction Problems 

Great changes have taken place since this first visit to England and Europe and any description of places or people would mean little in these days of war and devastation. There are some who may read these lines who may have copies of our long descriptive letters written. from many places in Europe, Africa and Asia-around forty years ago; copies of which are in our possession (17)

but which would not apply today. Consequently, we pass lightly over experiences of over a year of travel in Europe before actually starting for India. It will be enough to say that we were successful in promoting business that materialized later and have lived to inspect many water plants thus built; three of which were damaged in the first World War and at least two of which have suffered in the present war. 

Two experiences met with on that first tour of Europe, will bear relating. In Vienna, we like all visitors, made an early visit to the palace knowing that the 

Emperor Franz Joseph was in residence. Standing in the square in front of the main gate, outside of the grounds, our attention was directed to the sudden activity of the guards on the gate who had jumped to a stiff salute as an elderly man drove out in a simple carriage with driver and footman. As he passed us he turned slightly in our direction and lifted his hand as if in recognition, and thinking this must have been intended for someone beyond us, we turned to look, only to find that there was no one else in the square. We hurried after the carriage and finally learned definitely that it was Franz Joseph whom we had snubbed. Learning that he had often so noticed foreigners in the square, we made several other visits to the palace in the hope of making amends for our "faux pas" but never saw him again.

Riding on a tram in this same Vienna, our attention was called one day to a very dusky individual with head covered with a fez, selling slices of small watermelons from a push-cart and suddenly we were melon hungry. (18)

Stopping the tram, and going back, we pointed to the particular slice that appealed to us and in our best Italian asked how much. The reply was in some language that was not understood and we seemed to be getting nowhere fast. Pointing to our "tummy," we said, "American, no savvy." 

At that our push-cart man shouted: "Fo' God's sake, white man, don't you know a real nigga when you sees one. You sure made this nigga a heap of trouble to keep from busting out in English."

He was a Memphis darkey, seeing Europe behind a push-cart and it was good to see even a darkey from home.

Stopping over in Trieste which was in those days an Austrian city, we were to look after construction work there for several months. The plant was located approximately fifteen hundred feet above the beautiful Adriatic

Sea among as fine vineyard and fruit country as one can find. The view of the sea was superb and below jutting out into the water was the castle of Emperor Maximilian, who left this world in front of a Mexican firing squad.

In our search for suitable stone for making concrete for the building and foundations, we discovered an old abandoned road in which there was a large rock-fill not far from where our work was located. Taking possession, orders were given to have the rock moved to the crusher, which started a small riot. In the explanation that followed, it developed that the road and rock-fill had been built by Napoleon's soldiers enroute to the campaigns in 

Europe and Russia. However, the rock was used. (19)

While on that Trieste job, something new happened to us. We became sorely afflicted with an itch, the cause of which could not be found. Consulting a doctor, he amazed us with the assertion that we were merely lousy. 

Taking the "cure" we went gunning for the source of our affliction. A reading glass was all that was required to quickly find plenty of cooties on our Italian foreman's coat which we had foolishly permitted him to hang next to our own in the little shack which had been built for tools and drawings. One Italian coat was burned in the fire under the lead kettle and the foreman burned our shack that night, drawings and all. It took us about three weeks to replace the drawings from New York but a much shorter time to get a new foreman who received certain instructions relative to delousing himself permanently.

In later years in Egypt, it was our fate to have a somewhat similar experience in connection with aggregate material for concrete work. Our first contract there was hurried through because of a terrible cholera epidemic in that city, with the result that some errors were made in estimating. The particular item that concerned us most was where to procure the necessary rock. It was soon apparent that the only source of supply was from the same quarries that supplied the stones used in building the pyramids and when we computed the cost of securing stone from there and transporting it by canal to Alexandria, there were visions of a loss that would surely puncture our reputation as an engineer.

Our investigation brought us in contact with a (20)

French builder who asked why not use Egyptian pottery, and he showed us concrete so built. That solved the problem and we immediately advertised for pottery, with the result that quantities of everything from Roman lamps about the size of a large saucer, to tomb jars, two to three feet high, were soon acquired. A few mummified cats and bronze coins dating back to the Roman occupation of Egypt were found and these coins were afterward given to the British Museum in London. 

There was broken pottery from the river sand-bars and whole jars that probably were filched from tombs, but not too many questions were asked about the sources of supply. All were broken to proper size and better concrete was never made. We sometimes wonder if posterity will give us credit for our efforts to preserve Egyptian antiquities. 

Strange Winds and Their Effects 

A broken foot laid me up in Trieste quite awhile and this permitted me to take note of how interested the police seemed to be in my walks about the city on crutches. However, one visits few places in Europe where he can feel entirely free from such surveillance much of the time. The building construction of Trieste impressed us as unusual. Masonry walls were of extreme thickness, roofs were flat and all windows were double with probably twelve-inch spaces between. One set opened in and the other out and both were fitted with very heavy hinges and latches.

Everyone we questioned about this said it was (21)

necessary because of the "Borea," a wind of cyclonic force caused by the warm water of the Mediterranean Sea, drawing the icy blasts down from the Alps. As proof of the necessity of protecting trains from being blown off their tracks, they reminded us of the many miles of stone walls to be seen paralleling the railways on the journey south from Vienna. According to their records, this had actually occurred. Having ducked into a Kansas cyclone cellar one time we refused to be terrified. 

But we changed our mind during that three-day Borea blow when we saw a horse dragged over the sea-wall in front of the hotel by the carriage it was harnessed to. 

This same wind effect is found in other lands in a milder form. Calcutta, India, enjoys, for about three months each year, a climate much like that of Los Angeles, due to the warm water of the Bay of Bengal attracting the cold winds of the Himalayan range of mountains. This is but a gentle breeze. The same thing happens in Egypt when the "Kamseen" blows. This is a hot wind off the desert due, possibly, to the lower temperature of sea water. When that wind blows, you better put your books in a press or weight them down if you do not want the covers to buckle and tear off. The way ostrich feathers curl at such times is remarkable. 

Suez Canal, Island of Perim and Aden

On our way again from Trieste to India, we look back from the ship to the building of our creation, high on the cliff, little thinking that one day in the first 

World War to come, Italian cannon would shell it. (22)

Through the beautiful Adriatic, along the Grecian

Coast where our memory took us back to school days and troublesome Greek translations, our ship ultimately reached Port Said where it was coaled from hand-carried baskets, and took on a large search-light. Finally the coaling is over and the ship heads for the Suez Canal. 

The search-light furnished by the canal company costs approximately one hundred dollars per trip. This together with around two thousand dollars for a mediumsized ship and two dollars and a half per passenger, makes quite a tidy toll for passing through an eighty-sixmile long ditch dug in sand. It takes about fifteen hours to make the journey, inclusive of station stops. Your steamer is tied to the bank while another from the opposite direction passes.

Although the Mediterranean end of the canal is approximately six and one-half feet higher than the Red Sea end, there is no perceptible flow south. Friction loss together with the Bitter Lake, which forms a great "bulge" in the canal, probably has something to do with this. A range of hills of an average height of sixty feet was cut through without modern excavating equipment and twenty thousand fellahin laborers were employed. 

The canal was opened in 1869, and the Khedive gave a twenty million-dollar party to celebrate it. In later years our ship was stuck in the canal for two days while efforts were made to clear it of another steamer that was aground. This steamer was finally destroyed with explosives before it could be passed. 

Arriving at Suez, the canal company pilot and his (23)

portable search-light equipment is dropped and the ship heads for Aden thirteen hundred miles away. There may be hotter seas than the Red, but not to our knowledge. 

We bring our own mattress on deck and hope for enough breeze to bend that column of smoke that goes straight up from the stacks and drops soot all over the deck. The women sleep on one side and men on the other and sleep walking is discouraged. On one voyage made, a German annoyed the ladies by walking the deck on their side. 

He was warned and as he refused to desist, he was doused with the ship's hose. In the morning several of us listened to a tongue-lashing from the captain to the effect that he needed no help in running the ship and the German slept in his cabin for the duration of the trip. There was real discipline on shipboard in those days. 

Down at the southern end of the Red Sea is the

Strait of Bab-el-mandeb and in it, the Island of Perim, and thereon about half out of the water was a P. & O. liner. She ran ashore at night and there is an interesting story about the cause thereof. A year passed before she was hauled off and put back into service. 

The story is that the captain was on his last voyage home prior to retirement and celebrated by giving a dinner party during which he drank unwisely and too well with favorite passengers. Being advised by the navigating officer that the course set by him prior to leaving the bridge should be changed he forbade such a change being made. A second time he was warned that the course set would pile up the ship on Perim. He replied impatiently: "Then pile her up but do not change the course." (24)

And that is where she went. We had often heard the expression "obey orders if it sinks the ship," and this was proof as to what lengths men of the sea would go in carrying out orders.

There is a good story about how the British came into possession of the Island of Perim. Long years ago, a French man-of-war, dropped anchor off Aden where they were well entertained by officers of the British navy. Over their cups, the French let it be known that they were on their way to take possession of Perim. The 

British prolonged their hospitality so long that when the French vessel arrived at the island, they found the Union Jack flying and a sizable number of war vessels standing by. The British had beaten them to it and as the island commands the shipping lane on both sides, they did not do so badly.

Port Said in popular imagination, is the wickedest place on earth, because of the human riff-raff "washed up" at that port, but there are those who think Aden takes some beating. 

Kipling said: "Take me somewhere east of Suez 

Where the best is like the worst; 

Where there ain't no ten commandments 

And a man can raise a thirst."

Aden, as well-known now in these days of the war struggle, is a British possession which jutts out into the gulf of the same name and is important because of the strategic position it occupies, commanding as it does, the sea route to India. Kipling calls it the worst stretch of (25)

country in the world, it being desert and waterless, volcanic rock. Nothing grows there and all around are wild tribes who are so resentful of British occupation as to be quite troublesome.

We were sent there in later years to make a report on a possible water supply from an inland source and in spite of being well organized as to guards, food and camp equipment, we were not only scared stiff by sniping Arabs, but thought we would pass out from heat, dust, insects and thirst. Incidentally, there was nothing to the water supply and Aden still drinks distilled sea water purchased from tank wagons at prices that are appalling. 

There you bathe in sea water and if you do use fresh water in quantity, you save the dirty residue to nourish some little plant or tree that you try to grow. 

The British have held Aden since about 1839, and about all the trouble they have had is local and there has and probably always will be plenty of that. Formerly there was a large garrison and how the "Tommies" cursed and hated Aden and exulted when they could be transferred elsewhere. The heat is intolerable and insect pests are a separate curse long to be remembered.

Many lives have been lost in the struggle with the natives and past commanders were changed frequently. 

The situation became so desperate that finally airplanes were stationed there and much of the garrison withdrawn and thereafter, each time a British soldier was sniped at, a plane was sent over to drop a few bombs. 

Only a few such flights were necessary to convince the unruly shieks that they could not reach the "droning (26)

buzzards" with their bullets. As a result a single plane now is more effective than a whole garrison and until the Arabs learn to fly planes, the trouble seems to be over in Aden. However, present-day war conditions may have already altered that particular condition. 

Gateway to India and Our First Introduction to Golf 

From Aden it is a good five to six days to the Port of Bombay, a journey that monsoon weather can make memorable to the uninitiated. If you must visit India, pick the right time of the year which is not in the period of the monsoon. Bombay is rather a welcome sight to the weary traveler and as the buildings take form and hills appear in the background, you have the feeling that the Gateway to India is not so bad after all. We know better and you can have our share of it for six bits. 

Originally a Portuguese possession, Bombay came into the possession of the British sometime in the sixteenth century. About that time the French, Dutch, Portuguese, and English were contesting for India. All that remains of Portugal is the colony of Goa, on the coast south of Bombay and the area is approximately one thousand sixty-three square miles. While Indian in every other sense, the people of Goa speak Portuguese and are subject to the laws of Portugal.

On the east side of India are several French possessions, the largest of which is Pondicherry, a sizable colony south of Madras and here again the natives speak French, and are subject to French laws and ruled by a (27)

French governor. restrictions through carrying out contracts in those French Colonies, which contracts were written in French and every drawing had to show metric measurements as well as feet and inches. Try doing that once and see how you like it.

We had cause to remember such The difference between the two great cities of Bombay and Calcutta, is that the native section of the former is kaleidoscopic and noisy and it is so situated that most tourists pass through it. In Calcutta the native city is apart from the European commercial and residential section, making it possible to live there for years and know nothing about it. Calcutta is flat and stands on the banks of the muddy Hoogly River and is a city of clay. 

Bombay is a city of stone. Calcutta has been misnamed the City of Palaces by reason of rows of buildings on its principal throughfares. Bombay might be said to have castles, many of them not displayed in rows. It is variegated while Calcutta is uniform like London. 

Parsees, Hindus, and Mohammedans make up distinct parts of Bombay, but in Calcutta there seems to be only Bengali Babus, clerks, students, shop-keepers, mechanics, tailors, and shoemakers, all of whom look alike with little of the diversity that you see in the Bombay streets. Wives and daughters of the Calcutta Babu, do not appear in the open except on festive occasions and then they go in haste between their homes and the sacred river, half-clothed, shame-faced and silent. In Bombay, women of high caste move about freely, appear interested in everything, and chat gaily as they pass along. (28)

Thousands, including Parsee women and girls in good clean clothes, make a bright effect in the streets. Parsee girls go to school free from the bondage of the caste system and skip along briskly much as do our own American girls. Many of these same girls go to finishing schools in France or England. 

Our destination is Bangalore in the State of Mysore in South India. The journey, while not too comfortable, is made part way by a broad-gauge railway and the balance by narrow-gauge. Later we find that there are at least five rail-gauges in the country, the widest being five feet, three inches and the narrowest twenty-four inches.

However, more about these great railway systems later. 

We noticed numerous lepers at wayside stations and numerous deformed beggars who pestered us each time we tried to exercise when the train stopped. Arriving at Bangalore, we settled ourselves in the hotel and seeing what appeared to be a park outside, I went for a walk but hardly had gotten into stride, when the heavens fell. 

When I came to, I found a doctor working over me and learned that I had been struck at the base of the skull with a golf ball. This was my introduction to the game.

It cost me two days in bed, but as officials from the Maharajah either came or inquired about my condition, I felt it was worth it.

Indian Labor, Religions and What a Horoscope Can Do 

I learned that my work would be in the country some sixty-nine miles away and fifteen miles from the railway and the nearest place to purchase food and (29)

supplies. Finally located in a mud hut specially built for us to live in, which by the by, possessed nothing but bars in the windows (no glass or fly screens) and our neighbors were natives. My duties were to supervise erection of a large water works and the first modern mechanical filter plant ever to be built in India and the Far East. 

This together with a high pressure line fifteen miles long was to provide pure water for the famous Kolar Gold Fields and neighboring towns. These mines had produced gold long before the advent of the white man and have continued to operate down to the present day. 

We were fated to later spend many years in India and countries of the East and the lessons learned in the ten months required to build that first installation were to serve me well in the years to come. 

India is not a country, but a continent including many countries with fifty-five languages and dialects, with people ranging from fierce wild tribes in the mountains to wealthy merchants at the ports. Of the many races of people, the 

Hindu is in the majority and the Mohammedan next.

The former are separated into thousands of castes, none of whom will eat food in the home of the other; they will not marry out of their caste and members of two castes will not even attend the same school. This makes for dis-unity and it will probably take generations of effort to break down that curse.

We found ourselves in charge of several thousand coolie laborers including men, women and children. 

These people spoke four distinct languages among them Kanerese, Hindustani, Teligu and Tamil; a veritable (30)

Tower of Babel. Under the circumstances one could understand the necessity of printing the names of railway stations in as much as six separate languages. A cloudburst practically destroyed the reservoir dam which was to be the source of water supply and it was for the replacement of this dam that so much labor was required. 

We saw the dam that had been built centuries before, rebuilt by probably the same primitive method originally employed and without a single piece of modern equipment being used. Men, women and children carried earth from the borrow pits in bamboo baskets or sheet metal pans and placed it on the dam where it was watered by men from goat-skin bags hung on their backs and the entire structure was tramped or puddled with droves of water buffalo driven by boys. These great beasts thus packed the earth much in the same way as accomplished today by the mechanical sheeps foot rollers. In the years that followed I was jointly responsible for the construction of five other dams (one a rock fill) in India and Ceylon, using the same primitive methods first employed at Kolar.

On this our first construction work, we made friends of the native officials and engineers, a few of whom spoke good English having been educated in the engineering colleges in the country, under English professors. We also succeeded in acquiring a fair knowledge of Hindustani, the best known of the Indian languages. The friendships, made on that first job, have lasted down through the years and to this day correspondence continues with men with black skins and hearts of gold, (31)

men who as engineers would be a credit to the profession even on this American Continent. respect

One of the lessons learned was tolerance and for the other fellow's religion and customs. example, on this same first Indian contract, we were asked most respectfully to permit a delegation of Hindus, headed by two Brahman priests, to present a petition and, granting this, was assured of their great respect and their appreciation of our kindness, etc. They begged that what they were about to ask would not offend. It developed that they had observed that considerable leather was being used for pipe gaskets and other things, and they pointed out that inasmuch as they worshiped the sacred bullock, that no Hindu could conscientiously drink water that was drawn from the pipes. They also called attention to the fact that it was our evident intention to use lubricants with a tallow base for the machinery, and pointed out that with the Mohammedan's wellknown abomination of the pig, that they could not be expected to do differently. Although inclined to tell them to go jump in the lake, we ultimately made quite a ceremony of disconnecting pipe unions and replacing leather gaskets with rubber; also made changes in the lubricating systems to use mineral and castor bean oils to satisfy the Mohammedans, all this to the great amusement of two British officials who heard of it. That small effort was to pay dividends in later years, for it became widely known and others were only too glad to follow our lead.

Bombay and on the second morning he amazed passengers by deliberately shooting a sea-gull, the feathered (34)

Moral, never shoot a sea-gull.

Our job was to construct a modern filter plant at Alexandria and supervise changes and improvements to the water-works there, as previously referred to. That city formerly was and may still be a haven for criminals and exiles from many lands. For work of such magnitude, one needs an interpreter who can speak the many languages spoken there. The project included the construction of three large masonry settling basins for the pretreatment of thirty-two million gallons daily flow. 

The construction of these basins was a real problem by (35)

reason of the selected site. This site turned out to be the junction point of the old Roman underground aqueduct, built during their occupation of Egypt. The excavation work uncovered these structures of various sizes, eliptical in shape and constructed of Roman brick laid in mortar that defied demolition by common labor. 

Using explosives to clear away the structures that interfered with the new tank bottoms, it was necessary to attempt to block up the old water-ways which proved a long and costly job. The mortar used by the Romans is doubtless much the same as has been used in India and other Far Eastern countries for centuries. It is made from burnt shell-lime and well ground over burnt brick and Pharoah's heart was never as hard.

This Roman masonry brings to mind other marvels of the early Egyptian, whoever he may have been.

Mined probably on the Sinia side of the Suez Canal, copper was used in the making of helmets, swords, shields, industrial tools, wheels, pulleys, rollers, wedges, screws, drills that could bore through the toughest diorite and saws that could cut the massive lids of a sarcophagus, knives and edged tools of tempered copper, now a lost art. 

But one has only to visit the Cairo Museum to be convinced of the Egyptian's ability to temper copper. They made plaster of Paris, glass and glazed pottery. They knew the art of enameling and varnishing and developed the chemistry for many industrial purposes. They made tissues of the finest weaving known in the history of textiles, and specimens of linen made four thousand years ago show to this day a weave so fine that a magnifying (36)

glass is required to distinguish it from silk. See the

Cairo Museum and be convinced. It is worth all the annoyance of flies, fleas, and beggars. 

Ezion-geber, the center of the copper industry, was 

King Solomon's Pittsburgh of Palestine. The site has been partially explored and the great brick walls so far uncovered were designed and laid so as to get the full benefit of the prevailing north winds, with flues leading from one furnace room to another. To have built such a plant or rather series of plants, probably thousands of laborers had to be housed and fed, skilled technicians were doubtless employed, and great caravans were required to transport raw materials and the finished product. Archæologists have uncovered five such cities or plants, so located as to be equally subject to the high winds or gales which, evidently, was needed to provide the necessary draught. The interior of the walls still standing are green from the gasses given off in the refining process. 

Solomon, no doubt, was a copper king. He was also a master builder and he selected the only site for miles around where there was a sweet water supply and where there were dependable winds. His giant blast furnaces were three thousand years ahead of his time.

Broken crucibles found in the ruins, tell the tale of business for export and he, evidently, did a large export business through the Red Sea port of Aqaba. The site of the furnace was midway between the southern end of the 

Dead Sea and the port above mentioned. Slag piles for (37)

miles around indicate where the ore was mined and burned.

At the same time that the Alexandria plant was building, another smaller installation was going up at Mansurah, some one hundred miles south. Our assistant was in charge when along came the Fourth of July. 

Fire-arms were taboo in Egypt and firing a gun was just not done. However, this young American was homesick and without letting it be known, had a sizable hole drilled in an old anvil and before daybreak, he had installed it on an island in the river in front of the town where he proceeded to fire a twenty-one-gun salute.

When the town "gendarmes" got to him he had only one charge left and he was able to persuade them to allow him to fire that off, too. It took all day and the aid of consular officers to get him out of that scrape. Five other water plants were afterward constructed in Egypt but there were no more Fourth of July celebrations.

Lower Egypt has a climate that is unsurpassed and the sunsets from the seashore at Alexandria are seemingly beyond the artist's ability to paint. Near that city are miles of beach which was formerly the playground, for the Romans at least, for there are numerous masonry bathing pools built in the sand that are lined in mosaic patterns. These have either settled to considerable depth or have been tippd up and broken during storms. After heavy storms that are infrequent, natives may be seen panning the sand for cornelian and agate ornaments lost by the ladies of past generations. (38)

The building of new harbor works had brought to view through excavation work, great walls made up of Italian marble that was undoubtedly imported from Europe, some of the stones being as much as eighteen feet long by three feet thick and those thus uncovered were so fitted that it was difficult to find the joints. We saw those dynamited out of the way and such stones, together with many beautiful pillars of granite and a bath-tub of the same material and weighing approximately three tons, broken up for street building material. 

In the days referred to, antiques were too plentiful to be preserved, particularly if they were of rock. 

Egypt is indeed the land of blue sky and bright sunlight, and full moonlight nights permit playing badminton without illumination. However, it has its full share of pests, particularly fleas in season and flies the year around. Rainfall is almost negligible. For three or four months the flea is something to be reckoned with. 

One even goes so far as to put flea powder between the bed sheets and carefully crawl in so as not to choke. It is recommended that one allow dogs in the house sufficiently often to provide a ride for fleas that otherwise will amuse themselves on you. When the dogs go out they take the fleas along; rather hard on the dogs, however.

A Naval Battle That We Missed and Some War 

Experiences

The first World War played havoc with our business. Thirteen of our cargoes of machinery and equipment, made in England, found a watery grave through submarine and surface raiders. In one case, the building made to house a complete plant was empty almost three years, during which time the equipment was replaced a third time before arriving safely. It finally became necessary to erect a foundry and machine-shop and with native labor we were able to manufacture valves, pipe in six, and finally nine-foot lengths, and other standard equipment. But what a headache that was! Then along came Government moratoriums which prevented collec- (41)

tions being made for years. But "the show must go on.' 

And it did go on. 

The German cruiser "Emden" was the particular offender in the Indian Ocean and Bay of Bengal. She was here, there and everywhere. Today she would sink six vessels at the mouth of the Hooghly River, within one hundred and twenty miles of the port; calmly put all of the passengers of the six ships on a seventh; send them safely to Calcutta with the promise that she would play a return engagement soon. 

On one occasion she even wirelessed an oil depot to the effect that she was one of the British cruisers, (then trying to run her down, using the British code, too), to send down to the mouth of the river a cargo of certain oils badly needed. That shipment was actually loaded and was on its way to the delivery point when stopped by orders from the British. 

Navy which suspected something wrong. The Emden did not get her oil that time.

As previously stated we periodically visited other offices in adjacent countries and on one of these trips saw a little of the only naval battle that we will probably ever see. Our steamer was held at Penang, (a city on an island at the north end of the Federated Malay States), together with some twelve or more other steamers anchored in the roads. Orders from the naval authorities were responsible and we were told that the German Cruiser Emden was somewhere in the Bengal Gulf and that we would not be allowed to sail until she had been located. 

I had gone to a hotel and not sleeping too well, (42)

moved out onto the veranda where peaceful dreams were shattered at daybreak by a terrific explosion, followed by cannonading and a further great explosion. In pajamas 

I commandeered a rickshaw and hurried to the harbor where there was a pall of yellow smoke on the water and we could just make out masts sticking out of the water and men swimming about. A large four-funneled grey cruiser was going out to sea which turned out to be the "Emden." She had come in disguised with a fourth funnel made of canvas and in the early dawn was taken for a four-funnel British cruiser that was known to be looking for her in the bay. She evidently knew that the Russian cruiser "Jemtschug," which was about the same size as the Emden, was in Penang Harbor and it took two torpedoes and a few well-directed shells at point blank range to reduce her to a sunken wreck in about fifteen minutes.

She had also apparently intended to sink all of the merchant vessels anchored in the roads and a French torpedo boat that was there cleaning boilers, but a welter of smoke coming in from the sea, made her turn about and go out to investigate. This turned out to be a harbor boat but she evidently was afraid to go back into the harbor again and continued out to sea where she shortly met the French torpedo boat "Mosquet" that was on patrol duty outside. In view of the harbor, she blew that luckless vessel off the water, but not before the French had fired at her several times. She picked up the survivors and fled, knowing that wireless messages were telling the eastern world of her position. (43)

We saw the French torpedo boat "D'lberville" that was in the harbor take pot shots at the Emden as she went to sea, but found later that about all the damage she did was to puncture the funnel of a Japanese vessel that was anchored and in line of fire. Having traveled by French boats, we can realize how they might fire wildly under excitement and they certainly get excited. How the 

Emden ever refrained from sinking all or part of the other vessels that lay at her mercy in Penang harbor has always puzzled me. Our own ship's captain made the same remark later. The channel between the island and the mainland was probably a thousand yards wide and it did take nerve to go in that harbor and take a chance on turning fast in such close quarters, particularly with merchant shipping in the way to further limit turning space. Then, too, there was the danger that there might be a torpedo boat lurking inside which could sink her. At best the dangers were many and great.

What amazed us was the realization that the harbor fort of Georgetown did not fire a shot and that it was equipped only with obsolete guns. 

A few months later the Emden met her fate from the British (Australian) cruiser "Sydney" that caught up with her and chased her ashore on Keeling Island where she was deliberately beached. Her crew was taken prisoners along with Captain Von Muller. We afterwards saw some of the wounded at Colombo in Ceylon. Certainly the Emden earned a niche in the hall of immortal ships.

We are reminded of another naval event we saw at (44)

Singapore, the date of which cannot be recalled. In that harbor came a British tanker with her cargo of oil on fire and after all efforts on the part of port authorities had failed she was ordered to be towed into shallow water in a section of the harbor where she could be safely scuttled and refloated. By the time she reached the spot selected, she was so hot that her sea cocks could not be reached and about that time someone had the bright idea of asking the fort to sink her with their guns. In due time a spot was quickly painted on the forward part of the hull as a mark to shoot at and two shots were put through her hull which permitted her to rest on the bottom putting out the fire.

It seems that fort commander was brought before his superiors and asked for his authority for wasting ammunition under such circumstances and he was formally charged with the expense involved. Taking legal advice, this officer brought suit against the Marine Insurance Company which covered the sunken tanker claiming several thousand pounds sterling salvage for saving the ship. The upshot was that the insurance company paid for the ammunition and legal advice and the officer cancelled his suit.

On another sea trip during the war, we joined a ship at an Egyptian port that was substituting for a French liner sunk by a submarine. The substitute ship was one that had been a fine vessel in the regular passenger service, but for two years she had been used in bringing horses over from African ports. They had stabled horses in the salon and all the fine carved woodwork for which (45)

French liners were famous had been chewed as high as horses could reach. The ship smelled of horses and was about as unfit for passenger service as it could possibly be, but it was take it, or stay behind. 

The passenger list was made up entirely of French officers and men who had been through the war and bore wounds that had incapacitated them for further service. 

They were volunteers going to Indo-China to substitute, in civil life, for men able to go home for war duty. That was a memorable trip. My cabin companion was a

French general who had a body riddled with shrapnel. 

He actually had to be helped into bed but he was cheery. 

Every man on the ship was companionable and full of pep, even at boat-drills which every ship traveler hates. 

Two days out, just at daybreak one morning, we were awakened by cannon shots from the gun on the stern of the vessel. That was not at all funny and this self-styled hero remembers wrestling with a life preserver that just would not stay put. On deck there was nothing to see. 

We heard that a periscope had been seen and we zigzagged all day and were punished with having to live in those life preservers for hours but it seems doubtful if that submarine was not a "dud." Certainly there was plenty of "razzing" from the passengers for days to come. 

Driving a Car Is Hazardous

In our own organization at the outbreak of war, there were several Englishmen all but two of whom were sooner or later called to the colors. A few got home guard (46)

work, but others went to France, one of whom "went west" and one came back as a captain in the royal engineer corps. Another rose to fame, was decorated for exceptional service, remained in the army, and if alive, is doubtless on the front today. This man was out of his element in India and cut out for military life without knowing it. We recall an experience with him that is worth relating: He was a wealthy father's son and never had to depend on his salary. The father sent him what was probably the first automobile to come to India. It was a "one-lunger" that was fearfully and wonderfully constructed. It was a single-seater with a flywheel protruding up and between the legs of the driver. You started it by winding it up on the side instead of in front, as was done later on.

He was very proud of this car, particularly as it was the first in Calcutta. About a week after breaking it in, he drove up just as we happened to be looking out of the window. He called the door-man and pompously told him to put some water in what is now called the radiator-but it was something else in those days, the name being forgotten. Before going to our desk we saw the water poured in without much thought of where it was poured. Later he telephoned to advise that he was only three blocks from the office. But the car refused to budge. Later in the day he came in looking much dejected and said that he had taken the car to Dykes & Company, carriage and harness-makers whose business dated back to 1776. They had never seen an automobile before and were undertaking to find the trouble and had (47)

a miscellaneous lot of parts scattered around without having much idea what it was all about. "Where did you tell the door-man to pour that water this morning, and do you think he put it in the right place"? we asked.

It is best to draw the curtain on what followed. The water had been poured into the gas tank and the car had traveled three blocks on the gas left in the carburetor before it gave up the ghost. Those were the days (and for years after) when if your car was not fitted with the steering wheel on the right-hand side, you could not get a license to drive it, for all driving was to the left. Also a battery was unheard of. You had a magneto and no starter. Gas, or petrol as it was and is called yet, was purchased in two-gallon red cans and there were no stations to serve you. You bought from the one wholesaler or you did without.

If you went far, you carried extra tins of gas with you and a piece of chamois skin to strain it through. Yet that did not take out the "coughs." You drove on roads full of children, men, women, goats, bullocks and took tall chances at speeds comparable to "horse and buggy days." There are millions of bullocks in India which 

Nature gave cloven hoofs. These hoofs are shod with two-piece iron shoes which after being worn down to knife edge, drop off in the dust and lay in wait for the unwary motorist. His front wheel turns the half-shoe up just enough for the rear tire to catch it right, and what a puncture that makes! This, with the constant menace of killing someone, makes for little pleasure. (48)

We remember driving a Government official to his hotel and when we were almost at the stopping point, an officious native policeman pushed a poor coolie out of the way, but under the car. He was badly chewed up and lost, among other things, his right ear. We did the unusual and took him to the hospital. Weeks after, this coolie came to the office and begged the Durwan (door-man) to let him in. Permission being given, he came in salaaming to the floor. "What do you want"? we asked.

He replied, "I want the Sahib to see I am well again." 

We retorted, "You rascal, what you came in for was bachshesh; how much do you want"? "Whatever the sahib wants to give." (This is the usual reply.) "How about five rupees"? "Salaam, salaam, Sahib," indicating entire satisfaction. He got it and left quite happy before we could change our mind. This was equivalent to about a dollar and a half.

In the Madras Museum is a receipt that dates back to the days when Lord Dunraven was viceroy of India. 

On a tiger hunting expedition in Madras, he accidentally shot a coolie instead of the tiger. He sent a native clerk to settle with the family to take a receipt for any money paid. That receipt reads, "To one bloody murder, five rupees." Life must have been cheaper then. (49)

Railways in India and What the Traveler May 

Experience Few there are who realize the size of India. It has an area of one million seven hundred sixty-six thousand and eight hundred square miles, which is, roughly, eleven times the size of California, or half the size of the United States. There are approximately five hundred native states and provinces with a total population of three hundred and fifty million, less than two hundred thusand of whom are white. Think of it, two hundred thousand whites ruling that population! One native state, Hyderabad, is about the size of Italy, while little Laws has only five square miles. Climatic conditions vary in different sections, but it is generally only different degrees of worse. The rainfall varies from eight hundred inches at Cherra Punji to almost nothing in desert sections. Great railway systems span the country in many directions and, while lacking in comforts to which tourists are often accustomed, are good and dependable. 

Few countries ever presented greater problems as to large rivers, some of which have a habit of changing their course quite suddenly. Some of the world's largest bridges are found there.

One has the choice of first or second class accommodation which assures freedom from native passengers, but it does not prevent queer annoyances to whites. Some of you who read this book may have toured India.

If so, you will probably agree that making an extended trip there is an experience not met with in any other (50)

country. You doubtless recall that you had to purchase bedding, towels, soap and all of the usual essentials for comfort that are found in our North American Pullman service. All that the railway provided was a leather couch, which was a seat by day and a bed at night. You probably cursed such requirements, but later realized that with plague, small-pox and other diseases always prevalent, it would be a little hazardous to sleep on bedding that some other passenger may have used the night before. 

When we recall that on two occasions, we learned of trusted servants lending our clean bed-sheets to cover a native corpse on its way to the funeral pyre, we marvel that we are here to write about it. 

For an American, accustomed to the privacy and luxury of Pullman service, traveling in India has its drawbacks. All first-class coaches are of the compartment type, and unless you purchase the entire compartment, which usually entails buying four seats, you are in for a bad night. You take your chances and often find your worst fears realized. For example, you are peacefully sleeping when your train pulls into "Funnypore." The door opens with a crash and in comes a bunch of shouting coolies followed by servants. They turn on all the lights and proceed to fill up the floor space with suit-cases, trunks, hold-alls, lunch-baskets, pots, pans and other camp impediments. Finally in comes the passenger who has been hunting (or shooting as he calls it), and he brings in a couple of dogs who affectionately lick you, and when you protest, the passenger explains that they are merely friendly. If your (51)

protests are strong enough he may consent to chain them so as not to annoy you. In all probability he has a bagful of game shot hours before, the odor of which is none too pleasant; but in due course you and the dogs slumber together, regardless of the odoriferous game-bag. 

Your luck may be out, for your passenger may be a native gentleman who delights to ride as a "pukka sahib," first-class. The only thing he omits in the way of traveling paraphernalia is the kitchen sink, which he never possessed. Pots, pans, hubble-bubble, dirty bedding, shabby trunks, water-bottles, betel-nut kit, and what have you, not to mention several mongrel servants who have a habit of coming into the compartment on every and any occasion. Your traveling companion's great joy by day is to sit cross-legged on his couch, dressed in as few clothes as the law allows, and belch. At night he snores so as to be heard above the usual train noises. 

To this day we recall our glee on parting with one of these gentlemen after a miserable night of annoyance by his snoring and filthy habits. Leaving the train before daylight at a station where there were absolutely no accommodations, we were obliged to remain until daybreak when we expected to proceed to our final destination by elephant. It was a hot night and after getting out onto the platform, we discovered the tormenor's foot sticking out of the open window. Smoking a cigaret, we waited for the train to start, and as it moved we touched the hot end of the cigaret to the bottom of that foot. We have often wondered if the victim thought he had been bitten by a cobra. Certainly he yelled lustily. (52)

A big railway station is one of the most interesting places in India. The native has the wanderlust highly developed and he is never so happy as when on the move. 

Unlike the American, he likes to travel slowly and he enjoys every minute from the day he starts until he returns. He even gets pleasure out of missing trains, and regards time as no object when he travels. He has a vague idea that the "terrain gharry" that is to carry him leaves at say eight o'clock, and in order to be there in time he starts for the station possibly two to ten hours early. Arriving there, he spreads his blanket on the concrete floor, and either goes to sleep or gossips with some other happy-go-lucky, inexperienced traveler.

Time passes very pleasantly until train time and then our traveler either forgets which platform it leaves from, or probably he never knew, and with two or three minutes to go he dashes around madly only to find that his train has left. He may abuse the train and the staff, but more likely he takes it quite philsophically and returns to the place where he has sat most of the day, and settles down contentedly to spend possibly another twenty-four hours until it pleases God or the "Sirkar" to start another train. Cases have been known of men and women waiting patiently at the station for three to four days, during which time many trains left that would have taken them to their destination.

They are almost as happy in the waiting-room of the station as if they were on their train, for are they not to start-sometime? They have all of the comforts of home with them, and the fact that they have lost two or three (53)

days does not trouble them at all. The railway officials, who have to deal with this class of traveler, do their best and wonderfully, considering how much they are handicapped by the people themselves as to whether they leave the same day they arrive at the station, or the next. One cannot help thinking that the Indian to enjoy such a journey as third-class accommodation provides, must have the spirit of travel thoroughly ingrained in him. 

Stevenson expressed that spirit in one of his books when he says, "For my part I travel not to go anywhere, but to go." As previously mentioned, there are a variety of gauges, with good reasons therefor. Strange as it may seem, these range from twenty-four inches to five feet, three inches. The twenty-four-inch gauge goes up six thousand feet to Darjeeling; the five-foot, three-inch gauge tracks across the country between the east and west coast.

In Ceylon they use six-foot gauge tracks. We recall vividly the visit of a famous ex-cabinet officer of our own Government, who shall be nameless. The gentleman appealed to us to use our good offices to secure special privileges from the railways which would make it possible for him to study the Indian systems. Being in good standing with the East Indian Railway, and with the help of the American consul general, we were able to secure for this gentleman a private car, equipped with kitchen and servants which, while not just what he might expect from the Pennsylvanian System, was as good as could be offered. He was given every opportunity to see all there was to see on the broad-gauge lines, but (54)

instead of being grateful, that buzzard wrote the most scurrilous articles about what he had seen and experienced. Those articles were cabled back to Indian papers from the first port west of Bombay. To say it cost us our privileges with the Indian railways, expresses it mildly. I afterwards had to take a civil engineering examination to be permitted to do certain work, an examination for which I crammed for months. When I came up for it, I found that the chief engineer of the East Indian Railway would give it orally. When I realized the examination was hardly fair, I asked the Chief why 

I was being crucified and was asked, "Do you recall what a certain bald-headed American ex-cabinet officer had to say about my railway"? I passed, but about knocked my hat off getting under the wire.

Many other famous men and some that might come under a different heading, visited India and are remembered accordingly. The famous "Mark Twain" came through and was just in time to preside at the annual 

Lincoln birthday anniversay affair held yearly by the

American Colony. We met him at the steamer on the morning of this meeting and very carefully explained that there would be few Americans, but many British friends would attend and that it would be a drawingroom affair with full-dress being customary. Certainly nothing as to this latter stipulation could have been more definite. When our humorist turned up, he came in a seersucker coat, flannel trousers and a colored shirt and belt. At first it was thought this was a type of humor that might develop into something worthwhile, but not (55)

once during that evening did he say one thing that was funny and our English friends kidded us about it for years.

The Caste System and Some Effects and Customs 

Having spent nearly fifteen years of our life in India. and neighboring countries, we may rightfully comment on her virtues and failings. From Kipling to Miss 

Mayo, writers have attempted descriptions of her as she is today. We Americans rave over our superior sanitation, but we may not know that some of our habits seem filthy to high caste Hindus. We are proud of our achievements but do not always remember that India made some of the earliest and most important contributions to human knowledge, and bids fair to add others. 

Benares was old when Athens was a village in Greece. Universities of learning existed in Taxila and 

Nilanda long before Socrates was born. India excelled, too, in being the most warlike, the wealthiest and most artistic nation in the world. Elaborate Sanscrit treatises are in existence on the training of saddle horses, on medicine, music, astronomy, painting, dancing, love. These indicate that much was known that we have neglected or forgotten. The most modern types of miniatures executed hundreds of years ago may be seen. Some of these picture Mogul ladies playing polo in the sixteenth century. Chinese travelers of that day and age chronicled the grandeur of the Grand Moguls whose marble splendor still stands at Agra and Delhi. 

It is but human nature to overlook past achievements (56)

when faced with present-day Indian conditions of squalor and poverty. 

That the tourist is appalled thereby is not surprising. In the cities, lepers rub elbows with others unrebuked, as long as they are caste men. 

Children go naked until their fifth year, even though married. Men, too, often wear only a loin-cloth. When dressed, a man usually wraps himself in four yards of cotton cloth, and a woman may be completely dressed in ten yards of cotton cloth with not a button on it. That cloth will be so wrapped that it forms at once, a skirt, a waist, and even a shawl over her head.

As miserable as is the life of the average male native, with his earnings of less than seven cents per day; always hungry, diseased, without plumbing, lights, underwear, towels, dishes, chairs or any furniture but a rude bed in a mud hut, he nevertheless, lives the life of a king compared to the Indian woman. From early childhood to the grave she envies him. To be born a woman is indeed a calamity. As soon as she is old enough, she learns that a woman is something inferior and contemptible. Not only is she the slave of her husband when she is half-grown, but before she leaves her home she is the uncomplaining door-mat of the males of her own family. You see everywhere little girls of the poorer classes staggering around carrying their older brothers, who are not cripples but just superior beings by nature of sex; which gives them the right to eat first and have the best of everything. After marriage, she ends her existence in purdah; in an inside room devoid of light and decent ventilation-which causes the death (57)

of five times as many women as men by tuberculosis. Her only hope is in the hereafter of another world. 

The Indians are children. They have to be taken care of for the simple reason that they cannot take care of themselves. They lack good judgment, executive ability, administrative power. Perhaps the explanation lies in the wretched climate or the mold of their character is determined by the enormous number of child marriages. One can hardly expect a mother of twelve or thirteen to have the experience and wisdom necessary for the proper training of her child. Most Indians lie. 

They may be loyal enough to their superior officers or their masters, but as for being loyal to their Government, either Indian or English, or to a principle, they do not know what it means. Put an Indian in a position where he has a chance to enrich himself at the expense of the Government and he will invariably do so. Official dishonesty among Indians is practically universal. Indians know this and do not trust one another. They always prefer an English judge to an Indian because they are sure that the former will deal out impartial justice. In the other case, they, too, often get only what they can afford to pay for. After two hundred years of western training in such matters, they seemed to have learned little. Scratch an Indian and he is still an Indian under the skin, even when he has learned to wear European clothes, to speak English with scarcely any accent and to handle a knife and fork without being self-conscious.

It might seem that the Hindu with an overwhelming majority of seventy percent of the entire population, should be in a position to dictate to the rest of India. 

However, one must remember that unlike the Moslems, who are united with a common form of worship, Hindus are hopelessly split up on caste lines, constituting irreconcilable social antipathies. India can never govern herself under such circumstances. Left to themselves, the Afgans would overrun the northwest, the Gurkhas from Nepal would take Bengal, the Moslems and Hindus would be at each other's throats, and Germany, Japan, and Russia would be in the morning after the British stepped out. All the good work of getting India on its feet would have been broken down, and the country would be given over to looting and all that goes with it. 

In spite of ever apparent poverty, India contains vast riches. In the five years prior to 1919, that country absorbed forty percent of the world's gold production and in that year is estimated to have absorbed one-third of the entire silver output. Over five billion dollars worth of precious metals is lying idle in that starving country. Most of it is held by the princes whose wealth is well known, but a considerable amount reaches the lowly. With his usual stupidity, the Indian buries his hoard in the dirt floor of his hut or beats it into bangles to adorn his wife where it does him no good. India cries (59)

out for financial help yet it has not the sense to invest its own wealth.

The world insists that India wake up and clean up, that it stop having more children than can be fed, that disease be reduced by sanitation, not by religious ceremonies, and that women be treated more like human beings. In 1920, there were twenty-seven million widows, and it was estimated that of these twelve thousand were less than five years of age. The Hindu resents our comments and our foreign missinary efforts and probably gets quite a kick out of sending us their Swamies to preach and teach American women how to live a "higher life." American women pay real money to hear these educators from a country ninety-two percent illiterate. The Swamie's turban is looked upon in awe. This reminds us of a conversation we once had with Tagore after one of his American tours. His remarks on that occasion were to the effect that the "picking was easy" at minimum effort on his part. 

India's "untouchables" probably number sixty million. They have no rights of any kind and in some parts of the country, cannot even walk on the public highway, lest their shadows defile the high caste Hindu. They have now awakened to some extent, possibly due to the efforts of Ghandi and there are indications that they may ultimately, through education, demand rights denied to them for twenty-five hundred years. They are not criminals, not insane, nor more diseased than some castes, but they have endured as much or more than slaves.

earth. There are probably forty-five dialects and about two hundred different varieties of religions. Some of them are really funny and some of the ritualistic practices of Mohammedans, Hindus, or Jains, would, if practiced in our own country, send one to jail, if not to the electric chair.

Born, as they believe, to foreordained lives of misery, without rights usually accorded to an animal, these millions of people seem to be awakening to the realization that they need not continue to put up with the degradation meekly endured by their ancestors for centuries. 

The caste system, high or low, holds you to the caste you were born to, and on the lowest rung of the social ladder are the untouchables who have no caste. They cannot stand on the same pavement as a caste man. They must not allow their shadow to fall on him, and should it fall on his food it must be destroyed and the caste man must go through an elaborate purification ceremony. 

Untouchables may not enter temples or receive any religious rites. They live the life lower than dogs. They believe that they will continue endless reincarnations until every other Hindu has gone to Heaven. A passage in the Hindu scriptures reads: "Whosoever is guilty of the murder of a Brahmin, will, at his death, take the form of those insects which feed on filth. He will be reborn long after as an untouchable." A Brahmin must spit after mentioning the name of an untouchable.

The British Government has tried to help these people and has found many of them fully as intelligent as high caste Hindus. In Madras, the Government (61)

employs them as couriers and has found them trustworthy in transporting large amounts of silver. They have the advantage of being safe against robbery, in so far as Hindus are concerned, for the Hindu must not touch them. In Bombay they are often used as servants, and here again, no Hindu will steal from a house where there is an untouchable.

In the great war and during the fighting in the Mesopotanian section around Kut, there were several companies of these people. At the taking of Kut, men were needed to row troops across the rivers under fire of the Turks. The fighting men lay in the bottom of the boats and the others rowed, and these untouchables volunteered for that duty and many died like soldiers. But they carried the needed troops safely to the other bank of the river.

The most outstanding recognition of this low class, happened at Delhi during the visit of the Prince of Wales (now Duke of Windsor) to India in 1921. Previously he had greeted a crowd of them at Kyber Pass. At Delhi twenty-five thousand gathered to acclaim him, and he shocked all Hindustan by saluting them. There are now many indications that a revolt of the untouchables is coming. Already riots have occurred and high caste 

Hindus have been touched. Education will hasten the revolution against caste, which is certain to come sooner or later.

One's sympathy goes out also to another class of human beings, who, however, do not live the life of an untouchable. I refer to the Tibetan women who act as (62)

porters to Indian hill stations. Think of calmly getting out of your train and climbing into a comfortable sedan chair in which four coolies will carry you six or seven thousand feet up in the mountains. You turn over to women porters your baggage consisting of anything from a wardrobe trunk down to baby carriages, knowing that they will carry it on their backs to destination and a cost that one is ashamed to admit. The leader of these porters may be an elderly and much wrinkled dame, certainly a grandmother, and the dozen other females under her command, range down to children of fourteen or less and they all carry loads that would break us down the first ten minutes of the journey.

Women are the workers in the Himalaya sections and perform the duties of pack horses to railway stations and hill stations. Bent double, they are about as common a sight on the hill trails as taxis in our city streets. They carry coal, act as delivery carts, handle building materials, carry mortar up ladders, and even break rock. 

They are of the Mongul type, slant-eyed, flat-cheeked, sturdy, with black hair woven in plaits. They can be classified as Nepalese, Sinkianese, Bhutanise and Tibetans. They are intelligent, have a sense of humor and nothing amuses them so much as the look of amazement on your face when first being surrounded by a dozen or more, all clamoring to carry your luggage. Their men are content to lead a life of ease and let the women work. One occupation they might accept is to act as porters on some mountain-climbing expedition for it (63)

means good pay, good food for months, and few privations.

The reader may, naturally, wonder how such conditions as related, can continue and may feel that the British are responsible. One should bear in mind that they can only rule justly through tolerance and noninterference with religious and old age customs. They have stopped "suttee," the practice under which the Hindu widow was forced to allow herself to be burned alive on the funeral pyre of her dead husband. They have outlawed child marriage, which although it still exists, protects the child-wife until maturity. We do not believe that any other race would be so patient and uncomplaining. Officials, who are often members of the second or third generation of people who have helped to rule India, consider themselves but a small cog in the great empire wheel and they cheerfully endure famine, pestilence, illness and living conditions that few, if any, Americans would stand for under our own flag. 

We doubt whether any country has ever sent a finer set of men than England sent to India in the old days, and there is still plenty of evidence that the traditions of the Indian civil service is not dead. It is crowded with men who have given the better part of their lives to the difficult task of administration among an alien people. If others have grown rich out of India, such as the manufacturers of Lancashire and Manchester, it is not the civil servants or the army officers. After two or three decades of service, they retire, broken in health, to live out their days as best they can on a modest (64)

pension, under conditions in England for which life in India has unfitted them. All they have is the satisfaction of knowing that they did their work faithfully and well.

History shows that whenever England has taken over a country, she has brought order out of chaos. Her history is not without stain and there has been chapters that are now regretted by her people, but on the whole the British Empire has been a force for good. Her hand has been heavy sometimes but she has gone into lands drenched with the agonies of bad government, as in the case of India, and has left an heritage of justice and decency. She has turned waste places into productive lands and has created orderly cities. In India, she is certainly entitled to expect that public opinion would be a force toward the correction of such conditions as we have referred to. Only the bare necessities can be taken care of by the government under the most carefully budgeted revenues collected. 

England is today fighting the fight of the Englishspeaking people. She is growing a little worn, but we in 

America will soon have the power to supplant her, if indeed we do not already have that power. The situation calls for some kind of joint government, at least, of all of the English-speaking races, together possibly, with such others as have stable governments of their own. 

Reverting back again to the Hindu and his caste: it is nothing short of remarkable to what lengths he will go to abide by the ritualistic customs foreordained for him to follow. He worships in a dark, gloomy, sometimes (65)

very filthy temple, often ornamented by sculpture that borders on the obscene, with corriders from which shadowy gods and beasts look down on either side. The only illumination is from torches and it is no wonder that the walls are covered with the smudge of years. 

One wonders how such surroundings appeal to men of education, who have through their contact with European and American officials, known the comforts of clean, modern homes and furnishings. Many such men have and are successfully filling Government positions of responsibility and trust; yet there always comes the time in their lives when they retire from the world after having turned all of their worldly effects over to a son or successor relative, after which they revert to meager native dress and with no baggage other than the usual bowl, make long pilgrimages, usually on foot from shrine to shrine over long periods of time and often until death calls.

In the past two years we have received the usual farewell letters from old high caste Hindu friends, successful engineers and officials with years of proud records behind them and years of similar usefulness ahead, but in each case the die had been cast and nothing could alter their decision. Such letters pull the heart-strings and cannot be answered in such a way as to offend. They are but follwing the dictates requiring high caste Brahmins to so end their lives. A pitiful end for fine fellows whom we learned to love. Srinivasa Ainegar, Srinivasa Ainegar, Rungaswamy 

Ainegar, Khrisnaswamy Moudaliar, all queer names, but names of three of Nature's noblemen. May their (66)

final reward be all they may desire. Disillusionment for them is not possible.

One of the three named above, came to us years ago and told us of his great desire to educate two younger brothers who aspired to a law course. He admitted that he wanted very much to send them to England for such an education and that he could finance them to that end, but he added that, being Brahmins, his brothers could not leave India without losing their caste. Further explanation disclosed that sending the brothers away meant that from that day, they would be cut off from all communication with parents, brother or friends forever, unless they later elected to come back and accept the punishment of the priests which might mean years of pilgrimage in loin-cloths and with beggar bowls until such time as they had given up their hopes in life and become "native." In addition the family would be compelled to pay large sums into the temple and even that might not restore the boys to fellowship with members of the caste. 

After many long consultations, we assisted in the arrangements for financing the brothers who said their last good-bye and went to England where both entered Oxford University For years we were the only avenue of communication between the brother in India and the brothers in England and then we never dared to forward actual letters to either but passed on communication in our own hand-writing without actually mentioning names to either. Those young men came back to India where they located in a city in the northwest, hundreds of miles from their own home. They have been success- (67)

ful beyond their hopes and probably have long since learned that the loss of caste did not prove the curse that was prophesied They have never seen or heard directly from their own family or people. 

A member of a caste may deny his gods and indulge in vice and still remain in good standing, but let him so much as accept a drink of water from a low caste man and he will be tried before a tribunal. If he refuses to accept his punishment, his own family will disown him and he automatically becomes an outcast. That is Hindu law. Your religion and your lot are decided at birth and your caste determines your occupation-as father, so is son.

One of the three officials mentioned in the foregoing, was a widower and one day he came to us saying, “Sahib, I am going to marry again." "To whom"? was asked. 

He replied, "You will laugh when I tell you I do not know. You naturally expected me to be my own master in matters of this kind, but as strange as it may seem to you, my wife is picked by a horoscope and at the present time, eleven girls selected by my family and relatives are awaiting the results of the checking of their respective horoscopes and I will, of necessity, marry the one selected, regardless of whether or not she can bring me a dowry."

It so happened that the girl so selected did indeed. have the smallest dowry, but she was a wonderful wife in the years that followed. (68)

Mohammedans and India's Hoarded Wealth

Confucius, the great Chinese philosopher, was a teacher of ethics, rather than the founder of a religion. 

He claimed no miracles, performed none, and was conservative in his teachings. He said, "Do not do unto others what you would not have them do unto you,” five hundred years before it was said: "Whatsoever ye would that men would do to you, do ye, even so to them." 

This command of Confucius was negative; the 

Christian's command positive. Mohammed, appearing some five centuries after the birth of Christ, was the man whom two hundred million human beings still believe was the prophet of God and who still take the work of the Koran as the only authentic word of God. Mohammed wrote, or received, additions to his Koran whenever he found it necessary.

He apparently was an intelligent, powerful man, and was always fighting the "infidel." He nearly conquered all Europe and a slight victory where he met defeat, might have seen the Koran, not the Christian's 

Bible, read from pulpits. Mohammedans believe God wrote their Koran. Certainly it is written for all followers to suit the conditions that they live under. For the Eskimos it pictures a hell of ice because they fear cold. For the Arab in the desert, a hell of heat, devoid of water, etc. It is all things to all men with Heaven pictured as a Paradise to which believers go and where there are beautiful women and rivers of clarified honey, (69)

etc., etc. 

The "beauteous damsels" are to be provided with complexions like rubies and pearls, and fine black eyes. They are to be agreeable and kept in pavilions from public view. Whatever we may think of the Koran, we may be glad that it still wields an influence for good even though it has evil in it. 

Mohammedans are without caste and represent a united front against the Hindus. Although numerically half the strength of the Hindus in India, they have, and probably could again, reduce Hindus, Parsees and others to serfdom, if the British stepped out and gave India over to Indians. History would merely repeat itself. 

From the beginning of her rule, Britain laid it down that there must be full liberty of religious observance. Within that rule it has been England's policy to interfere as little as possible with the customs and institutions of the people. Flagrant violations like suttee has been forbidden. Much else has been accomplished and more will be, but such reforms as are necessary can only be brought about by reformed public opinion. From the mutiny of 1857, there has been no acute crisis in India. Beyond the usual riots, due usually to trouble between Mohammedans and Hindus, the people have enjoyed a long rest from the ravages of invaders and internal strife. 

Formerly Mohammedans pillaged and looted "the infidel." Today, under British guidance, Mohammedan rulers of native states, are content to rule fairly. Take the Nizam of Hyderbad, for instance, who rules over a country as large as Italy, and over fifteen million people and who may rightly claim the title of the world's richest (70)

man.

A Mohammedan, he rules over India's richest state, and has power of life and death over subjects of all religions who are treated alike. He has his own coinage, his own postal system, and railway, and maintains his own army of twenty thousand. His own estates comprise one-tenth of the entire state's total area. He is reported to have a treasure of five hundred million dollars in gold and it took years for three English jewelers to catalog his vast amount of stone gems. "He could bury six men in his diamonds," declared one of his officials.

Every year this treasure is increased by an additional four million dollars, part of which comes from taxes and part made up by ceremonial offerings received annually from noblemen, officials, and others whom he receives in audience at such times. You may ask why this modern Croesus does not invest his hoard rather than to let it pile up. The answer is that his religion forbids followers to receive interest from investments. His ancestors probably lived on a far more lavish scale and squandered wealth on favorites and establishments that do not fit in with the simple life of this modern ruler, a form of life that few can credit. He is almost miserly in his mode of living, being keenly interested in the welfare of his people and the state. 

Two other rulers, ranking second and third in wealth, are the Gaekwar of Baroda and the Maharajah of Mysore. Both are Hindus and are progressive and rule justly and well. The Baroda ruler is said to have the largest collection of pearls in the world, his pearl (71)

scarf alone is valued at three million, three hundred thousand dollars. One of his hobbies is to own a few cannon of solid gold, mounted on wheels of solid silver and drawn by oxen covered by jeweled and decorated robes that are worth more than any American would squander on a four-footed beast, not even Seabiscuit. When the 

Gaekwar journeys to England as he has done a few times, he charters a ship, loads it with retainers, food and water, so that wherever he is, he can have what he would use if at home, served by Indian servants. By such means he is able to keep in good standing with his caste. He has always taken an interest in the United States and gave us much to be grateful for in contracts. 

The Maharajah of Mysore was tutored from childhood by the British and in consequence was a regular fellow. One of India's most progressive rulers, he introduced the first hydro-electric power plant, the first modern water-works and filtration plant, and today cast iron, cast pipe, cement and many other products are made in his State. The Kolar gold fields, which have been in constant production well over a half century, pay him a royalty of forty percent and fabulous values in gold have been mined in that field. We, who have enjoyed his favor, have cause to treasure his friendship. His 

State alone kept us continually employed for over a period of many years.

Buddhists in Burmah and Cambodia

We wonder what Hitler or Stalin would do if they ever conquered India or even Burmah. Wealth beyond even their dreams of avarice, awaits them and their hordes, that wealth being only guarded today by religious scruples of India's people, who possess no arms nor implements of warfare. The Hindu has his temples, in some of which are gods bedecked with jewels that are always under the watchful eye of priests. Some of these temples, particularly in the northwest section of the country and Kashmir, have roofs made of gold plates. 

Buddhists temples, particularly in Burmah and Cambodia (in Indo-China) enshrine Buddhas that are often of alabaster with eyes of diamonds or emeralds. Others of sometimes hugh proportions, are covered with gold leaf to such thickness as to often distort the features greatly from their original shape. Year by year devotees (73)

What Buddhism Did for Ceylon 

Ceylon, too, had its Golden Age, its Buddhist temples and structures, remains of which can still be seen. 

Today "golden pillars," gem-studded thrones and pearlfringed canopies sound like Oriental exaggeration. But we may assume that these people reaped the first harvest of earth's treasure in gold and other precious metals which they probably had for the picking up from the river sands washed down from the mountains. These rivers seemed to have been full of it. The same may be said of wonderful sapphires of all colors and famous the world over, the supply of which has long been exhausted, as are the coast pearl fisheries that once yielded treasure by the bucketsful.

It is difficult to realize the conditions that existed in Ceylon two thousand years ago which was two centuries after the introduction of Buddhism from India.

It is enough to say that the introduction of that religion into that island, influenced to a great extent the development of that country and probably at that time, Ceylon was the chief emporium of the eastern world. The merchant fleets of India, China, Persia and Arabia entered its ports with silks, horses, sandlewood, slaves, etc., and there they traded with the Singhalese whose gems were coveted by rulers of every country. 

The whole island was probably fringed by stately palms as it is today and there was very likely an entire absence of the dense jungle that now covers the major part of the land. Rivers were then diverted to every (79)

natural depression that might serve as a storage reservoir, and the escape of water so stored was controlled by dams and sluices. Stone and masonry conduits conveyed the water for cultivation and provided for never-failing crops. Records indicate that there were around five thousand such reservoirs, remains of which can be seen today. Such engineering skill made their land the support of millions. How this was possible two thousand years ago may be understood by the perusal of records on thin plates of gold and silver and on the indestructible talpot-leaf left to posterity. 

Undoubtedly, forced labor made possible much that was accomplished. There was little resentment against the methods employed, for the Buddhists held their lands conditional and devoted a large share of their efforts to the king's service. Such service called for construction of irrigation works as well as temples and shrines. 

Compulsory as it was, this method was certainly productive of wonderful results and apparent general contentment. Today under a beneficent British rule, it would be next to impossible to banish the jungle and restore the major part of the land to its former usefulness. 

In the center of a stretch of cultivated land exceeding ten thousand square miles, and at a period when our own America was covered by virgin forests, was located the great city of Anuradhapaure. History tells us that here lofty palaces and monuments towered above other buildings which covered the land space of two hundred fifty-six square miles. Twenty square miles were allotted to the royal palaces, retainers and shrines and temples and (80)

other sections had each street assigned to a particular class of artisans where potters, blacksmiths, stone-cutters, carpenters, tanners, ivory-workers, etc., might be found. 

Another city, Polonnaruwa, grew to be even mightier, and history records that its royal palaces, lakes, gardens, etc., eclipsed the older city in splendor as it did in extent. The decay of these two great cities was apparent from the fourth century A.D., and war fostered by rival factions, together with the continual struggle with the Tamils of South India, hastened their downfall and both, ultimately, were abandoned to the spoilation of Indian invaders. Tamil hordes over-ran the island, destroyed the system of irrigation works and murdered people whose religion forbade the taking of life. While the records are not entirely clear, it is evident that the deliberate destruction of reservoirs and water courses brought death by starvation to many, and that famine and disease accounted for the total disappearance of those who survived the invasions referred to. 

Malaria may have been a factor in their disintegration.

Today there are hundreds of structures in Ceylon built by those early Buddhists, structures that have no particular beauty and which include no embellishment of gold or precious stones. For the greater part, they are great bell-shaped edifices erected over some relic of Buddha or his Disciples. They are always solid and were originally surmounted with a Thi, which is an umbrella-like ornament. Some of these dagobas are of huge proportions; the great Ruanweli as seen today, is a conical hill partially cleared of the trees that have (81)

grown on its sides for centuries. It was erected approximately in 200 B.C., and its original height was two hundred seventy feet with a base circumferance of nearly one thousand feet. Its foundation must have been of unusual design, for today there is no evidence of settlement to be seen. It is recorded that the foundation was started with a layer of stones beaten with pounders and trod by elephants whose feet were shod with leather shoes. 

On this stone layer was spread clay and upon this, brick. 

Over the brick was a layer of shell lime and burned brick plaster, then a layer of stone slabs and on that a network of iron. Over this was a plate of brass embedded in more plaster and over this, a plate of silver seven inches thick. 

We do not ask our readers to believe this, but we are repeating the historical facts as related and recorded. 

The very natural thought is why has not someone recovered the silver if it is there. Invaders have tried, and the damage done to this particular structure indicates that such efforts proved futile for certainly no excavation of any great depth was made. As for the British, nothing could ever persuade them to defile such a religious shrine under any conditions and if there is silver in that particular foundation, it will be there as long as England rules. 

England did ask, and secured permission, to cut a small tunnel into the side of another similar dagoba to study the construction; with the understanding that all material would be carefully replaced. Specimens of copper and iron nails and a bone dice were found during that work and it was noted that well-burned brick of huge dimensions were used in building the structure. (82)

The Ruanweli is but one of many and the greater Abhayagiriya dagoba was originally four hundred and five feet high with a base diameter of three hundred sixty feet. It stands on a raised platform whose square sides each measure six hundred feet, and under this platform is the base that covers about eight acres. The British have estimated further that with modern equipment, it would take seven years to construct such a mass at an expenditure of five to seven million dollars. Such are the Buddhists' shrines of Ceylon and to see one is to see them all. They are hardly outdone by the pyramids of Egypt.

The two great cities referred to and which are of unrivaled wealth and power, are today but heaps of ruins almost hidden by the jungle. Some attempts have been made at restoration work, but the task is too gigantic and costly to be undertaken, even if justified. The cobra and the monkey now rule and the visitor may well watch his steps if he is so bold as to visit such scenes of early civilization. 

One of the inexplicable happenings in our Indian experiences was the arrival there of an American missionary who probably had missed his calling. The event here recorded occurred, probably, thirty-five years ago and we are unable to recall the name of the missionary. (83)

He had been located in Japan and China and seems to have been a student of archæology and to have been proficient in deciphering inscriptions and writings in other languages than his own. He spun a tale of having, through old writings found somewhere in the eastern world, located definitely some authentic relics of Buddha. These, he stated, were buried in India and he went so far as to say that, when found, they would be in the floor of a Buddhist temple burned hundreds of years before and that the relics would be encased in a small crystal and silver casket.

He worried all and sundry with this tale, interviewed Government officials, when he could get to them, and very naturally he was cataloged as being an American "nut." He finally got the ear of a wealthy Parsee gentleman by the name of Tata, who being a good

Zoroastrian, could hardly be expected to be interested in finding parts of the anatomy of Buddha. On the contrary, he was so impressed with this unlikely story, that he agreed to finance the search that was ultimately made. 

To the amazement of Government officials and engineers, this missionary went to a place near the town of Patna and after several days of measurements that seemed to start from nowhere and which he would not explain, he set up stakes in a paddy field and said, "It is here." Permission having been obtained, excavation work was started and after weeks of removing earth by hand and at a depth of some forty feet, there was found the remains of an old wall and charred ashes that seemed to prove his statement that fire had destroyed a building (84)

there at some time or other. Ultimately, there was found a rough floor of hewn timbers that remained in some state of preservation. Under this floor was found a small silver-bound crystal case in which there were remnants of what appeared to be bones. Had that man been a 

British subject, he would probably have been knighted. 

As it was, he was made to turn over the find to the authorities who, after a considerable time, presented it to the Buddhists of Japan in a special ceremony. Our hero was accepted as being anything but a "nut" and gave several lectures and may have received some material remuneration but he faded out and we never heard of him again. We were in the excavation several times while the work was going on; saw the old timber floor therein, but we were not in the country when the casket was removed, but knew many officials who did see it taken out. 

The Great Moguls 

We have mentioned that in one of the structures described was found iron. That there were craftsmen of exceptional skill in the early history of Egypt and India is borne out by historical records and actual present-day evidences of such facts. A Greek historian writing of the invasion of Alexander the Great of India in 326 B.C., records that Indian chieftains of the northwest, presented the invader with twenty talents of steel. He also records that Alexander found Indian warriors better protected with metal shields and swords that were his own soldiers. (85)

Near the site of the City of Delhi in India, there were built six other cities of the same name over a period of at least ten centuries. Some were possibly the capital or chief city prior to the birth of Christ. These old 

Delhis are seen in the ruins scattered around the existing city in a radius of approximately forty miles. In one of these old ruins is the famous Delhi iron pillar. It is a forging sixteen inches in diameter and twenty-three feet, eight inches high with a decorated capital at the top. It is rust-proof and sufficiently soft to be easily scratched with a knife. From an inscription, the date of origin is given as about the fourth century A.D. It would be a huge forging if made today with modern facilities. A piece cut from the pillar was analyzed by the School of Mines and found to be pure malleable iron without alloy. 

At the site of two ruined temples in Kanarak in South India we have seen wrought-iron slabs formerly used for roof members that measured around twelve feet long, nine to ten inches wide and two to four inches thick. 

These were forgings and rust-proof and there is no authentic record of where they came from, or when. 

Certainly not since the British came to that country has there been facilities for making such forgings. At the great pagoda at Rangoon, previously described, is a bell that weighs forty-two and a half tons, and it is a single casting. The first whites, that came to India fortunate enough to have visited the courts of the Mogul emperors of that day, came back with reports of wealth and splendor that startled the outside world. Confirmations of such reports brought ambassadors from several of the great powers of that day and much competition for concessions and favors. India is too well-known to the average reader for us to quote much history. It might be enough to say that the Mogul rulers gave to the world monuments of beauty the like of which had never been seen before. The real show-places are Agra and Delhi. 

When Queen Elizabeth of England was signing death-warrants, and men of a so-called Christian civilization were burning witches, Akbar, a Mohammedan king, hardly more than a boy, sat on his father's throne at Delhi looking out over Hindustan. He saw how the conquests of his forebears had made foes of the Hindus. 

He heard the cries of the poor, ground down in the dust and stripped bare, and of the women carried off into slavery by the victor as customary in war. 

He promised an end to religious strife. He promised that 

Mohammedan and Hindu would be united under a just rule and women, as honored mothers of sons would be loyal to their kingdom wherein men could live in cities without fear, and in security on their own lands. (87)

Twenty-two miles from Agra, past famous Sikandra, the tomb of Akbar, where the Kohinoor diamond rested many years, a road lined with rows of ancient banyan trees stretches across the sunbaked plain to Fatehpur Sikri, the dream city of Akbar, empty and voiceless, standing today as when he left it. Some portions are unfinished with bits of carving and faced building stone scattered about as though the workmen had just laid down their tools. In many places their chisels might have left them but yesterday, so sharp are the angles of the carved surfaces.

Its preservation is due to the fact that for more than seventy years, Fatehpur Sikri and other Mogul structures of surpassing beauty at Delhi, Agra and Lahore, have been under the protection of the Archæological Department of the Indian Government. It is no longer possible for the native vandal to steal the inlays of semiprecious stones and priceless carvings, or to camp in the queen's boudoir, or cook their food on the marble floors. 

If a stone falls out the pieces are collected and replaced, or reproduced line for line. Fortunately, time has dealt gently with buildings in spite of neither wood, nor cement being used in their construction. Just why its royal builder ever created such a perfect city and suddenly deserted it at the height of its glory, will always be a mystery, as he, apparently, never gave any reason. 

Some lay this move to an inadequate water supply, but with unlimited wealth and resources at his command this theory does not seem to be correct. Others say its unusual construction shattered his ideals because it (88)

afforded him a clear insight into the nature of those about him whom he trusted and that, having built it to commemorate the birth of an heir who turned out such an utter failure and disappointment he could not bear the constant reminder, so abandoned it. Akbar so planned the city that he could personally inspect every portion of it without being seen. He would start at a given point and make his way through a maze of secret passages, whispering galleries, and watch-towers pierced with tiny holes. He could overlook his treasury and the record offices and see what the clerks were doing. 

When his ministers were in conference in the Private Hall of Audience, he could ascend narrow stairways hidden in the thickness of the walls and, through observation-slits set into bits of carving, overhear what they said. 

High up in a maze of ceiling galleries, he could stand and watch brilliant court assemblages in the public hall and one can easily imagine the nervous strain on his court when there was no knowing where His Majesty's eye or ear might be stationed at the moment.

There was the caravanseri with stables and cookhouses, where wayfarers of every caste and creed were sheltered and fed like honored guests. Iron rings for tethering elephants, camels and horses are still embedded along the edge of the outer wall. Advocates of all religions were welcomed at Fatehpur Sikri, where the emperor listened to their disputes and arguments with patient tolerance. Once he was courteous and plausible to the Christians to such a degree that they presented him with an image of the Virgin, but the next morning (89)

they saw Akbar, who was a Mohammedan, enter the chamber with his brow marked in Hindu fashion. 

His marriage with a Christian, a Hindu, and a Mohammedan princess for whom he built each a separate palace, including a temple for his Hindu queen, and the employment of a Portuguese artist to paint an angel for the Christian, led all three of the religious sects to claim him as a convert. 

In the great quadrangle opposite to the hall of public audience may be seen to this day the black and white marble squares set in the pavement and as perfect as when the emperor played chess in the cool of the evening. His pawns were sixteen beautifully dressed, bejeweled girls, who became the prize of the victor.

If you can get the old native watchman who walks at night through the empty halls and courtyards to talk in his own tongue, he will tell you that the soul of Akbar visits the "House of Dreams" where he slept. The watchman will take you into a small chamber sunk a few feet below the level of the ground floor and point to "Akar's bed." Many refuse to believe that that huge stone slab, supported at one end between massive columns, was ever a bed. A small square opening in the wall served the double purpose of letting in cool air and enabling the emperor to sweep with his eye the vast army encampment and the main city gate. High stepping-stones long since removed, led to it and after His Majesty had composed himself for slumber, water was let into the chamber level with the stones by a system that allowed it to flow in and out with the soft gurgle (90)

Shah Jehan and the Taj Mahal

Shah Jehan, the greatest builder of all, started his reign with a liberal use of money by which he secured the support of leading nobles and the army. He blinded a few troublesome relatives, sewed up a few in fresh pig-skins and put them out in the summer's sun to dry and die and in other ways he rid himself of people who might annoy him later, as was the custom of his predecessors. Inheriting from his father the greatest of the 

Mogul empires, he early showed ability to rule, and was on the throne from 1626 to 1656. While there is much to criticize in the life of this ruler, he was an engineer of exceptional ability and a lover such as the world has seldom known. He created the Taj Mahal, at Agra, the (92)

THE TAJ MAHAL AT AGRA

Dewan-i-Am the Diwan-i-Khas and the Jamna Musjid at Delhi, all monuments of unsurpassed beauty which were built or rebuilt during his reign. The first named was a tomb for his favorite wife; the second, his Hall of Private Audience; the third, his Hall of Public Audience and the last, a mosque that was of sufficient size to permit ten thousand separate prayers to ascend to Allah at the same time. Every building was a work of art and sufficiently massive to survive the ravages of climate, if not the invaders who sacked some of them. 

Chapters could be written about the Taj alone, but who has not at some time read descriptions of this masterpiece. It was the dream of a cruel monarch, the design of which originated in his brain. Through the assistance of craftsmen he also created a six-foot model built to scale from which the existing building was created. Perfect in every detail, massive enough to stand the ravages of time, white as the driven snow and as perfect in detail as a watch, the Taj impresses one with a feeling of purity. A famous engineer once said, "It is not architecture in the accepted sense, but something better, higher, finer; maybe poetry in craftsmanship, music in marble, an inscribed dream of hitherto unattained future caught and rendered solid but hardly real. Certainly the architect, who would be so vain as to visit it with the idea of seeking information in connection with his own work, would come away disappointed, or remain to enjoy its delights." We once asked a visitor who was seated with us in the garden looking at the Taj, what he thought of it, and his reply was, "Well, you know I think (93)

I could smash it with a well-thrown brick." That was his way of expressing how fragile it looked from where we were seated.

One could go on and on describing other buildings, tombs, palaces, and even masonry fort walls. I'timadud-daulah, tomb of the father of the Lady of the Taj, a birdcage-shaped marble structure, carved so fragily as to be easily damaged by a thrown brick, Agra Fort, Fatepur Sikri, Kutub Minar-all are worthy of a visit, and many return visits. If Shah Jehan could again walk through the bazar streets of Delhi, he might find little change there for the habits and customs of the people remain much the same as in his day. The contrast between wealth and poverty is always apparent and there is still little to bridge the gap between palace and hovel. 

Indians build well for others, build everything except houses for themselves to live in. Too often we hear the cry of tyranny of the existing British rule; also of the vast amounts in revenue taken from the country-statements that are both false and absurd. We, who have lived there, realize the patient effort of Government officials against odds that would break the spirits of the average American. It is enough to say that the Indian people, regardless of creed or religion, language or customs, never before enjoyed such liberty and prosperity as today under British rule, Gandhi's complaints to the contrary, notwithstanding.

With the fall of the last Mogul ruler India was taken over by the British, French, and Portuguese, and for many years they fought for supremacy and it has (94)

Ghandi and Religious Riots 

We mentioned the name of Ghandi. What a false alarm and headache he has been to the British. The gentle and mild-mannered little man that we knew and played tennis with in Madras forty years ago, is the same (95)

Mahatma Ghandi that is now such a thorn in the side of the British, and doubtless will so remain until he leaves this world naturally. The British are smart enough to see to it that his desire to die the death of a martyr is not accorded him. That title "Mahatma" makes us smile, for it means a saintly man who has renounced the world and is no longer interested in affairs thereof. 

Educated in both America and England, Ghandi went to South Africa and after a hectic career there, he was practically deported and returned to India a much disappointed man.

Only three classes may travel extensively in India, the others stay at home or lose their caste. Brahmins, Commissioners, and Mahatmas may travel, and Ghandi wanting to travel, and being none of these, he adopted the garb and manner of "Mahatma" but did not give up his cotton mill interests nor his politics. Posing thus as a holy man, able in law, he is today one of the cleverest politicians in the world. Anyone, who could go to London as he did, and sit bare-legged and barefoot at a round table conference as a guest of the British Crown with some of England's ablest statesmen, thus defying customs and conventions and get away with it, is a clever politician.

However, the halo of sanctity popularly ascribed to Ghandi was well tarnished at that conference for he returned home discredited. He went to that meeting claiming that he represented three hundred and fifty million people who would no longer stand being ruled by two hundred thousand whites. But he came back to (96)

find that his popularity had shrunk and that the illiterate masses the bulk of whom were victims of money-lenders who collect as much as thirty-seven percent interest which has always been compounded and makes slaves of them for their natural life and of their children after them-knew that he had accomplished nothing toward improving their condition in life. It is not surprising that they turned on him. We never heard of Ghandi preaching non-cooperation and refusal to pay taxes on industrial property either direct or through his family. 

If he is as poor as his way of life would indicate, then we are a Maharajah. The British have his number and while they may appear to use appeasement methods with him, they doubtless feel that to make much ado about nothing costs little and satisfies a few reactionaries, who otherwise might make an unnecessary noise.

Water Supplies in India 

In India it is not so much a choice of water supply, but rather is there water at all and how much, regardless of quality. The rivers are the repository for bodies, human and animal, and too often, pollution is further increased by sewage disposal. Some of the rivers are five miles wide at times, and have a tendency to roam around and depart from their channels. As an example, Clive fought the battle of Plassey with his guns posted on the bank of a river that is now probably fifty miles away from that battleground. Bridges, that have been (97)

constructed at great cost over streams, have been deserted by the river which went wandering elsewhere. 

When you build a water works that pumps its water from a stream, you, at least, may run the hazard of having your suction well cut off by a sand-bar. And then you will have to cut a canal through it and post men at the stream inlet (in low water) to ward off with poles the dead bodies that might otherwise find their way into your suction well. That has happened all too often. 

It goes without saying that under present-day practice, filters are a necessary adjunct to the plant. If you are not so fortunate as to have a stream to depend on, your supply is probably impounded in an earthen tank built centuries before when the town or village used the water all those centuries for domestic supply, washed their dead therein, their clothes also, and made a swimming pool of it. Thus you can better understand why as much as ten percent of the population annually left this vale of tears by the cholera route.

The water in these tanks is caught in the monsoon period. Under normal conditions when at a low stage prior to the rainy season it is covered with a green scum and would hardly be acceptable under the requirements laid down by the American Water Works Association standards. At one time in our life we were able to run simple bacteriological tests and our ability in that respect, was recognized by the Indian Government, and as a consequence, we usually were informed where cholera was rampant or likely to break out. Such advance information generally influenced our visit to the place (98)

indicated and we fear that a true statement of conditions as seen over a period of years, would definitely establish in the reader's mind that Baron Munchausen still lived. 

It will be enough to say that many times we have seen hundreds of bodies lying about being devoured by vultures, wild dogs by day, and jackals by night. Anyone, who has heard a pack of jackals give tongue at night, will realize what can happen to the back hair on your head. Battlefields cannot look much worse and making a visit to such places six months later, you will probably only see skulls, the rest of the bones having disappeared. 

To clean up such a condition, our usual procedure was to make the attempt to chlorinate what remained of the water supply, build a barbed wire fence around it and post guards to prevent abuses such as have been previously mentioned. Do not think that is easy, for more than once have we had to be guarded while making such drastic changes in the lives of people to whom sanitation means nothing. Then a small or large water works is built with funds supplied usually by the Government and a rate of consumption is established which is based on tank capacity and the necessity of making the supply last out the dry season-which is usually six months. Many such plants are built on a per capita basis of as low as five gallons per day. In some cases the pumps are operated only an hour in the morning and again in the late afternoon and all hydrants are located in the street where they can be seen and fixtures are usually of the self-closing type. Wasting of water under such conditions becomes a punishable offense. In only (99)

the larger cities is water piped into homes, and that is discouraged by both Hindus and Mohammedans who keep their women folk in "purdah" (behind curtains) and will tolerate no inspections from water department employees.

Our experiences include opening of plants under circumstances that strain the credulity of anyone not acquainted with conditions and religious prejudices of such people as make up the population of India. We have seen plants completed, water turned on and not used, simply because priests exhort their followers to stick to the ways of their forefathers and accept no water that comes to them through pipes. We recall a few cases where Englishmen for miles around, a handful at best, are asked to come in and walk around the village and publicly drink from the hydrants provided. A few demonstrations like this and some bolder natives will follow suit, and the battle is won. Not, however, until it is definitely established that certain specified hydrants are for Mohammedans, others for high caste Hindus and others for lower castes. One of the worst and most fatal riots that we ever saw was caused by a Hindu going to the wrong hydrant. All the same water but that made no difference.

It is so in prisons and in army camps. Men have been known to refuse to accept water and would have died from thirst had their religious demands not been met. When you consider a caste man will be left to drown rather than have him saved by a low caste man who might have rescued him, you can better understand (100)

Death Rate in India and Causes 

A census was taken during our residence in India and one can easily appreciate the difficulties of such an undertaking. It brought out some surprising facts but undoubtedly failed to account for millions of women, hid away in zenanas (harems) where women are completely concealed from the prying eye of the censustaker. The count was disappointing and showed that the deaths from the influenza epidemic a short time previous, amounted to around six million. The scourge (103)

claimed as many victims as did bubonic plague in twenty-two years. Infantile mortality is always high, averaging around one of every four born.

Some facts brought out as to overcrowding came to light as a result of that census. In one room twenty by fifteen feet, thirty-five Chinamen were found. At another time, fifty men were found to be making their home on bamboo poles stretched across the rafters of a stable in which horses were housed. In the slum districts of 

Calcutta, several houses had an average population of over five hundred. In some cases even the roofs were occupied. The enumerator found ninety men sleeping on one roof alone, and they were among the aristocrats of their class. The poor have to be content with a doorway.

Wild animals and snakes claim their share of victims and records of known cases show that around twenty-five thousand deaths annually may be attributed to snake-bite alone. There are probably countless cases that are never reported to the proper authorities.

Seventy thousand deaths by wild animals is not an excessive estimate. Leopards and tigers are chiefly responible, although elephants, hyenas, bears, wild pigs, and alligators take their quota. In a single year, Government records show that nineteen thousand, four hundred and seventy-six wild animals were destroyed, among which were one thousand, two hundred and ninety-five tigers, six thousand and thirty-seven leopards, two thousand, seven hundred and eighty-four bears, and two thousand, one hundred forty-seven wolves for which (104)

rewards were paid. When you consider that the native is prohibited from possessing arms and that his religious prejudice against taking life might keep him from taking part in such slaughter, the results are largely due to efforts of the whites, of which there are too few to make a business of hunting.

The Tourist in India and What He Faces 

In India one does not drink cow or goat milk in the raw state and live to a ripe old age. Often one meets the milkman coming to town with his two brass or clay pots fastened to a bamboo pole balanced on his shoulder. 

These pots are filled with milk and to keep it from splashing out as he runs along, he puts a tuft of grass or straw in the neck of each container. Arriving near town, he takes stock of his losses and removing the straw, proceeds to fill the pots to the required level by adding water from some convenient wayside source. It matters not what kind of water or how he puts it into the pot. 

Once we caught such a man actually soaking his pugrie (head covering) in water and wringing it out into the milk. Canned milk is the answer if you wish to live long.

One unfamiliar with conditions fails to understand the dangers that beset him. Cholera, plague, smallpox, and fevers are with you always. There may be a case of smallpox in the hotel where you stop, but the hotel is not closed for business. A plague patient may have died on the same mattress that you are asked to sleep on and there may yet be plague fleas to pass that dread disease (105)

on to you. The water being served you or even the fruit that you eat may be cholera tainted. No one living in the country ever eats fruit of which the skin has been broken. Water must be boiled if taken from a source not subject to reliable filtration. Vaccination against smallpox is a real necessity. You see smallpox stricken people often and frequently rub shoulders with them in railway stations and elsewhere.

The writer recalls such experiences as that of two couples who came to Calcutta during a severe epidemic of smallpox. Seven guests had died in one of the leading hotels just across from the Government House and your author, together with other American residents, arranged that one of us would meet the American tourists and warn them of the dangers and have them vaccinated. It was our lot to meet Mr. and Mrs. Darby and their traveling companions, Mr. and Mrs. Mellor the evening they arrived at the hotel. We explained our interest and suggested that they go with us and be vaccinated inasmuch as neither had been vaccinated for years, but they stated they did not believe in vaccination and would not be interested in going through with it. Later on these same people continued on their tour by way of Delhi and Lahore, and one of the husbands was removed from the train at Delhi and the other at Lahore, both showing all the symptoms of smallpox.

Through our efforts, arrangements were made with Government medical officials to move the two couples into a bungalow, have the wives vaccinated and give them every attention and medical care. Both men (106)

ultimately recovered. We saw Sam Darby thirty years later in Houston, Texas, where he was still trying to find some method of eradicating some of the scars on his face. 

They were just lucky. It fell to our lot to supervise the burning of bodies of five American tourists in a period over several years, one of whom was a young man killed in Mysore State by a tiger that he was warned not to hunt. Tiger hunting is not what it is cracked up to be.

The tiger too often turns hunter and is dangerous even in his death struggles. 

Touring calls for the purchase of bedding, towels, blankets, pillows, mosquito net, soap, etc., all of which are carried in a "hold-all." As previously intimated, you do not dare use similar articles furnished by the railway, assuming that they can be so procured, and that applies also to many of the hotels. You never know the physical condition of the last occupant of the bed or room you may have to occupy. Possessing our own folding cot which we carried on all of our trips, weather permitting, we usually slept out in the open air or at least on the veranda, rather than to spend our nights in a room, and so we are alive to tell about it. Quantities of fresh green cocoanuts kept on the floor of our train compartment provided a drink when thirsty, and that kind of drink is always safe.

Under the law, a dead body must be disposed of within twelve hours and in consequence your best friend goes and is buried often before you even learn of his illness. Under exceptional conditions and with no other alternative possible, we once built a rough coffin for a (107)

good Irishman whose name was Tim Murphy. Seeing him die in all the agony of cholera and having used up precious hours in preparing the coffin and grave, we as the only white men for miles around, buried him and repeated to the best of our ability the burial service. Our hope is that old Tim understood and forgave us for putting him away without the comfort that his Church could have afforded him. 

Wearing a sun-helmet is necessary even for sea bathing. Drinking of beer and wine is suicidal and plenty of tea means boiled water and safety. All this does not mean that a trip to India is not worth the risk. On the contrary, a trip at the right time of the year can be made with the minimum of risk and inconvenience. Enter the country in October and leave it by April first, and you will probably have missed both the excessive heat and the rains. Calcutta is particularly nice to visit in the winter. This is when the social and racing seasons are at their best. Calcutta races are comparable with the world's best and are by far the most colorful.

A Home in India and Its Troubles 

Maintaining a home in India has problems not experienced by our reader. The average home is in a brick and plaster building with large rooms and very high ceilings, windows that are probably without glass and fitted with iron bars, particularly if you live in the country. If so, you are away from electric power and light and in order to sleep and live with some degree (108)

of comfort, you will have suspended from the ceiling a pole or rod to which is fastened a piece of bamboo matting, called a "punka." A rope fastened thereto and carried through a hole in the wall to the outside of the house is pulled by a coolie and if he is efficient, you may get enough circulation of air to exist in some degree of comfort. The chances are the aforesaid coolie will go to sleep on you countless numbers of times and you wake up reeking with perspiration and ablaze with profanity. 

We have known the time when shoes and other portable missiles were gathered from the lawn the next morning, after having been cast at the offending punka puller to awaken him.

The six months rainy season during the monsoon makes life miserable, for whether at home, in your office, or elsewhere, you are wringing wet with perspiration and probably suffering from prickly heat. Even under such trying conditions, you observe the conventions and when asked to dinner you wear full-dress unless your hostess is humane and says on the invitation "short coat." 

Soon after you appear, your collar is wilted, your shirt bosom ditto, and the next morning your servant spreads your mold-covered garments over a great dome-like structure made of bamboo under which he puts a small oil stove to dry them. Then he tries to brush off the mold spots. Your shoes and leather belts suffer likewise and require cleaning daily.

A week or so before the rains are due your wife's silk stockings, silk garments, extra shoes, slippers and any other effects that are subject to rot, are packed in a (109)

tin box and carefully soldered up and not opened again until the rains are over. Rubber bands left exposed on your desk at such a time will after two or three weeks lose all their elasticity, your room walls drip moisture, and your pictures are ruined. Mosquitoes are always with you, as are the little lizards that hide behind the pictures by day and boldly hunt on the walls by night. 

Add to these a few friendly spiders and possibly a scorpion or two with a few centipedes thrown in for good measure, and you have a picture of the average home life in the country. 

And then you come down with fever. And when you think you have about reached the limit of endurance, a little bird comes around and screams his call which sounds like "brain fever." Starting with a low, slow call he rapidly increases the tempo until it is maddening and you are limp. At night the jackals serenade you with sounds that the devil certainly created and you long for that "little grey home in the west." In the city where you have electric fans, glass in the windows and other comforts, life is bearable, if drab.

After the rains, there are tennis parties, dinner parties and do not be surprised if on one of these occasions you recognize some of your own silverware on the table.

It was not stolen; it was only a case of your servants helping your friend's servants to decorate his table. You do not notice it, but think of the time when you may also be in a jam and your servant comes to the rescue with your neighbor's effects. You might be faced with having to give a party and know nothing about it until shortly (110)

before the meal is to be served. You do not get into a panic, but lay the cards on the table with your chief house-boy. Although he may have but an hour's notice, depend on it that he will not disgrace you. Just do not ask where he got the meat course, the fish or even the soup. Be sure that some of your neighbors have had a fair share of their dinner appropriated and that the sum total of such filching provides you with a sizable meal. 

Also be equally sure that such will be your lot sometime when your neighbor gets in a similar jam. 

Your laundry is turned over to the tender mercies of the "dhobie" and a bigger rascal never lived. He hies himself to the nearest water hole and there proceeds to wash your clothes by beating them on a rock. We believe it was Mark Twain who said that he saw for the first time in India a man beating unmercifully on a rock with a shirt. Each time the clothes come home, there are new buttons to be sewed on, iron-rust stains must be removed, and if you escape ring-worm through his use of polluted water, you are indeed lucky.

Your cook is another son of darkness and every day of his life he robs you through commissions that he adds to every item he purchases and you have to let him do the buying. Easier by far to let him do so than to try to force him to be honest for you cannot make him honest. The few cents that he takes daily will not amount to much. Allow him that privilege and it will pay dividends in good meals and loyalty.

We once attended a dinner party where most of the guests were strangers to us and it turned out that at least (111)

one of them was a stranger to the hostess. She, however, was typically British and graciously accepted the situation, knowing that sooner or later there would be an explanation for the stranger looked the gentleman. Well, during the meal someone addressed the hostess by name and the stranger looked up and asked if he had heard the name aright. Assured that he had, he asked, "But is this Mrs. Allan's apartment"? When told that she lived two floors higher in that apartment house, he left in confusion to continue his dinner in the right apartment. It did not seem to disturb anyone, certainly not our hostess who laughed it off. But 

Operating a home or "chummery," calls for "putting up" for days your official friends from other parts of the country, and our memory goes back to some of the elderly Scotch friends whose ability to put away quantities of whiskey and brandy was astonishing. we do not recall having seen one of them under the influence of liquor, nor did it seem to interfere with them in business or play. Such men, even at the age of sixty, would still play tennis and golf. It was customary to put a quart of Scotch in their room daily to replace the empty bottle that was usually found.

On tour you might visit them in their homes or quarters and you were given the best in the house and if your friend was away, the servants "put you up" and made you feel at home, even to the extent of having laundry work done, lending you one of the Sahib's shirts if needed. We recall our embarrassment on checking up on garments after such a trip and finding a shirt that did (112)

not belong to us. When we chastised our stupid servant for not remembering who it belonged to, his reply was, "Sahib you did not lose anything."

Maintaining a Home in India 

Sooner or later you miss some household effect and realize that one of your servants is robbing you. In that case you secure the services of a native attached to the local police force who comes in and seats all of the servants on the floor.

Without any preliminary talk he puts a tablespoon full of uncooked rice in the mouth of each and tells them if they swallow any part of it, they will go to jail. Then he proceeds to tell them that one of them is a thief and he is there to find him. Then he starts a tirade and quotes the curses prescribed by their different religions for such a crime and systematically scares them out of their wits. 

As soon as he thinks that the desired results have been obtained, he removes the rice from their mouths and if any one batch is dry and free from saliva, he knows he has the thief. We have never known such a test to fail. 

One of our trips took us to the outpost of India on the China border, which was fourteen days' trip at that time from the nearest rail-head. Porters carried our luggage and we arrived at the deputy commissioner's home in time for late tea. This man was virtually ruler over the State. He lived alone and it was about the last place we ever expected to need dinner clothes even though we had them with us. He shattered our hopes by casually (113)

mentioning that we just had time to have our "tub" before dressing for dinner.

In the after dinner conversation we expressed our surprise that he followed the custom of dressing, particularly as there were no other white officials in the State service. His answer was that an Englishman never forgets such conventions regardless of conditions and that further, if he so much as once appeared at dinner in any other attire, it would be noised about in the "bazaars" by morning that the "Burra Sahib" had slipped. He was most emphatic in his assertions that this would be fatal to British prestige. 

Government House and Social Rules 

To have business relations with the Government of India, one must first be on the Government list and that is not so easily accomplished. Being so listed, you are able to do business with the different departments or may accept employment. You have a social standing which endures just so long as you strictly observe the conventions. One must at certain stated times, pay a call on the Governor of the Province, which may only entail signing the visitor's book, but if the Governor's Lady is "fussy," you will once a year present yourself before her, dressed in a cutaway coat, top hat, gloves and striped trousers. Spats too, if you can get up enough nerve to wear them. You may stay with her five miserable minutes during which you are catechized. Our recollections of such ordeals are painful. If you are at the capital (114)

(Delhi or Simla) you should drop a card the Viceroy and in the native States you may pay your respects in a similar way to the ruler. Once yearly you must attend all of the social functions as levies of the Viceroy or Governor, State balls, garden parties, or other State gatherings of note. If you fail to be in regular attendance at such social functions, it is just too bad. 

Our first levy was that of Lord Curzon and up to that time we never fully understood the meaning of the word autocrat. Lord Curzon was such a stickler for conventions that no one dared to present himself before him unless correctly groomed even to the shoes on one's feet. Attending one of the levies, you are herded together in groups, up long stairways, through roped off places in rooms and ultimately you reach the throne room where not less than three A.D.C.s check your card of admission and in turn, your name is read and you bow and pass along. On one particular occasion, we were slowly mounting a stairway and an A.D.C. standing at a turn, said to the man in front of us, "I think the gentleman has forgotten to change his shoes." 

That poor devil had on lace pumps instead of the prescribed bow tie type and he had to turn back. It was learned later that Lord Curzon was a real tyrant when it came to such matters. 

It was our pleasure to be one of two Americans presented to King George V., who with Queen Mary came out to India for the Delhi Durbar late in 1911. The honor was partly due to observance of conventions or may have been due to certain pioneering work that gave (115)

us possibly more credit than was due. A brief account of that ceremony might be interesting. Being Americans, we politely but firmly "side-stepped" wearing a Court of St. James suit that every Englishman so placed would have to wear. That calls for knee breeches, silk stockings and a "tin" sword. Inasmuch as the only other

American, our Consul General, would wear a full dress suit, we elected to do likewise, but it was not without some effort that permission was given. Certainly the two of us looked somewhat out of place among all that finery. Such a presentation, particularly by the Governor of Bengal, as ours was, was productive of dividends in the years to come. To have been presented to the king was an asset in business.

We recall approaching the throne room in somewhat of a dazed frame of mind. Our Consul General being accorded the courtesy of "private entree" with other members of the diplomatic corp, had left us alone to face the ordeal. Within the space from the door of the throne room to where the King stood, flanked by the 

Queen's brother and another dignitary whose name momentarily escapes us, was room for five of us in line and the third man ahead was rather portly and looked even more so in his court suit. As he faced the King and made his bow, his tin sword caught on his leg and the result was so ludicrous as to cause coniderable merriment in which the King joined. That little episode lessened our nervous tension and made our presentation enjoyable. 

We had been presented to the King and Queen years before when they were Prince and Princess of Wales. (116)

The august gentlemen who stood in a row to the left of the King as we passed along the line of the diplomatic corps presented an interesting sight. We saw more gold braid and decorations on that occasion than we have probably seen since. Certainly it was an experience of a lifetime. Our plainly dressed consular officer stood out among them like a sore thumb.

State functions such as levies, State balls, and garden parties are attended not only by State officials, military and naval officers, judges, Church of England clergymen and their respective ladies, but also native rulers and, occasionally, one or more of them bring their wives. 

On such occasions one sees more pearls and precious stones, famous jewelry and silver and gold brocaded coats than seen elsewhere in a lifetime. Diamonds, rubies, emeralds and pearls are seen in dazzling profusion. To see a Maharajah decked out in golden pugerie with a bunch of egret feathers thereon, held in place by a diamond clasp worth the price of a city block, with ropes of pearls around his neck, a miscellaneous lot of other gems on his fingers and in his ears, dressed in a brocaded coat of extreme value, is the usual thing. It is the one opportunity for each ruler to vie with another in a display of wealth and they go the limit, but in so doing, usually use discretion and display good taste and judgment.

As stated, a few Maharajahs bring along their wives. 

These are men who do not have plural wives and who are not bound by caste rules. They are also probably men whose forebears were loyal to the Government in (117)

critical times in its history, and they and their descendants thereby enjoy special privileges and recognition. 

We have seen at a State ball, groups dancing the lancers and made up of the head of the church, navy, army, high court judge, viceroy, governor and their ladies, together with maharajahs and their maharanee consorts. Everyone taking part in such dances puts on a creditable performance. The "common herd" is not on the floor, but standing on the side-lines watching and it can be readily understood that those taking part would want to give them an eyeful. And they do.

The author has written of the necessity of being on Government list, but said nothing about the qualifications other than the strict observance of conventions. There are other determining factors. As an example, if you own a department store, you are in "trade" and that eliminates you at least from social life of the character above referred to. If you have a two by twice office and are a broker, or even an engineer, you are eligible. If you are a military officer who has married beneath your station in life, you may expect an invitation to the Government House but to stag parties only. Complicated, yes, but effective. 

Presentation to royalty reminds us that a "frock coat," used for one of the receptions for King George V., was brought to California where it ultimately was given to the salvage department of the Children's Hospital at Los Angeles. Being told that the coat had been made for and worn specially for the occasion mentioned, one of the ladies in charge displayed the garment rather (118)

prominently and priced it higher than she was wont to do. 

A negro minister coveted it but the price was more than he could pay. So he asked his congregation for a special collection to purchase it. For years he preached to them clad in that coat. On his death-bed he sent for one of the ladies and secured from her a promise that she would see that he was buried in it. This promise was carried out.

Elephants and Birds 

Our friendship for animals and birds takes in almost everything from elephants to the little Bengal minah bird, which is the only song bird in India. It has truly been said that India's birds do not sing and her flowers are without odor. These little minahs are not the talking kind found in other parts of the country, but are much smaller, not unlike our California mocking bird. They are little gentlemen and ladies. as well as little fighting devils who will tackle any feathered intruder twice their size. Their ability to put an opponent on his back by grabbing him by the ankles and tripping him up, is uncanny and what they do to their victims under such circumstances is plenty. Seldom will this victim come back for more. 

It was once our delight to tame a pair of them to come at our call. They visited us many times daily, knowing that they had only to make known their presence to be fed a special kind of cake which was always kept on hand for them in an aluminum box. This box (119)

we keep to this day in memory of the pleasure those birds gave us. They would bow and chatter away in the most friendly manner, visit us at meal-time and even knew our automobile and would fly down to greet us and then be on our window-sill when we arrived high above at our apartment. In flight they appear to have a circular disc under their wings which gives the appearance of whirling as they come toward you.

We cannot say anything kindly of the crow nor his feathered friends of India. They have at times almost driven us cuckoo. Crows in and around Calcutta probably number in the millions. They are larger than their 

American brother but are protected by Hindu religious prejudice so cannot be killed without causing riots. If you do not screen yourself in with chicken wire you may expect to lose belongings from collar-buttons to food. 

A crow is second to no other bird in cunning and curiosity. He has spoiled many a golf game by picking up a golf-ball and flying far enough away with it to satisfy his curiosity as to its food value and ruin your temper. 

This explains why one employs two caddies! One especially to keep far enough ahead to protect driven balls from these birds; keep a lookout for cobras and possibly retrieve floater balls that may fall into the many groundlevel water-tanks that litter up the course and provide the irrigation water. Speaking of snakes on the golf course, we remember that it took quite awhile after our return home to quit keeping one eye out for snakes while seeking a lost ball. 

Then there is a larger bird called the "kite," not (120)

unlike the American buzzard. His ability to sail into, across, or with the wind without any perceptible movement of a single wing feather is amazing. Studying his flight with binoculars, one wonders how he does it. This bird has an uncanny sight, missing nothing. Throw him food and he will pick it out of the air while in full flight, catching it in his feet. He dines at ease while sailing along and more than one outdoor luncheon or picnic has been ruined by his ability to sweep a meat plate from under your nose before you can retaliate. 

The battle in India against starvation makes for cunning in men, birds and beasts, and it brings out a degree of intelligence that is surprising. We have seen a village dog gnawing a bone and behind and in front of him will be several crows. Those in the rear will pick at his tail and try and get his attention away from the bone, and unless he is an old hand at that game, any effort on his part to get rid of his tormentors in the rear permits the crows in front to snatch the bone. It is a game that may be seen often. The crow often tries it out on the kite but usually with little success. He is able to hold on with his talons and fights back with beak and wings.

Our attention was once called to an unusual amount of noise among the crows near our apartment. Investigating we found that there was among them a white crow that was certainly being given the "once over."

The other crows suddenly adjourned to a tree and held a pow-wow that could have been nothing less than a court trial, at the end of which they flew back and (121)

literally tore that bird limb from limb. We have seen a wounded crow suffer the same fate in much the same way.

There are monkeys, both big and little, that are devilish and sometimes little short of being dangerous, particularly some of the large ones found in Bengal and in the foothills of the Himalayas. They range in size from the Singapore monkey that can be put in your pocket to the hill monkey large as an average-sized boy of ten years. In spite of their almost human antics and affection for downright deviltry, the "oscar" goes to the monkey.

The one animal that stands out in our memory is the elephant. We have known many and have ridden. hundreds of miles on their backs. A trotting elephant moves with so little motion that it is not at all difficult to go to sleep on his back, particularly if you are reclining on a padded platform so often used on such beasts for going long distances. We have known the joy of having one such old cow elephant in our possession for a very long time and, through kindly treatment and considerable petting, she was won over to the point where she would drop her work any time we appeared on the job for an inspection and come squealing to us, knowing that we were sure to have some little treat for her in our pocket. In her haste to get at it our pockets have been torn or our coats mussed more than once by her trunk. Her little eyes would fairly sparkle and she would sometimes follow us about, much to the distress of her mahout. Not often does an elephant show such (122)

This old animal worked from daylight until dark, pulling wagons, logs, pipe, lifting cases or machinery, carrying stone to the crusher in rope-woven baskets and our foreman taught her through her driver to lift valves up onto blocking to actually hold them against the steel tanks or filters where they could be temporarily fastened for final bolting thereto. One often sees wild herds of elephants in the forests and sees them rounded up and driven into specially prepared corrals, where they are ultimately tied separately to iron rings or stakes from which they are eventually driven away by ignorant Indian drivers, called mahouts. These natives apparently attain complete mastery over them in but a few days, or weeks at most. Wild elephants are to be found in many of the native States of the country and usually such herds are under the State Forestry Department and are watched closely by natives who are of a caste that work with these animals. 

That is particularly the case with the Mysore State.

These men are able to walk among these wild beasts, prevent them from straying beyond the State boundaries and keep records of additions to the herds. The (123)

animals get somewhat used to their calls, so much so that they readily become tame after being captured and are easily and successfully handled. 

It was our privilege to see an unusually large herd rounded up in Mysore, in the presence of the then Prince and Princess of Wales, who later became King George V. and Queen Mary. It was our duty to assist in laying out quarters for royalty as they were to remain in the camp for several days. We had time, too, to study the strength of the great stockade that surrounded the corral, as also the pitfalls and great gate that shut the elephants in. The herd was driven through a river prior to entering the lanes in the forest that led to the main gate. To see such a large number of elephants playing in the water and throwing it about, was a scene never to be forgotten. Once inside the stockade, the largest bull of the herd is singled out and three trained elephants are used to assist in the tying process. Two of these trained beasts saunter up on each side of the animal to be tied first, and a third elephant moves to the rear. The three mahouts, who are maneuvering their charges into position, are dressed in a breech-cloth and smeared with cocoanut oil. Almost before the old bull knows what is happening, he is pulled and pushed into position for tying to a prepared stake. He sees his mates treated likewise over a period of days. Tying them is often far more complicated than it sounds, and men have often been killed in so doing. In fact, one man was badly hurt on the occasion mentioned, but he was quickly removed from sight of the Prince and Princess who (124)

were comfortably seated on a platform together with other guests concealed from the animals by screens. 

Having selected such of the herd as wanted, the rest of the animals are turned back again into their forest home to grow and multiply against the day when they must again be rounded up.

Alternate starving and feeding, the captured animals soon learn who is master and they either become one more inmate of the ruler's stable, or they are set to work as they are in the teak forests of Burmah. Many are, of course, sold for only the very best are selected for the royal stables. A trip to the Burmah camps is an education. Near the town of Chittagong you can find them working from morning until night with a noon-day play time when they bathe and rest. 

If you value your life, do not wear white clothes for the elephant seemingly hates white. On into the forest your road becomes narrow as you reach the logging camp in a clearing through which flows a stream on which the logs have been floated. Thousands of logs are thus brought down and are carried to piles on the banks. 

Dozens of elephants, swarms of native workmen, rows of stables and piles of logs can be seen. There are several crews of elephants, some of which wear a sort of harness. A cow elephant will come out of the stream, dragging a log or piece of squared timber. She deposits it with almost human intelligence between "stacker" elephants and returns to the water for another. A pair of the stacking animals push their tusks down under each end of the timber and up goes the log to the height (125)

of a one-story house and onto the pile. Should one end protrude too far, it is immediately pushed into proper position to be even with other logs in the pile. All this without a single word of command. It is a sight to be remembered and you see feats of strength and displays of intelligence that must be witnessed to be believed. 

Afghanistan and the War That Never Ends 

You may have gathered from the foregoing that all is peaceful now that the British have dealt so many years with both Hindus and Mohammedans. Not so, for aside from the internal periodical riots that break out between the followers of the two religions mentioned, there is a war on the northwest frontier that never ends. 

Fanatical deviltry is always afoot throughout India and when Mohammedans deliberately kill cattle near a

Hindu temple (the Hindu worships the bullocks, you know) they the Hindus-retaliate by throwing a pig in a Mohammedan mosque (which pig the Mohammedan abominates) and there is likely to be some killing and all too often the British have to call out troops and settle the matter. Those are the riots that you read about so often.

Bordering on northwest India and entered through the famous Khyber Pass, lies Afghanistan. Through that pass have entered all invaders of India. First, probably, came the ancient cultured Dravidians who brought practical crafts and culture and iron. Then 

Christians, seeking the Promised Land, swarmed after (126)

them. Following, came Alexander's Macedonian phalanxes and after him the terrible Genghis Khan and his hordes of plundering cut-throats. Then came the Persians under King Darius. But it remained for the Mongolian Tamerlane to show the merciless qualities of his race. For two hundred years he held India in bondage. 

The offspring of these creeds and races now live in the troubled hills around Khyber Pass and, strange as it may seem, the British have never been able to subjugate these primitive people who spend their lives taking pot-shots at soldiers of the king. This is a war that never ends, and it probably costs Great Britain around two thousand men per year. Religion, obviously, is at the bottom of the trouble and there is no indication of an end to it in the lifetime of any who may read these lines. For thirty-six years it has been going on and it will probably continue just as long as men believe in the exclusive divinity of Mohammed, Allah and Christ. 

Battles are fought in the great Khyber Pass which winds up some thirty-three miles of country full of steep gorges to the Afghan border. Formerly there were at least forty thousand troops made up of the famous Bengal Lancers, Indian foot soldiers and cavalry. There is but a handful of British officers who command many races and creeds; many of them friends and relatives of the tribesmen that they fight.

It is not easy for us who live in America and are tolerant toward other religions than our own to fully realize to what ends these swarthy wild tribesmen of Afghanistan will go in their holy zeal, which (127)

permits no tolerance toward the white infidel, nor any other race that believes in other gods or prophets. If he is a believer, there are no half-way measures in his hatred of the infidel. These will kill for Mohammed and die for him with a zeal unequaled anywhere. These same tribesmen, strange as it may seem, come over and enlist in the British service and will be dependable to their oath of allegiance during their term of enlistment, so much so that they will without compunction, kill their own relatives and think nothing of fighting and killing their own fathers.

There is a story about a company of Lancers marching in the Khyber Pass when a burst of fire high up on the hills caused the death of a British officer. The troops took to cover and efforts were made to spot the sniper. 

Then a Pathan enlisted man volunteered to stalk the sniper, saying that this was country that he knew well. 

They heard a shot later and the Pathan returned smiling to report that he had liquidated the source of troublehis own father. Loyalty to their oath means more than filial devotion. When they are mustered out of the service, they go home, rejoin their tribe and become active against the British, fully as much as they were formerly on the other side. The officer telling that story, stated that he afterwards learned that the Pathan, who shot his own sniper father, rejoined his tribe without difficulty.

There is said to one expedient which would stop the war overnight; if the British would bury slain tribesmen in graves along with the bodies of pigs there would (128)

be a cessation of the trouble. But this the British, apparently, will never do. To be buried with a pig prevents the Mohammedan from going to Heaven and links him eternally with what they consider to be the lowest form of life. Under such punishment one's family would suffer for generations. There were cases in the Indian mutiny where such a course was followed, but apparently never again will such a procedure be tolerated. 

The British do not consider it "cricket."

One of the worst forms of this religious fervor is that of going "ghazi." When a troubled Pathan or

Afridi is jilted, or depressed, he works himself into a frenzy and announces that he is "going ghazi." He then distributes his worldly wealth to his family and friends and adorns himself in spotlessly clean clothes. This done, he is ready for a one-man crusade against the infidel. Next he polishes and oils his rifle then strips himself of clothes, all but bare necessities, so that he may pass on to the other world as poor as when he entered it. 

In this state he is a hero who must attain Paradise because he is to die fighting for his faith.

In such a state, while he makes up his mind to kill as many British soldiers as he can, while enroute to Paradise, it is not easy to stop him when he runs amuck, as he will eventually. He is likely to post himself high on a hill and from a vantage point there kill soldiers who pass below and when his ammunition is exhausted or his enemy catches up with him, he will run screaming at the soldiers, slashing with a short sword with a fury that makes it next to impossible to capture him alive. (129)

Some of our soldiers in the Philippines have had similar experiences with Moros. 

From the foregoing you might gather that a trip into Afghanistan is not exactly free from excitement, and if you could foresee what the roads were like as the author knew them, you would surely lose interest in the venture. The first stop is Jelalabad located in a fertile. valley at an altitude around two thousand feet above sea-level. Scattered around may be seen orange groves and other semi-tropical fruit trees. Here is located the 

Amir's winter palace and buried near is Halibullah Kahn whom we helped to make a member of the Masonic 

Lodge. Lord Kitchener foresaw the coming of the

World War and knowing that the Germans and Russians would in such an event try to enter India through 

Afghanistan, he personally persuaded the Amir to come down to the border to visit India as the guest of the British.

This ruler was taken all over India in a special train, reviewing troops at each stop and was lionized everywhere. He probably never knew that many of the troops he saw on that trip were seen more than once, for certainly they were moved about so that could happen. 

He arrived at Calcutta thoroughly impressed with the might of the British in India and it may not have been so difficult to get his signature to a treaty that provided for the neutrality of Afghanistan. His liking for Lord

Kitchener and Sir Henry McMahon was largely responsible for this being made possible and his loyalty to the British during the war was of the utmost value to them. (130)

During the Amir's visit to Calcutta, he was, through a special dispensation from the Masonic Grand Lodge of England, given three degrees in one day, a colorful ceremony it was, too. He also took some golf lessons and on his return to his own country, took back a number of sets of clubs and balls. Once home, he set about providing a golf course and taught the princes of the royal family that ancient and honorable game, which they (the princes) apparently did not care for. Later on and toward the close of the World War, his relatives "liquidated" him and buried him in the golf course, which thereby became holy ground. This put an end to golf in Afghanistan.

However, we are inclined to believe that the causes of the "removal" of Habibullah was his refusal to give in to the pressure brought to bear to join Turkey in her fight against the Allies. Repeated Turko-German missions to his capital at Kabul, kept his leaders stirred up and made the Indian Government very anxious as to the outcome. We believe that his Masonic oath was largely responsible for this stubborn refusal to break faith. Ultimately he was murdered, probably by his own flesh and blood, which event happened near the close of the great conflict and too late to have far-reaching effects. Though the Afghans immediately declared war on India, they suffered defeat and were glad to sue for a separate peace.

The trip on to Jelalabad is four days by horse-drawn vehicle, if you are lucky, but much less by automobile over desert, sand-dunes, through narrow defiles and over (131)

high passes from which you have a broad sweep of wooded country and snow-capped peaks. You descend again where signs of life may be seen. You meet caravans of horses, caravans of camels laden with fruits or carpets from distant places, maybe Persia, Bokahra or

Turkestan. Donkeys, water-buffalo, men, women and children impede your progress and you note that the men are heavily armed. They seem friendly enough but you have a feeling that you would rather avoid meeting them in the dark. On and on you go over the Lataband Pass at eight thousand feet elevation. Sharp turns and great precipices, where not many years ago, robbery was easy and the disposal of the victim more so is the terror of the traveler. The Amir put a stop to robbery by capturing the leader of the robber band and imprisoning him in an iron cage hung on poles on a peak where he was left to die in sight of the wayfarer. His bones are probably in that same cage to this day.

Entering the plain where Kabul is located, the Amir's garden, located some miles out, gives the weary traveler his first sight of the capital. There are city gates and customs officials to deal with; after which you can rest your weary body at the Dakhilla or the Kharya Hotels, both of which were once palaces. The food will be terrible and comforts few. We once had something to do with the building of an electric power plant for supplying light and power in Kabul and our assistants brought back many a harrowing tale from their long residence there.

We are reminded of an episode in the life of an (132)

American dentist-although we are not sure that he classified it as a mere episode. He was engaged by the Amir to go from Calcutta to Kabul to do some dental work for 

His Highness. The remuneration was fixed at ten thousand Indian rupees which were to be paid in silver prior to his return journey. He was met at the State border with an Afghan bodyguard and escorted in style to Kabul. Having finished his work, he was asked by the 

Amir to give him a few lessons in dentistry and to sell him all the equipment he had taken with him, including the drilling machine, so that the ruler might practice on the women of his harem. We never heard how the women fared.

There being no other alternative, our dentist parted with his equipment, gave the lessons asked for, and departed for home surrounded by the same bodyguard and having in his possession his money in silver. Then his real troubles began. At the first night stop he found that his bodyguard was on strike and demanded one thousand rupees be paid before they would budge. Having satisfied them to that extent they moved on to the next stop where the performance was repeated. This was continued all the way to the Indian border and our victim finally crossed into India with about one-third of the amount given him at Kabul. He had a whole skin and his complaints to the Amir probably never reached that gentleman.

While not exactly apropos, this reminds us of two friends who were together in San Francisco. Walking along the street, one of them happened to look up and (133)

see a sign reading, "MacDonald & Kahn." Turning to his companion, he said: "God help that Jew."

It also recalls another story that the American told who had just returned home from a trip to Scotland. A friend asked: "How did they treat you over there?" 

To which he replied: "Reluctantly." 

Siam and Bangkok

Much in the war news today is Siam, which has acquired a new name since we knew it, but having a new name does not seem to have improved its reputation. 

We made many visits to Bangkok and sections of the country that do not call for too many hardships, and you may have it all so far as our interests are concerned. 

Our interest in Bangkok in particular was the securing of a large contract for the installation of a complete water works plant, including filters. We found there a condition in the State Government that probably never existed to the same extent elsewhere. Under pressure from other powers and with spines of rubber, the Siamese turned over such departments of the Government as public works, sanitation, surveys, customs, railways, water supply, etc., to so-called commissions from as many powers, and for many years these commissions exercised almost complete control in their "sphere of influence." 

Thus it became necessary for us to deal with a French engineer who headed his department, and what a headache that turned out to be! We trumped his ace (134)

in a bridge game once and thought he would kill us.

Bangkok is one of the places that you do not visit if you can help it. Hot and sticky, it has smells that would out-smell any Singapore Chinese meat market. This is largely due to the city canals that also serve as sewers, and a tidal influence that empties the canals twice daily, leaving house-boats, fishing craft, dead dogs and other filth exposed for sufficient periods each time to make you wish you could trade olfactory nerves with a Chinaman. The rise and fall in the tide in the river in front of the city is as much as seventeen feet at times, and if you are burying your dead, you wait until the tide is out before attempting it, otherwise you will lower the casket into water and probably weight it down with bricks, which is not exactly pleasant.

It did not take long to sense that dealing exclusively with the gentlemen before mentioned, would mean prolonged negotiations that might result in nothing, so we commandeered the services of the American Minister to introduce us to the Prime Minister and members of his staff. Luckily, too, our Government had an official adviser to the King to rather offset the British financial adviser to His Majesty. Our man was an Americanborn Dane who went "all out" for us. The result was that we were accorded the special privilege of sitting down with the Prime Minister or his staff, or both, lay before them concrete proposals that were highly recommended to the King by Adviser Westengard, with the result that all hands were on the table at all times and (135)

skulduggery of the sort that some officials had the habit of winking at, was out.

We shall never forget those conferences around a huge table with high-backed chairs of beautiful design and workmanship. As is the custom, all Siamese, even the officials that we dealt with, chewed betel-nut, which as you know, stains the teeth and lips black or dark brown, giving the effect of a huge hole in the face. As we conferred, these men chewed and spat into enormous silver cuspidors, one for each member. These cuspidors were in the form of enormous vases of silver chased with beautiful designs. So high were these receptacles that it was only necessary to turn the head slightly to make use of the vessels.

Those were days when the art of typing was acquired by few, and as we could not procure a typist, we had to rewrite that particular contract five times before we had produced a satisfactory agreement. As a matter of fact, much of the trouble was due to increasing resentment by the French engineer over the way the matter had been taken out of his hands. Having tried about everything else, he came crying to us one evening, saying that because of his inability to get the contract for a French organization he would be deprived from receiving the Legion of Honor decoration that was to have been his reward. We were hard-hearted but agreed not to be interested in the distribution system in the streets, and which he later did "swing" to a French manufacturer. 

We had noted the soil conditions and tidal fluctuations, and wanted none of it. (136)

Long after, when the plant was completed and ready for operation, it was opened by the King. We came for the ceremony and were duly presented by the King's uncle, Prince Damerung, whom we had met several times previously. Before returning to Calcutta, we were invited to this nobleman's week-end home, situated several miles up the river. We traveled on his launch, and upon arrival, were turned over to house servants who provided us with a suite of beautiful rooms, and that is as far as they went. At dinner, to our amazement, we dined alone. The following morning our host eventually looked us up and seemed unperturbed by his lack of attention the previous evening. We were taken over the estate, saw his automobiles and motor-boats; later in the day he presented me with a very beautiful cigaret-case, which salved our social wounds. But I made the mistake of not keeping it in my pocket. Instead, I packed it in my case and going back to Bangkok later in the day on the launch, I opened the bag to better examine the gift only to find it had disappeared. Telling United

States Minister King of that experience, he laughed heartily and asked if we ever heard of an "Indian Giver."

Apparently I was not the first to have that cigaret case given to a guest, only to lose it through the ability of some servant to pick locks of bags.

One of the safeguards employed by the Siamese has been to never let the bar at the mouth of their river be dredged. That has always been shallow enough to prevent steamers drawing more than fourteen feet to go up the river. Prior to long range guns, that worked well (137)

case. enough, but might not be much protection now, and we do expect to hear that the Japanese are dredging just in As a result of this bar-restricting passage up the river to Bangkok, only small ships ply between that and other ports. They are hardly ships, but more properly compared to the character of the vessel that the "three men went to sea in." When ships on other runs get too obsolete to be used, they are sent to the eastern oceans, where they quickly accumulate a brand of cockroach, the like of which is not seen elsewhere; not to mention other vermin and filth. Nobody cares, for those eastern waters are really rough much of the year. 

The navigation companies need only provide the minimum of food-or what they call food. At times you need more hands than possessed by a normal person to stay put while you try to stow away a little nourishment. 

The Gulf of Siam is unique in that at the right season of the year one may see the most extraordinary displays of phosphorescent light in the water. We have sailed many seas and have often watched such effects, but nothing like that seen in the Siamese Gulf. One may there see the water literally aflame for hours around the ship. On one of the trips from Bangkok to Singapore in rough weather we sighted another vessel disabled and pitching badly. Hours were spent getting a fourinch hawser aboard and it broke three times on the trip. 

They finally brought home "the bacon" but at the cost of two extra days and hours of misery to passengers and crew, all of whom were living on reduced rations by that time. We have always noticed that English ships (138)

seldom run out of cold beef and potatoes, even if they never have salt with which to season the potatoes while being boiled, boiled they always will be as long as there are British ships and British cooks. That sort of provender you can always count on, or could before Hitler started this "ruckus." Spend a week or two on a British ship and land in Java and start eating highly seasoned food cooked in olive oil-that the Javanese cook provides you at a Dutch Hotel there, and then prepare to call the doctor, for you will probably require his services sooner or later.

Speaking of voyages; we are reminded of two 

Pacific crossings in which the international date line figured quite conspicuously. On a trip west our ship's passenger list included over ninety missionaries. We crossed the date line losing a day and that day happened to be Sunday. In the morning the missionaries came on deck to find no preparation for church services and the bar open. They complained to the captain who informed them that it was Monday, there being no Sunday that week.

On another voyage east, we crossed the date line on Friday, February 13, and we had two Fridays of the same date. In consequence, your author saw thirty days in that particular month of February, it being leap year. 

Yes, Ripley was told about it.

It is an education to see "fan-tan" played in Bangkok. The State or City, we are not sure which, gets a large “rake-off" and there are many places where that and other games are played nightly. You usually find (139)

the game played on great bamboo or reed mats, some of which are thirty feet in diameter. Around one of these mats will be found Chinese, Siamese, Malays and other races playing with coins of small denomination up to one thousand tical notes. The banker sits on one side with a huge chest of money in front of him and a number of croupiers, who assist in placing bets and raking in the money with long wooden rakes. Thousands gamble every day and night and all squat on the floor. There are the wealthy and the poor side by side and it is indeed. an education to watch the expression, or better still, the lack of expression, on the face of winner or loser. There is none like the Oriental when it comes to gambling. 

However, the most interesting point of play is to see the banker pay off. You have, let us assume, won ten ticals, coins that are larger than our half-dollar. From his seat on the floor twenty or thirty feet away, he expertly throws those coins in one stack to you and they fall directly in front and drop dead. You need never worry about any one of them rolling away. When it comes to smaller coins of low denomination, these being made with a hole in the center, the banker pitches you a whole flock of them fastened together on a string or reed. Truly one-half the world knows not how the other half lives and plays.

On one of our visits to Bangkok, we were persuaded by the chief of police to go with him to see an execution. 

It was the custom then to execute the criminal, if sentenced to death, just as near as possible to the scene of his crime. Had we understood what the ordeal meant, (140)

this probably would not have been written. However, we were on the spot and to show the white feather might ruin our chances of success in a certain undertaking which has been referred to earlier. We found the poor culprit tied down in a sitting position to an iron stake and seeing an executioner standing by with a heavy sword. We realized what might be expected. We had taken a camera along and although appalled at what was about to take place, focused it and at the critical moment dropped our eyes and pressed the button and with celerity that was not our custom, went back to our hotel room to lie down. When that picture was developed, it showed the sword in full view after it had passed entirely through the victim's neck, the executioner with one foot off the ground from the impact of the blow and the head had not even started to fall off. Although still in our possession, it is not included in this little volume. 

Japan and Her Business Methods 

Japan and the Japanese what disillusionment those names bring to mind. We had our part in carrying out a large contract there in the City of Kyoto. 

After that experience, we said that if the Lord ever forgave us for our gullibility on that deal, we would never set foot in Japan again. Fortified with patents taken out in that country at the suggestion of Japanese officials and with definite promises of another contract to follow at Tokyo immediately on the completion of the first, our equipment was installed, put into successful operation (141)

and accepted. Met with polite excuses for not being able to proceed immediately with the second promised contract, we went on our way. Not hearing anything further about the new project, we investigated, only to find that the job was being built, in fact nearing completion, and every piece of equipment had been copied from the first installation and had been made in Japan. 

Threats of patent litigation were met with polite, tantalizing suggestions that nothing so drastic be done, and our investigations and conferences with the American consular officials indicated that litigation would be useless. Such is the value of promises made in Japan. 

We had agents in Soerabaja, Java, who operated a machine shop and we often "kidded" them about the obsolete machines in use therein. They finally took our advice and bought a few lathes and other tools, supposedly from the South Bend, Indiana Machine Tool Company. In due course, these were received and turned out such inferior work that they asked us to come down and inspect these much vaunted American products. According to schedule, we arrived and saw machines that had the appearance of being well made, each having cast on the frames, "South Bend Indiana, made in the U.S.A." 

The trial of one of these lathes warranted calling for tools and dismantling it. What was found, indicated beyond doubt, that it had never been manufactured at South Bend and further inquiry elicited the information that the tools had been purchased through a Japanese agent who claimed to be able to sell them United States made tools at an attractive discount. Those machines (142)

were made in Japan and sold as American made products. That they were made from patterns which were exact copies of the American made machines, there could be no doubt, and the use of the name of the American manufacturer meant nothing.

In Calcutta in one of the public buildings frequented by purchasing agents, is a suite of rooms where there is (or was) displayed on specially made shelves and tables, a great quantity of pharmaceutical supplies put up in bottles and packages such as made for sale. 

These bore the name of Borroughs & Wellcome, a wellknown London firm. In addition there were other products equally well marked with names of other wellknown English manufacturers and in all cases the labels were identical with those on the English products. 

Every sample so displayed was "Made in Japan." That public exhibition of the counterfeits was to educate the Indian buyer as to what to expect if he did not purchase his stocks from reputable people. There never was so much as a peep out of the Japanese consular officers in protest that we ever heard of.

Years ago, Captain Bob Evans took his flagship of the American squadron into the port of Yokohama and when the chief officer of the port received him he recognized him as his former cabin boy. Since then no Japanese have been used in the United States navy service. 

British ship-builders formerly bid on Japanese naval vessels as probably also merchant ships. They built quite a few but there came a time when all bids were rejected. Later the Japanese themselves built vessels (143)

to the same plans and specifications submitted by the British bidders. That went on until it became time to give them a lesson in ethics, and when the next opportunity was given to bid, the same thing happened. The vessel was built in a Japanese shipyard and when launched, it turned over, much to the embarrassment of the builders. That time the British bidder offered a design of ship that would not ride the waves. It was the last time they were ever asked to bid. In our humble opinion they will be even more embarrassed when and if they ever try to take Singapore in the war now raging. 

In contrast, we recall, the Chinese middlemen, called "compradore" through whom our organization dealt at five port cities in the Far East. These men were actually subagents for the sale of equipment to natives or plantations whose credit was not acceptable to us. 

Those compradors purchased such equipment from us on terms providing long-time credit, for resale at any profit they could get. Our agreements with them provided that for all such equipment so supplied, regardless of whether sold or unsold, paid for by their customer or not, must be paid for in full on the next Chinese New 

Year following delivery by us.

We never failed to get a full settlement, for a Chinaman always starts the New Year with a clean slate. The year that the first World War broke out, we had over one hundred thousand American dollars outstanding on such transactions and were not too happy about it in view of the uncertainty that existed, but we did not lose a dollar. Whether or not these same Chinese could be trusted (144)

today is unknown but we rather imagine that presentday conditions have put a stop to such business methods. 

Abyssinia 

It was "Abyssinia" when we visited that country, but it is better known now as Ethiopia. In either case, it is a part of the world that is hardly worth writing home about. The gateway to Abyssinia is through the French port of Djibouti and what a terrible place. Only a short sailing distance from Aden, it is, in spite of being on the sea, a typical desert town with nothing to offer the tourist. 

From that port the French have a railway that winds its way from the plains to the high plateau on which 

Addis Ababa is located. What a journey! It is operated only in daylight hours. One stops for the night at Afdam. After all these years, we are not quite certain that we have spelled that name correctly, but we can say that the last syllable of the name is correct and applicable. At that time, the British, French and Italians had the country hemmed in from the sea and were talking of further "spheres of influence" which was another way of saying that "It will not be long, now, Boys." Years have passed and what we saw and heard in our brief visit would mean nothing in these days of war and reconstruction which may restore a deposed king to his throne.

We made our trip there to investigate for others the possibilities of water power development when it looked like American engineers would build a dam at Lake (145)

Tana, but it did not take much of our time to find out that it was a pipe-dream and that we were wasting our time. We know where there is a better prospect in 

Lybia, as far as making power is concerned. But neither project offers any market for the power. 

The capital is located on a high plateau with a good climate and that is about all. One sees camel caravans coming and going, vultures plentiful and hideous, beggars and more beggars, debtors chained to those they owe, warriors wearing European trousers, Roman togas and lion manes around their necks; chiefs wearing white robes and carrying wicked-looking swords from their hips, priests with white turbans, slaves with hundreds of saddled mules and in the background at respectful distance are gunbearers, personal servants, etc. Such is the picture. A babble of sounds met us at the railway station. Black as negroes, these people have features that might be Arabic or Jewish. Whatever their origin, they appear to be a fine race, untamed and unwilling to submit to much discipline. Barbarian maybe, but Christians as they interpret Christianity. Much could be done to develop the country and it is not unlikely that there are other resources of some value, other than the power development at the headwater of the Nile. Doubtless, much has been done by the Italians during their occupation of the country, but we venture the assertion that the Italian method of colonizing will not result in happiness or permanent good. However, this is one man's opinion.

Our visit was made during the lifetime of Emperor (146)

Manelik and while we did not see that old warrior, we did have a look at his cages of lions, and his lion cubs on the leashes. We saw his mounted drum corps, his band using a sort of square banjo for instruments, and we were duly impressed with some of his noblemen who certainly knew how to put on the dog. They were dressed in velvet, silk, leopard-skin coats, lion-mane head-dress and what have you. Such costumes were topped off with round shields said to be made of buffalo-hide and some were decorated with gold filagree work. They feed raw bullock to warriors and that class of men seem to be almost beneath the notice of some of the big boys who show no recognition as they pass. Everybody seemed to have a gun and it was a common sight to see one of the upper crust riding a horse and his gun-bearer following alongside on foot. Being a gun fancier, I could not help but notice the lack of standardization of such weapons. How they are able to get ammunition for so many kinds and caliber was something that could not be learned.

We recall that the Queen of Sheba, accredited in Bible history as Queen of Ethiopia, visited King Solomon and had quite a flirtation with him. It is said that she brought back an heir. From what we saw of the place, we feel that her conduct was perfectly justified and that no word of scandal should have been spoken. 

The Ethiopian race include the Negro, Bantu, and the dark-skinned races of Oceania. "Hail and farewell" was the way we felt about Abyssinia. (147)

Egyptian Monuments 

We once listened to a broadcast from the tomb chamber of the Pyramid Cheops, from where the voice of one of our personal friends called, "Hello, America." 

No wonder he added, "How queerly those words sound out of place here." Continuing, he said: "I little thought when I first went broadcasting, that it would fall to my lot to be the first man to speak to the outside world from the interior of this great pyramid. Fifty centuries have passed since Pharaoh was laid to rest in this chamber and I have a strange feeling that the world outside has ceased to exist; that I have been spirited across the centuries to an age when man was still young on earth. The only link between me and modern earth is this microphone."

We well remember our first climb up that steep inclined passageway inside the pyramid and into the tomb chamber. Also our amazement at the size of the great slabs that form the sides and ceiling thereof and our wonder how such slabs could have been transported there and put in place with such accuracy. The tomb chamber was lighted with flares. We closely inspected that great room and marveled at the ability of its builders who created with brawn and muscle alone. 

In the Egyptian desert, west of Thebes, stands two colossal statues of an ancient Pharaoh. Centuries of sand have worn down the features so that they can hardly be recognized. They are supposed to have been built about 

140 B.C., and were of comparative recent construction (148)

when you consider the great pyramid that dates back to around 2900 B.C. We are told that the calendar was introduced into Egypt in 4241 B.C., and that it only ended with the birth of Christ. Nowhere has civilization lasted longer. Apparently Cheops was the master builder of the pyramid that bears his name. Here stones to the approximate number of two million, three hundred thousand and averaging one and a half tons in weight, measuring one-seventh of a mile in length, one-tenth of a mile in width, four hundred and eighty-one feet high, with a base area of five hundred and twenty-five thousand square feet, forms a structure that we all know as one of the wonders of the world. Every stone was fitted so well as to make the joints hardly distinguishable. It is a miracle in the field of masonry. 

One marvels at the organization that for twenty years could provide a working force of around one hundred thousand men to carry, dress and lay stones for twenty years. Visit that 

Visit that pyramid today and note the lack of interest in such matters on the part of the tourist. 

He is too often far more interested in having his picture taken with him sitting on a donkey or camel with the pyramid or Sphinx as a background-to show the family back home. Ten to one, he is even too indolent to climb to the top or even to the tomb chamber inside, and thereby misses much.

Vandalism is common in Egypt, as for example the "removal" of the two famous "Cleopatra Needles" from

Alexandria, that one might adorn the bank of the Thames in London and the other our own Central Park in New (149)

York. What a job that was, and may we remind our readers of how William K. Vanderbilt spent one hundred and three thousand, seven hundred of his own dollars. to defray the expense of transporting and mounting the one in New York. The engineer, who handled the job for fifteen long weary months received" for his work one thousand, one hundred fifty-six dollars as compensation.

He must have heaved a sigh of relief when he finished it. 

That particular monument was a single piece of granite sixty-nine feet long and weighing approximately one hundred ninety-six tons. The vicissitudes of lowering it from its home base, where Cleopatra may have picnicked, or necked; wrapping it up in a wooden cradle, transporting it to tide water and loading it, was a small job as compared to hauling it through the streets of New York and raising it on its present location without the loss of a single hieroglyph. It traveled across town at the speed of ninety-seven feet per day and it is recorded that when crossing a railway, all trains were stopped for twenty-five minutes. The needle that went to London was loaded on a vessel that was towed, and during a storm it broke away-with the result that it was all but given up for lost. We confess to our own little part in the spoilation of Egypt, but we honestly believe that we gave her much more than was demolished. 

One wonders if the early people of Egypt engaged in the slaughter of quail as do coast residents of that country today. It is the only place we know where quail fly long distances, as they do in crossing the Mediterranean Sea in their migration to Egypt from south (150)

Europe. It is known when the mass flight may be expected and people gather at the beach before daybreak equipped with nets, tennis-rackets and even guns to pounce on these poor, tired birds. While he was shaving in his bath-room, one of our neighbors was hit in the face with bird-shot from some wild marksman.

Next to fleas, flies and beggars are the drivers of donkeys and camels. They pester you on every hand. If you are persuaded to take a trip in the desert, you choose a camel and you find that a doubtful pleasure. Camels are about the most quarrelsome of creatures. 

If you escape being kicked or bitten, you may count yourself lucky. At best you will probably remember the camel's gait and what he does to your stomach if you stay aboard long enough. 

Great Men Whom We Have Known

One of our most cherished memories is of our acquaintance with Lord Kitchener. Our first meeting with him was at Fort William at Calcutta where our organization was remodeling the filtration system and where orders had been given to complete all work before the arrival of Lord Kitchener, who was expected any time. It was his habit to stay in residence there for days. 

On an inspection trip there early one morning, at which time there was a bedlam of noise through riveting steel tanks, we were approached by two men on horseback, both of whom were in "mufti" and with no insignia to indicate that they were military officers. They were (151)

evidently out for a morning canter as was the custom of many riders at that hour before the sun got in its work. 

One of them demanded: "What is all this noise about"?

Noting that the soldier on guard was at a stiff salute and that our foreman had suddenly been stricken dumb, we apologized for the racket and stated that we were carrying out orders from the Assistant Commanding Royal Engineer, which orders necessitated proceeding night and day to finish before the arrival of Lord Kitchener. 

He asked, "Are you an American"?

Being answered in the affirmative, he said, "Well, I am Kitchener."

Our embarrassment was complete, but we politely explained with all due respect, that he must understand our position and that without orders from the commanding officer of the fort we could not stop. He "grinned" at his companion and moved away, while we sweat like a darkey at election, not knowing what would happen. 

In later years, at Government House, Masonic Lodge, and at tea at his lodge at Simla, we heard repeated the story of the American who refused to be intimidated. 

That episode brought us not a little notoriety and a full measure of joy in the presence of this man whose apparent dislike of the fair sex and many of his fellowmen, made him hard to know. His hobby, as a collector of old silver and pewter tea-sets and old arms, gave us a surprise and at least one of our prized possessions passed (152)

into his hands. Britain lost a wise statesman and her number one soldier when Lord Kitchener met his death at sea.

Sir Henry McMahon, one of India's foremost executives, was chosen to organize the great Durbar at Delhi, which King George and Queen Mary graced with their presence; to build the great steel grandstands; the throne pavilion; camps for thousands of soldiers; retainers, and royal quarters for native rulers and Government guests from many lands-together with countless stables and quarters for thousands of horses, camel and elephants that were to take part in the great pageant that will go down in history as one of the world's finest. His was a colossal task, including as it did the building of a great city in the plain; creating a pure water supply, electric power and light; sewage disposal, and in fact, everything required by a city. We had a small part in that great work in installing the water-works and filtration plant and inasmuch as that turned out to be a real problem with the time element being imperative, we probably owe our recognition and subsequent presentation to the King to Sir Henry. 

We knew Wilcox, the builder of Assuan Dam; Ross, the discoverer of the malaria-carrying mosquito; Sir 

Leonard Rogers (at one time our own physician) who was knighted for his many discoveries in connection with the elimination of tropical diseases. We count it equally as great a privilege to have known intimately Mr. William Mulholland, originator and builder of the great Los Angeles aqueduct. During long residence abroad, (153)

it was our custom to make periodical trips to Europe or to the States and to Los Angeles in particular where we had made up our mind to locate when and if possible. 

The author of "The Log of the Circumnavigator," says therein that we ultimately did (as he expressed it) "gather together our doll clothes and go to Los Angeles," where we soon learned to appreciate the old adage, “A prophet is without honor in his own country." Mr. Mulholland, being interested in our tales of the Far East, sent us on an inspection trip to see a large steel siphon that had collapsed through a cloudburst washout and which had but a short time previously been put back into service. 

He told us in detail how that great siphon that had collapsed completely was, through hydraulic pressure alone, restored to its original shape and put back into service.

He gave us photographs showing the conditions before and after this had been accomplished and later sent us a copy of his report. Having slides made of such photographs, we wrote a paper on the subject which was read before a meeting of Calcutta engineers. We had assumed that this story, backed with photographic evidence, would enhance our reputation and would literally be a "knockout" insofar as American engineering was concerned. It boomeranged and how! We doubt if there was one member of that aggregation who did not at least think "bologna" if they did not actually say so. 

There were no comments and we never tried again. Such is fame. 

An introduction to Thomas A. Edison through an 1 (154)

old mutual friend, not only made possible many happy and wonderful hours in that great scientist's company, but brought the author an offer of employment which was not acceptable. At the time and prior to the discovery of the great ore deposits around Lake Superior, Mr. Edison was interested in treating low-grade ore and had installed a complete plant in a hill section of 

New Jersey where twenty-three percent ore was being run through seven sets of giant crushers, the final product of which was a very fine grade sand. This was passed vertically between magnets and deflected the iron from the rock and the result was iron of extreme purity. As it could not be safely shipped in that form to blast furnaces, it was mixed with a bonding material into briquettes.

The breaking down of the ore necessitated the use of conveyor belts, motors, etc., and the consequent trouble arising from fine sand was enough to drive a master mechanic to drink. We wore a sponge fitted muzzle for three days before making up our mind that we could not be interested in the offer of a master mechanic but we count the hours spent in the company of that great man as among the richest we have known. 

One other great man we met was ex-President Theodore Roosevelt, "Teddy" to you. The American Consul

General for India was an elderly gentleman who suffered greatly from Calcutta climate and refusing to go home and not in those days having an assistant to whom he could delegate the necessary power of signature on documents, he made arrangements with Washington under (155)

which the writer served in his office on certain days during his absence. On one of these occasions, the office received a letter from Theodore Roosevelt, who was then planning his hunting trip to Africa. This letter was a request for a bolt of Indian-made "solar cloth," a sunstroke proof material then used in India. The cloth was purchased and dispatched to Mobasa, Africa, all in accordance with instructions, and we signed a two-paragraph letter enclosing shipping documents. There was nothing in our letter but a mere formal statement of the necessary particulars and my name was signed as "Acting Consul General." 

Eight months later on one of our periodical trips home, we joined at a French port the same steamer that was taking Mr. Roosevelt back to America. He had stayed over in England to attend the funeral of King Edward or this might never have happened. The second day out on our voyage across the Atlantic, we met his secretary and asked if it would be possible to meet the Colonel, explaining that there was no axe to grind, but that admiration for the man was our sole object in making such a request. That afternoon, being accorded the opportunity sought, Colonel Roosevelt greeted me cordially and asked: "Where do you hail from"? 

To which I replied, "Calcutta, India, Sir." 

He almost shouted, "Why, you sent my cloth to me, didn't you"? 

My answer was, "How do you do it, Colonel"?

The incident had really been forgotten and had not (156)

been mentioned to his secretary. Trivial as it was, he remembered the name signed to my letter.

We were privileged to sit in with him and his associates of the hunting trip and well remember the presence on board of several prominent politicians and newspapermen who were then starting his campaign for a "come-back" as a presidential candidate, a campaign that failed and started a cleavage in the Republican ranks that has never been closed. The morning of the day of arrival in New York we were on deck before dawn and were trying to make out shadowy shapes that seemed to be accompanying ships which the fog hid. 

These turned out to be United States destroyers that had been sent out to welcome this great man and before the ship arrived at New York there were vessels from big fellows to tug-boats, following in a line over a mile long and with whistles blowing. Photographs in our possession show him going ashore on the press boat, and the procession of welcome to him up Broadway was all over before we were through the customs. 

On our first trip to England we saw Lloyd George who was then a well-known labor agitator and who on one occasion made a speech in a hall in Birmingham where the crowd tried to do him bodily harm, and from which he escaped through the back stage entrance. We knew Ramsay McDonald when he came to India and harangued the natives openly against the Indian Government. History points to both as having been a power in British rule in later years and men who occupied the highest posts in the power of their Government to grant, which seems to prove the oft-made claim that the way (157)

to cure an obstructionist is to put him in a position where his decisions affect all of his countrymen and not one class alone.

One of our near-great friends was W. C. McCabe, city engineer of Calcutta, the man who in a country where there was not a hill for one hundred miles around, built a twelve-million gallon balancing reservoir of steel, seventeen feet deep and four hundred thirty-two feet square, mounted on three hundred twenty-four-inch beams as columns, braced together in trestles each twenty feet square. He determined to get those eightysix-foot beams rolled in full length pieces and finally found a Belgian manufacturer who would do it. When completed and hauled to tide-water, he could not find a ship big enough to bring them out, and he had to have them all cut in two and put together with fish plates on the job. I wrote that up in the proceedings of the 

American Water Works Association one year and for months afterward Mr. McCabe threatened to kill me on sight. 

Strange Oil Wells in Burmah

When the British took over Burmah and deposed the Burmese King, they pensioned off the members and relatives of the royal family and to some of the latter, allotted oil lands from which quantities of oil was then being taken through unusual operating conditions. These natives dug wells by hand that were nothing more than (158)

A BURMESE OIL WELL

square holes approximately four feet square and in which framed timbers were used as cribbing to the entire depth. There was, of course, no head frame or derrick, but a simple trestle-supported beam over the well to which was attached a single wheel pulley block. A rawhide rope over that wheel was used to lower the single workman into the well.

Usually one of the wells was operated by several members of the family who lowered father into the well and hauled him and the oil in large cans to the top. 

Inasmuch as there was so much gas present at the oillevel the operator could remain below but a few minutes and often not that long. Assuming that he had scooped out enough earth to reach the oil, his procedure was to send up as many cans full as possible in rapid succession and then climb into his sling and be hauled to the top to recuperate as best he could for another round later. The rest of the family all took hold of the end of the rope and ran it so daddy came up in a hurry.

During the first World War, these people learned of the gas-mask and were able to devise some sort of a similar mask which enabled them to remain below for intervals of an hour or more, thus decreasing the hazard and increasing the output of their wells- of which there were many. However, their allotted concessions bordered on the great Burmah oil-fields which were operated by modern methods and there came a time when the oil plane gradually lowered to a point below safe depths for the old type well. Some of these were as much (159)

as four hundred feet deep. We are not certain if any of the wells are being operated now. 

Ice from Boston 

An American druggist in Calcutta, India, was the first to think of using ice in the hot months of the year and he brought out a small quantity of some forty tons from a lake near Boston, Massachusetts. It came out in a sailing vessel packed in sawdust. Its arrival created such a sensation that the Government presented him with a silver cup. Immediately following this feat, the

Government started building ice-houses up and down the east coast, each of which was provided with a huge copper-lined vat. These being ready, supplies of natural ice began arriving by sailing fleets from Boston and they continued in that trade over thirty years until the manufacture of artificial ice in Calcutta in the early eighties.

During the first World War, the old ice-house at Madras was ordered demolished in order to provide a site for new barracks. This work was advertised under bids and an old Mohammedan contractor who remembered about the construction of the building, took the contract under the stipulation that he receive free any part of the material recovered. He had made a secret inspection of the building, found the copper still there, although buried in sawdust and secured the award. 

Later, he greatly embarrassed the Government by offering them the much-needed copper. His profits on the (160)

job were of such proportions on that account as to bring about an investigation through which several officials were rather harshly dealt with for being in ignorance of the existence of the copper. 

Hides and Jute

Few Americans know that at least some of the shoes they wear were made from skins brought from India. 

All such hides must be thoroughly disinfected under the supervision of a representative of the United States Government before they are allowed to be put aboard ships. 

The American Consular office must certify to this having been done. Collecting these hides is quite a profitable business, and though a dangerous one, should famine conditions prevail, the price often fluctuates. We once stopped to talk with the Jewish member of a firm of hide exporters and to pass the time of day, asked him: "How is business with you"? 

To which he replied, "Business is damned bad for we have not had a decent famine in two years." He meant that under famine conditions, cattle, sheep and goats died in such quantities as to make hides plentiful and cheap.

Probably you also do not realize that when you purchase a sack of potatoes or a piece of linoleum for your kitchen, both the sack and a part of the useful floor covering came from a plant grown in the eastern part of India, or Bengal in particular. If you think of the sunflower plant without the flower, you can visualize (161)

what growing jute looks like. It is cut and steeped in ponds of running water, the bark stripped from the stalk and freed of its green skin. The fiber thus produced is sent to the mills located in and around Calcutta on the banks of the River Hooghly where it is made into yarns, woven into sacking or in some form and eventually finds its way into cheap suiting or cloth. In 1911, which is the only year we have a record of, jute to the value of seventy-five millions of dollars was exported from India. 

Today the collection and conveyance of the crop to Calcutta mills, taxes the resources of the rail and water transport.

Hunting Tigers 

Often asked if we ever killed a tiger, our answer has been that we never lost one and we never cared to hunt others. It is our experience that unless well fortified with the assistance of experts in that sort of sport, and reinforced with the proper equipment, you are likely to have the tiger hunting, which is not funny On one of our first visits in the vicinity of the falls of the Kaverie River in Mysore State, we spent a few nights in camp and were told that tigers were about, and on two occasions we heard them close by and found tracks of more than one beast the next morning, near enough to disturb our rest on other nights that we remained there. One soon learns that where there is a camp-fire, as we had, the cats keep their distance. Just the same, our interest in big game hunting never reached fever heat. (162)

The postal service in India is exceptional, and operated in the rural districts under conditions that are hazardous, to put it mildly. Runners serve the outlying country, hundreds of miles off the railways and these fellows run in relays carrying only a short spear equipped with two or three tiny sleigh-bells. They carry on through tiger-infested country and many of them are pounced on and killed in line of duty. Records of such losses are appalling. The mail-bag is recovered and brought in with the minimum of delay.

Reference to tigers recalls a story of a hunting party gun-bearer. This native had been educated and, like most such would-be intellectuals, he had acquired quite a collection of unusual words which they are quick to use regardless of phraseology. The story is that a tiger was shot and evidently mortally wounded and the hunter went near to investigate and was hailed by our hero, who shouted: "Do not coerce him, Sir, his strength is not yet evacuated." 

Transfer of Money and Banking

Indian notes are printed with duplicate numbers on the ends and it is permissible to cut them in two and paste them together again. This allows for the safe transport of funds from some out-of-the-way section to another by these same postal runners. As an example: in our organization there were three men employed much (163)

of the time in correctly pasting together half-notes of the same number, halves of which came in at one time, followed by the other halves when the first were acknowledged as having arrived safely. Thousands of such notes are received, and it was not unusual to see hundreds come in one consignment as payment of contracts. The incorrect joining of notes of different number voids it until the correction has been made and the loss of one-half defers payment by the mint until a period of six months has passed, when, it will be redeemed-if the other half has not reached the mint in the meantime. It might seem that postal notes or checks might be substituted for such mutilation of currency, but all too often there is neither bank nor postoffice for miles around and there never seems to be any objection raised regarding handling such money, which is often separated more than once. Naturally, it is not too safe to handle any sort of paper money in a country where leprosy and other kindred diseases exist, but one gives all too little heed to such fears.

In business, you seldom ever see the inside of the bank that you do business with. In fact, it is rather bad form for you to be seen there, for you are supposed to send a native chaprassie, which is another name for office boy, with your checks and other deposits and the same man cashes your checks and brings in the money. His caste prevents him from being robbed, and he is honest and dependable. The banking procedure necessitates that he present his check for which he received an identifying number-which is ultimately called. It may be in a half-hour or much longer, at the option of the teller, (164)

who has never been known to hurry. His number having been called, the messenger boy, who may be fifty or more years of age, but is still called boy, goes to the teller who counts out the money and passes it to another for recheck and if the amount involved is very large, it is checked by still a third teller before our man gets possession. 

That would get a white man's goat, as you can understand.

Unusual Facts About India 

Stone quarrying in India is a trade carried on by members of one caste for probably as long as such a trade has existed. In the State of Mysore one can see granite telephone poles that are rough slabs, approximately twenty-five feet high, two inches thick and twelve or fifteen inches wide. They do not show a tool mark and were quarried in single pieces with fire. We have ordered engine bed stones that were so large and heavy as to tax the lifting and hauling capacity of the most modern truck of maximum capacity. We saw those great stones quarried by building a fire on the surface and cracking water through drilled holes around the marked out section. Later we saw dozens of men harnessed to a great frame-work of light bamboo poles carry those stones and place them on the foundation without other equipment of any kind.

In our day, there was not a saw-mill in the country. 

Logs were hand-sawed over pits, with two men below and one above and so accurate is the work that floor boards are sawed, planed, tongue and grooved by hand (165)

labor, as are window-sash, frames, etc. Carpenters work with little equipment and are almost as dextrous with their toes as with their fingers. Likewise there were no cement mills and no concrete mixers in use. 

The Indian uses a short-handled digging tool that is both a pick and a shovel. The blade is somewhat like a hoe blade and set at an angle of forty degrees to the handle on which it is fastened. He uses it both to loosen the ground and to throw it out of the ditch. Give him a long-handled shovel such as are used in America, and you will see him tie a piece of rope around the handle near the blade and use another man to help operate it. 

One pulls the blade into the earth to be removed, and the other throws it out of the ditch. You may make them use the shovel, but you cannot say how this shall be done. 

Any important trunk road is bordered with trees that are under the care of the Public Works Department of States and Provinces. Each tree has a number and it is watered frequently with water carried in a goatskin. Such trees are replaced when necessary and they are protected by law, it being a penal offense to break a limb or damage them in any way. India was almost deforested, and such trees as exist in the plains districts are likewise protected.

The first multiple arch dam was built in the State of Hyderabad to impound water in Mir Alum Reservoir and though built of masonry laid in mortar of the same mix as used by the early Romans, it is still in use. That dam was constructed at the beginning of the nineteenth century.

Rhododendron plants in the foot-hills of the (166)

Himalayan Mountains often grow as trees fifty or sixty feet high. One can, in such foot-hills at elevations around six thousand feet, pick in a short time enough varieties of rare orchids as to stagger the imagination. 

Floors of homes are often made of "crazy china" which means mosaic patterns made by using broken china. However, such floors are not recommended for walking on in bare feet. Incidentally, the supply of broken china is plentiful, and will so continue as long as 

Indian servants are employed. 

One buys silver plate or ornaments by weight, the particular article being balanced by silver rupees on the other end of the scale. The value is arrived at by adding say ten percent in rupees to the amount used in weighing, this indicating the selling price. Most silver articles are made from melting down coins, against which there is no law. That was true of gold as well, when we resided there. Indian silver and goldsmiths are so expert that both gold and silver thread, lace and foil are made all over the country, and with such skill is the wire prepared that the equivalent of fifty cents worth of silver can be drawn out to eight hundred yards in length. 

Remington typewriters and Singer sewing machines may be found in the most remote parts of the Far East and are sold and operated by men who cannot speak English. Typewriters are now often made with type characters of the country in which they are sold. Sewing machines are practically all of the hand-operated type and without frame or mounting. The operator is always a man and he prefers to sit on the ground or floor. 

If you live in some of the outposts of India or (167)

Burmah and happen to be bitten by a mad dog, something that can happen all too often, you must drop everything and race against time to the nearest Pasteur Institute where you will be treated. We have traveled across the 

Bay of Bengal, a three-day trip by steamer between Rangoon and Calcutta, with men, women and children who were all bitten by the same dog in some outpost in Burmah and who would have thirty-six hours by train from Calcutta before they could receive inoculations within the time limit of such cases. There were no such treatment stations in Burmah when we lived there. Hydrophobia takes a greater toll of human life than the records show, particularly among natives who would never allow themselves to be inoculated except under compulsion. 

If you should be a good Mason and of the visiting type, you can attend twenty-two lodges for twenty-two nights in the month and have twenty-two banquets served you in the same room in the same Masonic Temple. We have known such visitors. Attending such banquets you will observe that your drinking water is poured from a strong bottle that is charged, often beyond the limits of safety. It is a bottle of a certain shape, is sealed by a glass marble against a rubber ring and at no time can the marble be removed from the bottle, it having been placed there when the bottle was blown and "crimped" to form a barrier. We have been at such gatherings where one or more bottles were knocked over on the marble floor, thus causing loud explosions that set off dozens of other bottles and scattered glass all over the room with considerable injury to those present. One of our assistants spent quite a spell in bed after he (168)

dropped such a bottle on the floor between his bare feet and ankles.

Kipling wrote the words to the song, "On the road to Mandalay, where the flying fishes play," etc. Inasmuch as Mandalay is several hundred miles up the Irrawaday River where flying fish would be ashamed to be seen, we have often wondered where Mr. Kipling was the night before he wrote those words. He goes further and says: "Where the dawn rolls up like thunder out of China 'cross the bay." Inasmuch as India is the country across the bay, and China is inland and to the east, the man must have been punch drunk. Really, poetic license has its limitations. One so versatile as that could probably tell us what Methuselah did with all his Christmas presents. 

Reminds us of the Frenchman's idea of our own idiosyncrasies when he said, "Ze Americaine he is ver' funny. Ze Frenchman can nev' understand heem, jus' like ze Americaine cocktail. Firs' he put in whiskey to make ze drink strong, zen he put in water to make her weak, zen he put in sugar to make her sweet, nex' he put in lemon to make her sour, zen he say, 'Here's to you,' an' he dreenk her he'self." 

Indian Servant Petitions

You, who have been in India or may have lived there, doubtless remember your servant. Regardless of how you acquired him, you could not escape making at least a pretense of looking over his "chits"-otherwise known as character certificates. Some are interesting, to put it (169)

mildly, and too often they are the concoction of some semi-educated native writer with forged signatures. 

We recall one of the first we ever read. In English the writer extolled the virtues of the bearer and then in a few short sentences in French stated that no greater scoundred ever drew breath. There is no telling how long that particular certificate plagued the man who proudly submitted it without knowing its actual purpose. 

These same servants and house servants can on occasions produce petitions and pleas that are examples of a class of literature that reflects the oriental manner of composition in a foreign language, and are often amusing. A few examples might bring a laugh. The first is a petition to a Government official:

occurring very shortly to my beloved wife of bosom.

Magistrate for employment to move filth, etc., but light work, for which act of kindness will, as in duty bound, ever pray for your Lordship's long life and procreativeness.

Another read: 

"Honored Sir:- Having been amputated from my family for some years, and as I have complaints of the abdomen coupled with great conflagration of the internals and prostration of all desire for work, with also the disgorging of my dinner, I hope your highness will excuse me from attending my work for ten or nine more days, and in duty bound shall ever pray for the salubrity of your temper and the enlargement of your family. May you be blessed by God Almighty, which gentleman your honor much resembles.' 

Following is still another one: "Honored Master: Having heard of your almighty mercy and loving kindness to us worms, I tell you of my circumstances. By the grace of God and your Lordship, I have seven children, all babies and sucklings, and besides this abominable litter I have many males and female relations. What have 

I done that I should be blessed with such cursed trials? As your Lordship is my father and my mother, I write to request that you will take this worm and wife and suckles and relations, both male and female and provide for us from your bounty at a remuneration of twenty rupees a month. I cannot read or write, but by the grace of God and your almighty Lordship, I look forward to years of prosperity and happiness. Call us quickly that we may (171)

fatten on your love and gentleness. Your faithful worm and beast."

And now that our story comes to a close, there comes to mind an account of a famous debate between Stephen 

Douglas and Abraham Lincoln. Douglas spoke first and the crowd shouted with delight as he touched off his best oratory and scored with biting sarcasm. At the close of the speech, the applause was prolonged and tumultous. 

Then Lincoln arose and again the crowd broke into cheers and kept it up for several minutes and as the enthuasm partially subsided, Lincoln stretched out his long right arm for silence and when he had gained it, said: "What an orator Judge Douglas is." This unexpected tribute to the popular senator aroused wild applause. More and more easily the gaunt lawyer won silence and he went on with admiring exclamations saying: "How well rounded his sentences are."

He ended his compliments with that, and when the audience had again become silent, he leaned forward and said:"? "And now, my fellow countrymen, how many of you can tell me anything he said"? 

We leave the reader to draw his own conclusions as to why we relate this tale of the two great American statesmen and to make his own interpretation of any underlying moral. Honi soit qui mal y pense. (172)
