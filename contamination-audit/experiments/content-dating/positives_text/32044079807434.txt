The Nurse in Spokane, Washington 

Purpose. To assemble and compile such information on schools of nursing in general hospitals, particularly in Spokane, as will give the prospective nurse student an insight into the vocation of nursing before registering in a school. Particular stress is laid on available national statistics collected by the Federal government and by national and local associations which promote nursing education. Local physicians and nurses as well as prospective student nurses will find current information in this study.

General Status of Nursing Education 

Our protection against disease. The nurse training schools of America are inherently a part of hospital activity. The nurse's laboratory is the hospitalan institution founded at great expense and maintained at great cost for the relief of suffering humanity. In this laboratory new nurses must be trained to recruit the depleting ranks of the profession. Just what this human laboratory is can be no better expressed than in the words of our Retired Surgeon-General of the U. S. Public Health Service, Hugh S. Cumming. 

"Every hospital is an institution built as a bulwark against disease. Every hospital is battling for the health of the people, and is serving also as an instructor of the people. In this latter service its success is proportional to its personnel, its equipment, its contact with individuals and the ease with which it concentrates the latest triumphs of surgery and medicine, the newest tools of research and the most highly trained specialists in every disease. "Few people realize that the hospital as we know it is largely an American institution. Hospitals were originally places for the destitute, almshouses in fact, for the care of the poor, but the great modern hospital is in no small measure an American institution, developed in America, patronized by all classes of society, and recognized as a proper place for the seriously sick, regardless of position or class."

Early movements to train nurses.-Hospitals have existed for centuries, but modern nursing of a scientific nature was not born until 1854 under the vision and leadership of Florence Nightingale of England. Realizing the imminent need for the professional service in nursing the sick and wounded soldiers in the English Crimean War, she organized a group of 40 nurses and launched the first movement in the world to make nursing a vocation of professional character. After the War she returned to England and organized the Nightingale 

Training School for nurses at St. Thomas' Hospital, London. The primary object of this school was to train nurses to become teachers and instructors who would seek positions in other hospitals, and educate others for the nursing profession. 

These emissaries were called the "Nightingale Missioners".

In 1863 the Red Cross was founded by Henri Dunant, a Swiss, who submitted his plan of organization to the Public Utility Society in Geneva. The plan suggested and adopted followed closely that pursued by Florence Nightingale in conducting her campaign against disease in the Crimea. 

In 1861 the Women's Hospital in Philadelphia offered the first courses in nurse training in America. In 1872, the New England Hospital for Women and 

Children, Boston, turned out the first graduate nurse. The Bellevue Hospital, New York City, was the first in America to organize along Nightingale lines.

From these beginnings the movement for educating nurses has spread very rapidly until in 1900 almost 500 nurses' training schools had been established. 

The nurse situation in 1936.—At present some 1,476 nurse training schools in the United States are listed by the American Medical Association.' Student nurses enrolled in 1,258 of these schools numbered 65,046. In 1934 the nurse training schools graduated 20,762 nurses. In 1930 the U. S. Census figures showed that there were 294,189 trained nurses and student nurses in this country. In addition, there were 153,443 untrained nurses and midwives competing with the trained nurses. So much unemployment in the nursing field has resulted in recent years that there has been a decided tendency to abolish the smaller nurse training schools and to curtail the number of student nurses in the larger institutions. In fact, in the past 5 years the number of accredited schools has dropped 18 percent..' In view of this situation, it is advisable for the prospective nurse to "look before she leaps" into this life career. 

Opportunities in the various fields of nursing.—The Committee on the 

Grading of Nursing Schools found that 54 per cent of all graduate nurses enter the field of private duty; they nurse individual patients in the home or hospital. 

Greenleaf³ maintains that this field is over-crowded because too many nurses are being graduated and because the field is open to persons who have not graduated from standardized training schools. If state legislatures would prohibit non-registered nurses from practicing in this field of activity the profession would be less crowded.

The Committee also found that 19 per cent of the graduate nurses enter public health work, such as that fostered by city school systems and by city, county or state departments of public health, by non-official health agencies and by industrial establishments. About 4 per cent of the graduate nurses enter private office work, teaching, laboratory work, or Red Cross work. 

In his Leaflet on Nursing³ published in 1932, Greenleaf states that there were about 7,000 hospitals in the United States, but only 1,800 maintained nurse training schools. Consequently, 5,200 hospitals must depend upon the other 

1,800 hospitals to train their staff of nurses. According to the Committee on the 

Grading of Nursing Schools, only 23 per cent of the graduate nurses enter this field, known as institutional duty.

In Spokane there is a nurse training school in each of the three general hospitals, but the 18 smaller hospitals and sanitariums, which do not maintain nurse training schools, employ a staff of nurses who are trained elsewhere. Requirements for admission to nurse training schools.—Most nurse training schools require candidates for admission to be at least 18 years of and to pass a very rigid physical examination. Many require applicants to have been vaccinated against small-pox and inoculated against typhoid fever within the year preceding admission to the school. 

In 1934, 37 states required the completion of a four-year high school course; 

6 states, the completion of 2 years; and 5 states, the completion of 1 year. Washington requires four years of high school for admission. While these facts concern the legal or state requirements, many nurse training schools require additional educational qualifications. For local requirements consult Table 1.

The Committee on the Grading of Nursing Schools postulates five qualities necessary for a good nurse¹: (1) She must be skillful, (2) She must have keen observation and awareness of the patient's condition, (3) She must be able to apply general theory to concrete situations, (4) She must have ability to judge and organize, (5) She must have sympathy and understanding. 

The Nightingale pledge.-The code of ethics for nurses is probably best expressed in the Nightingale pledge:

I solemnly pledge myself before God and in the presence of this assembly:

To pass my life in purity and to practice my profession faithfully. 

I will abstain from whatever is deleterious and mischievous, and will not take or knowingly administer any harmful drug. 

I will do all in my power to elevate the standard of my profession, and will hold in confidence all personal matters committed to my keeping, and all family affairs coming to my knowledge in the practice of my profession. 

With loyalty will I endeavor to aid the physician in his work, and devote myself to the welfare of those committed to my care. 

A woman's vocation.-Like teaching and stenographic work, nursing is primarily a woman's vocation, although nearly one per cent of the student nurses are men. This situation exists because men nurses must invariably nurse male patients, whereas, women nurses may care for both male and female patients. The 1930 Census lists 288,737 women as "trained nurses" and 5,452 men, who constitute less than two per cent of the total. Fifteen schools of nursing in the United States admit men only and 57 other institutions accept male students.

In June, 1936, fifty-six girls, who were seniors in the high schools of Spokane, expressed a preferential desire to become nurses and many of them are now enrolled in the nurse training schools of the city. 

Length of training.—Each state designates the length of time that a student nurse must spend in training for the profession. In 1934, 37 states required 36 months; one (Kansas) required 30 months; one (California) required 28 months; 9 required 24 months; and one (Nevada) has no school and no requirement. The first 3 to 6 months of the nurse training course is known as the probationary period. If a student does not demonstrate her ability to master the work during this "try-out" period she is generally requested to discontinue the course. 

Hours of duty required daily.-The typical nurse training "school has a four-month period at the beginning of the course in which students are on duty for an average of two hours a day in the first month, three hours a day in the second month, and four hours a day in the third and fourth months".^

After the probationary period the number of hours of duty required daily as a part of the nurse-training course in general hospitals has been steadily decreasing until the past few years. In 1896 the 8-hour day was in effect in less than 2 percent of the schools; by 1920 a third had reduced the number of hours of duty to 8; and in 1931 two-thirds of the schools required only 8 hours." In 

1934 the Committee on the Grading of Nursing Schools found that 96 percent of the nursing schools required students to be on active duty in the wards 8 hours a day or more in addition to the time spent in attending or preparing for classes. In 88 percent of the schools they worked more than 48 hours a week on day duty and in 93 percent of the schools they worked more than 48 hours a week on night duty. This tendency to revert to the old practice has undoubtedly been caused by the recent "hard times", which compelled hospitals to depend more and more on inexpensive student help in the wards. 

The Committee states, “Unless class and study time are included the 48hour week is too long for student nurses”.

In schools connected with hospitals for the insane, however, about onefourth require 12 hours of duty; a fourth, 10 hours; and less than a third, 8 hours.5

The better training schools give three weeks vacation annually. 

The curriculum of the nurse training school.-The typical nurse training school now gives in its three-year course 761 hours of theoretical work and 7,095 hours of practice work in the laboratory or in the wards of the hospital. The curriculum published by the National League of Nursing Education suggests 885 hours of theory and 6,252 hours of practice. This theoretical work embraces classroom instruction in the professional subjects and in the six basic sciences: bacteriology, chemistry, dietetics, anatomy, materia medica and pathology. What a nurse student does.—The following page taken from the diary of 

Isabel Johnson, a student in St. Luke's Hospital, Spokane, Washington, vividly portrays her hospital activity.

IT'S ALL IN A DAY'S WORK 

Seven o'clock in the morning!—And with it comes a group wearing freshly starched aprons and bibs covering an equally neat striped uniform. All wait quietly while a night nurse reports patient after patient-his condition, medications, or any unusual happenings of the night. But alas! it isn't without interruptions, there's the telephone. "Yes, Mrs. Brown, Jimmie rested very well last night. No, he didn't cry after you left. Yes, Dr. Smith says he may go home today." With a nod of the Supervisor's head the group scatters, each to study his own day's schedule on a well planned work sheet. 

A well balanced day must begin with a well balanced diet. One by one the trays are taken from the ledge and carried to patient after patient. This means that it's breakfast time in the ward, but most certainly doesn't mean that all the patients are going to eat the food. Here is a despondent, neurotic lady who must be coaxed and encouraged to eat the first bite of cereal. There is a boy in a body cast unable to reach his toast, and one must lend a helping hand as the rounds are made from bed to bed. 

A warm bath and snowy linen leaves a happier, brighter face against the pillow when the nurse passes on to her next charge. The mid-morning is filled with the doctors making the daily rounds to see their patients, cheering and encouraging all those coming in contact with them. 

The ward is soon settled in order and the trays begin their travels for the lunch hour. A peaceful, quiet afternoon begins, broken only by an occasional visitor to see a sick friend. 

Down in room 48, a sweet, white-haired old lady quietly closes her eyes and slips away from this world, her hand slowly relaxing in that of the young nurse. But the calm assurance of both nurses and doctors passing in and out would betray nothing unusual. The facial expression must ever remain the same, whether caring for the new-born babe, or closing the eyes in death.

The afternoon is swiftly drawing to a close. Young student nurses, just learning the routine, aid patients in brushing their teeth and sponge their hands and face, preparatory to the evening meal.

Colleges offering work in nursing.—In 1931, 33 colleges and universities³ offered courses leading to a bachelor's degree in nursing, usually the Bachelor of Science degree. These schools seldom pay a monthly stipend to student nurses and the institution is affiliated with a local hospital in which student nurses receive practical experience. The course offered by these colleges is usually five years in length, two or three years of which are spent in regular college work, and the latter years in the school of nursing. In 1931 almost 4,300 students were enrolled in such courses and nearly 1,000 were graduated with degrees. The 

University of Washington, School of Nursing, Seattle, is the only college in the state maintaining a nurse training school. It enrolled 84 students in 1936. Those desiring a degree in nursing should request information and catalogs from the principal of the school under consideration. 

Accredited schools of nursing.-To be accredited by the Director of Licenses, Harry C. Huse, Olympia, Washington, a school must offer not less than two years of work in a general hospital. The Director of Licenses appoints a qualified nurse to inspect schools of nursing and supervise and maintain standards therefor. Such schools are accredited annually. The inspector must be registered in the State of Washington and must have had at least three years of practical experience after graduation and be, at the time of appointment, actively engaged in teaching and training nurses in the State. Likewise all nurses on the examining board must have had three years experience after graduation from a nurse training school. A list of schools accredited by the State Board of Nurse 

Examiners is given in Tables 1 and 3.

Selecting a school for training.-Make sure that the school selected will enable you to pass the necessary examinations when the course is completed. Select a school accredited by the American Medical Association or the State Board of Nurse Examiners. Consider only schools having at least 50 patients daily, so as to gain a variety of practical experience. Choose a school having a corps of instructors who are college graduates as well as experienced registered nurses. Be

Registration of nurses in Washington.-The Washington State law requires that no nurse shall be allowed to represent herself to be a registered nurse until she has passed a state examination held by the examining board. The applicant must be at least 20 years of age, of good moral character, and free from habits that would interfere with her efficiency as a nurse. After March 1, 1936, the applicant must be a high school graduate and also a graduate of an accredited nurse training school of a reputable hospital. Examinations are held twice each year at Seattle and Spokane, usually in January and July. Write Harry C. Huse,

Director of Licenses at Olympia, for exact date of examinations. The fee for the examination is $10.00. Questions include surgical nursing, bacteriology and communicable diseases, materia medica, dietetics, medical nursing, obstetrics, gynecology, hygiene, pediatrics, anatomy and physiology and the history of nursing. The certificate of registration granted to those who pass must be renewed annually in January upon application and payment of a fee of $1.00. 

The board may register nurses from other states without an examination if such state has standards practically the same as those maintained in Washington. 

The usual fees, however, must be paid. Failure to register annually entails a small fine and sufficient delay may result in cancellation of her certificate of registration. Certificates of registration may also be revoked by the board of examiners for good cause upon 30 days notice if a nurse is found guilty of fraud or acts contrary to the established code of ethics. 

In Washington it is legal for any one to nurse gratuitously or for hire but it is illegal to represent one's self as a registered nurse unless a certificate has been awarded by the Director of Licenses. This provision permits many unqualified persons to practice nursing in this state and tends to lower the standards which the nurse training schools are striving to maintain. 

Organizations to promote the nursing profession. In recent years many associations of nurses have come into existence. The National League of Nursing Education, the oldest national organization in the United States, was founded in 1893, and is now affiliated with the American Nurses Association, located at 50 W. 50th Street, New York City. Both organizations have done much to elevate the standards for schools of nursing in this country. 

The American Journal of Nursing, the official monthly magazine published by these two national organizations, is indispensable to those engaged in nurs- 15

ing. The Public Health Nurse, published at the same address by the National 

Organization for Public Health Nursing, is valuable to nurses engaged in public health work. The Pacific Coast Journal of Nursing, 72 Call Bldg., San Fran cisco, Calif., is a very creditable magazine on nursing and has special local interest for nurses practicing in states along the Pacific Coast. 

Active membership in the N. L. N. E. is granted only to those who have (1) graduated from an accredited school of nursing connected with a hospital having an average of 30 patients, or more, daily, (2) completed at least 2 years of training, (3) are registered in at least one state, (4) are members of the A. N. A., or are actively “engaged in an advisory, executive or teaching position in an educational, preventive, or government service". These requirements limit the membership in the N. L. N. The midwife in Washington state.—Since 1917 no one has been allowed to practice midwifery in Washington who has not been licensed to do so by the 

Director of Licenses, Harry C. Huse, Olympia, Washington. The certificate to practice midwifery must be recorded in the office of the county clerk in the county in which the holder of such certificate is practicing. Examinations, held twice annually, include questions on a great number of technical subjects in the field of obstetrics. The fee for examination is $15.00. An applicant for examination must present to the board a certificate or diploma from a legally incorporated school of midwifery in good standing, granted after at least two courses of instruction of at least 7 months each in different calendar years. The application must be endorsed by a registered reputable physician. Files at the county clerk's office list only about a dozen midwives in Spokane county. The 

State law requires these midwives to possess educational attainments scarcely inferior to those of professional nurses.

Compensation of nurses in training.—Nursing is one profession in which the student often earns pin-money while learning. In fact, nursing is about the only vocation in which a student meets her own expenses during training. The tendency of schools to compensate their students is revealed in the table which follows. In training schools in general hospitals, 15 percent offer no remuneration for services during training. About 46 percent pay less than $10.00 per month during the first year and more than 50 percent pay between $10.00 and $15.00 per month during the second and third years of the course. The remuneration is much higher in schools connected with hospitals for the insane, 48 percent paying between $25.00 and $50.00 per month during the first year.

In Spokane, before the depression, the hospitals paid their student nurses at rates varying from $60.00 to $144.00 per year, but more recently the situation has changed because hospitals cannot afford this expense. Then too, the hospitals are attempting to reduce the student enrollment by demanding higher entrance requirements and by charging tuition instead of paying a small stipend. Students in each of the three schools, however, receive full maintenance while in training and are housed either in cottages (Deaconess) or a nurses' home (St. Luke's and Sacred Heart). Sacred Heart Hospital, although diminishing by one dollar annually the allowance, still pays its students $4.00 per month after the probationary period, but the other two hospitals in Spokane grant no monthly allowances.

Compensation of registered nurses. The prospective nurse will wish to know something concerning the salary she may expect after graduation and registration. No current national figures are now available, but ten years ago in halycon days, the Committee on the Grading of Nursing Schools found that nurses on private duty in 1926 were earning a salary of $1,297.00 (median) annually, with partial maintenance. The Committee also found that public health nurses received an annual salary of $1,685.00 (median), with no maintenance. Higher institutional nurses were paid $1,500.00 (median) annually, with full maintenance. Floor duty in hospitals paid $1,152.00 with full maintenance.

In 1932 the Grading Committee' found that the staff nurses, excluding bedside and floor duty nurses, in hospitals received a median salary of $1,205.00 with full maintenance. The corresponding figures for 1929 were $1,274.00; showing that institutional salaries dropped $69.00 in the first three years of the depression.

In the United States Veterans' Bureau, nurses' salaries vary from $1,860.00 to $2,260.00 with maintenance. In the United States Public Health Service the salaries range from $1,920.00 for staff nurses to $2,700.00 for chief nurses. In the 

Indian Service staff nurses are paid from $1,800.00 to $2,100.00. In the Army and the Navy Nurse Corps salaries range from $840.00 to $1,560.00 with subsistence, the salary depending upon length of service.³

If the nursing vocation as a field for employment is not already overcrowded, it very shortly will be if the present rate of increase is maintained. 

The very rapid development in this field of education has become necessary because American home life has been breaking down. But wide-spread unemployment among nurses today may indicate that the "saturation point" has been reached or that people who need medical attention are unable to employ trained nurses.

The trained nurse in Washington State.-The 1930 census listed 4,588 trained nurses and 1,563,396 persons residing in the State of Washington, or one trained nurse for every 341 persons, compared with 1 to 417 for the United States as a whole. Washington therefore ranks considerably above the average in nursing care. 1 to 181. As Spokane appears to be better supplied with nurses than Seattle or

Tacoma, an unemployed nurse in Spokane may find a better opportunity for employment in either Seattle or Tacoma.

Spokane ranks second in the group with one nurse to every 181 citizens. Albany is first with a ratio of 1 to 176. For the 12 cities studied the ratio is 1 to 250. Nearly all of the specialists are engaged in public health work or in institutional duty. Only 8 other nurses had taken any graduate work in nursing but they do not consider themselves specialists.

Another grouping of replies shows that 224 are active registered nurses, 9 retired registered nurses and 5 non-registered nurses. The retired list generally includes those who have recently been married, who have families to care for or who have become too old for active service. 

Marital state and employment.—Almost 80 percent of the nurses are single, about 14 percent are married and about 6 percent are widowed or divorced. 

About 60 percent of the nurses reporting are regularly employed, over 33 percent are irregularly employed and almost 7 percent are unemployed. Irregular employment exists wholly among private duty nurses, who must wait for cases. 

Public health nurses in the city school system are often unemployed throughout the summer vacation, but they are not considered as unemployed in this report. 21

Practically all married nurses are irregularly employed or unemployed. 

Often they have home duties to perform and accept cases only for the purpose of earning a little extra money. In fact, most nurses who marry are practically on a retired list. It should be mentioned, however, that the education received in a nurse training school is a decided asset to a wife or mother in her home and that such training has not been in vain when a nurse decides to marry. 

Opinion of nurses.-Over 90 percent of the 230 nurses answering the question, "Would you again select nursing as a life career?", replied in the affirmative, but almost 10 percent preferred some other vocation. The nurses replying in the negative usually had very low annual incomes. 

Of 217 nurses voicing an opinion as to whether the nursing profession is over-crowded 126, or over 58 percent, thought it was, and almost 42 percent thought it was not over-crowded. Altogether 130 nurses named the field which they considered over-crowded: 112 nurses, (or 86 percent), named private duty or special duty; 6, general duty; 4, supervision, clinical or other specialization; and 6 nurses thought all fields were over-crowded. Two other nurses said the field was "over-crowded with unqualified nurses". 

On the other hand, 196 opinions were expressed as to the nursing fields least crowded: 59 nurses named public health; 22, psychiatry; 25, general duty; 

15, supervision; 14, specialization; 7, teaching; 7, institutional duty; 4, private duty; 1, office duty; and 42 nurses designated some specialized field, such as pediatrics, obstetrics, surgery, etc.

It will be noted that the fields thought to be least crowded are generally found in institutions and public health. It appears that all such positions in Spokane are now filled and that there will be few positions in institutions or public health work available to graduating or practicing nurses until present incumbents die, resign or change positions, or until officials see fit to create new positions. These fields will undoubtedly absorb a few qualified nurses from time to time and occasionally a hospital or a school board cannot secure certain types of nurses when needed, but at present the outlook in these two fields is not very promising. When such openings do occur there should be a few well qualified nurses for each of the major branches indicated in these replies. 

Age of nurses. The average age of a nurse in Spokane is about 30 years. 

This average, however, is very misleading because the inclusion of the ages of a few very old nurses makes the average non-representative of the group. The median age of 26 years tells a truer story because there are as many nurses under this astonishingly low number as there are above it. Of the 238 nurses reporting, 104, or almost 44 percent, are from 21 to 25 years of age. The low median age of nurses in Spokane makes it very doubtful if the average professional life of 22

a nurse in this locality is 17 years, as was found by the Committee on the Grading of Nursing Schools for the country as a whole in 1934.* 

Fourteen nurses reporting are 50 years of age, or over.

The average experience of the 9 nurses who have retired within the past year is 8 years but the median experience is only 4 years. 

The average professional experience of the 229 active nurses is 7 years but the median experience is only 4 years. When classified, it is found that 134 nurses, or almost 59 percent, have had from 1 to 5 years experience; 46, from 

6 to 10 years; 28, from 11 to 20 years; 17, from 21 to 30 years; and only 4, from 

31 to 41 years.

The reports of the 238 nurses show that 82 were trained in the Sacred Heart School of Nursing; 60, in the Deaconess; 50, in St. Luke's; and 46 were trained in schools of nursing outside Spokane. Thus, the training schools in Spokane supply almost 81 percent of the nurses practicing in the city.

Conclusions. The nurse in Spokane.—It appears from the foregoing discussion: 

1. That the United States has one trained nurse for each 417 people. 

2. That Washington has one for each 341 people. 4. 

That Spokane has one for every 181 people. Albany, New York, is the only city considered that has more nurses to the population than Spokane.

That at present 40 percent of the nurses in Spokane are irregularly employed. 

5. That private duty nurses are employed 60 percent of the time. 

6. That the average salary of a nurse in Spokane is $1,178.00. 

7. That the average salary of those not regularly employed in institutions and public health is $935.00. 

8. That the beginning annual salary of private duty nurses graduating between 1930 to 1935, inclusive, is $835.00. 

9. That the average beginning salary of nurses graduating between 1930 to 1935, inclusive, who found employment in institutions, is $786.00, generally with full maintenance additional. 

10. That the average institutional salary in Spokane is $1,548.00 in cash and maintenance. 

26

11. That the average salary of public health nurses in Spokane is $1,633.00 in cash. 

12. That specialists, including public health nurses, receive $1,699.00 in cash and maintenance. 

13. That institutional specialists receive the highest average salaries, viz., $1,718.00 in cash and maintenance. 

14. That the wide-spread unemployment among private duty nurses and the low salaries received by them indicate that this field of nursing is decidedly over-crowded. 

15. That all public health and institutional fields are already filled, but when nurses are needed in these specialized fields, it is difficult to find competent candidates. 

16. That the median age of a Spokane nurse is 26 years, indicating that many nurses have left the profession. 

It appears from the foregoing summary that there may be need for a few public health nurses and for nurses in rural communities, but all such positions are now filled. A few such nurses will be needed annually as new positions are created, or when present employees resign or are replaced. It is also evident that there is need for nurses who have specialized in some particular institutional field, such as obstetrics, pediatrics, X-ray, operating room technique, tuberculosis, psychiatry, anesthetics, dietetics, teaching, supervising, and general duty, since Spokane is not well supplied with specialists who are capable of filling positions in such fields when vacancies occur. There seems to be a surplus of private duty nurses, because many such nurses are today scarcely able to find profitable employment. The majority of graduate nurses go into this field where employment is irregular and wages are comparatively low. Those who would go highest in the profession must meet more than minimum entrance requirements, must enroll in a good school and must specialize by taking post-graduate work in nursing-for in a profession so rapidly becoming over-crowded only the fittest will survive. year

DEFINITION OF TERMS 

Anesthetist-A specialist skilled in administering drugs causing insensibility to pain such as ether or nova-caine.

Bacteriology-The science which deals with the study of micro-organisms called bacteria, many of which may cause infection and disease. 

Dietetics-That branch of science dealing with the selection, preparation, and prescription of proper foods for the body in health and in disease. 

Gynecology-That branch of medicine which treats of women, their diseases, their hygiene, etc.

Materia-medica-The material or subjects treated in the science of medicine. 

Median-When quantities are arranged in order of magnitude the one above and below which there is an equal number is called the median. 

Obstetrics-The art or science of assisting women in child-birth, and of treating the diseases incident to pregnancy and after delivery.

Orthopedic-A term applied to the correction or prevention of deformities, especially in children.

Pathology-That branch of medical science which treats of diseases, their essential nature, causes, and development, and the constructional and functional changes produced by them.

Pediatrics-That branch of medical science which treats of the hygiene and diseases of children.

Psychiatry-Medical treatment of diseases of the mind. 

X-ray-technician-One skilled in photographing the parts of the body by use of an X-ray machine. 34
