INTRODUCTION 

How strong are the impressions made upon the mind and heart of a child! How the memory of incidents lives on into the sunset years, binding and holding to far-away places and long-ago time!

Days that were joyous and days that were filled with gloom. Friends loved and lost. Friends unseen for long, long years are ever near in the wonderful Book of Memory. 

In the life of the author there has been more retrospection than introspection. More looking backward to the past than forward to the future or realizing the present. 

Yet, after all of life's vicissitudes-the remembrance of the South is pleasant-the mind-pictures are beautiful and dear.

Home and friends of that far-away time in the Sunny South are loved and cherished still. No hatred, no resentment to embitter and sadden may mar the swiftly passing years.

So, "with malice toward none and charity for all" I offer this record of the events of the first few years of my life as I have remembered them and as they were told to me by those who had participated in the happenings of that turbulent time.

MEMORIES OF THE SUNNY SOUTH CHAPTER I. 

ALABAMA 

Days of warm sunshine and balmy breezes, songs of birds and perfume of flowers. A road, white with sand, running past a house in a yard shaded by trees of live-oak, cedar and pine; a "double" house of hewn logs with a "passage" between the two parts. A kitchen, detached from the main building and shaded by a wide-spreading beech tree. A well with bucket, rope and windlass, a garden where grew mint and thyme, sage and garlic, bird-egg beans, "salad" peas, sweet potatoes and okra.

Across the garden two graveled walks crossing_in_the center of the garden and bordered with flowers. Lovely flowers, old-fashioned flowers. Their rainbow hues, the entrancing perfume, linger yet but their names are lost in the mists of time.

A path from the house to the "big road" where the leaves of the trees made moving pictures in the path and on the grass.

Across the wide, white road were the barn, cribs and other smaller buildings: the out-door part of my Southern home as I recall it in the very early years of my childhoodthe Sunny South.

And it is thus I like to remember it. 

The interior was not less pleasant and interesting to the questing and questioning mind of a child. 

Inside the house a wide fire-place, open windows admitting morning sunlight and balmy breezes. Bed-steads with posts reaching toward the ceiling; feather beds of luxurious size and softness, with "valances" concealing the trundle-bed underneath that was "trundled" out at night-that children might sleep and dream their roseate dreams. Beds whose furnishings were the work of mother's hands-feather ticks, straw ticks, sheets, woolen blankets and coverlets of blue and white-iris design-blue on one side and white on the other and quilts of quaint design and many colors made those beds a "thing of beauty and a joy forever."

Home-made furniture-chairs with seats made of thin, narrow strips of wood staunchly woven and frames of walnut or oak fashioned by a turning-lathe by hand-good to see, more restful and more enduring than modern upholstery. 3.

Carpets woven of bright colored strips of cloth-the old time rag carpet. 

In the detached building-kitchen and workshop combined -were the tools with which miracles were wrought. Cardsnot "bridge" nor "500" but cards that, manipulated by skillful hands, produced rolls-not money but snowy white rolls of cotton or creamy wool for spinning into thread. Windingblades on which the thread was wound into skeins for coloring and weaving; a big loom on which cloth of many kinds and grades was woven. The dyes used to color the thread before weaving were the dyes of long ago; copperas, lumpindigo, logwood and barks of trees-oak and hickory, from which lovely colors were produced-black, brown, blue, red, green and yellow-of varying shades. Skilled fingers, guided by a clear brain placed those colored threads in the loom so that when woven they made checks or stripes of any desired size or width. Miracles wrought by mother's hands, head and heart.

In the kitchen end a wide fire-place where coals of pine wood glowed from beneath old-fashion "fire dogs" (andirons). 

Above hung the iron pot-hook on which iron pots for cooking things good to eat were suspended. On the walls hung other cooking utensils. On a low shelf was a large iron oven with heavy iron lid. In this oven, placed on its short legs over a mass of hot coals and covered by the lid on which was placed more hot coals, could be cooked (baked) anything and everything that can be baked in a modern oven. Against the wall stood a safe and a cupboard-the work of hands skilled in carpentry-father's.

In the center of the floor was a large table (also the work of father's hands) and on this table could always be found a pitcher of milk, a plate of bread and a pot of butter, all neatly covered. From there we children—a big brother, four small sisters and a baby brother-could enjoy a between-meals luncheon without mother having to leave her work at cards, spinning wheel or loom.

When outside the children can know by the sounds inside what mother is doing; the swish of the cards, the whir of the winding-blades, the hum of the spinning wheel, the rattle and bang of the loom informs them. 

Mother of blessed memory! Of such an one we read in 

Proverbs, 31st chapter, verses 13, 19, 27, 28, 31: "She seeketh wool and flax and worketh willingly with her hands. "She layeth her hands to the spindle and her hands hold the distaff. "She looketh well to the ways of her household and eateth not the bread of idleness. "Her children rise up and call her blessed; her husband also and he praiseth her. -4.

"Give her of the fruit of her hands and let her own works praise her in the gates."

ALABAMA-MEANING: HERE WE REST 

Land of nightingale songs and magnolia blossoms, of fields of waving green and billowy white. Land where the muscadine climbs and flavors the air with its luscious fruit; where the passion vine trails its green leaves and purple and white blossoms, and across the fields of white and green, across the river and the forests the blue, blue mountains, bathed in sunlight or veiled in mist. 

Sunny South of early recollection, Somber South of later experience. A chill was in the air. A cloud seemed to shadow all our world. Neighbors that once were bosom friends passed by unsmiling. Children with whom we were wont to play in delightful harmony met us with timid, puzzled glances. 

The happiness of childhood is not easily quenched, so, the days went by, filled with work and play, the hurt almost forgotten. 

Father seemed more thoughtful, sometimes excited and often troubled. He read the newspapers diligently, with knitted brow. He attended meetings, the purpose of which we were not told. From these meetings he came each time, seemingly more excited, more troubled-even angry. From the few words we heard of low conversations with mother we learned the words Rebel and Yankee. The former we feared, the latter we trusted not to do us harm. Why, we did not know.

The cloud grew darker and spread, shadowing all our little world. The chill in the air increased-ominous of coming disaster. Still we knew not why. 

There came a time when men came riding to our gate calling for father. He would go out to them, genial and jolly as usual.

There would be a low-toned, brief conversation, after which father would return to the house, get his hat and coat and say to mother, "Sarah, I'm going with these men, I shan't be gone long." I saw mother's face grow pale and her eyes fill with fear but she said never a word. 

They rode away. Mother put away her work and went into the garden. We children ceased playing and sat in silence. Late in the evening father came home. We did not ask why he went away or where he had been. Mother knew why but not where. In this manner he was called away repeatedly. 

Each time the fear and dread that it caused grew greater. 

Finally, after being kept away a night and a day he returned. "Sarah," he said, "we are going away. We will start tonight; get ready." All our horses had been taken by the soldiers. 

How were we to move? 

An uncle of father's offered a team and said, "Goas quickly as you can.”

CHAPTER II. 

INITIAL SKIRMISH 

More than three quarters of a century ago a cloud "no bigger than a man's hand" hung over our country. It grew larger and darker. The light of peace was fading. A chill was in the air. Homes were wrapped in gloom and hearts were heavy with anxiety.

The warm, mellow light of the Southern sun seemed pale and cold. A storm of fearful magnitude was ominous. 

And yet it was said by many who watched "It will pass -we should not be afraid." 

But hark! What was that sound? 

Was it thunder? No!

It was the far-off sound of canons. 

Eighteen hundred Sixty-one. 

The storm was upon us.

Our country was whelmed in the horrors of war. 

The bonds of friendship were rent asunder. 

The ties of kinship were forgotten or ignored. 

Life-long friends met and passed with scarce a friendly word.

Families were divided. Some giving whole-hearted allegiance to the Cause of the South, enlisting, donning the uniform of gray and marching gaily away to fight, perchance to die, for that which they believed to be a Righteous Cause. 

Others standing firmly for Union were confronted by a great problem. To join the Southern Army was against their ideas of right. Their wish to enter the Union ranks was confronted by obstacles seemingly unsurmountable. 

How could those obstacles be overcome? 

How could they reach the Union camps? 

How elude the Vigilance Men who watched their every move? What course could they devise that would carry them safely to the Union lines? If they should succeed in reaching 6-

the Northern Army what would become of their familiesalone in a hostile land?

A few to whom these problems seemed unsoluable, to avoid conscription, hid in the mountains-venturing to go home at rare intervals.

Others, many that were friends and relatives of the Wileys, made the venture, arrived safely at some Union camp -enlisted and fought through the war and at its close came north-many to Illinois, where they met their families that had come from the south-alone-to meet father, husband, brother, son. Here some of them remained, having fallen in love with the state of their adoption-Illinois !

These men who reached the Union lines were men who were reserved in expressing their opinion in regard to the Rebellion-quiet, deep-thinking men, thus suspicion was allayed and the watchfulness of the vigilants eluded. 

A few seemed willing-even anxious-to express their opinions anywhere and everywhere-publicly or privately; unreservedly espousing the Union Cause, warning all of the seriousness of the situation and the probable results of war. 

Such a one was he of whom these chapters are written: 

James Wiley, son of William Wiley and his wife, Elizabeth 

Webster Wiley. William was a red-haired Scotch-Irish American, his wife a Virginian-half French, half English. 

From his father James inherited the stubborness of the Scotch and the Irish love of fighting; from his mother his dark eyes and hair and his impulsive temper. 

Born in Alabama in the year 1821 he was at the time of this writing in his early forties. 

He loved books and read many; diligently perused the newspapers for the latest word-from North and South, studying the "signs of the times" and forming opinions-pro and con.

In politics he was, he said, a Douglas Democrat and adhered to the Douglas principles until somewhere near the time of the Lincoln-Douglas debate he experienced "a change of heart" and became a Lincoln Republican. Whether reading the Lincoln-Douglas debate was the cause of the change the writer is not informed.

But the change was made, swiftly and surely and remained, growing stronger and stronger as long as life lasted. 

He was not a slave-holder but at one time was overseer of a large plantation owned by a Mr. Gardner. But he was a carpenter by trade and preferred that occupation.

How those women-alone-with little children, managed to make their way from the South, through the chaos-difficulties and dangers of a country in the throes of civil war, none who had not that experience could imagine. On their arrival here they became Northerners by adoption.

Their children and their children's children may well remember them with love and pride.

The old time southern women "of the Middle Class" with their skill in many industries, their strength of mind, their love of home and family-how wonderful they were. 

The following incidents were told to the writer and jotted down just as given 25 or 30 years ago. He spoke of this as The Initial Skirmish. I give the story as I heard it. 

In the year 1860 a meeting was held in the small town of Vienna, Ala., for the purpose of rallying the forces of the 

Southern Confederacy. Masten, a very wealthy slave holder was the main speaker. In the course of his address he said: "If the South will but show itself united and ready to adhere to its principles, England will help us. England has said that we need only to ask and she will 'bridge the Great 8-

Waters from Liverpool to Charleston with war vessels, land 100,000 troops and furnish all munitions of war'." 

After Masten came Wright, Butler, Elliston and others -all expressing sentiments similar to those of Masten. 

Then there was a call for Wiley. "Wiley, Wiley! Jim Wiley!"

He was forced forward-almost lifted to the platform. 

He stood for a few moments silent, seemingly trying to adjust his thoughts-deciding.

A TYPICAL MIDDLE-CLASS SOUTHERNER 

It has been said that Southern society at this period consisted of three distinct classes: The upper or higher class consisting of wealthy slave-holding planters, professional men and their families.

The second, or middle-class, made up of mechanics, small planters, school teacher, etc.

Into the third class was placed the so-called "poor white trash" or mountaineers. Well! The writer accepts this classification with the explanation that the arrangement is like a pie-The best part in the middle! The restless eyes flash. The mouth closes firmly. His every appearance is antagonistic. He knows some who are urging him to speak are aware of his Union sentiments. They expect him to retract all that he has heretofore expressed, both privately and publicly, against secession. He knows that in order to terrify and coerce Union men into joining their ranks Vigilance Committees have been organized and were everywhere-alert and watchful.

In the face of all this will he dare express thus publicly his honest convictions?

Erect, unflinching he waits for the tumult to subside, then speaks: "Friends and Fellow Citizens:-Mr. Masten has said that

England has promised to 'bridge the Great Water with war vessels, from Liverpool to Charleston, land one hundred thousand troops and furnish all munitions of war to aid the Southern Confederacy. I, for one, do not thank England for such a proposition. It cost us years of war and the sacrifice of many lives to rid our country of English tyranny. Why should England make such a proposition? Simply for the pleasure of seeing us divided and torn asunder as a nation." 

Here his voice was drowned by cries of "Take him down! Take him down!"

A man The crowd surged forward, threateningly. A mounted a box and called out: "An enemy at home is worse than five enemies abroad and I say Take him down!"

Soon he was in the hands of the Vigilance Commitee, hustled out of town and taken to a camp where men who were obstreperous were cajoled or coerced into submission. -9-

All their methods failing to subdue the stubborn Scotch and fight-loving Irish tendencies that were an heritage he was released and allowed to return home. 

Soon after companies of soldiers were made up, nearly all of whom were sent to Virginia. Every man who would not declare for Secession, or expressed Union sentiments sought a more congenial atmosphere-in the mountains. 

Some escaped to the Union army, leaving their families alone to make their way north if they could. 

Many of these men were captured and taken to a camp called Bragg's Camp of Execution. Anyone who brought in "refugees" received thirty dollars and the same amount for each horse brought in.

Others were tracked down by blood-hounds, caught by the followers and taken to this camp. 

It is the wish of the writer to show in these pages some of the trials and dangers that beset the Union man of the South just prior to and during the first years of the war. 

It was often asked by Northerners that knew little of the situation as it then was. "Why did not all Union men join the Union Army?"

Many did. The majority of those who did not were neither "weak-kneed" nor cowardly.

If the amount of danger to be faced is taken into consideration the evidence is in favor of those who stayed-the hardships and dangers of the Union men—in the mountains or in their homes were greater than of those who made their way to the Union Army.

We cannot tell the reasons that actuated all of them, but we believe that the majority were sincere in their wish that our nation should remain united. 

Wiley with all his heart longed to join the Union Army; but he could not or would not, conceal his sentiments. By his open and aggressive expressions he brought down upon his head such a storm of abuse, persecution and hatred that, from his very nature, he resolved to stay and fight it out. right there!

CHAPTER III. 

IN THE REBEL ARMY 

And so the days went by, each hour freighted with anxiety, dread and fear of danger lurking "just around the corner. 99 Often-sometimes in the day, more often at night-a call would be heard outside. "Wiley, come out.' 99 Wiley would get up and dress if it was night, answering cheerfully, 

-10-

"All right, boys, I'm coming."

With a hasty goodbye to his wife and the children, if any were awake, a whispered word of cheer and he was out and away with the men-some of them strangers, others, perhaps, men he knew, probably near neighbors. 

Out in the night-alone. Riding away with men that he knew were his enemies, knowing not what was intended; riding nonchalantly, talking cheerfully as if going on a pleasure ride, but with mind alert, thinking, planning, watching each move of the other riders; listening to their conversation, and at some auspicious moment, stating some reason why he should be allowed to return home-and-often convincing them.

The next day, perhaps, he would arrive at home, travelworn and weary but jubilant. It might be three or four days that he was kept away. Then hope in the hearts of the wife and children grew faint, but they still watched and waited for his coming. Then hope revived and joy reigned.

After months of such experiences, again the familiar call- "Wiley, come out."

This time the riders numbered about 20-men in the uniform of Confederate soldiers.

Promptly Wiley walked out, greeted them calmly and then asked: "Gentlemen, what do you want?" 

The captain, or leader, said: "We want you. We are taking you to the Confederate camp in Memphis, Tenn., where you are to be held until further orders." "All right, gentlemen, just wait until I get my coat and hat and I will be ready."

Another time of goodbyes, words of encouragement and cheer from the husband and father, then he rode away and again the home was desolate. 

He was taken to Memphis, Tennessee, where he was held as a conscript in a Volunteer Co. of eighty-three-Company D 

Infantry, called then the Second Confederate Regiment, afterward called the Fifty-Second Tennessee.

In July they were moved from Memphis to Fort Pillow, about eighty-five miles above Memphis. 

Here Wiley became very sick, but still stubbornly refusing to be mustered into the Confederate army, although he was offered a lieutenancy.

He still remained on the sick list and was ordered to the camp hospital-Dr. Wright, of Kentucky, was in charge. 

Three days he passed by Wiley's bunk with never a look in his direction.

On the morning of the fourth day as Dr. Wright was passing Wiley with his usual contempt of the obdurate 

Unionist Wiley raised up and asked: -11-

"Dr. Wright, do you or do you not intend to prescribe for me?"

Wheeling around the doctor regarded him with a look that was meant to "settle" the obdurate man, said: "There's nothing the matter with you, Sir!" "Then why did you send me here for treatment?" "You insolent puppy, I tell you there's nothing the matter with you!" "I fail to see any consistency then in your sending me here."

Wiley was holding on to his temper, but the doctor was livid with rage, and went away vowing he'd have Wiley courtmartialed.

Soon he returned bringing the brigade surgeon. Walking up to the bunk and pointing his finger at Wiley, he exclaimed: "There he is! There's the insolent puppy!"

The sick man concluded that, although he had not been "mustered in" he was ready to fight.

Springing up he seized a billet of wood which lay near and charged the enemy in the person of the irate doctor. 

He was caught by the brigade surgeon and pushed back in bed where he fainted. 

The surgeon turned to the fuming, swearing doctor and said: "Shut up! This is a very sick man!"

I leave the reader to imagine the doctor's chagrin when the surgeon whom he had brought to prove that Wiley was shamming uttered these words. 

The surgeon himself prescribed for him, but he would not stay in the hospital-he took his medicine in his tent. 

Late in the fall they were moved to Columbus, Ky. 

Here Wiley's stubborness and out-spokeness resulted in a "tilt" with Captain Hunley.

Thirty-two hundred dollars had been made up by the citizens and put in the hands of Captain D. R. Hunley, for distribution among the men of his company. Only a few of the men knew about the money held by Captain Hunley for them and those who did know were too much in awe of their blustering Captain to say anything about it—all except one. 

One day when some of the boys were complaining of the scarcity and lack of many things, Wiley, the irrepressible, said: "Well, boys, why don't you call on Captain Hunley for the money he has in charge for you?"

The knowing ones shut their mouths and shook their heads, but those who did not know, opened both mouths and eyes in astonishment. "What money?" came in chorus.

Thereupon Wiley proceeded to tell them of the thirtytwo hundred dollars which the citizens had raised and placed -12-

in the hands of Captain Hunley for the men of his Company and asked, "Have any of you received any of that money?"

Many replied in the negative. Others remained silent. 

Some one asked: "Why don't you ask Captain Hunley for your share, Wiley?" 

He replied: "Thank you, but I have no share in it. I am not a member of this Company nor of the Confederate Army, please understand, but Captain Hunley was given the money and I have seen a circular with his name attached announcing that he had received the money and held it in trust for his Company.

Soon Hunley was told what Wiley had said and he (Hunley) pretended to be very indignant and declared that no such circular existed.

Thereupon Wiley sought out a man who was going home on a furlough and asked him to get some of the circulars in Huntville, which he did and duly delivered two of them to Wiley on his return. This the captain soon heard and sent for both men.

When Wiley arrived at the captain's tent the man (Echols) who had brought the circulars was there and the captain was cursing him, calling him a "cowardly sneak." He walked up and said, "Did you send for me, Captain?" "Yes. Where is that circular you have been showing?" 

He answered, “I have it.” "Let me see it." "Will you return it?" "Yes." "Alright, but I'll read it aloud first." There were now a crowd of men pressing close to hear the reading. So, climbing upon a box, he proceeded to read it to the assembly; then turning it around so all could see at the bottom the name Captain D. R. Hunley in large letters. Then he handed it over and the captain quickly locked it up. 

Wiley watched the proceedings with a smile and said: "That's about the way I thought you'd keep your word,

Captain Hunley, but I have another." And he walked away with the other circular unmolested.

From Columbus the Company was moved to Camp Beauregard, thirty miles south of Paducah. 

There Wiley made application to the captain for a permit to go home to attend to legal business. "No," said Hunley, "you cannot have a permit." Wiley then showed him a letter from the Probate Judge, James Scruggs, stating that it was necessary for him to return immediately. Still Hunley said: "No. You shall not have a permit." "Very well, then I'm going home anyway." - 13-

And that night he started home-on foot-a distance of 

218 miles.

It was now November and the nights were chilly. 

After walking all night the first night-Sunday-he rested in the woods Monday until evening, then again took up the march for home, traveling through the woods by day and the road at night.

Tuesday morning he stopped at a house near the road. 

The woman was alone, her two sons, she said, were sick at Columbus and her husband had gone there to see them. She gave the tired and hungry man a good breakfast for which she refused to accept pay. She asked no questions, but quietly prepared a lunch which she gave to him for the need of the day.

While walking through the woods that day he saw a negro man and asked him the way to the railroad. The negro was so frightened he could hardly talk, but by dint of quiet questioning he ascertained that he could soon come to a river which there would be no chance to cross if he came to it after dark.

He pressed on, however, and reached the river before daylight next morning, crossed over a dam over which the water poured knee-deep

The river bottom was very dark and the traveler was obliged to go very slowly, taking the moon for a guide. 

Next day the country over which he traveled was more open so he concluded to stop in the woods. When night came he started on-darkness and danger right and left, front and back! This he knew but the goal was ahead-home and the business which demanded his presence. 

He reached the west fork of the Obion river before midnight. No bridge and no canoe in sight. There was only one thing to do if he continued his journey-and he did it-swam across!

The little pools in the road were frozen. To avoid taking cold he ran until he was warm and his clothing steaming. 

About two o'clock in the morning he reached the railroad, but became so sick that he could hardly stand. 

Not far from the track he saw a house and he made his way there. After repeated calls a man came to the door and asked gruffly: "What do you want?" "I am traveling and am sick. CHAPTER IV. 

ON THE WAY TO SHILOH 

On reaching Huntsville he went at once to the Probate Judge and Circuit Clerk to settle the business for which he came. "Why, Wiley! How did you get off?" asked the Judge. "I just walked off," he answered.

The judge regarded him a moment with a puzzled and anxious look, then said: "Jim, I'm scared for you. Sit down here at my desk and write back where you are."

He sat down and wrote: "D. R. Hunley, Captain Co. D, Second Confederate Regiment, Camp Beauregard, Ky. Sir: I am at home on the furlough which I tore off. Respectfully, James Wiley."

This was mailed and the writer went home. 

In a few days a lieutenant, a sergeant and two privates came to his home, called to him and when he answered, they said: "We have come to take you back to the camp, as a deserter." 

Wiley laughed and asked: 15-

"How can I be a deserter when I do not belong to your army?"

To this they did not reply, but urged haste. 

At Wiley's request they went with him to the judge who gave a written statement that Wiley's presence had been necessary and that they-both judge and circuit clerk-had so written to Captain Hunley. This was signed by both judge and circuit clerk.

But this written statement was not given to General 

Bowen immediately.

Wiley was sentenced to "durance vile" and kept therein forty-three days!

When at last the letter reached General Bowen, he immediately sent for Captain Hunley.

BUSHWHACKERS 

In the fall of 1863 part of the Union Army was camped at Paint Rock Station. Some of the people living near there, wishing to get me into trouble with the Union soldiers, told them that I was only pretending and that I was not, in reality, for the Union. 

Ben Harris, one of my enemies, went to the Union soldiers and said that if they would go with him to my home he would prove me a traitor to them. 

Some of the soldiers, ready for adventure of any kind, agreed to go with Harris.

A company was made up. Harris was given the Colonel's uniform and authority-pro tem, and started for my house, at 30

night, arriving there about midnight and called for me to come out, which I did promptly. 

Harris, in a voice which he vainly tried to disguise, asked: "Have you seen any Yankees around here lately?" "Yes, I see Yankess nearly every day." "Do you sometimes give them something to eat?" "Yes. My little daughters sometimes carry milk to them out at the gate." "Well, you help them then?" "They have taken nothing from us except one horse and one chicken." "Do you ever give them any information?" "They sometimes ask questions as you are doing and I tell them the truth-as I am telling you." "When did you see them last?" "Day before yesterday."

Here Harris became impatient and evinced it by swearing and declaring to the company that I was telling them lies. 

Seeing that his blustering wasn't impressing the soldiers nor frightening me, he continued in a more conciliatory tone: "But you are in communication with 'our boys' that are camped down on the banks of Flint River, near the Ashburn Ford."

I had been hanging on to my temper, giving Harris plenty of rope to allow him to expose his duplicity, but now I flared up: "Ben Harris, you sneaking coward! I know you! You know that there are no rebels on Flint River!" "Well," he said, "I heard they were there." "Pshaw! I know who started that report. It was old 

Miz Hornbuckle. But Henry Roberts and me rode our hosses all up an' down that river for miles Sunday and there was not even a hoss track there-except the ones our own hosses made. But I can tell you where there are several hundred. 

In the Hurricane Swamp and they are hidin' there. They're 

Bushwhackers; and, Mr. Harris, one of them is yore cousin.

Up spoke the Colonel then. (He had been keeping in the back-ground waiting for Harris to prove me a liar and a traitor to the Union cause.) "Do you know where that man lives?" he asked me. "Yes, Sir, I do." "Well, come and get on this mule and lead us there." 

I donned coat and hat and mounting the mule proceeded to lead them to the house of the man I had said was one of the bushwhackers that had been hiding in Hurricane Swamp and was a cousin to Ben Harris, the erstwhile leader of the company.

Mr. Harris, who had set out to find a traitor to the 

Union, was now being led by the man he had accused and attempted to trap. Imagine his chagrin and fear. - 31-

About three miles riding brought us to a small house near a large tract, called Hurricane Swamp. 

Several years ago a hurricane had passed over what was then a beautiful cedar forest and wrought such havoc there that travel by any way except on foot was impossible. 

Here, in the "Swamp", as it was called (although it was dry nearly the year round), was the chosen rendezvous of the bushwhackers-the terror and disgrace of the South. 

Riding up to the house the Colonel called, and a man appeared at the door. The Colonel asked: "Where are the bushwhackers that are hiding near here?" "I doan' know nothin' 'bout no bushwhackers," he replied. "Now that won't do. You do know and you've got to tell. 

If you don't we're going to hang you." "I don't know!" he asserted, anger and fear in his tone. "Get the rope, boys."

By this time the man's wife had come out and was standing by her husband.

When the rope was brought, she gave a prolonged scream and threw her arms around her husband and cried out: "Oh, what's the matter? What are they going to do?" 

The Colonel answered, in a kind but firm tone: "Madame, we have asked your husband to tell us what he knows about the bushwhackers that are near here. He persists in refusing to tell. If he still persists we shall be obliged to hang him." "Oh, Wat! Tell 'em! Tell 'em all you know," she cried frantically. Then, recognizing Ben Harris, she released her husband and ran to Harris, grabbed his arm and said: "Oh, cousin Ben, save him! Don't let them hang him!" 

Harris repulsed her roughly, saying: "I'm no cousin o' yourn! Git erway from me! We'll hang him, sure, ef he doan tell."

She then ran back to her husband and clasped her arms about him as if to protect him or die with him. (Love: how strong it is; in all classes and under all conditions!) 

Her husband tried to reason with her-begging her to go back into the house-telling her (while his death-like pallor and shaking limb belied his words) that they would not do him any harm-that he was sure they would not hang him. 

All to no effect. She still clung to him, begging and weeping. 

She was forced away and held while they placed the rope around his neck, then she fainted and was carried into the house.

Then the rope was thrown over a limb and he was again asked: "Where are the bushwhackers?" "I can't tell," he said in a weak, trembling voice. "Pull up, boys!" And the man hung suspended. A wild agonizing scream sounded from the door. The woman had regained consciousness and fought her way out. 32-

He was lowered and again asked to tell where the bushwhackers were.

Perhaps it was not so much fear of death as pity for the woman, his wife, who lay on the grass at his feet, weeping and moaning, that compelled him to say: "I'll show you where they are." 

I was told that I might return to my home. "Wat" was put on the mule that I had been riding and they started for the rendezvous of the bushwhackers. 

I was told afterward that several of the bushwhackers were killed, sixty-five taken prisoners and the camp completely broken up. 

And when they got back to the Union camp Harris was missing.

CAPTURE AND AN ECLIPSE 

Years ago when I was making notes of the happenings in the years '61-'64 as related to me by my father I felt that there were incidents that he omitted, thoughts not revealed and anxiety not spoken. So, if, occasionally, I have an urge to tell my side of it as I remember or as I have been told by others of the family, I trust the reader will pardon the digression.

This "foreword" is from the memory of a child; but it is painted in my heart and indelibly impressed upon my mind -these incidents of that night so long ago in Alabama! Where the darkies sang in the cotton fields and elsewhere: "Don't you heah de hammah's ring? "Don't you heah de hammah's ring? "Don't you heah de hammah's ring? "Dear Lawd! dey'm a nailin' Jesus down!"

I remember my sister and I were in our trundle bed. 

Evidently the Sandman had bestowed all our allotment of sleeping-sand upon my sister for she was sleeping soundly and I was wide-awake-feeling not the least inclination toward sleep.

Through the window I watched the stars twinkle and wink through the leaves in the tree-tops that were stirred by a gentle breeze; which should have had a sleep-producing effect but failed to send me to the Land of Nod. 

It was early winter but frost had not yet colored the leaves nor spoiled the flowers. 

I could see Pa sitting by the window looking out. Occasionally he would get up, walk out into the yard, where he seemed to be listening. I remember that somtimes men rode up-usually in the night-and called pa out, and after they had talked awhile he would come in, bid ma a hurried goodbye and go away with them. I remember also that mother 33 

---

and big sister would look worried-not saying anything-just being silent and abstracted. 

I wished pa would come in and go to bed. If he should be asleep when the men came, maybe he wouldn't have to go with them.

Was he listening now for the hoof-beat of the horses, the stealthy foot steps of the men, the Judas-like "Hello, friend" or the harsh command "Come Out."

I remembered that when this happened he usually went away for awhile. And mother was worried.

I think I had just fallen asleep when I was awakened by the firing of a gun near my bed.

PA WILL TELL THE REST 

One night early in the winter I sat at the window and looked out. I had been told that Confederate soldiers would come to take me prisoner tonight. Across the road I could see the stables, the corn-crib, the stock in the pasture, grazing on the late grass or lying on the ground. 

Home; dear of course, but there, across the fields are the homes of neighbors. Friends? I did not know. The thought was gall and worm-wood.

Alabama; meaning Here we rest, Land of rest, Land of peace, of hospitality, of beauty. 

How strangely, now, mis-named! From center to boundery it is filled with strife. 

My country! Gladly would I lay down my life for my country. On the field of battle, with my face toward my country's foes I would count it a glory and an honor to die, but to be taken from my home by those same foes-to meet a fate that would leave my family-my wife and six children-defenseless! It shall not be! I will out-general them. 

If they come tonight I'll go with them. But I'll elude them. And after I've taken my family to the North I'll come back. I'll fight them right here! and may God speed the right.

Worn out with watching, thinking and feeling, about ten o'clock I retired but had scarcely fallen into a troubled slumber when I was awakened by the barking of my dogs. I sprang out of bed and looked out of the window. The moon was shining brightly now and I could see a man standing near a tree in the yard. 

I called out: "Who's there?" "Come out, I want to see you."

I stepped out into the passage and, leveling my gun at the man by the tree, said: "Get out of my yard." 

As the man jumped behind the tree a canteen flashed in the moonlight and I knew he was a Confederate soldier. Immediately there sounded from behind the tree a clear, shrill -34-

whistle, and there came marching up the road a body of soldiers.

The captain came into the yard and advanced to within twenty or thirty yards of the house. "Halt!" I cried, raising my gun. "Don't shoot! Don't shoot!" he exclaimed, running and jumping the fence. 

Then turning, facing me he said: "I didn't come here of my own accord-I was sent by the 

Confederate authorities." "What are the charges against me?" I asked. "That your wagon is loaded with cotton to be taken to the Yankees at Clarksville, Tennessee.' "What are your orders?" "I am ordered to bring you and your horses to the authorities across the Tennessee river." "Very well, I'll go with you." 

I stepped back into the room and glancing around I saw my fourteen-year-old son, William, with a loaded and cocked revolver in each hand, pointing out of the window directly at the soldiers. "Pa, may I shoot?" 

For a moment I was sorely tempted to say "Yes", and fight it out then and there.

Again came the eager question of the boy at the window, "May I fire now?" I answered, "No, son; I'll go with them." 

My wife, who was now standing by my side, asked: "What will they do with you?" "O, they'll turn me loose tomorrow," I answered re-assuringly. 

I now started to uncock my gun; my finger slipped and the load was discharged, making a large hole in the upper floor. Now there was spread out on that floor a quantity of wheat and it came pouring down through that hole, making a terrific clatter.

When I glanced out to note the effect of the report, what was my surprise and amusement to see every one of the men that had followed their captain into the yard, making an ignominious rush for the road and when they reached the fence tumbling over it like frightened sheep-calling out to those who were still in the road: "Yankees! Yankees!"

I stepped out and explained, whereupon they came cautiously back and waited while I made ready to accompany them. 

They caught my hosses; the captain and one of the men mounted them and we started for their "Camp of Execution" across the Tennessee river. -35-

The men soon began talking about the fun they were going to have. I made no reply until one of the guards said tauntingly: "O, we're goin' to hev a heap of fun!" "You are?" I asked, "What about?" "Why, we're a goin' to sell your hosses and buy whiskey and terbacker so we kin hev a jubilee when we hang you." "O, very well," I said. "But you'll let me have some of the whiskey, won't you?" (I had not the slightest fear that I would test the strength either of Canfederate whiskey or Confederate hemp.) 

The captain deeming the public highway unsafe on account of the supposed presence of Yankees in the vicinity, they changed to an unfrequented and circuitous road, making the distance to camp about nineteen miles. 

After crossing the Paint Rock river with difficulty on account of back-water from the Tennessee river, they noticed that the light that had been gradually growing faint-now failed altogether and we were in complete darkness. 

The moon was in total eclipse!

After the soldiers had exhausted all their usual profanity and invented some unusual for this special occasion, I said: "Captain, you see now that you are doing wrong." "Why so, sir?" "Because even the moon refuses you the light of her countenance." 

More profanity was the only answer.

After wandering about in the darkness of the river bottom for hours, it seemed, a consultation was held (in which I was not invited to join) and it was agreed that they were lost. 

The captain was so afraid of "running amuck" of the 

Yankees that his wits refused him aid.

One of the guards went forward and spoke to the captain: "Captain Patterson, our prisoner says he can lead us out to the road." "Bring him here!" 

I was led to the captain and he asked, "Do you know where we are?" "Yes." "Can you lead us out?" "Yes." "How far is it to the road?” "About a quarter of a mile." "Well, I suppose we'll have to let you lead us out, but mind now:-if we find that you are leading us wrong we'll shoot you down!" "Pshaw! I don't want to lead you to the Yankees. It's too dark, they wouldn't know me and I might get hurt."

Notwithstanding this impudence I was put in lead-there was no alternative. -36-

In about thirty minutes they came to a private wagon road. "Now, here is the road, Captain," I said. "Well, lead on toward the Tennessee."

Soon we reached a small creek called Job's Creek. It was bank-full. The moon again gave a small amount of light. "Can these horses swim?" the captain inquired.

I assured them that both horses were splendid swimmers. 

But the riders seemed to be hesitating. "If you are afraid," I said, "I'll ride across and back." 

This must have "nettled" the captain's companion rider and he urged his mount forward, plunged in and-disappeared!

In a moment the horse's forefeet appeared above the turbid water, wildly pawing the air. After a series of snorts and plunges he reached the opposite bank-riderless. Straining our eyes we soon caught sight of a dripping figure crawling up the bank, sputtering, shivering and swearing. 

The captain had no choice but to follow.

The beautiful mare which he rode and for which I had been offered one hundred fifty dollars recently, swam beautifully, carrying her rider safely over with scarce a wetting. "Now," said the captain to me, I leave these men in your charge. Where will you meet us?" "At the foot of the hill near Cobb's farm." "Very well! Meet us there-and mind-no talking aloud."

Then the captain and his aide rode away, leaving me to pilot the other men across the creek, along the short cut to the foot of the mountain near Cobb's farm.

With some difficulty we succeeded in crossing on a drift composed of brush, logs and other accumulations. The logs rolled and tipped under our weight; the brush submerged instantly when we stepped upon it. Jumping from one tottering log to another, scrambling up from among the brush, we finally landed on the opposite side. 

All were wet and shivering. Some were trying to relieve their feelings by profane language. The "boys" really seemed to be out of humor! I wondered if they considered this part of the fun they anticipated.

We met at the place agreed upon. The moon had now withdrawn the "veil of her displeasure". With the increased light we had no difficulty in reaching the top of the hill where the captain called two men aside and said: "Go to 's distillery and get three gallons of whiskey and meet us at the ford of Honeycomb Creek."

The captain went down stream a short distance to where it emptied into the Tennessee river, to talk to the ferryman about getting across. He found that the ferryboat was kept sunk to prevent the Yankees making a target of it when the opportunity offered. While waiting the captain's return a negro rode up and stopped.

I said to the guards, "Why can't we ride across with this fellow?"

After a short consultation they concluded that would be a good plan. One of the guards asked: "Who will go first?"

Neither guard would consent to go first; neither was willing to be left alone with the prisoner. At length they decided that the prisoner should go first. He would be safe, surrounded by water on the other side. 

So, with the prisoner in the saddle and the negro riding back of the saddle we started across. The negro was none other than Henry-he of the bloodhound episode. We had recognized each other at first but made no sign. 

He had been on business for his master, who sent him at night, fearing he might meet Yankees, knowing that they (the Yankees) had been in that vicinity. As soon as we had gone out of the hearing of the guards we began to talk in whispers. "Lawd save us, Massa Jim! What mus' we do?" "Let me down in the shadow of the tree over there and return for the guards. But delay getting back as long as you can. I'll look out for myself and you must look out for yourself, Henry." "I'se not 'feared for myse'f, Massa Jim, but I'se scared for you. Do try to git clean away to de norf 'fore they shoot or hang you." I replied, promising that I'd try to take my family north to a place of safety adding, "But I'll come back!"

When we reached the shadows at the farther edge of the back-water I dismounted. Henry climbed into the saddle and as soon as the splashing of the horse's feet in the water could drown the sound of my feet I ran for life and liberty. 

Keeping as much as possible in the shadows I ran up the river along the high bank until I reached the foot of a hill (or mountain); there I stopped to breathe and listen. I heard the negro say: "Massa Guards, I caint do nuffin wid dis hoss. De watah berry deep an' he 'fuse to budge any furder."

I knew he was pretending this as an excuse for delaying. 

So, smiling at Henry's tactics, I pressed on until I was out of sight and out of sound of their voices. Then I paused to rest and think. What with the sunken ferry boat, the scattered company and the high water, I believed they could not succeed in re-capturing me that night.

Fifteen miles from home, in a region infested with bush- 38-

whackers, refugees and run-away negroes, I still had hope of reaching the home of a friend five miles up the river. I had never met his wife; if he were absent she might refuse to shelter me.

About daylight I reached my friend's home. Going to the gate I called, "Hello!" A sudden scrambling and shuffling of feet informed me that I had been heard but as no one answered or appeared I called again: "Hello, Tom! It's me-Jim Wiley!"

Someone peeped cautiously out of the window, then the door was opened and a hearty voice exclaimed, "Well, well! Come right in, Jim!"

In the house were the man whose life (he said) I had saved by assisting in killing the blood hounds, and another man. They had come in from the mountains to obtain food and spend the night. Both told piteous tales of fear, exposure and hunger. Most of all they deplored the conditions of their families-destitute and defenseless in some cabin among the mountains these brave women lived alone or with their children-which made their situation doubly hard. They carded the cotton and wool into rolls, spun the rolls into thread and wove the thread into cloth for clothing. They cultivated their small crops, trying in every way they could to "keep the wolf from the door", literally as well as figuratively; praying for the husband and father away from home-where they knew not nor when he could return. 

I was given a hearty welcome; breakfast was prepared and after that I was asked to relate my night's adventure.

Then I was allowed to retire and slept soundly until the next morning. After breakfast I went across the valley to the home of another friend. He also was gone on a relief expedition to the mountains, his wife said and added, "I'll ferry you across. I've heard my husband speak of you and I'll be glad to help you."

She led the way to the crossing where a boat was moored, and with skill and courage ferried me across. She told me all she knew about the armies, both Yankee and Rebel-where they were camped, the depredations of the bushwhackers, her fear of run-away Negroes and her anxiety for her husbandthe risks he was taking while on his missions of help for the mountain refugees.

There on the banks of the river, with words of thanks for her help and messages for her husband I bade her goodbye and turned my face again toward home.

I was now in a part of the country with which I was familiar. I traveled unfrequented paths when possible, sometimes through pathless woods-alert of eye and ear, lest I run unto undesirable company.

Walking along a path through heavy woods I saw in the dust the tracks of a bare-foot child—a small child-probably not more than three years old. -39-

I had seen no house along the way-no building of any kind for miles. There was no other track, so the child must be alone. I stopped and looked in every direction, but could see no house of any kind. But a child had walked here, alone, and recently. Surely so small a child would not be sent on an errand alone in these woods. I followed the track for, probably, two miles, then they turned aside and disappeared from the path. I walked on, looking closely but did not see the tracks again.

A little child lost in the woods or mountains! I couldn't go on-thinking of this. I went back to where I first saw the foot prints-thinking the child may have come back to the path. But there were no more tracks-no sign of a dwelling in any direction-just mountains, valleys and woods. Again 

I followed the path but could find no other tracks. 

I stopped and listened.

There was no sound of human feet or human voice. Still I listened.

Far away I seemed to hear a faint sound. It seemed to be a faint ringing, as of a small bell. I went toward the sound. It became more distinct. It was a bell. 

What connection the ringing of a bell could have with the foot prints of a little child on a lonely road I could not imagine; but it might be a clue, so I went on toward the sound which grew more distinct. CHAPTER X. 

POLITICAL PARTIES 

When the Constitution was before the people for acceptance or rejection in 1788 they were divided into two bodies. 

Those who were in favor of a strong and vigorous federal government, who wanted Congress to have plenty of power to regulate trade, pay the debts of the country and raise revenue. 

These were called "Federalists". They supported the Constitution just as it was.

Others wanted the old "Articles of Confederation" preserved and amended so as to give Congress a revenue and only a little more power; opposed the Constitution and wanted it altered. So Congress, to please the "Anti-Federalists", drew up twelve amendments to the Constitution and sent them to the States. Ten were ratified and opposition to the Constitution ceased, for awhile.

But as soon as Congress began to pass laws, difference of opinion as to the expediency of them, and even as to the right of Congress to pass them, divided the people again into two parties and sent a good many Federalists into the "Anti-Federalist" party. Many complained that the interest on the national debt, the great salary of the President, the pay of Congressmen and the hundreds of tax assessors made taxes too heavy. They complained, too, that men in office were too fond of aristocratic customs. The President, they said, was too exclusive and owned too fine a coach. The Justices of the Supreme Court must have black silk gowns, with red, white and blue scarfs. The Senate held its daily session in secret-not even a newspaper reporter was allowed to be present. They accused the Federalists of wishing to set up a monarchy. 

They began in 1792 to organize for election purposes, and as they were opposed to a monarchy they called themselves "Republicans". Their leaders were Jefferson, Madison, Monroe, John Randolph and Albert Gallatin. (This party was the fore-runner of the present Democratic party.)

At the election in 1796 John Adams was the candidate of the Federalists and Thomas Jefferson of the Republicans. 

Adams received 71 votes and Jefferson 68, so John Adams was elected president and Thomas Jefferson vice-president.

From 1793 to 1815 the questions that divided the people into Federalists and Republicans were all, in some way connected with foreign countries. The Federalists sympathized with England. Every Republican was a warm supporter of France.

At the close of the war in 1815 all this ended. There was no longer any foreign question to divide the people, and after 1816 the Federalist party never put up a candidate for the presidency.

For twelve years there was but one great party-the Re- — 42 —

publican party, which soon began to be called the Democratic party. 

James Monroe and Daniel D. Thompkins were nominated at Washington, February, 1816, were elected (there being no opposing candidates) and sworn into office March 4, 1817.

This administration was known as "the era of good feeling." 

After years of changing parties and changing names, in 1854 the question of forming a new party out of the wreck of the old was widely discussed all over the North and West. 

With this in view, a meeting of citizens of all parties was held at Ripon, Wisconsin, at which the formation of a new party on the slavery issue was recommended and the name Republican was suggested. After the passage of the Kansas- 

Nebraska bill a thousand citizens of Michigan signed a call for a state mass-meeting at Jackson, where a state party was formed, named Republican and a state ticket nominated on which were Whigs, Free-Soilers and Anti-Nebraska Democrats. Similar tickets were adopted in Wisconsin and Vermont and the name Republican used-as also in Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, New Hampshire and Connecticut.

This new party attracted much interest and there was a call for an informal convention at Pittsburgh on February 22, 1856. At this meeting the National Republican party was formed and from it went a call for a national nominating convention to meet June 17th, 1856, at Philadelphia, where John 

C. Fremont and William L. Dayton were nominated. The

Free-Soilers joined the Republicans and disappeared from politics as a party.

The Democrats nominated James Buchanan and John C. 

Breckinridge and carried the election. They were inaugurated March 4, 1857.

May 16, 1860, the Republican party met in convention at Chicago and nominated Abraham Lincoln and Hannibal Hamlin, of Maine. The election took place in November, 1860. 

Of the 300 votes cast Lincoln received 180, Breckenridge 72, Bell 39, and Douglas 12.

When it was known that Lincoln had been elected South Carolina asked her legislature then in session, not to adjourn until certain of the result of the election. 

On December 20, 1860, a convention of delegates, called by the legislature to consider the question of secession, for- 'mally declared that South Carolina was no longer one of the United States. Other Southern States followed her lead in quick succession.

In Lincoln's inaugural address from the steps of the Capitol he was careful to state just what he wanted to do. He said, among other things which he considered his duty, "The power confided in me will be used to hold, occupy and possess the property and places belonging to the government and to collect the duties and imposts."

One of these places was Fort Sumpter, to which he decid- 43

ed to send men and supplies. This was done, Major Anderson being put in charge of the Fort. General Beauregard, commander at Charleston, demanded the surrender of the Fort. 

Major Anderson stoutly refused to comply with the demand and, on the morning of April 12, 1861, the Confederates fired the first gun at Fort Sumpter. 

During the next thirty-four hours nineteen batteries poured shot and shell into the Fort which steadily returned the fire. Then, both food and powder being nearly exhausted and part of the Fort being on fire, Anderson surrendered; and on Sunday, April 14, 1861, he marched out taking with him the tattered flag under which he had made so gallant a fight. 

Lincoln held that no state could ever leave the Uniontherefore no state had left the Union. The states that had passed secession ordinances were, in his opinion, states whose machinery of government had been seized by persons in insurrection against the government of the United States. 

Therefore, when Lincoln made his call for 75,000 militia to defend the Union, he apportioned the number among the states-north, south, east and west, slave and free alike—according to their population. The states forming the Confederacy gave the call no attention but the loyal states responded immediately and soon tens of thousands were on their way to Washington.

While passing through Baltimore, in going from one railroad station to another, the Massachusetts 6th Regiment was attacked. Some of the soldiers were killed and several wounded. The troops were then obliged to go by way of Annapolis to reach Washington. On account of this delay it was late 

April before enough troops arrived to protect the Capital. 

Then came the Battle of Bull Run in which the Union forces were defeated. This was followed by the capture of 

Fort Henry by Flag Officer Foote; the attack on Fort Donnelson by General U. S. Grant, who, after three days of sharp fighting, made the famous answer to General Buckner's question as to what terms he would offer: "No terms except unconditional surrender can be accepted. I propose to move immediately upon your works. 

I am, sir, Very respectfully

U. S. GRANT, 

Brigadier General." 

Buckner at once surrendered February 16th, 1862. Thus was won the first great victory of the war. (McMaster's 

School History of U. S.) 

News of the fighting-of defeat and victories-were eagerly sought by the people of North and South alike; any and all chances for information were seized quickly upon and interpreted according to the wishes and beliefs of the seachers after truth. 

Month after month the fierceness of the fighting increased. The world rocked with the roar of cannon. The scream 44-

of shells drowned all sounds of nature: the murmur of gentle breezes, the musical tinkle of brooks and small waterfalls, the songs of birds.

The excitement, the tenseness, the hatred grew greater. 

The war fever ran higher and higher. Life-long friends became enemies. Ties of kinship were broken. Families were divided and the war went on. 

There were men in the Union army who believed that southern slave-holders should be allowed to keep their slaves but were against secession and firm in their loyalty to the 

United States.

There were many in the Southern army with strong convictions that slavery was a blot on our nation, but were unwilling to break the ties that bound them to the Southland. 

Others of stronger union sentiment made their way to the Union army, enlisted and fought valiantly through the war. 

These men at the close of the war came north to join their families at some place designated. All of whom the writer has knowledge, came to Illinois. Several families, as previously stated, settled in Effingham county.

Readers of these chapters may wonder and question: “Why, of all northern states, should these people choose Illinois as their home-a shelter in time of storm?"

Let us glance for a moment at the history of this state of their choosing.

When Illinois became a territory in 1809 the northern boundary was a line running due west from the southern point of Lake Michigan. When the statehood bill was passed in 

1818 the Southern part of the state favored slavery, which, if admitted to the Union with the first boundary lines, might have been made a slave state.

Congress moved the boundary-line one hundred miles north to 40°-30", adding thousands of square miles of territory, including what is now Chicago, and forming fourteen counties. Thus Illinois was admitted as a free state. 

In the intervening years from 1818 to 1861 Illinois made phenominal progress. Her various resources agriculture, mines, railroads, rich soil, great rivers and lakes were a "standing invitation" to all who were seeking a home in the north.

CHAPTER XI. 

AS A CHILD REMEMBERS 

(1864) We were going away. I knew not where. I watched the stowing in the wagon of various articles with which I was familiar-part of our home and our lives; a small part compared with what we were leaving-things cherished in memory.

But I was elated with the prospect of going away—all of us together. It seemed to promise a delightful adventure. 

Yet, back of the elation and delight, was a vague feeling of fear. Not deep, but sobering and quieting. 

Brother William, Mother with little brother in her arms, we four girls-Eliza, Susan, Rhoda and Babe, with a few neighbors were in the yard. Father was still in the house. 

The older people were in a group, talking together. We girls walked around in the yard out to the beech tree that stood near the kitchen. Susan said, "Let's go and look at the flowers in the garden. Perhaps we shall never see them again."

In silence we went through the garden gate down the gravelled walk, stopping occasionally for a lingering look at some favorite flower or plant. And so we walked the entire length of both paths that crossed in the center of the garden. 

How well I remember that walk-our last-through the garden; and the flowers- 

Flowers of the south! How lovely they were! Their fragrance and their beautiful colors safely held in memory but their names are lost in the mists of years.

As we came out into the yard I saw father standing in the front room door. He lingered a moment, looking back into the room as if reluctant to close it. Then stepping outside he closed the door-as if fearing the sound might be heard by those in the yard.

Goodbyes were spoken. The neighbors remained in the yard, standing in a group, as we took our places in the wagon So we drove out of the yard into the road. We looked back until a turn in the road hid from sight our neighbors and our home. -48-

In silence we rode away-our first objective a Union camp; our ultimate goal-Illinois. At midday we stopped where a spring, clear and cool, trickled down from under a rock in the side of a mountain. 

A fire was kindled. Cooking utensils were brought from the wagon, also things to cook-coffee, meal, bacon, sweet potatoes. Bread was baked in the "dutch oven", sweet potatoes were roasted in the ashes, coffee was boiled and bacon fried over the coals. After dinner the older people rested near the wagon but we youngsters romped among the cedar trees and played hide and seek among the big rocks. We were told not to be noisy. We obeyed but we wondered why we should. 

Soon we were again on the road, rested and refreshed. 

We, the younger members, relaxed from the strain and wrench of leaving home and friends, talked and laughed—even sang little southern lullabyes and snatches of songs we had heard the darkies singing as they worked in the cotton fields. 

That night we camped near a mountain with a great, dark forest close by. Again we were told to be very quiet and to stay near the wagon. Once when father and mother were talking together I caught the words "guerrillas" and "bushwhackers". I did not know, of course, what it was all about. 

Although I had heard the words the only idea I had grasped was that they meant danger-something to be feared. So I stayed near the wagon and kept very quiet. My eyes turned often to the darkness of the forest. A small thrill of fear was experienced with every sound that came from the forest. 

Supper was prepared and eaten. The fire was "banked" -covered with ashes-and mother arranged our beds in the wagon. Soon we were lying quietly in our beds as snug, almost, as we had been in our trundle beds at home to which we had so recently said goodby.

The Sandman must have failed to get the proper amount of sleep-magic in my portion of sand for my eyes persisted in staying wide open. All the queer thoughts of a too inquisitive child chased each other through my mind: What was a soldier camp like? What is a tent? How soon will we be there?

I had seen the soldiers in blue-some of them-riding by our house occasionally. I heard them called Yankees, those boys in blue. I remembered that they wore blue uniforms. 

I felt no fear standing in the yard to see them go by. Would we see them again? Maybe they would be at the place that was called The Camp. What was a bushwhacker? What was a guerrilla? Were they men or some kind of fierce animal?

Maybe the croaking I had heard that they said was a frog was just a bushwhacker and the screeching was only a guerrilla.

CHAPTER XII. 

JOURNEYING NORTH 

We waited in the Union camp several days for a train that would carry us toward Nashville, Tennessee, where father had decided to stop for awhile.

Each day in camp had been for us children-a new experience, an intense interest; watching the soldiers in their daily maneuvers, listening to the officers when they came to our tent to talk to father and mother, advising them of the situation of both armies as they had been informed. 

In answer to the question of joining the Union army then and there, and sending the family on to Nashville, the colonel said, "The Union army is nearing victory. Go on with your family to Nashville. There you and your son can get employment by the government-employment at work that is more needed now than more soldiers in the army are needed." 

So, on a clear, bright morning, a train stopped near the place where we had played on our first day in camp. Officers and privates, quite a number, were there to see us entrain. 

Soldiers assisted in putting our belongings aboard, and then the family. The train started and we were on our way. Goodbyes were waved from camp to car and back again.

Suddenly we were startled by the sound of guns and cries of "There he is! Shoot him."

Looking up the hill toward the Bowman house we saw coming toward us at the top of his speed Uncle Joe, the gardener from whom we had often received presents of fruit and flowers from his garden. He ran through our yard near where we were sitting. He was coatless and hatless, praying with every hard-drawn breath: "Save me, Massa Jesus, save me."

After him came men in pursuit-the same men who had asked the way to Dr. Bowman's. They were calling out and shooting as they ran, but evidently they were not trying to hit him he was still running down the hill toward the turnpike. 

Behind the pursuers came Aunt Mandy, Uncle Joe's wife.

She was running and crying. When she came near us she collapsed-went down on her kness, crying and praying, “O, deah Lawd save him! Don't let um shoot him!" 

Father said, "Stop crying, Mandy, and tell us what the trouble is." Mother also talked to her, asking what the matter was.

Moaning and ringing her hands, she said disjointedly: "Dey goin' kill him! Dey say he kill a man in bahbah shop three, foah weeks ago. He never say anything about it -jis ask me to hide him an he bin in room upstairs evah since. 

Today dey come an say dey goin' kill him. Dey say dey goin' search de house. Dey go upstairs an' begin openin' doahs; 

Joe, he heah dem an he clime out de windah and swing down de vine an run. Dey go ketch him! Dey'll kill him! Ō, deah 

Lawd, what kin I do?" 

Father said, "Now, Mandy, stop crying. Rest awhile and then go back home. I'll go to town and find out all about it. 

I'll find Uncle Joe and tell you all I've learned. They won't kill him. I really think they didn't mean to kill him. He may have to stand trial, but he may prove innocent."

And she went, mother accompanying her part of the way and trying to soothe and console her-leaving her quieter and more rational.

Father went immediately to town, was gone about two hours. When he returned he told us what he learned. 

Uncle Joe had gotten into a fight with another negro, the weapons being razors. It happened that Uncle Joe got to give the first lick and slashed his opponent's arm as he threw it up to protect his face and neck and as the man stumbled and went down, he lay as if dead, with face and neck covered with blood from his slashed arm. Uncle Joe seeing him lie thus thought of course that he had killed him, sought safety in flight, hiding until scared out by the searchers. He learned also that the man's injury was not serious, that he held no grudge and did not wish to prosecute. 

They had caught Uncle Joe and would hold him till morning and then release him.

It was after nine o'clock, but mother insisted that he go to the Bowman place and take the good news to Mandy, and the other servants. He did so and received profuse thanks and blessings from all.

We learned later that it was a mere farce, staged to frighten the negroes so they would quit fighting with razors. 

And the mystery of the closed door and the loaded tray was solved.

Father made the acquaintance of several officers in the camp across the turn-pike and asked mother's permission to invite two of them to have supper with us. She consented and they came. They were interesting talkers (I was told) and the evening passed pleasantly. They seemed to enjoy the supper and were frank in their praises. (Mother was a good cook.)

At the close of their visit one said, "Wouldn't the boys enjoy cooking like this?"

The other soldier said emphatically that they would. 

Next morning mother said to us: "If I should cook a basket full of good things to eat, would you girls be willing to take it to the camp and sell the things to the soldiers?" 

Susan and I answered quickly, "Yes, yes, we would." (Rhoda was the timid one and could not have been hired to go into the camp.)

So, the eatables were prepared, the basket was packed and we, wearing our best dresses, with our hair nicely curled, marched bravely down the path, across the turn-pike and into the camp, carrying our basket.

Mother continued to bake and we continued to sell as long as the camp remained in the grove. 

My sister and I enjoyed selling to the soldiers and became quite well-known in camp-remembering for years some small incidents connected with our business adventure and the names of some of our customers.

Then it was rumored that the camp would move soon; also that a battle would be fought near Nashville ere long. 

A day came when we knew the camp was moving. Tents were being taken down, guns unstacked and guns and tents were loaded on carriers. Flags were in evidence everywhere. 

At last the boys marched out of the grove, on to the turnpike and faced north.

We stood on the stone fence beside the road and watched them go, until we could see them no more. Gone from our sight and from our lives for all time, but Susan and I never forgot the time when we sold pies, cakes and rolls to the soldier boys.

After the camp moved we saw nearly every day troops being moved, all going toward Nashville. Often cavalry rode by, so many that the eye grew tired of watching and our feet grew weary of standing on the stone fence so long. 

We were awakened one morning not long after by a strange sound-a queer, seemingly distant rumble. The earth seemed to tremble and vibrate.

Outdoors in the light of the early morning we looked toward the city. It shone dimly through a veil that seemed made of smoke. Then we realized what it meant. The rumor of battle was being verified, and it was nearer-much nearer to us than we had thought possible. 

Soon we could distinguish the boom of the cannons from the roar of the guns. The earth beneath our feet vibrated to the roar of the guns and was jarred from the booming of the cannons.

Our house was deserted. Breakfast was forgotten as we all stood straining our eyes to see and our ears to hear some- -60-

thing tangible-something beside smoke and noise. And as we watched and waited, something caused the smoke to rise -up above the battle-perhaps a breeze-I only know that suddenly we could see the ranks of soldiers-both sidesmoving, moving, a thrilling, awe-inspiring sight!

This battle, of which we were eye-witnesses, must have been the one recorded in history as follows: "Hood, in a rash moment, had besieged Thomas at Nashville; but, Thomas, coming out from behind his intrenchments, utterly destroyed 

Hood's army."

I wish that I might have a fuller account of this battle for my own information. Perhaps my readers may be versed in history that will be proof, pro or con, if this battle we saw was the one mentioned.

As I remember the event none of us that were watching the battle from our hill expressed fear for ourselves; perhaps because we youngsters knew so little of what a battle meant. What the older members felt was not expressed in words. They stood in tense, grim silence. 

The sun came from behind a cloud and changed the smoke screen from great billows of drab darkness to a lurid, but impenetrable scene of orange and old gold.

A brisk breeze lifted it and again we could see the battle! 

Men charging, recoiling, rushing on again. Guns with bayonets flashing; flags dimly seen. Then the lowering cloud of smoke and all was again hid from view. * * Evening with surcease of battle sounds. 

Morning and the news of a victory for the Union army. 

For fourteen months we lived in the cottage on the hill, with its winding paths, its live oak and evergreen trees, its stone fence that encircled the foot of the hill and its turnpike stretching away north and south. I used to wish I knew to what unknown places it led. * * Days, weeks, months had passed. 

Lincoln had issued the Emancipation Proclamation. 

The war was nearing an end. 

Our intended destination, from the time of leaving oùr home in Alabama, was Illinois.

Relatives and friends from counties near Madison county where we lived had preceded us and father decided to join them as soon as he ascertained in what part of the state they were located. How he accomplished this, I have forgotten. 

But I have not forgotten the bleak day in March when we landed in the little village of Watson and found in or near the town some of our old friends and relatives. Living in or near were two families of Livingstons, two families of Hightowers, part of a family of Wileys and a family named Blevins.

It had been more than a year since we had seen these people-some we had never seen until we came to Illinois. 

But they greeted us with real southern hospitality; welcomed us to their homes and assisted in finding a place of abode, a log cabin near Little Salt Creek in Watson township.

There were other families living in cabins very much like the one we had found for a residence-not very near but near enough to be considered neighbors in that early day. And very good neighbors they proved to be. 

As time passed we grew accustomed to the manners, customs and dialect of our new friends. The southern home and the old time friends were not forgotten but grew more remote in memory and the mind-picture dimmed, but was never obliterated. Remote and dimmed it might be, but cherished always.

CHAPTER XIV. 

ILLINOIS 

We had arrived. This was Illinois, our long thought of place for a permanent home; a place of peace and freedom from strife and war-fare. We were not to be daunted by cold nor troubled by clouds. We had donned warm clothes in anticipation of northern temperature, snow and ice, "Linsey" dresses, woolen hoods and stockings of home-knit woolen yarn. 

Very few of the relatives remain in this county. Many have crossed the Great Divide. Some returned to the South after the war was over. Some of the Wiley descendants are in 

Arkansas, some in Oklahoma. Three sons of my uncle,

Thomas Wiley, are in Muskogee, Oklahoma. William S. Wiley and James M. Wiley are ministers for the Baptist church, their work calling them to different states. Leon Wiley, a son of William Wiley, is a Sunday School Secretary for that church and his work requires that his residence be in this state. He, with his lovely wife and three bright children visited the writer in her home recently. His home is in Du Quoin. 

Ed Ryan, a second cousin of the writer, and his wife live in Watson and have charge of the telephone exchange at that place.

Mrs. Laura Ryan Brand also has a home in Watson, where she spends the summers and staying in the home of a daughter in Chicago during the winter months. 

Another cousin, Bettie Livingston, grew up in this township, married a Mr. Lieb. She passed away several years ago, but there are several sons and one daughter still living in the west part of the county.

I, being the only living member of the James Wiley family of eight, take this method of offering greetings and good wishes to all of them.

Our particular "castle" (every man's home is his castle) 62

was situated on a hill that overlooked the bottom ground adjacent to Little Salt Creek. Great, tall trees-hickory, oak (several kinds), sycamore, cottonwood, elm, maple and many other kinds, grew thickly as far as could be seen. The wind that moaned or shrieked, or whistled in the tops of these trees sounded like nothing I had previously heard in wind music (?) and the emotion that the sound produced was not one of pleasure. But this was Illinois. We were all together. We were safe and were thankful.

There was much that seemed very strange to us, but we children grew accustomed to it soon. We soon were saying "Paw" and "Maw" for the "Pa" and "Ma" (short a) that we had always called our parents. 

We stopped saying "tote" when some of the children at school laughed at the word which they had not heard before. 

To our great relief the teacher called attention to it. He explained: "Tote", he said, is a proper word. It means 'to carry'.' To fix their minds more firmly, he continued: 99 "I heard one of my pupils say, 'I packed a bucket of water'. Now pack means to force articles close together, to make firm and solid. You can't do that with water, can you?"

This settled, for both sides, the words "tote" and "pack". 

Nevertheless, we carefully refrained from using the word "tote" again.

The family of A. J. Parks (Uncle Andy) was our near neighbor. The six girls belonging to that family were: Eliza, Rachel, Mary, Mamie, Sarah and Frances. Of these six girls only two are living, Mrs. Rachel McCallen, of Effingham, and 

Mrs. Mary Hursh, of Watson.

Of the four Wiley girls only one is living, the writer of these reminiscences. When we ten girls, ranging in age from four years to the early 'teens consolidated our forces things were rather sure to be lively.

Father quickly made acquaintances with the neighbors, soon got used to difference in language pronunciation, customs, etc.

Mother formed few close friendships. She liked the people, neighbored with them and visited with them sometimes. 

She seemed to feel as an alien in our own U. S. And when our great sorrow came-the death of brother William, shortly after our arrival-she seemed to feel that all the days were empty of interest, robbed of all happiness. She wanted to be busy, to be engaged in the kind of work to which she had been accustomed in the old home in the south. 

Accordingly a spinning wheel, a loom and other articles necessary for the work were procured and she began work, weaving carpets and rugs and, later, cloth. Wool was spun into thread (yarn). We were taught to knit socks, stockings, mittens and gloves for all the family.

Busy all day at wheel or loom or with knitting, mother became more reconciled-could again laugh with us and 63

planned future tasks. The rattle of the loom and the hum of the wheel brought "surcease of sorrow."

In the fall of 1864 the political situation was changed. 

The Democratic party denounced Lincoln and insisted that the war was a failure. They were demanding "peace at any price."

On the other hand was a faction of the Republican party finding fault with Mr. Lincoln because he was not severe enough, because he had done things they thought the Constitution did not permit, and because he made the conditions by which the seceding states might send representatives to Congress.

Between these two was a party made up of Republicans and Democrats who insisted that the Union must be preserved at any cost. These men held a convention and, dropping the name "Republican" for the time being, took the name of "The National Union Party". They re-nominated Lincoln. For 

Vice-president they nominated Andrew Johnson, a Union man and a War Democrat from Tennessee. 

The Radical Republicans held a convention and nominated 

John C. Fremont for President, and General John Cochran for

Vice-president. This last named party demanded one term for a president; the confiscation of the lands of the rebellious states; the reconstruction of those states by Congress (not by the president), vigorous war measures and the destruction of slavery forever.

The Democrats nominated George B. McClellan and 

George H. Pendleton.

Their platform demanded a cessation of hostilities with a view to a convention of the states. They described the sacrifice of lives and treasure in behalf of the Union as "Four years of failure to restore the Union by the experiment of War." 

McClellan, in his letter of acceptance, repudiated both of these sentiments. He said, "Union first-and then peace; no peace can be permanent without Union!"

In reply to that platform that stated that the "War was four years of failure", McClellan said, .... "I could not look in the face of my gallant comrades of the army and navy . . . . and tell them that their labors and the sacrifice of so many lives, so many of our slain and wounded brethren had been in vain.” 

Fremont and Cochran withdrew. 

In November, 1864, Lincoln and Johnson were elected. 

On March 4th, 1865, they were inaugurated and sworn into office.

It was obvious that the end of the war was near. 

On April 2nd Lee had announced to Davis that both Petersburg and Richmond must be abandoned.

On April 3rd General Wertzel learning of Lee's plight, proceeded to Richmond and found that Lee was in full retreat.

April 14th was the fourth anniversary of the evacuation of Fort Sumter. On that day Anderson, now a Major-General, visited the fort that he had defended so bravely and, in the presence of the Army and Navy, raised the tattered flag he pulled down in 1861. 

That same night (April 14th, 1865) President Lincoln was assassinated as he sat in his box in Ford's Theater, by an actor, Charles Wilks-Boothe. After shooting the President he leaped from the box to the stage and shouting, "Sic semper tyranis", made his escape and mounting a horse rode away.

He was hunted by soldiers, found and refusing to surrender, was shot.

When the news came that our President, Abraham Lincoln, had been assassinated, a pall of gloom and darkness was over the land. It seemed to encompass everything. We did not laugh as usual and our play was only half-hearted. 

Questions-unspoken-ran through our minds: What can we do without a president? Will the war begin again? 

Shall we go back to Alabama?

We were relieved when told that Mr. Johnson was our 

President. Not such a good president as Mr. Lincoln had been but perhaps suffice.

Gradually the gloom lifted, the world again was bright. 

Happy childhood! The blessing of forgetting sorrow.

Spring with all its variable phases was with us-an Illinois spring than which none could be more intriguing. If, as the old adage tells us "Variety is the spice of life," surely April in Southern Illinois has a generous allotment of spice. 

We soon grew to love Illinois in all seasons.

Spring, the season we could begin to go bare-foot and roam the woods and hills looking for "sweet-root" which, when found, we ruthlessly pulled from the ground, an ambitious plant that had the idea of growing into a big hickory tree. 

The sugar trees were tapped and the sap boiled into maple syrup and maple sugar. 

Summer, when came days of sunny warmth, songs of birds—many birds that we had listened to in our southern home and some that were new to us. Then woods and fields were decked with wild flowers. Fields and gardens were planted-all nature seemed alive. 

Autumn was the time to gather nuts and wild grapes, of which there was such a bountiful yield, the vines climbing to the tops of young trees and decorating all the branches with bunches of luscious fruit. We watched the frisky squirrel in his nimble preparations for winter.
