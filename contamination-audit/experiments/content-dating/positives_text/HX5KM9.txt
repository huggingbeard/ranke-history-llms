CHAPTER I 

NIFTH AVENUE, Youth and Spring-the perfect 

Manhattan cocktail, heady to the most inured and even more potent for those young things whose own joyous laughter is but part of the brew! This conflux of youth that every year is so mysteriously new, how it sparkles and flows through the Forties and Fifties, bubbling to the tops of the buses in its conspiracy with May to bring sober, wintry people to its own mad state of intoxication.

On a late spring morning an atom of this compelling force was doing her bit in the May revel just by reason of being. The world should ask nothing more of one who is twenty and has hair the color of a new penny and eyes of that blue-green shade displayed in every shop window. The world asked nothing, but there were heavy odds that it would get something, for this atom who called herself Pamela South had decided it must accept more than the copper hair; it would take what was BENEATH the copper hair-and like it! Which is a circuitous way of introducing Pam as a normally determined young writer, who might have quailed had she suddenly viewed a line-up of all the other inspired younglings who had brought to the city potential messages in their folding typewriters. But writers don't get

Strangely enough, such complacency was being justified by results. Pam had come to New York with every nerve taut for the "struggle" she believed must precede a world's recognition. But, surprisingly, the world had recognized her at once and bowed and smiled. She had sold her first story quite promptly, and her second and third with scarcely less delay. It seemed incredible, and she was elated, but something akin to disappointment took the edge from her elation. 

Pam wanted all that the world held, but she wanted so much more what it withheld! True, the magazines that had accepted her work were not strictly "first class." Ahead of her was still the thrill of girding her armor for siege at the gates of the mighty.

Pam looked in every mirrored window she passed. 

Each glimpse she caught of her beauty gave her naïve happiness, for she was too intelligent to be ashamed of this Narcissus like habit. She loved beauty, wrote it into her fresh little stories; why not enjoy the blessing of having it physically? Heads turned in her direction, always. She gloried in that, too, but only briefly, just as her delight in her reflected image lasted no longer than the inspection. After that, her mind busied itself with many things of greater-or perhaps lesser-import. She thought of ideas for plays, for stories and scenarios, and enthusiastically scribbled them on any scrap of paper in her purse. Thoughts came too fast to be trusted to mental notation.
