INTRODUCTORY 

There is so much of autobiography in this story that it probably ought to be published anonymously. But the truthfulness of the scenes and conversations is so evident that the secret would soon be out. And perhaps one is not to be criticized for exposing even the details of his own love affairs, provided he has had only one of them in his life.

The story is based on the theory that truth is stranger than fiction. It is the story of a young couple who struggled along through life, as most couples have to do, and finally win a fortune rather than fall into it by marriage. Their adventures are as dramatic and varied as are those of any characters in fiction. 

There are no long descriptive passages for two reasons: first, because of a natural aversion on the part of the author to elaboration; and secondly, to conserve the reader's time. The style is the style of the short story. Even the Wilson Essay is constructed on the frame of the short story with a single effect of patriotic feeling and ending in a sort of denouement.

CHAPTER I COLLEGE LIFE 

"Good morning, Kid," said James Colt, as he greeted his room-mate, Larry Warren, one cold, crisp morning in January in Wister Hall, on the Presby College campus. "Hello, Jim, how's everything this morning," answered Larry. "Kid, I'm not sure whether to call you a genius or a durn fool," continued James. "I'm probably both," replied Larry with a questioning smile. "Why do you wonder about such a point?" "I just got done reading your latest piece of writing there on the desk," explained James; "and I don't know how to classify it, even though I am supposed to be a special student of English. 

I wouldn't let such things lie about so recklessly, Kid, if I were you."

Larry looked at the piece of paper which he had left on his desk upon retiring the evening before. Upon it was written a sonnet he had finished after a certain amount of pondering on the previous evening after his room-mate had gone to bed. "I don't imagine you'll be bothered with that piece of literature in your text-book this term," remarked Larry, "even if I am careless with it; and by next year you'll be through college. So, why worry?" "Without fooling, Kid, that one line sounds as though it has something in it, whatever it is," answered James; "so that I'm sometimes set to wondering whether I don't have some kind of a genius for a room-mate." "That's not for me to answer," rejoined Larry; "but an idea 11

flashed into my head last night and I sat down and expressed it in a sonnet, in about half an hour's time. It seemed as though the thoughts, words, and rhymes just fit together and I jotted them down. I lay no claim to being a poet, even if I am a genius of some kind." "Well, I don't know what you've written, Kid, but whatever it is, it's not English," said James. "It seems to be a summary of some of the things we have been discussing recently in the various classes," continued Larry. "And that will be an easy way for me to keep the ideas in mind for the next examination. You know I'm supposed to be a star pupil in psychology and philosophy." "I shouldn't wonder, Kid, only don't take yourself too seriously; it's not good for the health," advised James. "Do you remember the first day in Philosophy class when you gave Sammy that inspired definition of character? I can see you yet rising, hesitating and digging down into your sub-conscious mind and bringing forth that original definition: character is our inherent moral qualities. I remember it myself. I don't believe even 

Sammy ever thought of that one before." "I'll admit I never thought of that definition before," laughed

Larry. "But I just couldn't think of flunking the first recitation in Sammy's class, and I wanted to give something different from what the others in the class had hashed over, such as, 'character is what we are,' and so forth. I remember Sammy's face lighted up when I gave my definition, and he said, 'Yes, yes, that's exactly right; can you elaborate on that a little?'

You know I never was much for elaborating, but more for condensing matters into a few words." "By the way, that's probably what you've done in that sonnet," suggested James; you may have reduced a couple of books in philosophy into a few lines, or a few mouthfuls. That may be a real poem after all." "If it is, it wouldn't be recognized even by your English 

Profs in this day and age," Larry rejoined. "All they know is the old fossil types of stuff following certain forms and styles of another age. My stuff is at least original, whatever it is,"

"You're right about that, Kid," replied James. "I've noticed that in playing baseball you have a system all of your own, the funny part of it is, it works." "Oh, y-e-s? Well there's a reason for me having a system all my own in baseball," answered Larry. "I'm glad you noticed it. You see I'm smaller and lighter than the average second baseman, and I have to make up in speed and quick thinking what I lack in power and size." "And I suppose in the class-room," interrupted James, "you have to make up in thought and originality what you lack in pure memory and bluffing qualities." "I hope you're right," said Larry, "but even originality is not an asset in the average class-room. The one who follows the old rules and forms is the one who gets away with the highest marks, it seems to me." "Don't get sore now, Kid, just because I've been getting higher marks than you," responded James. "I know you used to write better freshman English themes than I, but I couldn't help it if I got better marks than you did." "No, I don't begrudge you a thing," Larry replied, "only it just goes to prove that marks are generally given according to form and style rather than substance. Besides, I believe as a rule you wrote better themes than I did, but even when I did write an exceptionally good one, I didn't get credit for it, while you got credit even for the poor ones on your 'Rep' I suppose.' 

"You should worry, Kid," said James; "they can't keep a good man down, and genius will out." "That's what they say, but it is up to us to prove that proposition," retorted Larry. "The road to fame, they say, is long and rough, and the path of the genius has often been a perilous one in the past. That's the reason I shudder to think of being a genius." "Cheer up, Kid," chuckled James, "you're not in any very great danger. Furthermore, it's time to eat. Let's go!"

During his vacant period after breakfast, when Larry was alone in his room, he could not help contemplating the conver-

sation he had had with James. He picked up the sheet of paper from his desk and mused over the sonnet as follows: 

ON IMMORTALITY 

O Time, thou on, forever on dost run, Like a true line, existing, yet unseen;

Accompanied by boundless Space thou'st been, Like the radius with the sphere begun: 

Two beings created, together unspun, Like two hearts in love each on each doth lean; 

The Soul and the Mind like ye two, I ween, Run on together forever as one. 

But where go we frail mortals of the dust? 

O Soul, would'st allow us to suffer decay? 

If so, how provest thou such to be just? 

Leaving us, what is it thou takest away? 

I take what thou leav'st, I leave but the rust; 

I take, if thou givest, forever and aye.

Could it be possible that there was something of the immortal in this bit of verse? 

The next to the last line, "I take what thou leav'st, I leave but the rust," seemed to be a good modern interpretation of what takes place when life leaves the body, to depart-no one knows where. But this interpretation still left the age-old problem of life and death as much in the dark as ever. 

Yet, what caused these thoughts of connections between immeasurable Time and Space, and the Soul and the Mind, to run through Larry's consciousness and finally to be expressed in verse? Is that the way poetry is written?

Larry could not answer the question. But he knew that the idea of Time, extending as an endless line from any point one might choose, on into Infinity, was original, and gave a sort of mathematical exactness to his conception of Time. And then he thought of Space as beginning at the center of a great sphere and extending in all directions indefinitely beyond the reaches of

the stars and even beyond the limits of his imagination. 

Thus Time and Space became to him more like realities of existence in which we live and move. Beginning at the center of the sphere as does the radius, which may run in a straight line in any given direction to the circumference of the sphere no matter how far extended, is Time. And extending from the center also in ever expanding circles as circumferences, is Space. 

And as there can be no sphere without a radius, so there can be no Space without accompanying Time, and vice versa. 

Although these comparisons seemed to prove nothing, yet they did give Larry a more definite conception of Time and Space, and even of the sphere and its elements, than he had ever had before. 

Furthermore had not the Fourth Dimensionists and the Relativitists practically proved that Time and Space are one and the same thing.

At any rate Larry was getting to the point in life where he wanted conclusive proof for all controversial questions; and he was willing to grope far and wide for evidences of coherence in all parts of the Universe.

So that when he turned upon himself to find the counterpart of Time and Space, he hit upon the idea that the Soul and the Mind were merely relative conceptions of Man comparable to those of Time and Space.

In fact it is more than likely, he thought, that no other creature on earth except man has any conception of Time and Space, and therefore no idea of Soul and Mind as entities in the Universe. Biology and other sciences have traced the development of life from simple to more complex forms by means of adjustment of inner organs and relations to outer objects and relations. For example the organ of the eye was developed in response to light waves impinging upon the nerve ends, and the necessity of developing the creature the better to adjust itself to its surroundings. Is it not possible, therefore, that Man has simply carried this adjustment and development a little further than any other creature until he has gained a definite conception of Time and Space that are unknown to them. 

In fact Man's adjustment to Time (the past, the present and

the future) is his most important consideration in life. As he peers into the past and the future to better adjust himself to his surroundings he even develops a sense of religion and a knowledge of his Maker and the One to whom he feels responsible.

These speculations, though they were getting away from the field of Philosophy and into the field of Theology, led Larry to ask, But what becomes of the Soul after I am gone?

The only answer was that he had been alloted a certain portion of Time, perhaps 30, possibly 70 years; and at the end of that period in which he had employed his allotment for the development of a Soul which bore his imprint, he was obliged to give up that portion of Time to the great source whence it came. 

So that his Soul upon returning to Eternity took with it whatever increment he had added, and left behind those baser elements of the physical world in the form of rust or refuse. 

And so this latter idea was expressed by Larry in the line, "I take what thou leav'st, I leave but the rust."

While thus engaged in thoughtful revery Larry was rudely interrupted by the d-i-n-g d-o-n-g of the old college bell summoning him to the class-room.

CHAPTER II BOYHOOD DAYS 

Thus we find Larry in the midst of a college career, the approach to which had been long and devious. For this youth was not born with a silver spoon in his mouth, as the saying goes.

On the contrary he was one of a family of eleven children whose father might well be described as a frontiersman, or as a backwoodsman, even though all were born and reared in the heart of American civilization in Lancaster county in the southeastern part of Pennsylvania. On account of the fertility of the soil and the abundant agricultural crops this county is sometimes called the Garden Spot of the world. 

But not all of the land in Lancaster county is fertile and tillable. And back in the 90's, in 1892 to be exact, when Larry was born, there was still much woodland to be found in the south and west portions of the country bordering the Susquehanna River. That was before "the blight" had taken its toll of the numerous chestnut trees that covered the country-side and furnished a profitable timber business, such as the production of railroad ties and trolley and telephone poles, as well as being a source of delight for parties of gay chestnut hunters in the Fall of the year. 

In his younger days Larry's father, George Washington Warren, was a wood-chopper. He was a lover of hunting and other sports. The out-door life had caused him to develop a healthy and robust physique. As a result of his environment he had developed the frontiersman's philosophy of a happy-go-lucky life that bade a man live for the joy of living. Frugality was too likely to be regarded as miserliness in his community. 

As a young man, however, Larry's father had acquired a tract of woodland, reasonably well located, which in frontier fashion

he converted with his own hands into a habitation for his young wife and two children. Cutting down the trees of chestnut and oak and hewing them into timbers with his own hands and tools, and with the aid of a carpenter and some friends, he proceeded to construct, not a log cabin, but a two and one-half story house of logs, a type of house in vogue in those days, especially in the sections where timber was plentiful. The house proved to be none too large, for at one time the whole family of thirteen, the parents and eleven children, made their home there. 

The house is still standing and the eleven children are all living, but not the parents.

As the family grew and as time passed by the land was cleared for farming and more ground adjoining was purchased until the place became a farm of some consequence. And since the business of wood-chopping became less and less, it became necessary to pay more attention to farming in order to feed the many mouths in the Warren family.

So that by the time Larry, who was the seventh to come into the family, became old enough to do his share of the work, farming was the chief occupation. And so he learned little of the art of wood-chopping in which his father and older brothers excelled.

Larry's mother was a quiet, sturdy little woman with a quaint sense of humor who took life, however it came, with a smile. She was too busy with her own family, to which she was deeply devoted, to become enmeshed in any of the affairs of the neighborhood. It seemed as though her own personality was completely absorbed by her family and was then carried forth into the community and out to the world at large. No sacrifice was too great for her to bear for one of her family, and no task was too great if one of her neighbors was in distress. 

It was not until Larry had grown to manhood that he learned the great significance attached by his two grandmothers to his coming into the family. Either through legend or superstition they regarded the arrival of the seventh child in this family as a propitious omen; and they decided to commemorate the event

by making a patchwork quilt to be presented to Larry, but not until the time of his marriage. 

Neither grandmother lived to see the day of Larry's marriage, but their plan was kept a secret and was carried out by Larry's mother and her sister in due time. The quilt was a brilliantly colored piece of work, its pattern being of the Temperance Tree.

Larry could scarcely remember his two grandmothers, but his maternal grandfather, Albert Thomas, he could remember well. He was a veteran of the Civil War and lived to be eightyfive years of age, which fact enabled him also to live through the hectic days of the World War.

Without tracing Larry's family tree back too far suffice it to say that all of his grand-parents were efficient users of the "Pennsylvania Dutch" dialect which none of their children learned to use. Larry's forebears were French, Hollanders and 

Scotch rather than German, as many would presume to be the case on account of the queer dialect closely resembling the German language spoken in most parts of Lancaster county in those days.

It is said that when William Penn sent settlers into the Lancaster county district from the vicinity of Philadelphia, he saw to it that a colony of fighting Scotch and Irish was established in southern Lancaster county to act as a buffer state between the peace-loving Quakers and the Amish and Mennonites of

Chester county and eastern Lancaster county, and the savage Indians then numerous in western Lancaster county and along the Susquehanna River. Whether that supposition is correct or not it is a fact that southern and western Lancaster county are inhabited by a different type of people than those of the eastern and northern sections. 

But you will wonder how anyone living within thirteen miles of the city of Lancaster and within seventy-five miles of Philadelphia could be regarded as a backwoodsman. It does seem incredible in this day and age of the automobile and the airplane. But back in the 90's automobiles were practically unheard of and hard roads were about as plentiful as canals. You

will understand better when you are told that Larry lived at 

Starville, on the edge of what is called Beggar-row, and a trip from there to Lancaster by horse and part way by electric trolley car was an event-something to be undertaken only by adults. 

The result was that Larry was old enough to count change and sell strawberries on the markets before he ever saw the city of Lancaster.

But one of the good features of the isolated country life is that one is thrown upon his own resources a lot. Each community must develop its own social center. This usually develops about the public school; or perhaps there is a Lodge hall, where special meetings can be held. It happened that the village of Starville, though very small, was the meeting place of five fraternal organizations and near to it was established a Methodist Camp Meeting which conducted annual sessions in the summer.

This Camp Meeting was a religious rallying ground for the people of the community as well as a gala Home-Coming event for many who had left the neighborhood for other fields. The 

Camp was a little city in itself. Like magic almost over night each August one hundred and fifty tents would spring up and be filled with occupants. Some regarded the place as an outing or summer vacation, while others found in the meetings an outlet for their religious fervor.

It was at these meetings that Larry was privileged to hear many of the ablest preachers of the day. He heard the Scientists and the Evolutionists denounced from the pulpit by powerful and dramatic speakers. The war between Science and Religion was at its height in the days of his youth. 

This aroused Larry's curiosity. He resolved, some day, to end this controversy between Science and Religion by solving the problem to the satisfaction of all concerned. Both sides could not be right. 

It was

Larry attended the little red school in his district. the only school there. There was neither a grammar school nor a high school in the vicinity. The teachers as a rule, however, were capable of carrying their pupils beyond the grammar school

and on into high school work. But there was no curriculum worked out with the idea of advancing into high school. 

Larry's determination to end the war between Science and Religion was, without doubt, the reason why he later was to be found within the walls of learning of a college. Yet the road to college was not mapped out for him in advance, although he was given every encouragement to be studious. 

An incident which shows the bare existence of this rugged family among the hills of southern Lancaster county at the beginning of the twentieth century, when Larry was eight years old, took place in school one day. With a "trapping" system in vogue Larry generally stood at the head of his class. Although he had a mile and a half to walk to school he seldom missed a day and did so only when it was most urgent because missing a day meant going to the foot of the class.

Upon returning to school one morning, after having missed a day, Larry was called aside by his teacher, who inquired why he was not in school the day before. With unusual frankness 

Larry looked up and replied: "I tore my pants and didn't have any other ones to put on till Mom fixed them."

The teacher laughed heartily and asked no further questions. 

The only way for Larry to realize his ambition to reach the places of advanced learning was to become a teacher and work his way forward by degrees. Fortunately at that time the State

Normal Schools offered high school courses to those who did not have a high school in their home district. 

Larry finally made his way into the Normal School through the assistance of his older brothers and the fact that one of them now lived in the town where the school was located. Here he had not only an opportunity to use his full abilities as a student but also to display his prowess at baseball, a game at which he had grown proficient even in the isolation of the small village of Starville. 

Playing baseball was another avenue through which Larry might possibly make his way into some college. The charges of Professionalism, however, which sprang up in the college world against students who made money playing baseball put

a damper on this aspiration. Larry considered it very unjust that anyone should be refused to use his talents in any form of employment to the end of gaining an education. For playing baseball is work. Furthermore Larry could not see what professional baseball had to do with college football, which was an entirely different sport.

At any rate Larry continued to carry on until he had graduated from Normal School and taught one year in a public school not far from his home. He had taken as short a cut as possible through Normal School and as soon as his term of teaching was over he returned to that school partly to get some extra credits and partly to play baseball, since the Coach had written to him to the effect that he was needed on the team that 

Spring. 

Like a flash the big opportunity soon came. Through the recommendation of his Coach, Larry was given an athletic scholarship by Presby College, one of the best small colleges in the country. He was overjoyed. There was no doubt as to his accepting the offer even though he had no idea that the scholarship would cover only a small part of his annual college expenses.

The scholarship also carried with it the requirement, as the word implies, that the recipient be a good scholar of fine character as well as an athlete. Larry could meet the requirements and nothing could deter him from accepting this chance to gain one of his life's ambitions—to get into college. With him baseball, although he loved the game, was a means to an end. But would it always be that?

CHAPTER III GIRLHOOD DAYS 

Just a few years before Larry Warren saw the light of day in southern Lancaster county there was born in the eastern part of the same county, a distance of about 13 miles from Lancaster also, a baby girl. She was named Marie, daughter of 

Aaron Stotthaus, who lived in the heart of the Amish and Men nonite section. 

Marie was born in the humble environment of a country hotel. On her mother's side she was descended from a haughty 

French family, the DeHavens. Her father was the son of a local Dunkard preacher. Force of circumstances, however, and the opportunity to make some money, no doubt, had driven him into the hotel business.

Aaron Stotthaus was wise enough to see also that his wife's natural ability as a cook would stand him in good stead in the hotel business as it was conducted in those days. Her table soon became known far and wide as both hospitable and healthy. 

With Aaron's shrewd business sense and a flare for showmanship the Stotthaus stand became a center for horse sales, shooting matches, pig and fox chases, and anything to draw a crowd. 

Although Aaron Stotthaus was out to make money and to make a success of life, yet he was not one of the type wilfully to make gain out of another's misery. As a bar-tender he would use his wiles to throw the crowd into a happy and friendly mood by being the first to give a "free treat," or "set 'em up" as the expression goes. But when any individual seemed to be overdoing things, or staggering from drinking too freely, he would tactfully refuse him more drink and prevent him from throwing his money away, if possible.

As a boy Aaron had spent some years in a country store as a clerk. He was naturally shrewd and frugal and had inherited

also a natural tendency to look for the better things in life. 

Consequently when some of the children were coming to the age where the environment of the hotel seemed detrimental to them, he purchased a small country store in the midst of an Amish settlement. His judgment in this move proved sound, for in a period of twenty years, he often said, he never lost a dollar through an Amishman. And as to the hotels, they were not frequented by either the Amish or the Mennonites. 

The Amish are a quaint people who believe in sticking close to nature. They wear the plainest of home-made clothes and purchase nothing unless it is absolutely needed, and then it has to be pleasing in the sight of God. Telephones, automobiles, radios and the like are regarded as luxuries not acceptable to God's wishes and therefore not worthy of being enjoyed by them. For this reason the Amish are probably the only people in America who are immune from high pressure salesmanship. 

No churches, highly ornamented or otherwise, grace their community for their meetings are held in selected homes from time to time.

Everybody in the new neighborhood spoke Pennsylvania Dutch. It is not surprising, therefore, that Marie became proficient in its use. She was one of a family of ten children. She seemed to have inherited all of the best qualities of both of her parents. As she grew to girlhood she became her father's most attractive and efficient clerk in the store. mother's aptitude for cooking and sewing. father's parsimonious attitude toward dress that caused her to develop, as she grew older, a taste for good clothes.

Perhaps it was her

Aaron Stotthaus was over-frugal and saving. He was of the type who thought that the tighter the shoe fits, the less likely will the child wear it out jumping rope. He was strictly the "boss" of the family and saw to it that nothing went to waste about the store. In spite of the fact that they were on the shelves, new shoes and new dresses were a sacrifice only to be made on rare occasions.

Marie received her schooling in the same little red school in which the Amish refused to be "learnt." She was one of the

few bright spots in the life of the teachers of that school. She was alert with figures (she had to be to clerk in her father's store) and showed an aptitude for composition. Her school days were cut short, however, when her mother's health broke under the strain of life, and Marie was prevailed upon to remain at home in order to relieve her mother from the housework as well as to perform the duties at times of the clerk in the store.

In a few years' time, after her mother had recuperated considerably, Marie began to see what a loss she, herself, had suffered in quitting school so early. This had deprived her of those qualifications required in almost every field of endeavor in her day and age. Truly, she could have married Hiram

Fischer, a prominent member of the Plain Sect. He had paid court to her for some time and would have given up everything but his religion in order to win her. 

But, although Marie had learned to love the driving of a fine horse, and was convinced of the genuineness of Hiram's courtship, yet she could not see herself being tied to the sink in a farm kitchen for the rest of her life, even if the farm was one of the finest in the county and was clear of all mortgages. 

There was only one thing for Marie to do. The only vocation open to her, on account of her meager education, was the nursing profession. She could sacrifice her life to nursing as Florence Nightingale had done. Besides, by entering a Nurses

Training School she could free herself forever from Hiram Fischer. There could be no better way of informing this generous admirer that his ardent wooing must be in vain.

No sooner said than done. The chairman of the Examining 

Board at the hospital was quick to see the essential qualities of this natural born nurse. And although she had never heard of such subjects as physics and chemistry, yet she scored a hundred in arithmetic and gave a clear, sensible answer as to why she wanted to become a nurse. So the board waived her weakness in the more modern subjects and called her first to duty in the new class.

The three years of training were also three years of educa-

tion for Marie. Nothing worth while escaped her eye or ear. 

Thrown in contact with the most influential doctors in the city, and caring for well-to-do, as well as less fortunate people in the hospital, Marie observed and absorbed all of the gentler characteristics to be found among those who became her acquaintances.

She became a cheerful entertainer as well as a gentle worker among her patients to such a degree that her technique was recognized by the leading medical men of the city, and her success in the private nursing field was assured long before her graduation from the institution. 

But Marie's hope of an easy separation from her former admirer was not so readily realized. She had to go back home sometimes; and even though her visits were short, they could by no means be kept a secret at such a public place as a country store.

Neither could Marie escape taking an occasional drive with her old sweetheart, as he considered himself. On one of these drives he tried to press his case to a conclusion. He could feel that his hold on this girl was slipping; but he would not lose out without knowing why. Like a licked dog, he would bite back as a last retort. "Perhaps," he said, "you have found someone more entertaining than I, among your friends at the hospital. I hear the nurses are a lively crowd to get in with." "Oh, I don't know," she replied, "they are about the same as other people when you learn to know them." "I'm glad to know that," he said, "placing his arm about her waist; then you mean to say you are no different since you entered training than you were before?"

She was driving at the time, but was letting the lines lie loosely on the dashboard while the horse walked leisurely along. 

The bright moon threw streaks of light here and there across the road where it shone through the trees. 

She took one hand to arrest his arm as it tightened about her and asked, "What do you mean, I don't see how I could be much different in so short a time?"

"I mean," he faltered, "that maybe you'll get such new fangled ideas into your head that you'll grow to be so different from the kind of girl I'd like to marry. I love you, so much,

Marie," he begged, "won't you give up training and come to live with me?" "Oh, I couldn't give up training, and I can't give you any promises about getting married," she replied. "There are lots of nice girls among your own people you could marry, Hiram. 

Why do you waste your time with me?" "But it's you that I like and I won't marry any one else," he countered. "Promise me this, if you must finish training to be a nurse, that you won't let your learnin' make you wicked like some I hear about." "Well, I'll promise you that, Hiram," she said with deeper thought than Hiram ever suspected. For in the hospital she was now in a position to see life both at its worst and at its best. The pure freshness of a new-born babe is life at its best; and the wantom wrecking of a strong body through dissipation and disease is life at its worst. 

A short while later, with Hiram standing at his horse's head by the hitching-post, Marie bade him good-night, as it turned out to be, for the last time.

CHAPTER IV BOILING APPLE-BUTTER 

"Larry," said his mother one morning early in the month of

September, "I'd like to get some apple-butter made one of these days before you go back to school." "All right, Ma," answered Larry, "I guess we can find time to gather some apples and take them to the cider mill for you. 

I may as well get at it right away."

This was not an unpleasant task for Larry for more reasons than one. For Larry, too, had a sweetheart in his younger days.

She lived at the old mill where cider was made and feed was ground for the farmers about Starville. That is to say, she was a sweetheart in a way, because Larry had never confessed his love to her, nor she to him.

It was some distance over the hills and through valleys which led by way of a winding course down along a winding stream to the mill, where Larry was wont to go at times to see Irene. 

As a young girl she was tall and well developed beyond her age. Larry had met her at a little gathering of friends at her neighbor's house on the hill just up the road from where she lived. Larry took her to be a girl nearly out of her 'teens, when he had first met her. But later he found out that she really was not much more than beginning her 'teens. 

So that in the evening as the neighborly gathering of friends was breaking up, Larry asked to walk home with her and she seemed delighted. It was not a long walk, but it had started to snow during the day and by evening there was a white coating several inches deep on the ground. So they had to plow their way with their feet through the loose snow down the road to the mill. Larry's mission performed, that first evening, he returned to the party and was taken home by his brother, with whom he had come there.

This comely lass appealed to Larry. Her quiet smile, blue eyes and girlish expression were a picture to behold, and Larry at one time missed no opportunity to find her company even if it meant, as it did once, that he had to trudge home on foot, the distance of five miles through the mud from her house. 

When Larry discovered, however, that she was so tender in years his love for her, if love it was, turned to the kind of love one has for a sister or other near friend. 

On the present occasion Larry set to work with a will and soon had a wagon load of apples ready for the mill. He would have them ground and the cider pressed out of them in the afternoon while in the morning of the next day the butterboiling would begin. An apple "snitzing" party was hurriedly called for that evening to prepare the apples for the boiling. 

Boiling apple-butter, the old way, is an all-day process. Beginning before sunrise with a barrel of cider, this is boiled and boiled until only half of it remains. Then several bushels of apples, pared and cut into snits, are cooked into the remaining thick liquid. Both these processes are very slow, because if the fire is made too brisk the cider and half-cooked apples will boil over and much of the butter will be lost. 

This process seemed too long and slow for the patience of Larry. He would try out a theory of his own. He didn't see any sense in his mother and himself stirring and running their legs off from before sunrise in the morning until after sundown, which it usually was when the butter was ready to be taken from the half-barrel sized copper kettle in which it was made. "Here, what are you doing there, boy," called Larry's mother about nine o'clock in the morning after the cider had boiled down, and it was time for adding the apples to it. "Putting in some more apples," said Larry simply. "Wait a minute till I see," requested his mother. "I'm afraid you'll be boiling my butter over and we'll lose half of it."

Just then the foam began to rise and the apples and liquid in the kettle gave a sign of being ready to flow over the sides of their container. Larry grabbed another bucket of fresh apple

snits and dumped them into the mass. The foam subsided and the boiling became normal again. "Here, here, that'll never do," called his mother; "you'll have so many apples in there pretty soon that the whole thing will boil over." "Never mind, Ma," said Larry; "you just run along about your other work and I'll watch this until the apples are all in. 

I'll call you, if anything serious happens." "All right," replied Larry's mother; "but you'd better scatter that fire a little and not make it so hot. I'm afraid you're going to have trouble."

But Larry had a theory of his own. He didn't think it necessary to stand there all day just to get the apples cooked. Instead of putting in a few apples at a time and then waiting until they were thoroughly cooked, Larry conceived the idea that each time the mass got good and hot and was about to boil over he would throw in some fresh snits, which would cool the mass down, and at the same time rapidly cook the fresh apples. He was not sure at first how his theory would work out. Perhaps in the end he would have so much green material in the kettle, when the fresh apples were all used up, that the whole thing would explode, as it were, and be blown out of the large kettle. 

So that it was with some trepidation that Larry carried out his new theory of boiling apple-butter and saving time. His idea was right. He had the apples all in the kettle in much less time than by the old method, and the real business of boiling and stirring the butter was quickly under way.

There was still plenty of stirring and cooking to be done. 

Taking turns with the stirrer, as the long-handled, wooden paddle is called, Larry and his mother and a few others of the family worked on into the middle of the afternoon. "Gosh!" said his mother; "that butter's getting pretty thick already. Run and get me a spoon and saucer, Larry.”

Larry quickly responded with the request. He was anxious to see his theory vindicated. He watched his mother dip some of the butter into the saucer. Then she quickly turned the saucer upside down. The butter clung stickily to the dish.

"Hemph, that's funny," she said, "this butter's pretty nearly done already. I wonder what's the matter with it." "Nothing's the matter with it, Ma; didn't you notice how quickly I got all the apples cooked into it. Well, I saved all that time this morning. This butter began to cook a couple of hours earlier than our butter usually does." "I guess you're right, Larry," she said. "The butter seems to be done. We'll get the crocks together and take it off the fire."

And so the apple-butter was all placed in crocks and stored away for safe keeping long before sun-down; and Larry had put a new theory into practice.

CHAPTER V ON THE VARSITY

Larry took in the whole situation even to the extent of noting the direction of the wind and the effect it would have on fly balls.

As expected the left-handed Navy hitters were hitting the ball consistently and lustily toward second base. But Larry was everywhere in the way of the ball. His speed was dazzling, and he was as supple as an acrobat. Like an Evers or a Maranville (he was about their size) he had a system of his own, and it worked.

The Navy was continually getting men on bases, but nearly always Larry would execute some kind of a play that would check their rallies. For instance in the fifth inning the first Navy man up got on base. He danced a short distance off the bag and waited as Larry camped under a fly ball, which came down just inside the second base line, not far from the pitcher's box. 

The play was an easy one but the runner on first wanted to have something of a start in case Larry should drop the ball.

Larry had surmised this. As the ball descended, therefore, he set himself in position so that the instant the ball struck his hands he could relay it to first base. Catching the ball with his hands near his right shoulder, Larry gave a snap of his wrist and like a flash the ball shot into the first baseman's glove. In fact the baseman caught the ball as though he was preventing it from hitting him in the stomach. So speedily did the Kid, as he was called, execute the play that the runner on first base was caught flat-footed and had no chance to beat the throw back to the bag. 

So the bases were cleared with two out, and the pitcher had nothing to worry about.

The Navy picked up another run in the sixth inning, but so did Presby, and the score was now three to two in favor of the 

Presby team.

In the seventh inning the Navy threatened again having two men on base with two out. The next batter hit one high and far toward right field. Larry started with the crack of the bat toward the right field foul line. No one could tell where that ball would land after striking one of the strong currents of air high above the field. The right fielder first started back and then forward when the wind caught hold of the ball. Meanwhile the base-runners were heading toward the home plate. 

The right fielder soon saw that he was hopelessly out of the

race for the fly-ball, so he called to Larry, "You take it Kid."

But that was nothing compared with what happened in the ninth inning. Presby had scored two more runs and were leading 5 to 2. It was the last turn at bat for the Navy after which the game would end. The Navy was desperate and was putting on a final rally. Such rallies are always disconcerting to the opposing team in the final inning.

There were men on first and second base with only one out.

There was a smack of the ball against his glove and down he came with the sphere. All in all Larry had accepted twelve chances and made one error in that game besides scoring the first run and driving in two other runs.

Just what part the extra sweat-shirt played in Larry's success that day cannot be told. On three occasions the Navy rooters voiced their approval of his uncanny defensive plays, however, and every man on his own team from the Coach down wondered how he did it.

Tom Trout knew the secret, but he never gave it away because he wanted that last ball for a souvenir of the game and he promised Larry that he would keep the secret provided Larry gave him the ball.

Needless to say Larry handed over the ball after running over to second base as the side was retired; and the game ended. 

At the end of the college term both Larry and Freddie Gibson, the slow ball pitcher, were given berths on an Industrial League team in the coalfields of southwestern Virginia. They both had a good season except that near the end Larry contracted the typhoid fever germ which was prevalent in the town where he played ball, and during the last week his work was very listless. The germ was working in his system but he fought it off, without knowing it, until after he got home a week later. 

He even played in a game at home after his return, but it was not long afterward that he became so ill that he had to be removed to a hospital, the one from which Marie had recently graduated.

In fact she had learned a lot more about Larry and his past life than he ever suspected. He planned all the nice things he would say to her and how he would express his appreciation when she came to say goodbye to him. He never could break through the mask of her professional air to talk personally to her. And she would be released that very day.

As Larry sat there in the wheel-chair in his room that afternoon, in came Marie. She was wearing her street apparel and her airplane hat, as Larry called it. She was there to say goodbye.

She took Larry's hand and wished him a speedy recovery. He thanked her and tried to collect his thoughts of the past few hours; but before he could bring his organs of speech to function, she was gone. CHAPTER IX A FLITTING 

"Marie, we'll leave it to you to get the dinner today, so you go on up to the new place and get things in shape," said Marie's mother early on the morning of the day they were moving from the old store for a new life of ease in retirement. "Alright," said Marie; "but what'll we 'fress.' It'll take something to feed the bunch that's helping us to do this moving." Marie was soon loaded into a spring wagon along with the potatoes, which would have to be pared, the sauerkraut and spare ribs, and all the other articles enumerated for dinner. 

She would be the first to take possession of the new home along the main highway leading from Lancaster to Philadelphia by way of New Holland and Blue Ball. Her first introduction would be to the kitchen, where she could be counted upon to produce a meal worthy of the force of men required to do the moving. 

For there would be plenty to move. Having lived at the store for twenty-five years the Stotthauses had accumulated enough furniture and other furnishings to fill several large hay-flats. 

By the time Marie had things straightened out in the new kitchen, two large wagons had already arrived and were unloading things in the yard. Besides the regular living-room, bedroom and parlor suites, there were three old-fashioned walnut chests, which were antiques of the DeHavens of pioneering days,

an old grandfather's clock, handed down from the Stotthaus branch of the family, and an old-fashioned book-case, now called a secretary.

Then there was the old-fashioned china-closet that is never opened except to display the rare pieces of Stiegel glassware and willow-china kept in it for safety.

Aaron Stotthaus, after long years of careful saving, was now ready to retire from the store business. He had accumulated, in the last twenty-five years, enough capital to do this. He was shrewd enough to see, also, that after the War days there might be a change in business conditions. He would sell out when the selling was good.

He not only had saved money, but he had invested wisely in the long run; so that now he could sit down and watch his money working for him. Another reason for his retiring, however, was that his health was no longer equal to the task of tending the store.

He could retire, not because he had run a country store, but because he had run a country store in a big way. He did most of his buying in large lots from Philadelphia concerns, while he shipped to Philadelphia such produce as butter and eggs. But the business in which he practically made his fortune was a novel one. He was the first to see the advantage in the new parcel post system set up by the Post Office Department of the Government.

Here was an opportunity to deal with the Amish people whereever they might be settled. He sent goods as far west as the state of Washington and northwards into Canada, where there are also Amish settlements. He got in touch with them by advertising in their own paper.

At one time the large stores of Lancaster and Philadelphia had on hand large supplies of what were called remnants of cloth. 

Not only this but he got an option on all the left-over pieces at the factories where cloth is made. The brighter the colors, the better. And the odder the pieces, the better also.

The sateen and cotton-delaine were ideal cloths for making the home-made clothes of the Amish people. Purples, greens, and

reds, in all their shades, are used by them for making quilts and comforts and rugs. The blues, browns and blacks are used for dress apparel. The materials always had to be plain without figures, designs or blended colors for them. And no one knew this, or the other demands of the Amish, better than Aaron Stotthaus.

This goods came to the store in boxes so big that Marie often had to get inside the box and hand out the bundles, each of which contained exactly fifty yards. And as it came, so it was sold, fifty yards at a time. This saved time in packing and unpacking when the goods was shipped by parcel post. 

It was not unusual either for the Amish of the local neighborhood to drive in their quaint yellow wagons a distance of fifteen miles, just to do business with Aaron Stotthaus. For the Amish would all swear by him, and he would swear by them.

So that it is no wonder Aaron Stotthaus was now prepared to sit down and take things easy. He owned stock in several concerns, chief of which was stock in one of the Lancaster banks, now worth several times what he had paid for it. He had not always invested wisely, however; for he had once been one of those to sink a goodly sum in the swamplands about the City of 

Buffalo, N. Y., without so much as seeing them. He finally found out through one of his friends, who had also invested in this stock selling scheme, that the well laid out plan for a real estate development was located in a swamp. The friend in his travels had stopped to look over the property he had bought. 

Once settled in his new home Aaron Stotthaus took great delight (it was the only thing he had to do) in checking up on his investments and seeing how they were growing. This was especially true up until the Fall of 1929, when the big stock market crash came. After that everything seemed to turn the other way.

Trolley company stock, of which Lancaster county had one of the finest systems in the country, became almost worthless. 

Automobiles were replacing trolleys as a means of transportation. 

So that the comparatively expensive country lines to Quarryville, and Strasburg, the only boroughs in southern Lancaster county,

and to Millersville, Columbia, Mountville, Marietta, Mt. Joy,

Elizabethtown, Manheim, Lititz, Ephrata, New Holland and 

Terre Hill, became a burden to operate, and some of them had to be disbanded and replaced by buses. Few of his stocks were paying dividends any more. Such became the condition by 1931 that looked so bright in the year 1918, except for the World War, which was now raging at its highest. 

By nightfall the deck was clear again about the new homestead. The furniture had all been put in place and a light supper was prepared by Marie.

CHAPTER XI HELPING A NEIGHBOR 

There was a crack of the .22 caliber rifle and down went the shoat to its knees. Larry had been asked during his Spring vacation to assist one of his country neighbors in the butchering of a pig. It was not to be a large animal and Larry with Sam Craig and his wife could easily do the job themselves, Larry was assured. 

Larry had done the first part of his job nobly; that was shooting the pig. But Larry did not know that he was to be the butcher-in-chief. He had expected Sam Craig, himself, to hold that position.

It was just about sun-up and there lay the dead animal on the ground, that is it was almost dead. It had dropped to its knees and then fell over on its side stiffening its muscles as it did so. It would lie stark still for a minute or two and then it would begin to kick and struggle like a wild deer. "Who has the knife?" said Larry; as he saw no one was making a move to stick this animal and bleed it. "Here it is, I have it," said Sam. "Well, why don't you use it," said Larry. "Stick the pig before it begins to kick." "I can't stick it," answered Sam. "I never stuck a pig in my life. I was counting on you to do that." "Neither did I," said Larry. "But hand me that knife. We've got to bleed this animal somehow." 

Larry had often seen his father deftly stab his butcher-knife through the skin on a pig's throat and then thrust it on through the fat and flesh until its point struck the jugular vein. 

So he took the knife, jabbed it into the pig's neck, made a couple of extra thrusts and then withdrew it. To his surprise out gushed the blood with a spurt and began to flow to the ground. The pig began to twist and kick viciously while Sam

threw his weight on it to hold it down, lest it might break a leg which would mean the spoiling of a nice ham. 

Thus the butchering got under way and Larry, who began as helper was immediately promoted to be the chief butcher. 

The large iron-kettle of water was boiling hot and by the time the pig was completely bled and lay still on the ground the men had the scalding trough half full of water. Larry took a bucket of cold water from the spring and added it to the hot water, as he had seen others do, lest the skin of the pig should get seared by the too hot water which would cause the bristles to stick. 

Everything was hustle and bustle and events moved along nicely from scraping the bristles off the pig to hanging it up to be dressed.

But after this was accomplished there was another lull. Who was going to dress the pig, that is take out the insides and cut the animal in pieces so that it could be taken down and turned into hams, shoulders, sausage, pudding and lard.

Again Larry was elected. He had seen this done often enough but never had paid close enough attention to learn the details of the process. But he took the sharpest knife he could find and went to work.

The first step was to run a slit full length down the front and middle of the pig cutting through the skin. Then by more careful manipulation of the knife the slit was cut deeper until the inner cavities of the animal were reached. Larry proceeded cautiously at first but soon displayed the prowess of a veteran.

He balks every time we try to drive him out the lane."

CHAPTER XII IN THE ARMY 

Cook! Are you awake?" a woman's voice called softly through the corridor of the Army hospital at midnight at the large embarcation camp. "Yes, yes, I'm awake. What is it Nurse, that you want?" answered Cook, who occupied bed No. 56. "Don't you go to sleep," replied the nurse; "there's a big nigger prowling up and down the hall out there and I don't want to be left alone here in this office." "Gee, Nurse it does mah heart good to hear you call a niggah, a niggah, as he ought to be called. Ar' you from the South, too?" asked Cook. "No, I'm not from the South but I had a nasty experience with that big Coon this morning, when I went to take his pulse. 

And I'm not taking any chances. I heard the boards squeaking in the hall a couple of times tonight. It's getting on my nerves,' she replied. 

"Well, don't worry Nurse, about any niggahs when I'm around. 

Ah won't do any sleepin' tonight," Cook assured her.

It was Marie speaking. She had enlisted in the Army Nurse 

Corps late in the summer of 1918. The spirit of adventure and the desire to do her "bit" had driven her to take the step which was made with Larry's reluctant approval. She was lauded by her home doctors for her patriotism, for she could easily have pleaded poor health following the attack of typhoid fever.

It was now the first of November 1918, just two years since Larry had recovered under her care at her home hospital. There was a dearth of nurses everywhere at this time on account of the "Flu" epidemic that was sweeping America as well as the battlefields of Europe.

The Embarcation Camp to which she was assigned for duty

was like the neck of a bottle through which all of the soldiers leaving this country for France had to pass. All soldiers reaching this point in poor condition of health had to remain until they were well, and those returning from Europe had to be held for two weeks' inspection and "de-lousing" as it was called. 

So that nursing here was done on a wholesale scale, one nurse being in charge of as many as 150 patients at a time. 

The influenza epidemic had added enormously to the congestion. Robust men from the valleys of the South and from the plains and mountains of the West would arrive here in sound health only to keel over, like flies before nicotine, when they were stricken by the treacherous influenza germ. When the cold weather came it was not unusual to stand the corpses of men, tied in sacks with a tag to them, against the walls of the morgue in order to conserve floor space. Coffins could not be supplied fast enough to care decently for the dead.

Events moved at a killing pace. Marie had passed her requirements for over-seas duty and was scheduled to leave November 10. There was some delay and before she finally got aboard the ship that would take her "somewhere in France" the Armistice was signed. Bedlam broke loose in the camp, and all over the world for that matter. Since she had purchased her complete over-seas outfit, Marie was a bit disappointed. It seemed like a drab existence to go back to camp duty with the excitement of the war over.

Cook was a young man from the southland who had gone back into the ranks too soon after having had the measles and was having some trouble with his eyes which kept him confined to the hospital. Everyone just called him Cook. He was not really ill and proved a handy man about the place to assist the nurse on duty on many occasions.

Marie felt grateful toward him for the many little favors he had done for her. So that it was not surprising that one evening in spite of rigid rules against a Private being alone in the company of a nurse, Cook found himself in the little office alone with Marie. The honor of the nurse as well as that of the Private was at stake in case they were discovered.

Marie knew this but she was inclined to sympathize with the average Private in the army because he was the under-dog and had to bear the brunt of many injustices. So she tolerated Cook's presence even though she feared her action might cause trouble. 

And that's what happened because before many minutes along came a Second "Looey." Cook hearing his footsteps approach, took no chances, but, hiding his own face as best he could, rushed into the hall and knocked the Second "Looey" cold and made his escape. Cook had acted to protect Marie's name from scandal rather than his own.

This episode grew out of one of those necessary rules of discipline in the army which make life miserable for the Private. A nurse may not be seen alone with a private without losing her honor, while she may go anywhere with the officers, some of whom do not expect her to have any honor.

While Marie was having these trying experiences in the Army Nurse Corps Larry was learning something of army life himself. 

He had been called in the draft during the summer for limited service. About that same time plans were completed for creating the Students Army Training Corps. The large colleges and many of the smaller ones were converted into veritable army training camps filled with what remained of the former students of the colleges and also with a new crop of youngsters just out of the high schools.

The idea was to keep the colleges from closing their doors altogether and give them a chance to assist in building up the strength of the army. The college courses were revised with the object of giving special training in specific lines of army work. 

Although Larry was not required to participate in all the drills of his company, he took part in them whenever he felt capable. 

It is remarkable how the system of picking out for a certain job, the man best fitted for performing the task, worked out in practice. There were many in Lancaster county who were opposed to bearing arms under any circumstances. These were used as chauffeurs, blacksmiths, mechanics and the like. One of

Marie's cousins became the chauffeur of one of the high officers

in the war and performed services equal to that of many a soldier at the front with a rifle.

Larry finally became enrolled on the army list, the Vocational group were about ready to move on, another step nearer to the 

Embarcation Camp.

"It's the truth," rejoined the other. "He is the first one to die of the 'Flu.' They just took him over to the camp hospital yesterday."

Incredible as it seemed, Larry had to believe the story, and with a further check-up he found it was only too true. The parents were expected to arrive for the body that afternoon. 

This brought the war nearer home to Larry than anything else. 

He knew Marie's younger sister would be heart-broken. She had only been wearing the engagement ring for a short time.

CHAPTER XIII ON WITH THE DANCE 

A new joy seemed to come into the world with the closing of the great war.

Thousands of families were re-united after the fierce conflict. And thousands of sweethearts like Marie and

Larry went back to their normal ways of living. The earth for a time seemed to be surrounded with a new air of freedom. 

Aristocratic forms of government everywhere had given way before the hosts of democracy. Woodrow Wilson, whose high idealism had attracted the attention of the world in the closing year of the World War, carried his message of peace and goodwill to Europe in person where he sought to make secure for the future the things which had been won at such great sacrifice. 

The hopes of people everywhere were high. A League of Nations was established. It gave promise of removing the threat of war, which in the past had always hung, like an ominous cloud, over the world. There was reason to believe that the nations of the world would soon adapt themselves to the ways of peace, and that happiness would reign supreme everywhere. 

But things did not look so bright to Larry and his fraternity brothers at the college. Not having the means to join and keep up with a large National fraternity in college, Larry had joined a local group, or club, that was more within the range of his pocketbook.

This group, which was recognized as a fraternity of ten years existence, had rented a house near the college campus. During the time that the college had been turned into an army camp, however, it was not possible for the boys either to rent the house to the government, or to make use of it themselves. On the campus the fraternity houses were turned into barracks. 

As a consequence, when college reopened after New Year's, the boys of the local group found themselves in a hole, With the

disbanding of the S. A. T. C. this group lost all of the new men it had pledged. For one reason or another none of them returned to college. There was practically no money in the treasury and there were hardly enough members left to make the Eating club a going concern.

Under these circumstances it was doubtful whether the fraternity would be able to survive. The boys decided there was only one man who could pull the fraternity together. That was Larry 

Warren, who was immediately chosen president for the balance of the college year.

Larry had always taken an active part in the affairs of the fraternity, but the problem he faced was almost an impossible one. He looked the situation over, however, and jotted down a few points he wanted to bring out before the group when installed as president.

After thanking the boys for the trust they had placed in him Larry said: "We have reached what might be called the critical period in the history of our fraternity. Our treasury is empty, we have lost all of our new members and it is doubtful whether we can keep even our own Eating club going, which is the soul of the fraternity. As I see it there is only one way out; and that is to get more new men, and get them quickly. "This will immediately bring money into the treasury and restore our Eating club to a paying basis. So that I am going to ask not only the Rushing committee, but every man in the fraternity to get out after new men. There are plenty of good ones around and we've got to get them immediately. After that is accomplished we can proceed to concentrate our efforts on other problems. "It is my hope that by the time Spring is here we will be ready to make a special effort toward obtaining a charter from a national fraternity; and before the year is over we ought to have our petition in their hands."

The boys immediately saw the light and set out with a new hope. Everyone put his shoulder to the wheel and by the end of the week half a dozen new men were pledged to the fraternity.

These were immediately admitted to the Eating club and by the

time they paid their initiation fees the fraternity would be pretty well on its feet. In fact one of the new boys, upon learning the circumstances, paid his fee in advance. 

Larry was careful in choosing his committees, selecting the man best fitted and qualified for his work. He placed Paul Neale, former president and English student, at the head of the national committee with a view to getting a petition in shape in the Spring. 

He chose Tom Pratt, the most socially inclined member of the group, as head of the Rushing committee. All in all Larry showed a kind of ability no one suspected him of having. 

His strength lay undoubtedly in his ability to see what was needed most at the time and pointing it out to the rest so that they would know what they were aiming at. 

In this way no powder was wasted.

Marie took an interest and a delight in all of Larry's affairs at the college. So that by the time graduation week came around for Larry she was prepared to give her heart and soul to the event. There were many gala affairs among which was a sort of private dance put on by the boys of the fraternity. 

Larry had tried hard to get matters in shape for preparing a petition to the national fraternity. But in spite of Paul Neale's. ability as an English student it seemed impossible to get him to act. The main trouble was possibly that he didn't know just what ought to be said in the petition.

Larry knew what he wanted done. So he hit upon the idea of writing the petition himself and having Paul edit it, so to speak. This plan worked out admirably and within a week's time the boys had their petition all ready for the printers. 

In a jubilant frame of mind on account of this success they decided to stage a little party and dance of their own during graduation week.

Marie had spent not a little time and money in preparation for the event. Her own ability as a seamstress saved her some money, however. She had some ideas of her own about dress that she always wanted a chance to work out. Here was her opportunity. She purchased some very expensive material of a gold and black color and made herself a brilliant skirt such as were

Larry lifted his opponent clear off the floor and let him fall limp upon it. The referee began a slow count that was longer than the Dempsey-Tunney disputed count, but to no avail. The account of the battle in the college paper gave Larry's opponent credit for being the gamest fighter in the game.

CHAPTER XIX SOME FISHING

The boats are already down by the river and the tide will be just high enough by the time you get down there. See that you bring back a good mess of flounders for Therby to cook." 

Flounders are an oddly formed fish that seem to be all twisted out of shape. If I recall rightly they have only one eye in the middle of the head, and they just flip and flounder about in the water much as a leaf would do were it heavy enough to sink. 

Larry set a couple of the boys to digging clams for bait, while he and the rest of them began straightening out the lines and putting on new hooks. For this was the first fishing trip of the season. Marie had just arrived at the camp and she looked on with interest. She had not known anything about 

Larry's prowess as a fisherman. It rather amused her to see him equip all the lines and hooks for the boys with meticulous care. The lines are simply dropped from the side of the boat in this kind of fishing. 

Soon everything was in readiness. The boys had arrived with

the clams and off the party went. There were two boat loads; that is, small boats that are rowed by oars. Larry put one of the older boys in charge of one boat and then got into the other himself.

The blue swells of water were now rolling in from St. Mary's bay to the mouth of the Sisaboo river. This was a fine place to catch flounders, although the boys were not allowed to row too far out toward the bay, where the water would have been too deep for their lines anyway. 

Larry soon hauled in a "whale" of a fish. It was one of the nicest flounders any one of the party had ever seen. The boys were pulling on their lines, but bringing them in without any fish.

Soon surmising what the trouble was, Larry explained to the boys, especially the newer ones, that they should run the hook through the muscular part of the clam in baiting it, much as one would place a worm on a hook. In simply piercing the hook through the clam the boys had been giving the flounders a chance to suck the whole thing loose without catching fast themselves. 

Larry kept on pulling in flounders and once in a while a yell would come up from the other boat, indicating that someone. had just landed fish. There was always a rivalry to see which boat would bring in the most fish. And the boys in Larry's boat were now getting better results also. 

So that after about an hour's fishing, when the tide again started to drift back toward the bay, and the flounders with it, the two parties heaved their oars and headed for the shore. As the boys from the other boat peered into Larry's boat to see what results he had had, one of them cried, "Holy mackerel, where'd you get all the fish? And look at the size of that one; who caught him?”

After the commotion of landing and unloading the fish was over all hands helped to tug the fish up the steep bank to the camp. When they arrived at that place the director was there to greet them. "My goodness, Larry," he said, "you have enough fish there to feed the camp for a week; how'd you catch them all?"

After one of the boys explained that Larry had caught the most of them the director took a good laugh and told Larry he'd have to teach the boys to do most of the work and not do it all himself.

Later on Larry had a fine morning's sport trout fishing in one of the small streams that flows out from the many inland lakes in Nova Scotia. The boys had been on a ten mile hike and stayed over night near one of the streams. It had rained during the night and on such occasions the trout come down out of the lakes toward the mouths of the streams.

After Larry had got the boys off early for their hike back to camp (he, himself, would be taken back by machine), he rigged up a hook and line while he was waiting. In about half an hour's time he caught two dozen nice trout. It was the best trout fishing Larry had ever seen.

On this hiking trip Larry had occasion to be rebuked by the director of the camp. The point where the boys were to camp and sleep over night was far up the river. There was an old wooden bridge across the river near where a wood-pulp mill had once been.

While the boys hiked the ten miles Larry was taken by machine. He also had with him some of the heavier articles of equipment in order to lighten the burden of some of the smaller boys on the hike. 

Larry was warned not to cross the old bridge with the machine, which was a Ford, because the bridge was not considered safe by some. But when Larry came to the bridge there was a team of very large, heavy oxen crossing it. Larry waited as this pair of fat, slick animals came toward him and ambled by. Larry thought, if that bridge could carry that pair of heavy oxen it certainly would bear the weight of an old Ford car. 

Of course the boys went back to camp and told how Larry had saved them from lugging their eats, some blankets and other things across the bridge and up the hill. When Larry returned from the trip, therefore, the director wanted to know why Larry had crossed the bridge. "Did Therby say you should go across?

He knows better than that, Can I trust nobody to do what

I want done?" exclaimed the director, who was a stern master. "No," said Larry, "Therby wouldn't even stay in the machine.

He got out and walked across. I was responsible for the whole thing."

Then Larry explained the circumstances to Mr. Clay, and the reason he had taken the chance, if chance it was. Later when they were alone they both had a good laugh over the matter. 

The director said that Therby had told him that he had nothing to do with it, but that he thought Therby untruthful. He couldn't understand why Larry would disobey orders. And so the incident was closed.

On the trip back from Nova Scotia by boat Larry became seasick. "What's the matter with you, Larry," said Marie, as Larry entered the stateroom on board the vessel bound from Nova 

Scotia to Boston. "I'm losing my equilibrium," said Larry. "I think you must be; you look pale enough. Better come in and lie down," advised Marie. 

And lie down Larry did. But that did no good. Seasickness is seasickness, whether one is lying down or standing up. And when Larry said he was losing his equilibrium, he was correct. 

For seasickness is caused by the same organs that control our equilibrium.

These organs are commonly known as the semi-circular canals located within the inner ear. The organs are two in number, one in either ear. But each organ is composed of three distrinct semi-circular canals filled with liquid, much like the glass-filled tube in a spirit-level.

One of these canals lies in each of the three planes of space representing length, breadth and thickness, the three dimensions of space. By reason of the pressure exerted against the nerve endings within the canal by the liquid which moves to and fro, as the head is moved, these organs control our equilibrium, or our ability to stand or sit erect without dizziness.

If the head is shaken rapidly the liquid naturally flows back and forth rapidly, causing us to become dizzy. In the same

way on a boat the vibrations from the engines, and the continual swaying of the boat cause one not only to become dizzy but to become sick at the stomach. There must be some connection which also disturbs the nerves of the stomach and causes vomiting.

Larry used to think that since these organs are the means by which we adjust ourselves in space that probably they had something to do with the sense of time also. But later he discovered that the cochlea, farther back in the ear than the semicircular canals, and far more delicately balanced, was a more likely means by which we acquire our notion or feeling of time. 

On this occasion Larry was badly off his equilibrium. For usually as soon as one sets his foot upon solid ground after leaving the boat, he regains his composure in all respects. But in this case there was no immediate relief for Larry. He had probably been somewhat bilious when he started on the trip back and this aggravated his stomach condition. 

In fact when he got back to Boston he had to stay at a hotel there over night. He and Marie were left behind by the rest of the party, which moved on in the evening by boat to New York. Larry wanted no more boat traveling for that day. 

They had quite a scare in the hotel room that evening after Larry had become somewhat better. The baby was sitting on the bed beside where Larry was lying. He was near the edge of the bed and for some reason decided to take a tumble backwards. He went straight to the floor and landed on top of his head. The heavy brussels carpet checked the force of the fall, however, and he scarcely cried.

The next morning the trio left Boston by train instead of by boat in order to complete their journey home.

On the way up to Nova Scotia the whole party of boys and camp instructors made a sight-seeing tour of Boston, that old renowned city of Revolutionary war days. Among the sights that touched Larry deeply was the remodeling of the old Frigate Constitution, or Old Ironsides, as she was called, in one of the docks. Larry recalled how he had been stirred by reading of the adventures of Old Ironsides in his first history book. There

was no more startling page in American history than that about 

Old Ironsides, Larry now thought.

It occurred to him also that this vessel had been hewed from the native forests of America, much as his father had cut down the trees and hewed the logs to build the house in which Larry was born. The hull of the Frigate Constitution was made of hard oak wood, which gained for it the name of Old Ironsides.

CHAPTER XX THE REAL ESTATE BOOM 

We next find Larry in a real estate office in Lancaster, Pa. 

He is not asked to return to the Lakes School and his credits from teaching in a private school of another state are not good in Pennsylvania, especially in the city schools.

He, therefore, grasps the opportunity to enter the real estate business. It would do him good anyway, he thought, to learn something about real estate and about selling. In fact, he had a feeling that no matter what he did, what he learned proved to be worth more to him than what he earned. "Here's a sure sale," said Larry's employer the first day he was in the office; "if we can only get this property on our list. 

Suppose you run out there and tell him we have a lot of prospective buyers and that we may be able to sell his property. 

Get him to sign this card giving us the right to sell his place. 

It'll mean seventy-five dollars for you right smack, if you get it for $6000 or less, because I have a buyer who wants the place." 

Seventy-five dollars, mused Larry to himself. It would take half a month to earn that elsewhere. Certainly he'd get this property to sell.

Larry made a bee-line for the place, but the husband was away working and would not be home till lunch time. Larry was excited. He ran home to tell Marie of his chance to earn seventy-five dollars that day. "Oh, well, now don't get too much excited," said Marie. "You don't have the money in your pocket yet." "No, but I'm going right back there at dinner time and see that man. He wants to sell the place, but he'd like to sell it himself and not give anyone else a chance to make money on it," explained Larry. "What do you have to get him to do?" asked Marie.

"Get him to agree to have us sell it for him. We have a buyer already," Larry answered. "The only thing I'm afraid of is that he'll ask more than $6000 so as to make up the commission to us, which will be $150." "And do you get half of that?" asked Marie. "Yes, but I'm afraid he'll ask about $6200, and then our buyer will balk," said Larry. "Well, go to it, old man," urged Marie. "Seventy-five dollars would look pretty good." 

So off Larry ran again, whistling as he went. "How do you do, Mr. Bear. Warren's my name, from the 

Graef Real Estate office. I see you have a sale sign on your house. Why don't you let us try to sell your property for you? sell?" questioned Larry. 

Are you pretty anxious to "Yes, I am," said Bear. "I want to move to the country." "How much do you ask for it?" inquired Larry. "I've been asking $6000," replied Bear. "Would you take any less for it in a quick sale?" Larry asked. "No, I won't take any less yet. I might have to some day, but I won't now," Bear assured. “Well, I believe we can get that for it. We have a number of prospective buyers and it's a nice looking place. Would you mind signing this card allowing us to sell it, if we can? It'll do no harm even if we don't sell it," said Larry.

Larry didn't really know that the card he was asking Bear to sign meant that the Graef Real Estate office would be the sole agent to sell the property and could collect $150 commission by law, no matter who sold the property. So it would have made a very great difference whether he sold the property or not. "No, I don't mind signing that; but how will I know if you sell it?" said Bear. "Oh, we'll bring a buyer around and let him see the house; and you'll hear from us, if we sell it all right," answered Larry.

Bear signed the card and Larry bade him good-bye. 

So that was that, thought Larry. Seventy-five dollars the

same as in his pocket. He could eat a good dinner that evening. 

Back to the office he went with his good news. It would be best to wait a day or so, however, before telling Bear that his place was sold, else he might become suspicious of something. Still it wasn't safe to wait. Properties were moving pretty lively just then, and Bear might sell his, himself. "Maybe we'd better get somebody else to buy that property anyway," said Graef. "My buyer has been in to see it and Bear would know him, and he might think the sale fishy." "But I can't see why someone else should buy the property," 

Larry said. "So we can sell it right away from him to the other fellow and Bear won't know who's really buying it. That's a little trick you'll learn more about after you're here awhile," explained Graef.

Larry thought he understood. The outside man would buy it from Bear and transfer the sale right away to the man who really wanted the property. Larry wasn't sure it was altogether straight; but he supposed that's the way real estate had to be sold. And he came here to sell properties, especially so, if there was seventy-five dollars a day in it.

But such quick sales don't often happen in real estate, as Larry would find out later. This just happened to be a case where a close friend of Graef's wanted a certain property and wished to see Graef make a little money on the deal, providing it didn't cost the buyer any more money. 

At any rate the place was sold to the go-between man, sight, unseen. Graef had collected a down payment of $400, so he and Larry went to see Bear at his home two days later at noon. "Well, I see you still have your sale sign on the place," said Larry. "Oh, yes, it's still there," said Bear. "You may as well take it down," interjected Graef. "Your place is sold already. We've got $400 here for you." "You don't say," replied Bear. "Will you come inside a minute?"

They entered the house and Graef handled the details of

the case while Larry looked on and listened. Bear thought the deal had gone through unusually quick, but then he knew nobody would throw $400 away unless he wanted the property. So the buyer must be real.

The necessary contracts were produced and signed. "What'll I do if this fellow backs out?” asked Bear. “I don't believe he's even been here to see the place." "He saw the place from the outside," Graef answered; "and we assured him it was all right. If he backs out you'll have his $500 extra the contract calls for here, for the one who breaks it."

Graef laughed. And then he procceeded to turn over his own check, which he had pinned to the contracts, to Bear for the sum of $250. "I thought you said you had $400?" Bear interposed. "Oh, we did, but we took out our $150 right away. You might as well have that paid, and off your hands and mind," advised Graef. "I guess that's about right," chuckled Bear; "then I won't have any more to do with you fellows, will I?” "That's right," said Larry; "unless you have another property to sell. If you do, let us know."

They bade Bear good-bye and departed gleefully. When they got to the office Larry was given his half of the spoils, and then he was in the real estate business for real. 

Later on Larry put through another clever deal, he thought. 

The man who rented a certain place would let no buyers in to see the property because if it should be sold he would have to move. The property belonged to an estate and would have to be sold. The price was right and two or three firms had sale signs on it. Larry knew somebody would sell that property; so he put on pressure and convinced a buyer to take it as he saw it from the outside only.

A day or so afterwards he dropped around to tell the renter the place was sold, and take down his sale sign. He thought he was doing the renter a favor, because he would thus have more time to look for another place.

But the renter took exception to the deal, claiming that he was being forced out of his home before he was ready. He had several little properties of his own on his hands, and he wanted to build a home before long, but these properties had him tied up. "Why don't you sell them?" suggested Larry. "Sell them?" queried the renter; "they don't sell so fast down on that street. There's four of them together, too many in one place to sell right."

Here was a man who didn't know anything about real estate, thought Larry. He didn't know values were going up and that certain speculators would be glad to get four houses like those. "How much do you want for them?" asked Larry. "Maybe 

I can sell them for you." "Eighteen hundred apiece, just what I paid for them, after taking off your commission," was the reply. "That doesn't sound bad," said Larry. "Do you care if I sell them for twenty-five dollars more, and make a little my self?" "Not at all," was the reply. "I'll be glad to get them off my hands and then I can build for myself."

Larry quickly got hold of one of the speculators, who bought the houses for $1800 each, and put them up for sale right away on easy terms for $2300 each. The proposition was $300 down payment, and the rest was to be paid as rent by the month. 

Settlement was to be made the next first of April, and long before that the speculator expected to have them off his hands, and into someone else's hands. And so the boom was on.

CHAPTER XXI THE BIG FIRE 

"Where's the big camera. Someone find Charlie, quick," came from the city desk of the newspaper. "Charlie's away out in the country taking some pictures," someone replied. "Good-night, I wonder if he took all the cameras with him," groaned the city editor. "No, here's the big one," said Larry, who was quickly on the job, and now a reporter on the newspaper. "Who can use it? Larry, you take it and get some pictures of that fire, and don't come back till you get them," was the next remark.

Larry grabbed the camera, examined it as he put on his hat, and off he ran. The fire was only a couple of blocks away, up 

North Queen street. It was one of the big moving picture buildings burning. Fire had started in the film room in the middle of the building.

Larry was soon on the scene, but the fire engines were ahead of him. Traffic was blocked in the front of the building, but then nothing could be seen from there. So far, one passing the front of the building would not know that there was a fire. 

There wasn't even any smoke.

Larry examined the camera again and did all the things he thought he had seen Charlie, the regular photographer, doing to it on occasions when they went together to get pictures, and took a snap of the front of the building, but had no way of knowing whether it had registered. Anyway this snap would not look like a fire, that he was sure of.

So Larry scrambled over the fire hose and followed its course along the side of the building. Wherever the fire hose would lead there ought to be fire. He soon ran up against a blank

wall, however, where the smoke was thick, but the fire was not. 

Still he was going somewhere. 

He discovered a streak of light toward the rear of the building and went toward it. It proved to be an alleyway leading back to where the fire could be seen. He made for the place where there was a blaze and reached it. Others were clicking their cameras. Larry looked over the situation. He had two more films left in his camera. 

He didn't believe one could get a good impression of the fire so close to it. There was little but black smoke and black walls, he thought. Looking up toward the clear sky, he saw a tall flat-roofed building half a block away. He made a bee line for it. No one knew why.

It was a big garage. Inquiring inside as to how he could get on top of it, he was informed that a trap-door leading through the roof from the top floor was the only means of getting out upon the roof. Someone on the top floor could help him to get out.

Up to the roof he hurried. From here there was no doubt about where the fire was. Larry set his camera for action and took a snap.

Then he set the lens for a little greater distance. 

By this time the fire had reached its maximum. The water from half a dozen fire hose was pouring into the furnace of fire as part of the roof dropped. Up rose a cloud of steam and smoke. 

It was really a wonderful scene, had it not been so tragic. 

Larry's camera again clicked and he headed for the photographer's with his films. There could be no waiting; the films must be developed immediately. 

Larry reported to the office, and took a little time out for lunch, after which he would call at the photographer's to see what happened to his films. He thought one of them was pretty good, himself, but he didn't know what would appeal to the city editor. Taking pictures was a new game to him. 

He laid the pictures in front of the city editor, who nervously fingered through them. He wondered to himself whether the opposition paper would have a good picture, and he none. In a second his face began to beam.

"Ah, Larry, it's a beauty," he said.

Larry had given up the real estate business in the spring following his entrance into it. He realized that real estate would move slowly during the summer, and he liked the idea of having a regular salary which he could obtain on the newspaper force, although the salary would be small to begin with. 

There was an opening for a farm editor. Larry had been born and reared on a farm and was interested in the farmer's welfare. Beside he had done some writing in school and wanted a chance to develop his ability along that line, if possible. So he would tackle the job.

But newspaper writing is different from any other kind of writing. That is, the form of the stories is different. It is necessary to pick out the outstanding fact in the story and write it at the beginning, then follow up with the other material. 

Larry didn't seem to be able to get started on his stories. It was like putting the cart before the horse at first. But he picked up a little, and felt at home getting out among the farmers and hearing them airing their troubles and theories. 

He started on the evening paper.

But now Larry was working for the daily paper. Both were owned by the same firm. He was transferred from all farm work to court reporting and putting out a farm page once a week. 

He had used a small snapshot camera for his farm pictures, but never had occasion to do camera duty about the city. 

When a fire broke out, however, while the real photographer was out in the country, an emergency arose, and Larry would be either the goat or the hero, except that if he were the hero no one else would ever know about it.

He had not seen Larry on top of the roof half a block away. 

Neither had anyone else seen him.

CHAPTER XXII A PEACE PLAN 

The failure of the United States to join the League of Nations was always a mystery to Larry. Surely, he thought, there must be a weakness somewhere along the line in our handling of such problems.

In the first place he could not blame Woodrow Wilson for going to Europe in person and taking a hand in establishing the League, even if he wanted to blame him, because no one else would have succeeded or tried to succeed, in that endeavor. 

The fundamental principles on which the League was founded, moreover, were the same as those on which the democracy of the United States was founded. Yet it was impossible to get the United States Senate to consent to joining the League in spite of the fact that any nation could withdraw from the League in event it found the company incompatible. 

Where was the weakness? Larry thought he had found a clue to it when he considered that, when the United States government was founded no definite method of making treaties with foreign countries was provided. In fact, no specific method was necessary at that time. The provision of the Constitution giving the President the power to make treaties "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate" was admirable. And ever since the days of Washington the Presidents have done well in conducting the business of treaty making with individual nations. 

But when it comes to such problems as the matter of joining the World Court or the League of Nations, which means international dealing and co-operation with all the nations of the earth at one time, thought Larry, the case is different. Furthermore, when the United States was established, he recalled, care was taken to keep separate the judicial, the executive and the legislative branches of the government. No provision was made

for them to co-operate and arrive at conclusions as a unit. 

Larry conceived a plan, therefore, by which these three branches of government could unite in solving such questions that proved a stumbling block for other methods of treatment. 

So that, when the Bok Peace Award was offered for the best plan by which the United States could co-operate for World Peace, Larry had his plan all ready for the contest in advance. 

It was as follows:

Use the conference method in bringing together the executive, the judicial and the legislative branches of our government to bring about the entrance of the United States into the World Court and the League of Nations. 

It could be done in the following manner: with the President as chairman, the conference would consist of the Secretary of State, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, a man selected by the President especially conversant with the question in hand, and three members from each of the two major political parties in the Senate, to be selected by the respective parties themselves.

Their function would be to work out a set of provisions, or reservations, under which the United States would join the League or the World Court, whichever the case may be. The results of their finding would then be passed upon by the Senate, or rejected as the case may be.

This method would do away with the charge, whether just or unjust, that any branch of the government was usurping the powers of the others. The conference could not be "packed" by hand-picked selections. 

Sample conferences would probably have been as follows: 

(For the World Court) 1. President Coolidge 

2. Secretary Hughes 

3. Justice Taft 

4. Elihu Root 5. 3 Republican Senators 6. 3 Democratic Senators 

(For the League) 

1. President Wilson 

2. Secretary Colby 

3. Justice White 

4. Colonel House (?) 

5. 3 Republican Senators 6. 3 Democratic Senators

The President would have no vote and all decisions would require a two-thirds vote, or six of the nine votes in the conference. Thus, if the only opposition votes should be the three 

Republican or the three Democratic Senators, it would be evident that the vote was entirely for party prejudice, and it would not have power to veto the work of the conference, nor would it bear much weight with public opinion. 

Larry's plan was brief and to the point, while the winning plan contained three thousand words. Larry's only comment was to the effect that if any one had had an idea it wouldn't have taken three thousand words to express it. 

Larry had planned what he would do with the fifty thousand dollars. First, he would pay all his old debts, and would reward those who had sacrificed for him, especially those of his own family. Then he would pay off the mortgages on the properties of all his nearest relatives, and would charge them no interest on the money he left in their properties. At the same time he would give his wife a vacation, either in Florida or California. After that he would settle down at something, he didn't know what. No wonder he was sullen, therefore, when the prize went to a plan that has never been heard of since.

CHAPTER XXIII THE WILSON ESSAY

"No, I don't think so. The times move at a faster pace now than they used to. With the present day means of publishing the news all over the world as fast as things happen, there is evidence that such things as Wilson's greatest savings, which are included in my essay, will be recognized for what they are worth much sooner than they would have been recognized in olden times. 

In fact, I may be able to make use of this essay some day myself. You were the one who originated the hope-chest idea in this family anyway; you ought to know," Larry bantered. "Oh, well, have it your way. I'm satisfied," said Marie; "only

I do hope that you do something pretty soon to pull us out of the hole. I'll swear I'm going back to nursing pretty soon unless conditions change around here," Marie threatened.

"I'm not opposed to your doing it, but I certainly don't like the idea of not being able to support my own family. After working at a place for three years one certainly ought to be paid enough to live on decently, even if he doesn't earn it. He has to put in the time, and the company as a whole is earning money. 

I'm going to see what can be done," said Larry.

This conversation between Marie and Larry took place at the end of the year 1927. The news had just been sent out that the judges in the Woodrow Wilson Essay Contest had decided that none of the essays submitted warranted a prize of $25,000 (which Larry had hoped to get), and no prize would be awarded. In fact, the judges of the contest went further than that. They said that none of the essays was "fit to publish." 

This was adding insult to injury. Larry considered that he had put the efforts of almost thirty years of study into his essay and he refused to admit that it was not even "fit to publish.' 

He had read three volumes of Woodrow Wilson's writings and sayings, most of which he had read and digested before when they were published in the newspapers, and then he had taken the best quotations, set them down as he read along, pieced them together and produced what he considered a masterpiece. He had an eminent professor of history read it before sending it in and that gentleman had pronounced it almost perfect in English and deep with thought, considering Larry's age, which was just under thirty-five. 

Another idea came into Larry's head. Perhaps the Wilson

Essay Contest had really just been called off because Charles A. Lindbergh had stolen the whole show that year by making his famous flight across the Atlantic ocean.

Lindbergh's flight was heralded as was no other event in the history of the world. There was no denying Lindbergh's heroism. 

But the event was celebrated much in the manner in which the crowning of a new heavyweight champion of the world is celebrated.

Larry also thought that some one had made capital out of the Lindbergh celebrations by taking advantage of the public during its great excitement, and inaugurating a new orgy of

speculation and inflation, which bubble burst in the year 1929. 

Not that Lindbergh was in any way responsible for this; for he was sensible enough to ask, "Do I deserve all this?"

With the directing intuition which carried him safely over the Atlantic, even without modern instruments for air navigating, Lindbergh sensed that there was something of the bombastic in the celebration accorded him. 

Another thought occurred to Larry. Why did not Lindbergh's immediate superiors recognize his latent abilities, not only as an aviator but as an organizer. As soon as he had accomplished the flight across the Atlantic everyone bowed to him in his field. 

Financiers and others were ready not only to consult him but to make him president of their companies.

Could it be possible that Lindbergh knew much more about aviation the day after he crossed the Atlantic than he did the day before he crossed? Larry could not see how. It was the old, old story, he thought, of genius not being recognized, even by the genius itself, sometimes.

Larry was pressed hard to support his family according to the standards upon which a newspaper man's family is supposed to be kept. His skimpy salary could scarcely be stretched to pay the interest, taxes and food bills, to say nothing of clothing and numerous other items. Although he had not fallen heavily under the spell of instalment buying, yet he had purchased some necessities for Marie in this way, such as an electric washing machine and a sewing machine.

He again thought of a way to make use of his Essay. He would send it to a magazine. If only he could get it published, then he knew his reputation would be established. 

But the magazine did with it as had been done with some former verses he submitted, returned it to him with thanks because it did not meet the immediate needs of the publishers. 

He didn't bother much about the verses because he knew he was no poet; but when it came to his essay, into which he had poured his whole soul, Larry was bitterly disappointed.

WOODROW WILSON* 

"Those who suffer see." "The world must be made safe for democracy." "This is a war to end war." 

So said Woodrow Wilson, and in so saying he gladdened the heart of the world.

It may be said that if we know a man's motives and purposes we know that man as he is. In seeking to know Woodrow Wilson, for this reason, it is well to search out from among his words and deeds the underlying purposes which impelled him to speak and act as he did in life. 

It so happened that it fell to the lot of Woodrow Wilson not only to state his own purposes in life in his time of trial, but also to state the purposes of all mankind in one of the darkest moments in its history. Here we have a man whose position in 

American history can be compared with only two other Americans, George Washington, the Father of His Country, and Abraham Lincoln, the Savior of the Nation.

What those men did for America it fell upon Woodrow Wilson to do, or try to do, for the world. Washington was primarily a man of deeds and action; Lincoln was a man of character and expression; while Wilson was a man with all these qualifications. 

Not by his own choice, but by reason of circumstances it became necessary for him both to interpret the meaning of the World War to humanity and to see that its purposes became a reality. 

The purpose of the Revolutionary war, on the part of the American Colonies, as expressed by Thomas Jefferson in the Declaration of Independence, was to gain their freedom. (That purpose was brought to fruition by the deeds of George Washington). The purpose of the Civil war as stated by Abraham 

Lincoln was to preserve the Union of the United States. The *The quotations used herein may be found in the New York Times and in the Selected Papers of Woodrow Wilson, published by Mrs. 

Woodrow Wilson.

purpose of the World War was to end war. This purpose was stated and its attainment made possible by Woodrow Wilson. 

Before all the world, therefore, Woodrow Wilson stands out as the man who clearly stated the true purpose of the World War and made feasible the accomplishment of that purpose. 

We may be too close to the aftermath of the World War to appreciate the fact that it was a war to end war. Many may find it difficult to believe that the end of great wars has come. 

In appraising the works of Woodrow Wilson, however, it is not necessary to prove that the end of war has come. It is sufficient to point out that the World War was fought with that purpose in view by him, and it was the first war that was so fought. It was Woodrow Wilson, moreover, who gave utterance to this issue of the war and thus raised its purpose to a moral plane that was acceptable to all nations and rallied the righteous forces of humanity to a single standard that was irresistibly carried forward to victory. "If this is not the final battle for the right, there will be another that will be final," said Woodrow Wilson at Suresnes cemetery, France, after the war had ended. 

Woodrow Wilson, himself, did not contend that the end of wars had absolutely come; but he was sure that the World War had been a war to end war. That is enough. There may be more great wars in the future, but if there are, they certainly will be fought with the object of ending war forever. That is for humanity to decide.

Fundamental Americanism 

Woodrow Wilson's fundamental conception of Americanism is most aptly and succinctly expressed in his address at Suresnes cemetery, France, on Memorial Day, 1919. This speech was delivered over the graves of fallen heroes in an atmosphere much similar to that in which Abraham Lincoln delivered his memorable Gettysburg address.

On that occasion his searching mind looked back upon the fundamental principles on which America was founded, inter-

preted those principles in terms of the present, and then gazed forward into the future to which he hoped those principles would carry America.

In a vein and style not unlike those of Lincoln's Gettysburg address, though probably more oratorical and less purely literary, he said, in part: "The League of Nations is the Covenant of governments that these men shall not have died in vain. I like to think that the dust of those sons of America who were privileged to be buried in their mother country will mingle with the dust of the men who fought for the preservation of the Union, and that as those men gave their lives in order that America might be united, these men have given their lives in order that the world might be united. Those men gave their lives in order to secure the freedom of a nation. These men have given theirs in order to secure the freedom of mankind; and I look forward to an age when it will be just as impossible to regret the results of their labor as it is now impossible to regret the result of the labor of those who fought for the Union of the States." 

So exalted and complete is this passage that it will bear no comment.

Serving Humanity 

"America was born to serve mankind," declared Woodrow 

Wilson in a Memorial Day address at Arlington in 1917.

It could as well be said that Woodrow Wilson was born to serve mankind. Through his whole life may be traced an increasing purpose in the service of humanity. As a teacher, an author, a college president, a governor of a state, and as President of the United States, he met a rising succession of opportunities which he grasped in order to carry out his purpose of serving humanity to the utmost.

Born in Staunton, Virginia, the son of a clergyman, who was his early teacher as well as his father, Woodrow Wilson procured a training and education that were the exact opposite of those of Abraham Lincoln, who was born in a cabin and prac-

tically never attended school. Both men were alike, however, in their devotion and service to humanity. Both practiced the 

Golden Rule in their dealings with men and nations. They would have been great men under any circumstances. 

In an interview on the Mexican problem in 1916 Woodrow Wilson said: "America will honor herself and prove the validity of her own principles by treating Mexico as he would wish Mexico to treat her." That is the application of the Golden 

Rule in the conduct of nation toward nation. It is the rule that Woodrow Wilson adhered to. 

But America is to be praised the more not because she produced such men as Lincoln and Wilson, but because the American democracy is probably the only form of government that would have placed these men at the forefront of the affairs of the nation in its times of critical peril. 

There have been men in the past with as high ideals, even higher ideals (if we may regard Jesus as a man) than Abraham Lincoln and Woodrow Wilson. Their idealism possibly did not surpass that of Thomas Jefferson, who declared, "all men are created equal," to say nothing of the poets and authors of the past who dreamed and wrote of the brotherhood of man, the end of war, and peace on earth.

But never in history, before the time of America, had such men been raised to the pinnacles of power and practical leadership in the affairs of nations. Too often they have been trampled in the dust, or nailed to the cross. That is why we are especially challenged today to keep the American democracy pure, to keep open the paths of equal opportunity for all men, and to keep the world safe for democracy by creating a democracy of the world.

Though not of as lowly birth as Abraham Lincoln, yet Woodrow Wilson had as deep a faith in the common people as had Lincoln. Both carried their appeal to the people in whom they believed 

In his homely way Lincoln said: "God must have loved the common people because he made so many of them." Woodrow 

Wilson said: "The life of society, the life of the world-has con-

stantly to be fed from the bottom. It has to be fed by those great sources of strength which are constantly rising in new generations."

Let no nation, therefore, nor any group of society, assume that it can long endure apart from the common feeding sources of humanity at large. For just as the sea without an outlet becomes a stagnant pond, so does the sea without a feeding source become an arid desert.

The Family of Nations 

As truly as George Washington was instrumental in giving vitality to the new-born government of the United States of America, so was Woodrow Wilson instrumental in breathing the breath of life into the League of Nations before he retired from the active stage of world affairs. And as Washington was called the Father of His Country, so may Woodrow Wilson truly be called the Father of the Family of Nations. 

Woodrow Wilson had the highest regard for the ideals and examples of the founding fathers of the government of the United States. None was more eloquent in praising them than he. But he deemed it his duty and the duty of other living men, rather to extend those ideals, interpret them in terms of the present and apply them so as to build for the future.

And even as the Revolutionary and Civil wars resulted in the birth and regeneration of an American democracy that has expanded and far surpassed the fondest dreams of the founding fathers, so may the World War yet result in a democracy of the world under the Covenant of the League of Nations that will far surpass the most exalted dream of any living man. 

The American democracy, too, had its darkest hours in the first days of its coming into being. The period just following the close of the Revolutionary War has been described as the "critical period" in American history. Stout-hearted men at that time were doubtful as to whether the victory for the Colonies had been a blessing or a curse. Under such circumstances was born

the democracy of a nation. Should we expect a democracy of the world to be born more easily?

Can it be true that the heart of the world is bleeding today because America has betrayed both her friends and her foes in the late war by withdrawing "into a sullen and selfish isolation," like a cruel father deserting his home and leaving the new-born babe of his own flesh and blood to the feeble mercies of a brokenhearted mother cast out upon the seething sea of human wretchedness? If that is so, then Americans may well take thought of the days that are to come. Dare they take a lesser view of their duty to humanity than did those valorous founders of the nation. and, at the same time, face not merely the judgment of their fellowmen, but also the judgment of a radiant and righteous God? 

Shall not America rather again hearken to the voice of Woodrow Wilson with respect to the League of Nations as she did when he led her to the heights of thought and action in the case of the repeal of the Act on Panama Canal Tolls, when he said: "The large thing to do is the only thing we can afford to do, a voluntary withdrawal from a position everywhere questioned and misunderstood."

Liberty Is Alive 

That Woodrow Wilson's idealism was developed early in life and that his interest in America and humanity was also highly cultivated at an early age is clearly shown in an address delivered by him on April 30, 1889, when he was but 32 years of age, on the one hundredth anniversary of the inauguration of George 

Washington as President. 

On that occasion he said: "Liberty is not something that can be laid away in a document, a completed work. It is an organic principle, a principle of life, renewing and being renewed. Democratic institutions are never done they are like the living tissue, always a-making."

He declared further: "Today we should stand close to the thought and close to the hearts of those who gave our nation

life. The tasks of the future are not to be less, but greater than the tasks of the past."

How true this latter statement proved to be in the case of Woodrow Wilson, himself, when twenty-five years later he was called upon to guide the Ship of State through as perilous a storm as it had ever been forced to weather. 

In performing this duty Woodrow Wilson merely had a greater opportunity to try out his idealism by the acid test of practical application. He lived to serve humanity, and was at his best when brought hand to hand with the momentous problems of humanity.

Under his direction the Ship of State became a beacon light that was looked up to for guidance by friend and foe alike. The peoples of all nations rallied to his standard to seek the shelter of peace.

His statement of the terms upon which the World War could be ended agreeably for everyone was accepted by the leaders of all nations. And then with the dawn of peace the 

Ship of State glided safely back to the quiet harbor from which it sprung.

But this was not a time for resting on the oars. With much of his strength sapped away by the trials of the World War,

Woodrow Wilson showed his tenacious qualities by fighting harder than ever for the things of the spirit. Many soldiers had given their lives on the field of battle in order to win the war. 

Woodrow Wilson was now ready to give his life in order to secure a lasting peace. And so, armed only with a dauntless spirit, he went forth to battle for the right. With mottoes such as, "The right will prevail" and "Justice and only justice," he knew he could not fail.

In closing the crowning address of his varied career at Suresnes cemetery, France, Woodrow Wilson in the midst of legions of living soldiers, gathered about the graves of their departed comrades, said: "There is something better, if possible, that a man can give than his life, and that is his living spirit to a service that is not easy . . . to stand against purposes that are difficult to stand against."

In this faith Woodrow Wilson fell like a soldier fighting to the

end against those purposes which he feared might "break the heart of the world." And in the spirit of so doing he made the heart of the world glad. 

That's what Woodrow Wilson means to me.

CHAPTER XXIV BIG BUSINESS MERGERS 

Things were breaking badly for Larry but the worst was yet to come. As though his troubles in making ends meet for his family were not enough the business merging "craze" came over the country. Not only did large concerns buy up, or unite with, the small, but two or more very large concerns would sometimes combine and reorganize for still bigger business. 

With banks, newspapers and other concerns the process was the same. And now this process was to have its crushing effect on Larry. For the only two newspapers in town were going to combine, or rather one was buying out the other. This meant that about half the employees in Larry's department would be dismissed. 

And unfortunately Larry made a bad mistake about this time.

He wrote a head on a story that was very displeasing to one of the regular advertisers. The latter made a big kick, and the writer of the "head" was ordered to be dismissed, no matter who he was. Larry's salary was small enough, considering the standard of living supposed to exist in the United States, and his service recently to the company was probably becoming like his salary. He was a discontented worker. 

His dismissal caused Larry to reflect. The editor of the Sunday paper, some time back, had asked him about writing a farm story for the paper. "It's near springtime and there ought to be a good farm story floating around," the editor had said. "Yes," replied Larry, "I suppose there is, only I know I won't write the kind of story you want written. I refuse to talk about prosperous times for the farmer when I know they do not exist." "Don't be foolish," the editor answered, "write the stuff you can get good money for."

Larry wrote a story, but wrote it according to his own notions, and of course it was not acceptable. But Larry was editor of the farm page on the daily, so he fixed the story up and used

it himself on his own page.

The title or "streamer" line was, 

"What's Wrong With Agriculture, and Why-And the Remedy." 

That was in the Spring of 1927. Larry began his story by saying that every business man should be interested in the farm problem because if it were not soon solved every other business man would find himself coming through what the farmer was going through. No one paid any particular attention to the story, but by 1931 it proved to be true. 

Larry also said that the delusion that the farm problem will solve itself must soon be abandoned; it is like any other problem, it will have to be solved by some one, he had said. 

He looked up the old story and among other things he found a statement to the effect that it is possible that we are confusing our high standard of living with our high cost of living. 

He knew that costs had been boosted so high that a decent living was beyond the means of more than fifty percent of the people. He had government figures to prove this, and he knew it only too well from his own experience. 

The high cost of living, as Larry put it, had nullified the high standard of living, because the man who has to spend as much, or more, than he earns, for a living, is no farther ahead than the man who earns little and has to spend little for his necessities. 

That the extreme use of the high protective tariff system had led to the extremely high costs of living in the United States was generally admitted. But the argument was advanced on the other hand that those who earned more had the fun of spending more. This was all right up to a certain point, Larry surmised, but when a depression should come, in the wake of instalment buying and expensive modes of living, the crash would be colossal. Larry always thought that, had it not been for Lindbergh's flight across the Atlantic in 1927, and the resulting excitement and re-inflation in values, there would have been a crash in the year 1927, such as the one that was postponed until the Fall of 1929. If it had come then it probably would not have been so severe as the later one, because the fall would not have been quite so great,

Larry felt that the farmer was the victim, and the goat, of the extreme protective tariff system. The farmer was led to believe that he would benefit from the tariff along with the rest, while as a matter of fact he got little benefit from it, and at the same time was forced, through that system, to pay the bills of the other fellow. For while the cost of farm necessities rose higher and higher, the prices the farmer received for his products either stood still or declined after a certain point was reached. 

After the farmer was forced off the market by this process, Larry concluded, everything along the line began to slip and the only hope through more protection was to shift the burden from the farmer to the working man. That was the situation in 1927. 

This, Larry thought, could not work long, because the working man was already burdened and he had nothing to fall back upon as the farmer once had. In the days when new agricultural territories were being opened up and when the soil was virgin, the farmer, as a whole, could stand the pressure from the high tariff abuses because he could just smile, raise bigger and better crops, and that made up for the loss. The good-natured farmer did not stop to ask where the pressure was coming from; it was easier to pay the price and keep on going.

In this way, Larry claimed, agriculture really should be given credit for paying for the building up of our immense manufacturing industries. And now the farmer is bled white through the process, Larry had said. (The same situation developed later, in the year 1930. The tariff was again given a boost in that year, but its dire results took on still another angle. Many foreign nations retaliated and the result was a great falling off both in our export and import trade.)

The large manufacturer, Larry also had recognized, was in a position to get around the tariff barriers that had their bad effect on other industries, by simply building factories in other countries and selling the product as a home article and not subject to tariff regulations. Thus our own factories built in other countries were brought into competition against our own home-

owned factories and labor. The results of the process were just beginning to be felt, Larry feared.

Larry had also written an editorial for his paper on the tariff. 

He had said: "The world needs a solution for the complex tariff problem," and went on to explain that the problem should be handled from an international point of view. Economic and commercial rivalry, he thought, had been responsible for most of the great wars, even our own Civil war. 

He explained that free trade should be looked upon as an ideal condition toward which the world must move. In gradually working toward free trade the barriers between nation and nation would be slowly removed, until finally the nation best fitted to produce a given article would produce it and supply it to other nations at the least possible cost. Furthermore, the free trade principle existing between the various states in the United States had worked out ideally for us. Why we should adopt a system, the exact opposite, toward other nations, was not plain. 

Larry figured it out this way: extreme free trade leads to an ideal, or idealism; while extreme protection leads to destruction. 

Who could fail to choose properly between the two?

But there was no good now in idle reflection. Larry was out of a job at a time when jobs were beginning to get scarce. Although business was supposed to be booming in 1927 and 1928 there was something backward about it. A sort of false optimism had been preached from the highest places. Big men pledged their reputations to the statements that business was good and getting better. At the beginning of each new year bombastic utterances were sent forth through the press to the effect that the country was due to see a greater prosperity than had ever been dreamed of.

Larry began to look around. Perhaps he could get back into the real estate business. But everyone advised him that things were very quiet in that line. The price of properties was drifting downward and everyone held on to what they had as long as they could, and those who could have bought either were afraid, or wished, that properties would go lower. They neither wanted to buy higher or sell cheaper than they had paid for their properties.

Larry finally landed in the life insurance business. Being assigned to a debit in one of the poorer classed districts of the city he began to see life as it really was. People wanted insurance, but when it finally came to a choice between buying insurance and buying something to eat, the insurance man always lost out. 

If a family had been pretty well loaded up (they were often over-loaded by pressure selling) with insurance, they would decide to drop it or cash in their policies. The loss had to be stood by the man on the debit, if he was not man enough to resell his customer. But Larry was not the one to sell the people something they didn't want, or rather couldn't afford, if he knew it. 

So that when an opportunity came to purchase a grocery business in the Spring of 1930 he scraped up enough cash to make the first installment on it and went into business for himself. 

This was a sad mistake, for instead of business beginning to pick up during the summer it gradually slipped backwards, and carried Larry with it. His overhead, counting the installments he had to pay on his equipment, was too great. It took capital to stay in business in those days. Larry never worked harder in his life; but no amount of industry would have pulled him through, considering the competition of the larger chain stores. 

And by the end of the year 1930 Larry was bankrupt, and couldn't pay his creditors. He had a crash of his own. There were now three in the family, two little cherubs of girls having come into it.

Fortunately at this point Marie could step into the breach. 

She would go back to nursing. She had wanted to do this before, but Larry would not consent to it except as a last resort. Now he had to submit because he had the rent to pay and the only jobs Larry could get were selling jobs, on commission only, and there were few buyers to be found for anything. 

Marie had come to believe that she had tied herself to a kitchen sink after all, and she wanted to get away from it. A lawyer had advised her to get a divorce. 

The nursing profession, too, was over-crowded, but Marie soon was called for a case. She still had her old technique. Patients who had called her in for a day would keep her for a week be-

cause she made them so comfortable. She always took night duty because in that way she could spend a few hours each day helping about the home, and entertaining the kiddies. Larry worked at odd selling jobs, but whenever Marie was called out he made it a point to stay at home.

Larry had once laughed at actors on the stage portraying the young man who had gotten himself in so deep that he could not pay his electric bill; his telephone was disconnected, and there seemed no avenue of escape. But now it was not a laughing matter. Larry was in the same fix, only worse, it seemed to him. 

Marie had just reported an accident in which she broke the last cup with a handle on. Two bank failures recently had nearly wrecked the rest of his family. Something had to be done, and done quickly.

He hit upon the idea of digging into Marie's hope-chest and trying to publish his Wilson essay. Perhaps the public would again be ready to look up to Woodrow Wilson, following the harrowing experiences of the past year. He sent the essay, together with an outline of his plan to a publisher. Perhaps it could be worked into a book of his life story. And that, kind reader, is the story you have just read. The publisher accepted it, and 

Larry's troubles were over.

CHAPTER XXV HIS OWN THEORY (The Ascendency of Man) 

Although Larry had set out to end, some day, the war between science and religion, yet when he reached the point where he might do so he found that the war had already ended. For the leading men both of science and of theology were already won over to the theory of evolution, on which the old battles had hinged.

Larry was surprised to find that even his old professor of philosophy, who had formerly been a minister of the Gospel, was a believer in evolution, which means the orderly development of all life from the simple to the complex by a continuous process. In biology class he had traced the development of living things from the simple amoeba, which can be seen only under a microscope, to the paramecium, a small swimming creature, on up to the hydra, the earthworm, and finally to the frog and all the creatures of the animal world. 

Larry did not doubt that man also was the result of this process of orderly development from the simpler to the more complex forms. He believed, however, that in conducting the war between science and religion the men of science had placed too much emphasis on the liknesses existing between men and the lower animals, such as the apes and the monkeys. 

In pointing out that man, limb for limb, and bone for bone, is constructed just like a dog, a cat, an ape or another highly developed animal, the scientists had seemed to intimate that man is nothing more than an animal. The important thing, as Larry saw it, was to find out what constitutes the chief difference between man and the lower animals. For there is a difference, a difference as great as that between a creature such as the earth-

worm, without a special eye with which to see, and the ape, which is far inferior to man, though not so far below him in physical make-up alone. "What a vast new world," said John Fiske, "was opened up to the first creature to develop an eye with which to see." ." In tracing the process of evolution the scientists had found that all the organs of the special senses, such as touch, taste, smell, hearing and sight, had developed in response to a demand for them for better adjusting a creature to its environment. For instance the inner ear developed in response to sound waves which continually kept striking against the nerve endings, and finally resulted in a special organ for intercepting these waves and carrying their message to the brain over a special set of nerves. And so it was with all the sense organs.

Now since the development of the senses played such an important part in the advancement of the higher forms of life over the lower forms, thought Larry, the same process must have played the most important role in the advancement of man above the other animals. But what new sense could man have developed which would distinguish him from all other animals? He had often heard of the sixth sense, the inner sense, and so forth, but in this age of scientific investigation those terms were too vague to be accepted as facts.

Larry was searching, not for a sense, but for an organ of sense not yet discovered. In the first place it must be hidden away somewhere from the view of the naked eye. And it must be something which the other animals do not have at all. For this reason the vivisection of lower animals would lead nowhere. It would be like hunting an eye in an earthworm. In fact, Larry could see that this was the main difficulty in the way of finding a solution to his problem. One could not practice vivisection on human beings. Nor was Larry practiced in this art. His only hope was to find out what others may have discovered in this field, and place their findings together for a solution of the problem.

In his study of the psychology of William James, Larry had learned that man has a sense of time, which plays an important

part in his life. It is true that time, the past, the present and the future, are as real to man as is the light of day. But whence does it come, and what is its substance? This at first seemed an insurmountable obstacle in Larry's path. But on the other hand, he asked, what is light and whence does it come, and what is its substance. Of course he knew that light travels in waves, which mostly for us come from the sun, but this did not explain its substance nor what it really is. There is a means, through the eye, by which man interprets light, but what it really is, is as much of a mystery as what time is. It is possible, therefore, for man to have a sense of time without knowing what time is. 

So thinking of man's adjustment to time as a means of improving his condition in relation to his environment, Larry was reminded of Herbert Spencer's description of man's conception of time. "There appears to be," he said, "an adjustment between an inner periodicity and an outer periodicity." This is a continuation of Spencer's theory that all life progresses through the process of the adjustment of "inner relations to outer relations."

Besides dealing with periods of time man also has to do with a quality of time known as rhythm. According to Haeckel our sense of rhythm originates in the far inner reaches of the inner ear at the very end of the cochlea, which is spirally shaped like a snail. Here, thought Larry, is a possible source of the origin of our sense of time. Haeckel had noticed that in the higher animals the organ is more highly developed. 

Larry thought he saw a sign. For is not the snail, or cochlea, patterned after the curve of the evolute? It seemed to say here is the secret of evolution; within lies the answer to the problem you are seeking to solve. What goes on within the cochlea, which also contains the organ of Corti? Investigation showed that only the very highest apes and man have this organ of Corti, and in man it is much more highly developed than in the apes. 

It was too bad one did not have the means of conducting a series of investigations by studying these organs, first, in a number of children two years old, or younger, in age, and secondly, on children about five years of age. For it is somewhere along

about the second or third year that we begin to remember things, that is to record an impression on the brain that may be referred to later. No doubt a distinct change in this organ takes place, Larry thought, between the ages of two and five years. 

For memory, no doubt, is linked up in some way with a sense of time.

Although the more modern scientists have divided and placed into about a dozen different classes the various senses of the body, Larry did not feel that their discoveries had much bearing on his own particular theory, because they were all senses of minor importance and possibly were only sub-divisions of the originally recognized five senses of touch, taste, smell, hearing and seeing.

It is important, however, that we bear in mind as we consider the development of the sense of time that a sense of space goes hand and hand with it. We adjust ourselves in space by means of the semi-circular canals within the ear, and therefore we get our conception of space through these same organs which are closely connected and allied with the two cochlea. In fact, the theory that time and space are one and the same thing, and are co-existent, has long been held by all theorists. 

So that just as we gain a notion of light through the eye, and of hearing through the ear, so do we gain our notion of Time through that little snail shaped organ in the far inner reaches of the ear, or rather through the elongated portion of it including the organ of Corti, which Haeckel found was the source of our sense of rhythm, which is a quality of time. 

Consciousness, Larry would define, as the awareness of duration, or the knowledge that time exists and the sensing of its presence as we would that of light. The old cow standing quietly chewing her cud, looks blankly into space and time without knowing they are there; while the human being is aware of time and space the same as he is aware of light and sound. This awareness of time and its feeling of duration, Larry would describe as our consciousness, which results in mental states and conditions.

Man not only sees and hears and forms ideas, but he also is

conscious of these things through his awareness of the duration in which they exist. While he is aware of a sensation, a picture, an idea or a thought, he records it in his brain in the form of an impression on the gray matter for future reference. 

These mental pictures, as we call them, can be recalled to our consciousness time and again, if we wish, sometimes with difficulty, however. This recalling, comparing and contrasting of mental pictures, or ideas developed from them, is a process of thinking. A thought or an idea is the recognition of a relation, or a new relation existing between two mental pictures, or ideas. 

A new invention or discovery, is simply the recognition, or establishment, of a new relation existing in the Universe. 

In beginning the study of psychology a study is made first of the physical basis on which thoughts and mental states are built. 

All our knowledge, in this respect, has its beginning in the impressions we get through the senses of touch, taste, smell, hearing and seeing, chiefly. Through them messages are received and sent to the brain where they are recorded as stated above. But the fact that man retains them there indefinitely is what differentiates him from the other creatures. And the reason man does record and retain them there is because he is aware of their duration at the time of their first appearance, which is being conscious of them.

The reason that no creatures, other than man, are capable of keeping this record on their brain is that they have no sense of time, Larry thought, except possibly in a very rudimentary degree. They have no special sensory organ of time, no awareness of duration, and therefore pictures pass through their eye, go to the brain for the time being, but then disappear almost as quickly as they came. There is no accumulation of mental pictures, mental states and ideas in the brain of the lower animals, while man does accumulate these normally.

Not only that, but when man recalls his various mental pictures and ideas in making comparisons and seeking new relations, he is carrying on a mental process which deals with existences enclosed within himself. The exercise of the brain cells and tissues is an exercise that is comparable to any other physical ex-

ercise. It develops them, that is the brain cells and tissues, so that they become stronger and stronger, the same as do the muscles of the athlete or the blacksmith. The unusual size and complexity of man's brain, compared with other animals, is due, therefore, to this exercise of the brain. 

The sum total of these mental existences is what we call the mind, or the substances upon which the mind acts. 

In his study of psychology Larry found it difficult to bridge the gap between physiological psychology and mental psychology.

The fact that we recognize all our contacts with other things and beings through our senses by touching, tasting, smelling, hearing and seeing is a part of physiological psychology, because it deals altogether with physical contacts.

But when it came to studying mental states there was a jump into an entirely new field without any connection with the physical world he had been dealing with before. This Larry could not understand. He thought that there ought to be a connecting link between the things he had already learned about sensations through the senses, and the mental states, which certainly were a natural outgrowth of those former impressions one obtained through the senses.

That is what led him to believe that there must be a new special sense which makes this connection possible. He found in a sense of time all those elements which would seem to bridge the gap. Through the means of the sense of time and of consciousness, which is awareness of duration, we are able to make an indexed record in the brain of all the sensations and perceptions we collect through our senses, he thought. The place where these records are kept is physical, that is, the brain; the means by which we place them there and recall them later, is mental.

The process of memory is very closely related to the element of time. Memory involves a process of connecting two parts of time, the past and the present. But memory cannot exist without first having kept a record of past events, and thus the means by which this record is made possible is the founda-

tion of the mind, and the connecting link between the physical man and the mental man.

Larry recalled that when he had been sick in the hospital with typhoid fever he had lost all sense of time, all sense of coherence, and even of existence. As far as he knew, he did not exist, nor was there a world existing about him. He had no memory of anything that had happened within a period of eight weeks. 

Afterwards, when he began to seek a cause for this phenomenon, therefore, he came to the conclusion that the whole thing was caused by a diseased condition of his sensory organ of time, whatever it may be. With a high temperature the blood rushes through the arteries with a pulsating flow. One can easily see how such a flow would affect the delicate organs of the far inner ear, the cochlea and the organ of Corti. The tissues are mere films to begin with; and any disturbance such as rapid blood flow and inflammation would readily upset the whole process of functioning. So his delirium was an understandable case. As soon as his temperature remained normal the delirium disappeared.

Delirium and unconsciousness differ from each other in this way, Larry thought: Delirium is caused by an interference with the functioning of the sensory organ of Time; while unconsciousness is caused by an injury to the brain cells, as by shock, which prevents the messages as they come through the sensory organs of sound, light and time, and so forth, from being registered in the brain. In other words messages may be prevented from reaching the brain for two reasons: first, because the sensory organ is injured and its messages therefore being arrested at the start, and secondly, because of an injury to the receiving apparatus, or brain cells, which prevents the message being registered after it is sent. 

In his younger days Larry had regarded the mind as being constructed like a great, large balloon rising above his head, though attached to it. But now he had come to regard the mind as merely a part of his own organism, working through the medium of the brain cells, where all records of events and former ideas are kept.

The whole system, he thought, is like a self-playing (not automatic) piano, in which the body is the piano with the keyboard in the brain; the soul is the invisible player on the stool; while the mind is the power back of both which makes the piano play.

The soul and the mind, however, Larry thought, are as one being acting together and always co-existent, like time and space. 

In fact they may be composed of time and space and may be parts of them, he thought. So that he could see how he, himself, could even be, or become immortal, as are time and space. This idea is expressed in the first sonnet in this book, although probably it is not expressed quite so clearly there, because when the sonnet was written Larry had not yet conceived of the above theory. 

Larry thought that as we hear through vibrations, or waves, of sound, striking against the nerves of the ear; and that as we see through means of vibrations, or waves, of light striking against the nerves of the eye; so do we sense Time through vibrations, or rather periodicities of time, striking against the delicate nerves for that purpose located within the cochlea and organ of Corti. 

Of course these waves, or periodicities of time, probably are more rapid even than waves of light. They may be a part of the "external periodicity" of which Herbert Spencer spoke; and we may have an "internal periodicity" which corresponds to it, and through which we make an adjustment and connection with time itself.

Here was practical proof of immortality. For it makes clear not only how man can understand something about time and space, but that he really is part of them, and remains part of them after death. Perhaps he still retains a separate individuality, and perhaps he simply becomes an indefinite part of the whole Universe after death. That we shall know, he thought, only in the hereafter; but Larry believed that there was the greater possibility that one retains his own individual personality, even after death.

Larry had once observed that the line between right and wrong is a true line; it has neither breadth nor thickness. This,

he thought, was the reason why it is so hard to distinguish sometimes the right from the wrong. In the great struggle of the 

World War, for example, it was difficult for a long time to tell which side was in the right and would finally win out. It was easy to pick one's choice of sides, but it was not so easy to prove that one was truly on the side of the right. 

Under his new theory Larry could see how there might be two spirits in the Universe, one good and one evil. Each individual, therefore, he thought, is given an opportunity on this earth to choose on which side he will stand. If his good qualities and deeds surpass the evil ones during life, then he gives back to the source from which his spirit came something of good, which endures as such in the hereafter. If, however, his evil deeds and qualities should surpass on the whole, his good ones, then he would pass on to the future as an evil influence. It is up to each one, he thought, to choose in which group he shall remain in the hereafter.

That is what Larry meant when he defined character for Sammy, his esteemed professor of philosophy, as being our inherent moral qualities. If the good qualities predominate over the evil in life, one is classed with the good; if, however, the evil qualities predominate, he is classed with the evil. The greater the ascendency of the good qualities over the evil, the greater the rejoicing in the future life. 

NOTE: There is no place in this theory for a "missing link" between man and the lower animals. There is no need for a "missing link," which theory has been very misleading. As to those who hold that time is a blank form, and is unknowable, I would answer them by saying, so are light and electricity unknown, as such, yet we do not doubt that they exist.

Furthermore there can be no such word as time, which is existent in all languages, without there being something in reality corresponding to it, whatever the form.

For man, therefore, there comes into existence the new element of time, which some describe as the Fourth Dimension, which may be correct. The theory of Relativity may still be in advance of this theory. 

My hope is that my own theory may be a connecting link between what we already know and the theory of Relativity. 

Regarding the question as to whether there is a God, my answer would be the same as to the question of the existence of time. There cannot be such a word as God in all languages without there being a God corresponding to the word in reality. 

Once we develop a sense of time, by means of which we peer into the future, we then develop the religious sense, which tells us of God.

This theory assumes that time and space are in a constant state of vibration, the waves being infinitely rapid, and infini-

tesimal in size. It is through them, therefore, that we get our conception of the infinite and the eternal.

There is no interference between light and time waves because the time waves are so much more rapid than light waves, the same as there is no interference between sound waves and light waves because light waves are so much more rapid than sound waves. In this respect also it seems altogether probable that the force of gravity can have an influence upon light waves causing them to bend as Dr. Einstein has pointed out. And it is also probable that what we have formerly regarded as the ether, or empty space, is really time and space in infinite vibration.

A Princeton professor some years ago discovered, through tabulating the words most commonly used in our daily language, that the word time is the most commonly used noun in our language. This would indicate that time is the most important thing to us in our life, and I believe this to be true. 

As hearing is the awareness that sound exists, and as seeing is the awareness that light exists, so is consciousness the awareness that time exists. It is the recognition of duration. 

The nearest to exact proof of this theory is to be found in the psychology of William James, in his unabridged edition, Volume I.

CHAPTER XXVI A HISTORY 

As this book was being finished Larry learned that there was some trouble in finding a satisfactory one-hundred-word history of the United States as an inscription for the bust carvings on 

Mount Rushmore, of George Washington, Thomas Jefferson,

Abraham Lincoln and Theodore Roosevelt. 

Larry jotted down the following as a possible inscription. Of course he thought that a carving of Woodrow Wilson instead of that of Roosevelt would round out the periods in American history better. But on second thought he presumed that possibly

Wilson, like Washington, stood at the beginning of a new period in the history of the world; and so he let the name of Roosevelt stand as proposed.

INSCRIPTION 

Behold the builders of the Ship of State! 

These men by their deeds deserve to be called great:

GEORGE WASHINGTON-Who breathed the breath of life into the new-born government of the United States of America in the darkest hour of its coming into being;

THOMAS JEFFERSON-who gave expression to the aspiration of the American people to gain a greater freedom for themselves and for posterity;

ABRAHAM LINCOLN-who welded the sundered nation into a more perfect Union;

THEODORE ROOSEVELT who was the exemplar of that virile 

Americanism displayed in culminating the World War for the freedom of mankind.

Larry considered the advisability of holding this inscription out because there might be a prize offered later for something of the kind. But he finally decided in favor of using it at once. 

Counting the compound word "new-born" as one word the inscription includes exactly one hundred words.

Larry was very particular to mention this because he knew that if the judges should decide to count the word as two words they would pronounce his inscription ineligible for the contest and throw it out.

POSTLUDE Being Larry's Views of George Washington and Entangling Alliances 

Much use is still made of the quotation from George Washington's Farewell Address in which he warned against the United

States joining in entangling alliances. 

This quotation is used without any hint as to the circumstances under which it was made and without any regard for the context of the address in which it was used. At that time both 

England and France were playing for the separate friendship of the United States. France in a way had the upper hand because she had recently helped the Colonies to gain their independence. 

The Colonies, and afterwards the United States themselves, naturally felt indebted to France. And French statesmen were wise enough to use this foothold in order to gain trade, and possibly a military alliance with the United States. 

While always courteous to France and ever mindful of the debt of gratitude due France, George Washington could not but see that the trade of England, the mother country, and the friendship of England must necessarily be the dominant factor in the success and growth of the new American nation. Consequently, although he could not say so in direct language, Washington felt it incumbent upon himself to warn the people of the United States of the ulterior motives that might lie back of any individual offer of friendship and alliance that might be proffered in the near future, either by England or France.

These two nations at that time were practically enemies; at best, they were competitive rivals for the ascendency in the colonization of the world. France naturally could not forget the recent loss of her colonies in America through the French and Indian war. She was ready for any opportunity to get even with Great Britain for that old score. So that is why Washing-

ton went somewhat out of his way to explain the best course for the United States to follow in the near future. 

Anyone, however, who tries to stretch his statement to mean that he was opposed to any kind of co-operative union among the nations of the world, either does so with wilful intent to misrepresent the facts or with no understanding of the fundamental principles upon which the Union of the United States was formed under the directing hand of George Washington, both in the Constitutional Convention and as the first President of the 

United States.

Washington was a believer in a strong central government, with as much power as possible vested in the Executive. It was upon this point that Washington and Jefferson differed most widely as Presidents. Jefferson stood for a more liberal policy of the Executive and believed in giving as much power as possible to the individual states. 

It has even been hinted by historians that had Thomas Jefferson been in America at the time the Constitution was framed and written, he no doubt would have opposed it in its present form, and no doubt his influence would have been strong enough to weaken it almost to the point to which the old Articles of Confederation had been weakened. 

The Constitutional Convention almost broke up over the question of whether a state should have the right to withdraw from the Union. The question was finally evaded and was left an open one. It later developed into the Civil War. Had Jefferson been in the convention, he no doubt would have insisted on the right to withdrawal. Had he, or anyone else, succeeded in this move, it is doubtful whether the Union would have lasted very long, and it is even likely that today we would have four or five nations covering the same territory that is now occupied by the United 

States of America. 

When we come to consider the Covenant of the League of Nations, we find the same important question facing the various members. The right of withdrawal has not been denied to any nation. This is an advance over the Constitution of the United 

States, in which the question was side-stepped, but which ques-

tion was finally settled by the Civil War at great cost of life and happiness.

This is not an evidence of weakness in the Covenant of the League of Nations. It is evidence of that confidence which is necessary among the nations of the world, if the League is to be a success at all. It is a different situation than the one which faced the states of the American Union. The League is a Confederation of States while the United States is a Union of states. 

The League of Nations has, however, sufficient central authority vested in the Council and the Assembly to be effective. 

In fact, it is this very evidence of authority that has been instrumental in making a few nations wary of it. It is the kind of authority Washington would have favored in the make-up of the League had he been one of its founders. It is a sign of strength and not a sign of weakness in the League. In fact, it is a necessary requisite for the lasting success of the League. 

Without this the League would, ere this time, have gone the way of the old Articles of Confederation in America. If the League has shown any weakness, it has been because it did not have the power and influence of the United States back of it. 

Suppose the state of Virginia, through the influence of Thomas Jefferson, had refused to join the Union of the United States and Virginia had set up a separate government of its own. 

Neither George Washington nor Thomas Jefferson would have been eligible to the Presidency, and Virginia by expanding to the westward would soon have been a formidable nation, a veritable thorn of contention in the side of the United States. 

So that when anyone today preaches the doctrine of isolation, and the doctrine of no alliances, unless it mean no alliances with an individual nation, or small group of nations, he should stop to consider what such a doctrine would have meant to the United States of America in the day of that nation's coming into being. The success or predominance of such a doctrine would have meant defeat for the Union, or if not defeat, then indefinite delay and hindrance in the path of its development and growth.

George Washington was opposed to the forming of "partial"

alliances, but, were he living today, he certainly would favor a Union, or League, of Nations the same as he favored and sponsored a Union of all the States in his day and age. 

AS POOR RICHARD WOULD SAY 

We have heard so much about economy and thrift in the past decade and have seen so little of its efficaciousness that it is time we stop to wonder whether we have not been advocating and propagandizing the wrong thing or principle. 

We see so many advertisements with Benjamin Franklin's sayings on thrift, that we seem to have forgotten that he ever made such bold and prophetic expressions as "Unite or Die" and "If we do not all hang together, we shall all hang separately." 

Were it not better for the nations of the world today to be eulogizing such mottoes as the ones above, rather than each one grabbing for the other's dollar. Thrift is all right in its place, but we should not forget that to set it up as a goal is sure to lead to miserliness and greed instead of to any enduring virtue.
