AGENCY.

Q. A large transportation company bought out a local carrier, but no change was made in the appearance of the business, which was carried on under the former name, with the former owner in charge. In reality the former owner was an agent of the company, and as such agent had authority to settle claims up to $50.00. He told the plaintiff, who claimed a suit of clothes was negligently lost by the carrier, to get another suit like the lost one, and he would pay for it.

The new suit cost $150, and the plaintiff, being unable to collect from the apparent owner of the business, sued him. Then plaintiff discovered the true state of affairs and discontinued that action and sued the owner corporation. 

Can he recover?

A. Yes. The bringing of suit against an agent is not a bar to a subsequent suit against the undisclosed principal whose identity had not been disclosed. Limitations as between the principal and agent of an apparently general authority, not brought to the knowledge of third parties, do not affect the rights of the latter. 

In this case the agent was acting within the apparent scope of his authority. 

Brooks v. Shaw, 197 Mass. 376. 2. 

Q. John Doe and Richard Roe signed the following: "This agreement made this first day of June, 1932, by and between the X corporation, by its agent, and John Doe, WITNESSETH, 

That John Doe agrees to deliver one hundred thousand feet of sound white pine timber to the X corporation, on or before the first day of September next.

The X corporation agrees to pay the said John Doe, for the above timber, the sum of $20.00 per thousand feet, when the same shall have been delivered.

IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the parties hereunto have set their hands and seals on the day and year first above written. 

John Doe (Seal) 

Richard Roe, Agent (Seal)"

AGENCY. 

John Doe performed his promise and sued Richard Roe in an action of contract for breach of the agreement. What result? 

A. For defendant, Richard Roe.

If it clearly appears from the face of a document that the contract was intended to bind one who by the technical rules applied to sealed instruments was not bound, the seal, if unnecessary, may be disregarded as surplusage. 

In this case it clearly appears from the face of the contract that the plaintiff intended to deal with the X corporation and not with the defendant. A seal was not necessary to validate this contract. 

Blanchard v. Blackstone, 102 Mass. 343. 

Cook v. Gray, 133 Mass. 106. 

Milton v. Mosher, 7 Metcalf 244. 

Seretto v. Schell, 247 Mass. 173. 3.

Q. Defendant employed A. B. as salesman to solicit orders for its product, the salesman entering into a written agreement under seal with the defendant that he would take orders, written or oral, only at the defendant's list prices. The salesman made a written contract of sale with the plaintiff of certain goods at a price lower than the list price. The written contract with the plaintiff recited that the defendant "by A. B. agent" agreed to sell the goods. It was signed by the plaintiff and by A. B. in his own name, without any descriptive language with the signature, and without a seal, though it recited that it was sealed. Defendant refusing to ship the goods except at the list price, plaintiff sued for breach of the written contract. 

Should he recover? 

A. Yes.

The rule is well established that a principal is bound by the ostensible powers which he gives to his agent, whatever may be the limitations of that authority as between them. 

The agent in the instant case had authority to sell and to make contracts. The authority to sell under the circumstances included an implied power to make contracts fixing the price. 

Bascombe v. Inferrera, 271 Mass. 296.

The instrument in question was sealed. In Massachusetts the necessity for a tenacious substance upon an instrument to constitute a seal has been abolished. Any recital that the instrument is sealed gives it the effect of a sealed instrument.

G. L. (Ter. Ed.) chap. 4, sec. 9A. 

The undisclosed principal rule does not apply to sealed instruments but if it clearly appears from the face of a document

Q. In an action against a street railway company it appeared that the plaintiff had signaled an approaching street car to stop at a regular stopping place; that it had done so; that as he stood in the street near the door of the car waiting for passengers to get off, he was injured by a sign on the car falling upon him. He requested the Court to rule that the defendant owed him the duty to use the highest degree of care which was reasonably practical in running its railway. 

Should this instruction have been given?

A. No. One waiting in the highway for a trolley car does not become a passenger until the car has stopped and he has come in contact with the vehicle preparatory to entering. Until he became a passenger the plaintiff was not entitled to the instruction requested. 

Duchemin v. Boston Elevated, 186 Mass. 353. 5.

Q. A merchant in Boston purchased a carload of goods in 

California and shipped them to Boston by through freight. The car was transported over three different railroads, A, B, and C, A being the carrier to which they were originally delivered, and C the carrier delivering the car in Boston. On receipt by the merchant the goods were found to be in a damaged condition. 

Has the merchant a right of action?

If so, against whom, and what must be proved to make out a case?

A. The merchant can sue the first carrier. 

By the interstate commerce law, a carrier undertaking shipments in interstate commerce is obliged to make a through contract. The merchant should prove the contract, delivery of goods in good condition and the receipt of the goods in damaged condition. 

Atlantic Coastline R.R. v. Riverside Mills, 219 U. S. 186.

BAILMENTS AND CARRIERS. 

The merchant could also sue the delivering carrier. "When goods are shipped over connecting lines and arrive at their destination in a damaged condition there is a presumption that the damage was caused by the last carrier. . . . But the presumption may be rebutted, and the terminal carrier may show that the damage occurred on the line of the connecting carrier, that the goods were received by it in a damaged condition, and that it was not at fault, the burden being upon the defendant to show such facts as would relieve it of liability. . . . The Carmack and Cummins Amendments have not deprived the delivering carrier of his common law remedies; they give to the shipper certain rights against the initial carrier." In suing the last carrier, the plaintiff must show delivery of the goods in good condition to the first carrier and the delivery of the goods by the last carrier in a damaged condition. 

Lyon v. B. & M. R.R., 261 Mass. 251. 6.

Q. D (defendant) hired a yacht of P (plaintiff), agreeing to keep "said yacht in repair, and to pay all its running expenses, and to surrender said yacht at the expiration of this contract to the owner, in as good condition as at the start, fair wear and tear from reasonable and proper use only excepted". The yacht was destroyed during a storm and without negligence on the part of D. 

Was D liable to P?

A. Yes. A bailee for hire who agrees to give up the chattel in as good condition as at the start is liable for injury to the chattel even though occasioned by inevitable accident. 

Harvey v. Murray, 136 Mass. 377. 7.

Q. A invited B to spend the night with him at A's house. The next morning A directed his chauffeur to drive both A and B to their respective offices. On the way there was a collision between 

A's car and a car driven by D, and both A and B were injured. 

Both drivers were negligent. A and B each brought suit against D. 

May either or both A and B recover under any circumstances, and if so under what circumstances?

A. B may recover. A guest may recover against a third party whose negligence concurring with the negligence of the host or his agent has caused injury to the guest, so long as the guest has not entrusted himself entirely to the care of the host or his agent and is in the exercise of all the care which ordinary caution requires. 

Shultz v. Old Colony R.R., 193 Mass. 309. 

Keyes v. Checker Taxi Co., 275 Mass. 461. 

Bullard v. Boston “El”, 226 Mass. 262.

Q. A stranger in Boston went into a high-class jewelry store, attracted by a sign in the window stating "Watch repairing by skilled workmen: satisfaction guaranteed," and left a $500 watch for repairs, saying he must have it the next Saturday. On the 

Friday following the manager decided that, on account of other work, he could not have the repairs completed by Saturday, and sent a clerk with the watch to another concern of equal standing which agreed to do the work at once. On the way to the other store the clerk while carefully walking on the sidewalk was run over by an automobile operated by a drunken driver. In the accident the watch was broken beyond repair. 

What are the owner's rights against the owner of the first store?

A. In a bailment for mutual benefit the bailee owes the duty to exercise ordinary care with reference to the goods. 

There is no wrongful assumption of control by a bailee to whom articles are sent for repair in placing them with another person to do some or all of the work undertaken by the bailee unless expressly or by implication there is an understanding that the bailee himself should perform all the labor. Whether there was such an understanding is a question of fact. 

Rourke v. Cadillac Co., 268 Mass. 7.

No facts appear to show that the plaintiff and defendant had any understanding that the defendant should perform the labor. 

The sign did not change the operation of the rule. It did not specify that the work was done necessarily on the premises. The concern to which the first store delegated the work was also a store of equal standing.

BANKRUPTCY.

Q. X is the holder of a promissory note in the sum of $1000 made by M and endorsed by I. M and I are adjudicated bankrupts. I had waived presentment, demand and notice of nonpayment. After a dividend of 25% had been declared and paid to other creditors by M's estate, X proved his claim against M's estate for $1000.

For what amount is X entitled to prove against I's estate? 

A. $1,000.00.

A creditor may prove for the full amount of a note against both its maker and endorser, and may collect from both estates dividends on such proof until his whole debt is satisfied. Where proof against the estate of the endorser is made after part payment by the maker, the proof must be limited to the balance due on the note after deducting the part payment. In this case proof against the estate of the maker was made after the declaration of the dividend. The holder would have only a preferential right in future dividends of M's estate. This is not equivalent to a dividend in his favor or actual part payment. 

In re: Swift, 106 Fed. 65. 10. 

Q. H (holder) is the holder of a note made by M (maker) and endorsed by E (endorser). M goes into bankruptcy. H does not prove his claim against M's estate, as E is financially responsible. What may E do? 

What general principle of equity is involved?

A. E may prove the claim in the name of H. Whenever a creditor, whose claim against a bankrupt estate is secured by the individual undertaking of any person, fails to prove such claim, such person may do so in the creditor's name, and if he discharge such undertaking in whole or in part he shall be subrogated to that extent to the rights of the creditors. 

The general principal of equity involved is that of subrogation. 

Bankruptcy Act, sec. 57 i. 

Morgan v. Wordell, 178 Mass. 350. 11.

Q. A trustee in bankruptcy sues to recover, as a voidable preference, a payment which the defendant concedes to have been made

BANKRUPTCY. 

11 to him as a creditor by the bankrupt when insolvent, within four months before the filing of the bankruptcy petition. The effect of the payment was to enable him to obtain a greater percentage of his debt than any other creditor of the same class. The trustee believes he can show that the defendant had sufficient facts to put him on his inquiry, and that if he had inquired he would have discovered that the payment would so operate; but he is convinced that the creditor made no inquiry, and did not know that the payment would so operate. (a) What, if anything, should the trustee attempt to show? (b) What are the prospects for his obtaining a judgment?

A. (a) In order to recover a preference the creditor must have known the existence of a state of things which, if known by a business man of ordinary prudence and intelligence, would cause him to believe the bankrupt was insolvent and intended to give a preference. 

Atherton v. Emerson, 199 Mass. 199. 

Bankruptcy Act, sec. 60, cl. b. 

Bankruptcy Notes, pp. 87-88. (b) The trustee's prospects of obtaining a judgment would depend upon his proving by a preponderance of the evidence such facts as would have put the creditor as a reasonable business man on the inquiry. If such facts are proved, the creditor's good faith would be immaterial. 

Atherton v. Emerson, 199 Mass. 199. 12.

Q. A corporation, being insolvent, paid a dividend. The stockholder D had no access to the corporate books and records. 

When receiving his dividend he had no reason to believe the corporation insolvent, but in fact honestly believed it to be in a flourishing condition. He spent the dividend. The corporation there after is petitioned into bankruptcy by creditors who were such at the time of payment of the dividend. P, its trustee in bankruptcy, sues D for the amount of the dividend. 

Which should prevail? 

A. P should prevail.

By the G. L. (Ter. Ed.) chap 109A, sec. 4, "every conveyance made and every obligation incurred by a person who is or will be thereby rendered insolvent is fraudulent as to creditors without regard to his actual intent if the conveyance is made or the obligation incurred without a fair consideration." The good faith of the defendant in receiving the dividends is immaterial as they were mere gifts. 

Powers v. Heggie, 268 Mass. 233.

Q. Your client B tells you, and you, knowing the facts, agree with him, that he is insolvent and that if certain troublesome creditors continue to press him, he will inevitably go into bankruptcy in a month or two, but that if they will give him more time to pay, his business prospects are such that it is likely he may be able to pay all his debts in full. He has some cash on hand, and being in fear of bankruptcy asks your advice whether he may properly do forthwith the following things, and what the consequences are likely to be if he does them and within a month thereafter goes into bankruptcy.

A. He would like to keep Y, a troublesome creditor who is familiar with the whole situation, quiet by buying from Y a goodsized supply of goods actually needed in B's business, paying cash in full therefor.

B. He would like to pay in full your unpaid bill for legal services and expenses over the past two years. 

C. He owes his father money on a note. His father knows nothing of B's financial straits and has no reason for suspecting them. B would like to pay his father's note in full forthwith, paying at present no other creditors than his father and yourself. 

D. He proposes to transfer to you $10,000 to be applied by you from time to time on account of services and expenses for him, provided he does not go into bankruptcy; with the idea that if he does go into bankruptcy, you will thereafter employ him for a while in some capacity or other until the balance of the $10,000 shall have been exhausted. 

What will you advise him?

A. (a) B could legally do this. 

In a preference the transfer of property is for a pre-existing debt. If, then, a debtor receives adequate consideration at the time of the transfer, there is no depletion of his estate and consequently no preference. 

In re Wolf, 98 Fed. 84.

Comparone v. Caplan Co., Inc., 270 Mass. 74. 

(b) This would be a preference. It would be a transfer of property for a pre-existing debt with intent to prefer the creditor while insolvent. Moreover, it could be recovered by B's trustee in bankruptcy because you as attorney knew you were being preferred. 

Bankruptcy Act, sec. 3 (a) (2), sec. 60 b, cl. (b). 

Atherton v. Emerson, 199 Mass. 199. (c) B's payment to his father would constitute a preference. 

See (b), supra. However, so long as his father had no reason to

suspect B's insolvency, B's trustee could not recover back the preference. Advice to this effect would be unethical, of course. 

Bankruptcy Act, sec. 3 (a) (2), sec. 60 b, cl. (b). 

Atherton v. Emerson, supra. (d) This would be a fraudulent conveyance. Obviously unethical-also an act of bankruptcy.

Woodard v. Woodard, 216 Mass. 1. 

Bankruptcy Act, sec. 3 (a), cl. (1).

I would advise him to call a meeting of his creditors and explain the situation to them fully. I would tell him that if he pays any of his creditors to pay all in pro rata shares so as not to commit an act of bankruptcy. I would tell him that if he pays the creditors in pro rata shares he could not be forced into bankruptcy unless one of the creditors sued him and attached his property and the attachment was not vacated within thirty days, or unless a creditor through legal proceedings obtained a preference and he failed to discharge the preference at least five days before a sale or other disposition of the property affected by such preference. 

Bankruptcy Act, sec 3 (a), cl. (3), (4).

Q. The treasurer of a corporation drew a corporation check payable to his own order and endorsed the check to one of his creditors in payment. of a personal bill. On the same day the endorsee of the check presented it for payment to the bank upon which it was drawn, and the bank refused payment. Notice of non-payment was duly given to the corporation. The endorsee thereafter brought suit upon the check against the corporation. 

At the trial it appeared that the corporation was not indebted to the treasurer, and that in drawing the same the treasurer was attempting to misappropriate corporation funds. 

Can the plaintiff recover?

A. The plaintiff is a holder in due course and not subject to the personal defense set up by the corporation. 

According to G. L. (Ter. Ed.) chap. 107, sec. 75, a holder in due course is a holder who has taken the instrument under the following conditions: 

1. That it is complete and regular upon its face; 

2. That he became the holder of it before it was overdue, and without notice that it had been previously dishonored, if such was the fact;

3. That he took it in good faith and for value; 

4. That at the time it was negotiated to him he had no notice of any infirmity in the instrument or defect in the title of the person negotiating it.

Section 48 of the same chapter says that "An antecedent or pre-existing debt constitutes value."

In this case the check was payable to the treasurer and endorsed to the plaintiff. This was not sufficient to constitute knowledge of an infirmity. 

National Investment Co. v. Cory, 222 Mass. 453. 15.

Q. The defendant sold to the plaintiff a note, which bore a forged endorsement of the payee's name. The defendant when he sold the note endorsed it "without recourse. The defendant did not know that the payee's name had been forged. 

Is the defendant liable in any form of action, and if so, what? 

A. Yes.

He is liable in an action of contract for breach of warranty. 

Every person negotiating an instrument by such qualified endorsement as "without recourse," warrants among other things that the instrument is genuine and in all respects what it purports to be and that he has a good title to it. 

G. L. chap. 107, sec. 88. 17.

Q. X in Chicago in response to an order from Y in Boston shipped to Boston a carload of corn by the D railroad company. 

Upon shipping the corn, a bill of lading was issued by which it was consigned to the order of Y. X drew a draft upon Y for the price of the corn, attached it to the bill of lading and forwarded. both to a bank in Boston. Y paid the bank the amount of the draft, and the draft and bill of lading were delivered to him. Y endorsed the draft and the bill of lading to P, as security for an advance which had been made by P for the full amount of the draft. Six weeks after the arrival of the corn in Boston the D company delivered it to Z in compliance with an order of Y, Y having represented to the D company, falsely, that the bill of lading was still in his possession. P sued the D company, alleging conversion of the corn. 

Should he prevail? 

A. Yes.

If a carrier delivers goods for which a negotiable bill has been issued, the negotiation of which would transfer the right to the possession of the goods, and fails to take up and cancel the bill, he shall be liable for failure to deliver the goods to any one who for value and in good faith purchases the bill, whether such purchaser acquired title to it before or after the delivery of the goods by the carrier, and notwithstanding delivery was made to the person entitled thereto. 

G. L. chap. 108, sec. 14.

A bill stating that the goods are consigned or destined to the order of any person named in such bill is a negotiable or order bill. 

G. L. chap. 108, sec. 5. 19.

Q. The defendant, without getting any money for doing so, wrote his name on the back of a negotiable promissory note before it was delivered to the payee, who took it in payment of a preexisting debt. The payee then endorsed it in blank to the plaintiff, who gave value for it, but who knew all the circumstances of defendant's signing. The note was payable on a day certain, and at maturity the plaintiff telephoned the maker that he, the plaintiff, was coming over to see him about the payment of the note, but the maker told the plaintiff it would be useless, as he had no money. 

The plaintiff did not go to the maker, but the next morning went to the defendant and told him his telephone conversation with the maker, and its result, and demanded payment. The defendant told the plaintiff he would pay, but asked the plaintiff to come again the next Saturday. The plaintiff went in Saturday, but the defendant then refused to pay.

Should the plaintiff recover? 

A. Yes.

Value is any consideration sufficient to support a simple contract. An antecedent or pre-existing debt constitutes value. 

G. L. chap. 107, sec. 48.

An accommodation party is liable on the instrument to a holder in due course, notwithstanding such holder at the time of taking the instrument knew him to be only an accommodation party. 

G. L. chap. 107, sec. 52.

Presentment for payment is necessary to charge the endorser of a negotiable instrument. 

G. L. chap. 107, sec. 93.

The fact that the maker has informed the holder that presentment would be useless could be considered as nothing more than notice of the maker's insolvency, which might or might not be true, and it did not alter the conditional obligation of the indorser, and make him responsible without any presentment whatever. 

Lee Bank v. Spencer, 6 Met. 308. 

However, an oral promise by the indorser of a promissory note to pay the same, made, without being misled, after maturity of the note, and with a knowledge that there has been no demand for payment upon the maker, and of all other material facts, is a waiver of the want of demand and notice, and renders him liable on the note without a new consideration, although the note is payable on demand. 

Matthews v. Allen, 16 Gray 594.

Therefore, although the defendant was not liable because of failure to present the note to the maker, he is deemed to have waived the failure by his new promise to pay. 20.

Q. The payee of a check receives it from the drawer on 

Wednesday, drawer, payee, and bank being all in the same city. 

The payee goes to the bank on Saturday morning to cash the check and finds that the bank has been closed as insolvent one hour before his arrival. 

What are the rights of the drawer and payee?

CONSTITUTIONAL LAW. 

A. A check must be presented for payment within a reasonable time after its issue or the drawer will be discharged from liability thereon to the extent of the loss caused by the delay. 

G. L. chap. 107, sec. 209.

In Massachusetts the rule is that where the drawer, drawee and payee of the check are all in the same city or town, the check should be presented for payment before the close of banking hours on the day after its delivery. 

Gordon v. Levine, 194 Mass. 418.

Therefore, assuming that the drawer had sufficient funds to cover the check upon deposit the drawer is discharged and the payee must look to the bank as a creditor of the bank.

Q. The Legislature desires to reduce the period of the statute of limitations in actions of tort for personal injuries from six years to two years. (a) Can this be done so as to affect rights of persons who have been previously injured? (b) If so, what provisions, if any, must be inserted to make the statute constitutional?

A. (a) Yes. The Statute of Limitations affects only the procedure. It does not change substantial rights. (b) A reasonable time must be allowed in which to bring the action. The legislature is the sole judge of what is a reasonable time unless the time is so short as to be unreasonable upon its face. 

Mulvey v. Boston, 197 Mass. 178. 22.

Q. The income of a lawyer includes the following items:- 

(a) His compensation as a member of the Board of Bar Examiners. (b) Fees for legal services performed for a national bank and paid by it. (c) Interest on bonds of the City of Boston owned by him. (d) Compensation as a trustee in bankruptcy.

May these items or any of them be subjected to an income tax by the State or by the United States?

A. (a) His compensation as a member of the Board of Bar 

Examiners can be taxed by the state but not by the Federal Government. The Federal Government cannot impose a tax upon the salary of a judicial or other officer of a state. His office has tenure, duration, emolument and duties fixed by law. It is clear that he is a state officer. 

Collector v. Day, 11 Wallace (U. S.) 113. 

Metcalf and Eddy v. Mitchell, 269 U. S. 514. (b) No state can tax the officers or agents of the Federal Government. However, every employment in the United States service does not constitute the person thus employed an officer or agent. Here it appears that the tax is imposed on the income of one who is neither an officer nor an employee of government and whose only relation to it is that of contract, under which there is an obligation to furnish service, for practical purposes not unlike a contract to sell and deliver a commodity. The tax is imposed without discrimination upon income whether derived from services rendered to the state or services rendered to private individuals. 

In such a situation it cannot be said that the tax is imposed on an agency of government in any technical sense, and the tax itself cannot be deemed to be an interference with government or an impairment of the efficiencies of its agencies in any substantial way.

Should objection be sustained? 

A. No. This is not hearsay. 

Hearsay evidence, generally speaking, is hearsay within the meaning of the rule, only when such evidence is offered to prove the existence of some fact other than the statement by some third party. Q. (a) A married man purchased a house with his own money, taking title in his wife's name. Thereafter the wife without cause left her husband. May the husband in any form of action recover the property? (b) A man and his wife purchased a house in which to live.
