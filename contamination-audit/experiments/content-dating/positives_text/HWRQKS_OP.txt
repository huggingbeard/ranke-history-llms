The Nature of Wisdom 

There is a certain swiftness, simplicity and finality about wisdom's utterances which is disconcerting to the extreme to those of us accustomed to snail along toward truth, carrying our load of proofs upon our backs. 

There are two ways of arriving at truth; one through the process of reasoning; the other through a kind of sight. To the senses our environment. the 2

universe, appears to be a vast jig-saw puzzle, the pieces of which are of different shapes, colors and dimensions scattered about in great disorder. It is the task of the intellect to match facts and ideas about the pieces and to construct a satisfactory picture out of them. It is a tedious process. A construction in time where one color leads to the selection of a like color and one shape to a shape that will fit into it; and if half of the pieces are missing the resulting picture will be hopelessly deformed.

But wisdom arrives at knowledge differently. It perceives by a kind of sight. Its vision may vary greatly in acuteness but always there results a spatial representation of reality, a picture of actuality. The picture, because it is a picture, contains within it all the essential parts and no more, and each part in its proper place. Wisdom dispenses with the time element. It sees wholly and at once or not at all. Wisdom rests upon a sense of harmony. Whatever grouping of elements in a situation to wisdom sounds like a chord of music is accepted as normal. Whatever sounds like a discord is unacceptable. Wisdom arises out of the most fundamental part of man's being, in a region where, alone, numbers, periods and harmonies ring. Wisdom has a fine ear for music. It hears when divested of the sounds of the senses. In its nakedness it senses a situation in its nakedness, stripped of appearances. It detects the smallest discordant note emanating from man, whether in the field of science, religion, or philosophy, whether in words or in acts. For wisdom is the ear of the soul. 

It hears reality.

Reason, the intellect, intuition are tools for exploring the world of appearance. Even they delve deep into the natural world-as far as the atom, the electron and farthest star. But wisdom is an eye which sees through the veil of appearance and represents to 3

Religion 

It may be a digression but at this place it is necessary to explain what is meant by the term religion used by the writer here and in a former study. In the study "Evolution of Mind" it was stated that for reasons to appear later Jesus was not considered to belong to the class of founders of religious faiths, such as were Moses, Zoroaster and Gautauma.

The reason for that assertion is that the term religion as used by the writer is considered to be a very broad one, taking origin from a fundamental factor present not only in man but in all conscious life. In an evolutionary series runs a thread. It determines all evolution or growth, causes mutations or leaps upward in any series, and is a guide to any growth. The unit in any evolutionary series is acted upon by this spiritual energy. In a series composed of units which are aware of environment, or possess consciousness, as we say, such as the higher animals, units from time to time sense the urge of this spiritual directive force within them.

Man also from time to time senses this genetic urge. 

At various times and in various places the urge has been called by different names: a voice, an impulse, a call, a hunch, a still small voice, the voice of God, a spirit, the spirit of Truth, the Holy Ghost, etc. But whatever the name by which it is apprehended, the spiritual directive force is the chief cause in man of the rise of some kind of religious belief, and in animals of some kind of appropriate response, provided the directive force is followed.

Therefore the presence of an awareness of the urge to do certain specific acts, and the awareness that the

O F WISDOM urge is good or beneficial is very common in all conscious units of evolution. It is present in man. It was present in Jesus and in the evolution of the church, and will continue to be present in any subsequent or higher evolution which conceivably may occur. 

Jesus was not primarily a founder of religion. He was a generator of a new life—not a generator of some new kind of abstraction or intellectual conception but a real biological variation. Out of this new kind of life spring a new way of life and a new faith. It is true that Jesus taught many things commonly received as religious preaching and teaching. But in this, in part at least, He was attempting a restoration of ancient but lost conceptions of Deity and man's relation thereto. In so far as intellectual or intuitive conception of God and ethics went Jesus did not add much to the aphorisms of Hillel, or to the teachings of the later prophets.

It is in the nature of Jesus' life and death and in the dissemination of His life that He stands wholly apart and above any religious founder. Man before 

Him had religious beliefs, but so had the birds. To conceive Jesus as being the founder of a religion, of even a purer religion than was extant, has probably caused more misunderstanding of the nature of the Christian life, not only on the part of antagonists, but also on the part of Christians themselves, than any other conception relative thereto.

Every unit of evolution inherits most of its attributes from the past. Jesus in common with all men inherited his body and brain from his human ancestors. 

He, too, in common with all men of his locality and generation inherited the ideas afloat and prevalent in Israel at the time. These included the religious thought of the day, the teachings found in the sacred books and the general notions prevalent concerning nature and the world about. The world into which He was born was different from our world. It was a kind of spiritualistic world-a sky inhabited by angels and demons; an earth wherein it was thought a person could move its mountains had he enough faith; a god, omnipotent, distant, exclusive, jealous; and man suffering, waiting in a kind of suspense for something to happen. Various Messianic dreams were abroad, various notions concerning life, death and immortality.

All the common knowledge, dreams insight and superstition of the day, Jesus inherited.

A first unit of any evolutionary series comparable in magnitude to the evolution of man (mind) or the evolution of life (animals and plants) differs tremendously from contemporary units. The unit so differs because it is a supermutation, the recipient of a gift and urge sufficiently powerful to stamp its likeness upon its progeny throughout one of the grand subdivisions of total cosmic evolution. Herein is where Jesus differs from any other human before Him. The Wisdom of Jesus 

The theory of evolution as developed demands that the generator of the new and higher stratum to follow the evolution of mind be the embodiment of wisdom? Was Jesus wise? Did he possess a wisdom beyond the capabilities of any human before His day? 

If Jesus made the assertions credited to Him by his biographers He was either a lunatic or else in possession of facts impossible for man to perceive. If He was in possession of all the great facts pertaining to Deep Environment, which is God, He would have been wiser than man. If He could see these realities in operation as in a picture He would have possessed a gift such as none other had received before Him.

Whenever Jesus touches upon life He speaks with authority—that is, He knows all, sees all. He sees God as a father, close and as near as the hairs on one's head. He sees the sacrifice of self written large at the

He discerns His own children of the spirit down through the centuries and the manner in which they are begotten. He sees free will written across the sky and in the heart of man. He sees free will crushed by inherited sin and sees infinite justice binding up the wounds. He sees God's justice as indistinguishable from God's love, and both shining eternally upon the evil and the good. He sees God in action down through the ages. Strata of evolution-a Kingdom of Heaven on earth, an evolution of justice in Heaven. He sees death as an illusion and life, like matter or energy, indestructible. He sees death as the precursor of life. He sees Himself partaking of God's nature. He lives as 

God would live-confined in the infirmities of the flesh. He speaks as God would speak-confined in the superstitions of the mind. He dies as God habitually dies in lifting creation unto Himself. 

Suppose Jesus never said the things credited to Him by His biographers but that the sayings and acts are what later editors thought a divine son of God should have said and done. There remains Pentecost, and later on, human mystical experience to account for. 

Fortunately the writer does not need to appeal to anything except that which the theory of growth demands. 

The theory demands that Jesus in the flesh was more than man, less than God, and the embodiment of wisdom.

The Nature of the Church 

As perhaps before mentioned the universe is composed of octaves. It is like a harp, the alternate strings of which, when struck, yield a harmony. One string is organic evolution composed of animals and plants. The adjoining and next higher string is man or the evolution of mind. The two are unlike. They differ as do the notes c and d in a scale of music. Because of the alternation of attributes present in any growth series we know that the string which succeeds the evolution of mind will, when struck, harmonize with and will resemble the evolution of organic life. 

As the predominant characteristic of animals and plants is their ability to adapt themselves, to conform to superficial or contact environment, so the predominant characteristic of units in the evolution of wisdom will be their power to adapt themselves to and to become like Deep Environment, which is God. Animals are imitators of their immediate or sensory environment. Sometimes, as in protective mimicry, they imitate closely that environment, but always in a general way they remain imitators of their surroundings. If their medium is water they are wave-like, sinuous, flowing. If their medium is earthy they are relatively rigid, plant-like, sedentary. And so in the evolution of wisdom we should expect the units composing it to become in various degrees adaptors to and imitators of an environment-the environment being God.

The animal is an animal because it has been born an animal. The unit in the evolution of wisdom to be such must be so born. Man cannot through any effort of his will or intellect achieve a new eye, a new vision, but, as with the animals, the new faculty becomes his only through inheritance or as a gift. 11

Again the manner of propagation in the evolution of wisdom will be like that witnessed in organic evolution and unlike that seen in the evolution of mind. In the world of ideas growth occurs by means of addition and multiplication. Facts are added to facts, ideas are joined to ideas; in the end a magnificent structure results from the joining together of its various building blocks. But in the evolution of wisdom the procedure is not so. Here the method of growth is very similar to that seen in animal and plant life. Here is evolution, growth, not by addition and accretion of elements but through division and subdivision of a first and a subsequent unit.

An amoeba, one of the most lowly single-celled animals in reproduction, divides into halves. These again divide and subdivide continuously. When there are a million amoebas the original amoeba still lives in its parts. Therefore in the evolution of wisdom we should expect the method of propagation to resemble that found in organic life rather than that found in the world of ideas. According to orthodox Christian belief 

Christ after the resurrection was bequeathed to His followers and to this day still lives in their natures. 

Like the amoeba Christ is not dead but still lives in the subdivisions of Himself.

The nature of the Church therefore will be like its progenitor-Christ. It will be an imitator of Deep Environment, or God. While it will inherit all the attributes of mind it will grow, not by accumulation of ideas, arguments and proofs, but by animal-like dissemination of its life.

Time becomes timeless, for he has found a pearl without price. And so Jesus, after His great experience, fled into a wilderness where it is written he fasted forty days.

One enters a period of self-questioning, of inner argumentation, when one tries to weigh the value of the new against the value of the old. Jesus therefore, when the period of dreaming about His wonderful experience was over, began to struggle with this problem of adjustment, of choosing between two worlds-the new world of seeing and the old world of reasoning.
