FOREWORD This book, entitled "," 

is but a brief story of labors of love contributed by an exile of Erin to the building of the dawn of an Irish Ireland. 

By an Irish Ireland I mean an Ireland intellectually free from the centre to the sea.

I mean an Ireland as the Divine Architect in the beginning designed it. Her resurrection therefore must faithfully follow the lines and designs of the Original Architect:—namely, Irish material for the building, Irish language, Irish games, music, dances and songs; Irish laws, Irish industries and economics;

Irish self-centralization; Irish art, science and native culture. 

Some say the book should be entitled "The Birth of a Nation" I do not think so. Ireland's birth as a nation antedates most of the oldest countries of Europe. And though she has passed for over seven hundred years through the furnace of unmentionable alien enemy persecutions, she has never even once relinquished her 

God-given right to be free.

Today she stands triumphant without a stain upon her banner -led by a great statesman, whom Almighty God has raised up like unto another powerful man of destiny, Franklin D. Roosevelt-to take his country out of the wilderness and into her rightful place of prosperity, holiness and happiness. 

Today, Ireland like America is marching upward and onward with her face to the stars. She is coming through the crucible of centuries of atrocities as fresh and as fair as if nothing had ever happened to her.

Millions of her kith and kin around the world today, with heads erect, are watching and waiting with calm and confidence for that long looked for broadcast: "IRELAND A NATION ONCE 

AGAIN." Those who may try to delay the broadcast will dig their own forgotten graves. The countries of the world admire Ireland's dauntless and unbreakable courage, and are willing and anxious, with one accord, to welcome her back to her rightful place in the ranks of the free nations of the earth. All liberty loving Americans are with her in her fight for freedom.

Father Fielding is known as the father of Irish athletics in

Chicago. He was founder and first president of Gaelic Park, an institution that has brought health and happiness and joy to thousands of Irish boys and girls in our city. It was there as a boy that I first learned to dance an Irish horn-pipe, to sing an

Irish song, to watch the prowess of Irish athletes, to listen to Irish oratory and admire the many happy matrimonial contracts that were made there. Gaelic Park must always remain a lasting monument to Father Fielding in the City of Chicago. 

In 1904 Father Fielding induced Tom Kiely of Tipperary to come to America and compete for the world's championship at the Olympian games in St. Louis. Kiely took all the athletic prizes at the Exposition, and then insisted that Father Fielding, whom he had known in the athletic arena in Ireland, should take off his coat for the sake of the homeland. He took it off and emerged with five gold medals.

His virile body contains a heart of iron which cannot be swayed when right must triumph, a heart of flint when wrong would tempt a feeble soul, a heart of steel to strengthen the faltering footsteps of a weaker brother, a heart of gold when charity or friendship calls.

A man's man we might style him, also a scholar and always a gentleman. His well-known greeting to the stranger who visits his house, which we call "KILKENNY CASTLE," proves the lavishness of his hospitality: "Come in the evening or come in the morning, Come when you're looked for or come without warning: 

Kisses and welcomes you'll find here before you, And the oftener you come here the more we'll adore you.”

One of the outstanding features in the life of Father Fielding is that in the most crucial crisis he never gives offense, never hurts the feelings of another.

With the ancient Dutch proverb ever before his eyes: "No language-no nation," in 1899 he organized the Irish language League in St. Bernard's Hall, Chicago, little dreaming that he had begun the first real Sinn Fein movement in America. Realizing that he was filling an empty void in the hearts of the exiled sons of Erin in this country, he resolved to give to America the best that is brought from Ireland. Soon the sweet tales of traditional folklore were introduced, the writings and songs of the ancient bards exhumed, and the pure, Catholic, Celtic dances revived. His was a stupendous task, but the antithesis of two civilizations, the Irish and the English, was to be made manifest to a world which placed them upon the same plane. Gathering a handful of staunch followers, who realized that if the Irish of Chicago were to be united (Sinn Fein-we ourselves), they not only must pray together and learn together, but must play together. Then he began the revival of the ancient Tailten games, which had been buried in the vaults of history for over 750 years. A plot of ground was purchased and a park erected, which Father Fielding called Gaelic Park, where the thousands of exiles from Erin might meet and dispel the loneliness of the land of their adoption by seeing again the games and the dances of their native land and hearing again the songs and stories crooned over their cradles by saintly Irish mothers.

From The Irish People, Chicago, Jan. 31, 1919. 

REV. FATHER FIELDING ON GAELIC LEAGUE Prominent Priest in Speech Delivered Several Years Ago Outlines Objects of League Leading Toward Results of Today 

We publish the following extracts from an address entitled "The Object of the Gaelic League," delivered by Father Fielding at a Night in Ireland celebration in the Y. M. C. A. auditorium, March 17, 1902.

The meeting was held under the auspices of the Irish Language League of Chicago, of which Father Fielding was then the state president.

Father Fielding's address, from the standpoint of nationhood, is interesting, for the world's savants are at present giving out convenient definitions of nationality and telling us what constitutes a nation. It also goes to show how far Father Fielding was abreast of his time on Irish affairs seventeen years ago. 

No intelligent person who reads it will say that Irish and Saxon civilization are similar or inseparable. 

ADDRESS 

The Gaelic League, which was founded by Dr. Douglas Hyde and the late Rev. Eugene O'Growney about twenty-five years ago, set out to revive the Irish language and the national characteristics of the country.

Its principal object was to make Ireland Irish and thus educate her away from England. The Sinn Fein movement, which is the logical outgrowth of the Gaelic League being educated in its school, is now rendering an account of its stewardship and reflecting credit on its alma mater.

Its object in America is the same as it is in Ireland, namely, to make Ireland a nation in the true sense of that word. That a country may be called a nation it must have certain national characteristics. If it loses these national characteristics, or if it imitates or adopts the characteristics of another nation, it forfeits its right to distinct nationhood, and at best is but a province 3

or a colony. What are the characteristics that constitute a nation and that give its people an individuality of their own-that will make them self-centered, self-reliant, self-respected, selfdetermined, independent and distinct from the people of other nations? First and most important is their own national language, without which they cannot have a national literature nor a national history, though they may contribute to build up a literature for other countries, contribute to the building of the countries themselves and even fight their battles. There are various other characteristics that constitute a nation: as national music, national dances, national songs, national games, national art, national industries, national mode of thought, world outlook, etc. When a country loses those national characteristics it ceases to be numbered among the nations, and its people cannot be called nationalists, but colonists. people lose their national language they are said to be 

When a truly conquered. Tacitus said, over ago, that the language of the conqueror in the mouth of two thousand years the conquered is ever the language of slaves. Archbishop 

Trench once said, “A nation which allows her language to go to ruin is parting with the better half of her intellectual independence, and testifies to her willingness to cease to exist." 

Now let us just for a few moments remove from our eyes the green glasses and look at facts in the face. Has Ireland lost or is she losing her national characteristics? Is she adopting English literature, English songs, English music, English dances,

English games, English politics, English manners, customs and speech, English dress, English ideas and English goods? It there any danger that she will finally adopt English religion? Is it a fact that the native Irish are leaving the country in thousands and that the Englishman, the Scotchman and the Jew are taking their places? Is it a fact that when Ireland was Irish Ireland was holy?

Is there any danger that Ireland, once a great nation and the light of Europe, will eventually become a Godless English colony? These are the questions that the Gaelic League of Ireland and in America is dealing with today. It is having an uphill fight, not as much against English forces as against Anglo- Irish forces. May He who enlightens the minds of men give light to those anglicised Irishmen to see the error of their ways in openly or secretly opposing a movement that is endeavoring to save a once great nation from national extinction; that is en-

LO deavoring to save the children of the Gael in Erin and in exile from the baneful effects of Saxon civilization, with its shameful lack of education, with its monstrous heresy of superior races, with its greed for blood, with its horrible prostitution of the sacred motive of patriotism to encompass the most revolting butchery, with its myriads of jarring religions that pass all thinking, with its laden materialism, its perfected immorality, its gross and groveling superstition. And the aggregation is wantonly called by the names of the "New Thought," "The Practical Life," "Science," "Progress and Civilization." From those things we ask every Irishman to help the Gaelic League to save the children of the Gael. And from such a civilization we pray the Good Lord to deliver his ever faithful Irish people.

OUR NATIONAL GAMES, DANCES AND MUSIC 

It is a mistake to suppose that the Gaelic League confines its sphere of action to the mere cultivation of the national language and literature of Ireland. It also encourages the revival of Irish games (especially hurling), Irish dances, Irish music and Irish songs. It has given a new impetus to the much neglected study of Irish history. It is the guardian of Irish traditions, and has aroused the Irish people to the necessity of fostering Irish art and cultivating and promoting their much neglected industries. It shows the true causes of emigration, and gives the remedy. It has struck a chord which vibrates through the fibers of every national movement. It is the national movement itself, and the most important movement before the Irish people today. It is a fact that must be reckoned with.

A POPULAR KILKENNY PRIEST 

Address to Father Fielding on the Occasion of His Departure for Ireland 

On Monday evening, June 9, a large and representative meeting of the Gaelic societies of Chicago was held in Holy Angels' Hall, Oakwood boulevard and Vincennes avenue. The occasion was the regular quarterly session of the State Board of the Gaelic League, but additional interest was given to the meeting on account of the fact that Father Fielding was to say "goodbye" to his numerous friends before leaving the city on an extended visit to Ireland. Amongst those on the platform and in the meeting were noticed P. F. Holden, National Secretary; John C. Gillespie, State Vice-President; Joseph Mulhearn, State Secretary; John 

O'Grady, State Treasurer; Daniel Sheehan, Mrs. Rose McHale,

State Directors, and Rev. Brother Finan, of Notre Dame University, representing the Gaelic League in Indiana. The report of the committee having in charge the arrangements for the approaching Gaelic picnic having been read and adopted, Mr. Jos. 

Mulhearn presented the following resolution, which was carried unanimously:-"Recognizing the importance to our movement of an early and complete settlement of the controversy regarding the Gaelic Chair in the Catholic University at Washington, we again request the members of the A. O. H. to demand an investigation of the whole question at the National Convention to be held in Denver next month. To bring this matter more clearly before the great society of Hibernians we direct the State President, Father Fielding, of the Gaelic League in Illinois, to appoint a committee to appear before the various divisions and explain the necessity for prompt and decisive action." 

Some other routine business having been attended to, Rev. J. 

K. Fielding vacated the chair on the motion of the National Secretary, Mr. P. F. Holden, and State Vice-President Gillespie took his place. 

Mr. Holden then read the following address to Father Fielding,

a Gaelic copy of which, suitably illuminated, will be sent to 

Father Fielding in Ireland:- REV. AND DEAR FATHER:-The Gaels of Chicago, gathered to gether in regular quarterly session, learn of your early visit to Ireland with mingled feelings of pain and pleasure. We can illafford at the present time to be deprived of the intrepid captain who has fought with us in so many battles for the recognition of the real Irish National movement, and whose presence amongst us was at all times an assurance of victory. The many influences we have to fight against, while our people are still largely unacquainted with our hopes and ideals, leads us to fear the consequences of an extended absence, but we know your prayers will be with us and the Atlantic ocean will carry us many messages of wise counsel from "The fair hills of Eire," while you shall be the guest of your Motherland, and the accredited ambassador of her children in exile. Whatever you may have to say to our brothers in the old land will be approved by us, for we are too well acquainted with your honesty of purpose and genuine love of Ireland, as well as your signal abilities, to doubt for a moment the wisdom of any advice you may feel called upon to offer. In view of the great wave of Irishism now spreading over the old land we cannot doubt that your sentiments will be received with enthusiasm, and your visit hailed as a timely blessing by the brave souls, who from the hallowed halls of Maynooth, from the historic and beautiful capital of Ireland, from the heath-crowned summits of Slievenamon, from the forage-covered slopes of the Comeraghs, and from "many a mud-wall cabin" hurl defiance to the Saxon, and the Saxon ways, bending their united energies to the regeneration of the oldest nation in Europe. Father Fielding feelingly replied to the address and speeches, expressing his appreciation of the kind things that had been said of him, and exhorting his friends in Chicago to keep up the fight for an Irish Ireland. He pledged himself to work for the Gaelic cause in Ireland as earnestly as he had done in America. In the course of his speech he touched upon many important Gaelic League matters, particularly the University question, and the O'Growney Removal upon which he was in close touch with his hearers, and apparently voiced their sentiments. He concluded his speech with an eloquent peroration, which was warmly applauded. Since coming to the city Father Fielding has interested himself in everything Irish, and has been the means of creating a vigorous and growing sentiment in favour of the Gaelic movement, with the result that Irish music and our fine old Irish games are being heartily supported at the present time, while language classes are springing up in all quarters. The Gaels of Chicago remember this and are grateful to the young soggarth who has broken down the barriers of prejudice and taught them to be proud of what is distinctively their own. They trust his visit to the Old Land will nerve him to greater things for the cause he loves so well, that it may give him a much-needed rest, and that he may enjoy to the full the many pleasures of the beautiful little island on which "our hopes are set."

From the "Kilkenny Journal," Kilkenny, Ireland, Aug. 27, 1902.

THE GAELIC REVIVAL IMPORTANT AND ELOQUENT LECTURE BY THE REV. 

J. K. FIELDING 

"The Time Has Come for Plain Speaking in Ireland"

THE EMIGRATION PROBLEM Foreign Puritanical Ideas Partly Responsible A Plea for the Restoration of Our National Customs 

Speeches by the V. Rev. Canon Doyle, D.D., Captain the Hon. 

Otway Cuffe, and Mr. P. Kennedy, B.A. 

On Monday night the Rev. J. K. Fielding, President of the Chicago Gaelic League, delivered an important lecture on the Gaelic revival in the Courthouse, under the auspices of the Gaelic League. Father Fielding was accorded an enthusiastic reception by the people, and the city bands and a torchlight procession paraded the streets before and after the lecture. The St. John's 

Brass Band also played some national airs during the evening. 

There was a large attendance at the lecture, and nearly all the city priests-regular and secular-were amongst the audience. 

The mercantile community of the city were very poorly represented, and this fact, considering the importance of the subject chosen by Father Fielding, and its bearing on the industrial life of the country, was commented on very largely. The chair was occupied by the Rev. J. Dollard, C.C., President of the Kilkenny 

Branch, and the Mayor (Ald. P. Hoyne), also occupied a seat on the platform, and amongst the committee of the Kilkenny Branch present were: Miss O'Brien, Miss Donleavy, Captain the 

Hon. Otway Cuffe, Vice-President; Ald. J. Nowlan, Mr. N. Kenny, Mr. T. W. O'Hanrahan, J.P., honorary treasurer; Mr. W. Kinchella, Mr. P. Kennedy, B.A., and Mr. W. M. O'Kenealy, honorable secretary. 10

The proceedings opened with a chorus in the national language by the juvenile members of the League, the pianoforte accompaniments being ably played during the evening by Miss Donleavy.

The Rev. Chairman then briefly introduced the distinguished lecturer to the meeting. 

Rev. Father Fielding, who, on coming forward, was received with great cheering, said: Rev. Chairman, Very Rev. and Rev.

Fathers, ladies and gentlemen: It is a privilege and a pleasure to me to be in a position to address a few words to the people of Kilkenny on one of the most important phases of the Gaelic Movement, namely, the emigration question. Much has been said, and written, and resolved on this question during the past few years, but little had been done to keep the people in the country. (Hear, hear.) Our conservatism at times may have been justly condemned, our lack of humour may have been questioned, but our ability to make speeches, to draft resolutions, or write newspaper articles has never been disputed. We can win the world's championship at any one of these events even without preparation or practice. (Applause.) Why, if it were possible to collect and string together all the speeches, resolutions, and newspaper articles concerning Ireland and its people that have been given to the world during the past 25 years the aggregation would carpet this island. And what an interesting carpet that would make! (Laughter.) But, thank God, the tide is turning. The Irish people are beginning to think for themselves; too long have they permitted and paid others to do the thinking for them. (Hear, hear.) The Gaelic League is a thinking body, knowing what true nationality consists in, and having the best interests of this country at heart. I address myself, then, to the Gaelic League. But should my remarks give offense to those who are not Gaelic 

Leaguers, I pray them pardon me, for no offense is meant. I would ask them to remember that the truth is sometimes bitter and gives offense, that such mistaken offense raises discussion, discussion thought, and thought leads to the truth; and as you know, one of the unwritten rules of the Gaelic League says: 

Thou shalt not indulge in raimeis or inflict us with plamas. The time has come for plain speaking in Ireland. The Irish people must be told the truth at any cost, and knowing it, they are in duty, justice, and conscience bound to follow it without the fa-

vour of any man's hand or the fear of any man's face. (Applause.) I presume all who have given any thought to this subject are agreed that the present exodus of young people from this country is alarming. Forty thousand left the country last year, that is over a hundred a day. If this continues the Gael will become extinct, and his country will become a Godless English colony. I should not like to state it as a conviction-but I cannot sometimes help thinking at least it seems a judgment upon those who were entrusted with the traditions of this country and the welfare of its people, that the Gael should now turn his back upon them and go away from his own country. Is it possible, then, that the life-blood of this nation is oozing from a wound caused by ourselves? It has been repeatedly stated that the principal cause of emigration is due to a lack of industrial education and agricultural depression at home; still I have met farmers from Waterford, Kilkenny, Tipperary and Wexford who complain they cannot get farm hands at any cost owing to emigration to England and America. To my mind agricultural depression at the present time does not reach the root of the evil, nor is it solely responsible for this gaping wound. We must go further back-back to the time when we began to lose our own national characteristics: when we began to swap our own Keltic civilization for that of the Saxon; when we began to look abroad for our ideas and for our ideals for our models and for our happiness. (Hear, hear.) Then it was that we became foreigners in our own country, and emigration for us was made easy. This emigration-made-easy process or nation-killing system was slow but sure. It practically began with the introduction of a foreign system of education, against which there was no bishop or priest in Ireland to protest save one, whose advice shall live for ever enshrined in the hearts of the Irish people "The Lion of the 

Fold of Juda"-John McHale, Archbishop of Tuam. (Applause.)

He insisted that the Irish people should have an Irish education, and would not allow this foreign system of education into his diocese. (Hear, hear.) All honour to his memory! These schools which we now call "National" taught the Irish children to sing- "I bless the goodness and the grace that on my lot have smiled, And made me in those Christian days a happy English child." (Great laughter.)

Irish students who went to Maynooth in those days came out Englishmen, speaking and preaching the Word of God to an Irish-speaking people in the language of the "Reformation.” 

The "Landed gentry" and the red-coat men who rode to the hounds sometimes accompanied by the priests, spoke "grand English." Of course the people began to imitate them and looked upon their own language as vulgar, and in a sense responsible for their ignorance and poverty. Even the respectable beggars considered the language of this country vulgar. Hence to be deemed "respectable" one must speak English. In other words the Irish people were too "dacent" to talk Irish. Thus we lost our language and with it our best heritage. (Hear, hear.) The convent schools next appeared on the scene, and, needless to tell you, the convent schools of Ireland and the colleges, too, taught the children to be anything and everything except Irish. They taught them not only the English language, but also English mannerisms, English dances, music and songs; English rugby football, cricket, croquet, the piano, and ping-pong. (Laughter.)

Foreignism had been carried to such an extent in those institutions that parents later on sent their children to England to acquire "a Henglish haccent," and thus complete their education. (Laughter.) Thus those who were to be the future fathers and mothers of the Irish nation were made foreigners from the beginning. (Hear, hear.) With the advent of the political movements which had a corrosive effect on Irish nationality-Ireland ceased to be self-centered. The politicians changed our bu reau of information from Ireland to Westminster, and taught us that success in British politics was Irish nationality. Hence our false standard of nationality. (Hear, hear.) Their collecting tours in the United States, together with the exaggerated accounts given by the press of this country, tend to attract our people's attention to foreign lands, and lead them to believe that money can be had on the bushes in America. It is no longer the economic reason that is driving our people out of Ireland. They are being drawn out by a moral reason. Their hearts and their hopes are in foreign lands. These are some of our foreignizing and emigration-made-easy forces in Ireland. (Hear, hear.) The newspapers now began to publish articles about the advantages of emigration, advertising free transportation, and free grants of land to be had in Canada, Australia, and America. In some cases the "National" press of Dublin advertised for young Irishmen

wishing to join the English army. We had 30,000 of them there ready to fight England's battles when the Boers declared war on Saxon civilization in South Africa. It is true that landlordism, evictions and manufactured famines drove numbers of our people out of Ireland. Emigration caused by such agencies was involuntary, and it was next to death to them to leave; but emigration now seems to be voluntary, for the young people are now leaving the country without shedding a tear. I have met young men and women in America who had left good positions in Ireland, some of them having given up their farms. I asked them was it poverty that caused them to come to America, and they replied: "No, Father, but there is no fun in Ireland now." Ah! how often have I heard this and similar expressions from our young Irish boys and girls in America. "Shure there is no fun in Ireland now. The "patterns" and the sports, the hurling on the green, and the dances on the cross roads are things of the past. 

Even the pipers and the fiddlers have left the country. We had no place to go, no place to meet, no place to amuse ourselves except in the public houses. Shure there is no fun in Ireland now, Father, and how could you expect us to stay there." Many a time and oft, have I heard such sayings from young Irish boys and girls who have seen better, brighter, and happier days than they ever saw or will see in America. Such sayings and scenes were enough to bring the tears to my eyes and the red drops from my heart. (Applause.) I have lived in a city of two millions of people, five hundred thousand of whom are either Irish exiles or the children of Irish exiles-I have shared in their joys and in their sorrows-I have taken a personal part in the revival of their national language, their national games, their national music, dances and songs, and I tell you as my firm conviction that the killing of those national characteristics of our people here in Ireland has driven more young people out of this country than did the famine, and continue to drive them out in greater numbers than either agricultural depression or lack of industrial education. The young Irish mind cannot remain a vacuum, and no matter what may be the social condition of our people, no matter how you improve their means of living, if life for them continues dull, heavy, and monotonous, they will seek in other lands the pleasures and pastimes denied them at home. And is not rural life in Ireland today dull and monotonous enough to break down the lightest and the brightest Irish heart; and are

not the rural districts suffering most from emigration? (Applause.) Here, then, to my mind, is the key to the problem. The country is losing its charms for the young people and they are going away from it. The cross road dances were abolished and the pipers and fiddlers who played the national music of Ireland were driven into the poorhouses, and those who escaped the poorhouses or the parochial blackthorn went away to America. No wonder the Gael is leaving his country. Some of you remember how the boys and girls of the little villages used to meet at the cross-roads on Sunday afternoon and enjoy themselves, telling stories, singing their own songs and dancing their own beautiful 

Irish jigs, reels and hornpipes. (Applause.) And what innocent amusement! dancing their own national dances that are peerless even at the present day from a standpoint of science, agility, and above all, morality. (Hear, hear.) The old folks were there telling tales of their youth, croning or lilting some ancient Irish airs, sometimes taking the place of the piper and anxiously watching their children "dance each other down." Those little gatherings were very popular; and apart from preserving the 

National music, dances, and songs of the country, they helped to break the monotony of rural life and to keep the boys and girls in the little villages. (Hear, hear.) Abuses crept in from time to time, but instead of correcting the abuses and encouraging the youth of Ireland to dance their own dances, those gatherings were dispersed and the National dances of Ireland abolished altogether. Now our young people are dancing foreign and, I regret to say, indecent dances that were unknown to our Catholic ancestors. (Hear, hear.) No wonder the Gael is leaving the old land. You also remember that even when the day's work was done the village boys took out their hurleys and practiced their national games; and though they worked hard during the day they found rest and recreation wielding the caman as the sun went down. I have seen the games of all nations, but for science, for prowess, for manliness, give me, before them all, the Irish game of hurling. (Applause.) I have heard an American who once visited this country make the same remark, adding that he would make railroads through a regiment of English soldiers with one team of Irish hurlers. We sent some Irish hurlers from

Chicago to fight for the Boers, and they proved the truth of this assertion. (Applause.) When I consider the care and encouragement and the support given by other nations to their National

games, it is, indeed, painful to behold the lack of interest, aye, sometimes the opposition shown the National games of Ireland by those whose duty it is to provide innocent amusement for the people and keep them in the country. But here it will be urged that the practice of those games on Sunday is a violation of the Sabbath. This is an objection of men who have no faith in their country. Let me answer it. Thank God Ireland is the only little spot on the face of the earth into which Puritanism has never entered. Our Catholic fathers having given the greater part of the day to the Lord did not consider the recreation or a few hours spent practicing their National games on Sunday was a violation of the Sabbath, and this, mind you, before Puritanism or Protestantism was born. Are we going to introduce Puritanism into Ireland now? Again, it has been objected—and this is the objection of the politicians—that abuses have crept in, and therefore the games should be abolished. What a logical conclusion! 

Political conclusions are always remarkable for their logic. (Great laughter.) If abuses from time to time creep in whose duty is it to drive them out? I presume it is the duty of the 

English Government. (Great laughter.) But they say they cannot drive them out, then why are they clamouring for Home Rule?

Must all abuses remain in this country until we get Home Rule? 

No wonder the Gael is leaving the land of his birth! In connection with these foreignizing influences in this country permit me to read to you a letter addressed to the Secretary of the Gaelic Athletic Association about 18 years ago by a man, who like John of Tuam, brought his popularity with him to the grave-whose memory shall live long behind him in this country, because he touched the heart strings of its people-Archbishop Croke of Cashel. "The Palace, "Thurles, "December 18, 1884. "My Dear Sir-I beg to acknowledge the receipt of your communication inviting me to become a patron of the Gaelic Athletic Association, of which you are, it appears, the Hon. Secretary. I accede to your request with the utmost pleasure. "One of the most painful, let me assure you, and, at the same time, one of the most frequently recurring reflections that, as an Irishman, I am compelled to make in connection with the present

aspect of things in this country, is derived from the ugly and irritating fact that we are daily importing from England not only her manufactured goods, which we can not help doing since she has practically strangled our own manufacturing appliances, but together with her fashions, her accents, her vicious literature, her music, her dances, and her manifold mannerisms, her games also and her pastimes, to the utter discredit of our own grand national sports, and to the sore humiliation, as I believe, of every genuine son and daughter of the old land. 

Ball-playing, hurling, football kicking, according to the Irish rules, casting, leaping in various ways, wrestling, handy-grips, top pegging, leap frog, rounders, tip-in-the-hat, and all such favorite exercises and amusements amongst men and boys, may now be said to be not only dead and buried, but in several localities to be entirely forgotten and unknown. 

And what have we got in their stead? We have got such foreign and fantastic field sports as lawn tennis, polo, croquet, cricket, and the like-very excellent, I believe, and health-giving exercises in their way, still not racy of the soil, but rather alien on the contrary to it as are, indeed, for the most part, the men and women who first imported and still continue to patronize them. "And, unfortunately, it is not our national sports alone that are held in dishonor and are dying out, but even our most suggestive national celebrations are being gradually effaced and extinguished, one after another as well. No doubt there is something rather pleasing to the eye in the get-up of a modern young man, arrayed in light attire, with parti-coloured cap on and racquet in hand, who is making his way, with or without a companion, to the tennis ground. "But, for my part, I should vastly prefer to behold, or think of, the youthful athletes whom I used to see in my early days at fair and pattern, bereft of shoes and coat, and thus prepared to play at football, to fly over any number of horses, to throw the sledge or winding-stone, and to test each other's mettle and activity by the trying ordeal of three leaps or a hop, step and jump. "Indeed, if we continue travelling for the next score of years past, condemning the sports that were practised by our forefathers, effacing our national features as though we were ashamed of them, and putting on, with England's stuffs and broad cloths,

her masher habits and such other effeminate follies, as she may recommend, we had better, at once, and publicly, abjure our nationality, clap hands for joy at sight of the Union Jack, and place ‘England's bloody red' exultantly above the green. "Deprecating, as I do, any such dire and disgraceful consummation, and seeing in your society of athletes something altogether opposed to it, I shall be happy to do all for it that I can, and authorize you now formally to place my name on the roll of your patrons. "In conclusion, I earnestly hope that our national journals will not disdain in future to give suitable notices of these Irish sports and pastimes which your society means to patronize and promote, and that the masters and pupils of our Irish colleges will not henceforth exclude from their athletic programme such manly exercises as I have just referred to and commemorated. You may now enquire as to the success of our people in America. 

Well it is true that we are there in greater numbers-some say 20,000,000 to 25,000,000-though the official Catholic directory of the United States gives only twelve million Catholics of all nationalities. It is also true that we were among the first to come to America; still, with all our boasted millions, we have made no imprint of our civilization on that country. All visible things in 

America are either English or German. We brought very little true Irish nationality with us to the United States, therefore we had little to give. The reason to my mind is: whatever little 

Irish manners or customs we chanced to bring with us, we either left them on the docks in New York or managed to forget them on meeting our friends in America, who told us that those things were not American. In other words our people were led to believe that they cannot become good American citizens and at the same time retain their Irish manners, customs and traditions. 

We swopped our own things for foreign goods, and thus broke the bonds that bound us to the Motherland. Hence your millions in America are practically lost to you, and it is up-to-date ramais www

to talk about over 20 millions in America, or greater Ireland beyond the sea. With all the millions of our race in America, two or three hundred of them millionaires, there is not a sick bed endowed in a poorhouse in Ireland by one of them. (Hear, hear). 

Here again we have the answer to the question-Why are the Germans more successful than the Irish in America? To my mind it is because the Germans brought their own nationality with them to America, the Irish brought none. The Germans brought to America their own technical education; their own language, history, and literature; their own manners, customs, and traditions; their music, dances, and songs, and they guard those things with a jealous care and will not swap them for American shams. 

They make no cringing apologies for their presence in America. 

They are not ashamed of anything German, nor afraid to assert their rights. The bonds that bound them to each other in the Fatherland still bind them in America. They help and assist each other and are a power in that country. The only nationality we brought with us to America is our English "brogue" a hatred of England, and a green ribbon, consequently we were like a ship without a rudder at the mercy of every wave of sentimentality and sham. 

The Germans escaped those waves because they were anchored to their nationality. Hence their success. I may tell you that the hearts of the rank and file of our people are still true, but they are used, especially in the societies, by the professional Irish patriots and the American ward politicians who laugh and grow fat on their simplicity and patriotism. This, then, was the first work of the Gaelic League in America, namely, to save our people from the demoralizing influence of the professional Irish patriot and the American ward politician. Needless to tell you we are meeting stern opposition and abuse from both because we are driving them out of business. You can keep your people in the country by building up an Irish nation on Irish lines. This can be done by nationalizing everything, by encouraging your national characteristics, by encouraging Irish industries. Buy nothing, if possible, eat nothing, wear nothing but what's marked "Made in Ireland." (Hear, hear.) Thus you will keep your money in the country and keep your people in the country, too. You must come back to your original moorings. In other words, you must

For your country's sake, for your children's sake, for God's sake, for every sake, don't swap your Irish civilization for that of the Godless Saxon. (Loud applause.)

IRELAND A NATION

Editorial from The Kilkenny Journal, Wednesday, August 27, 1902.

TRUTHS-PALATABLE AND OTHERWISE 

Criticism, where it is healthy, honest, well directed, and savouring nothing of interested motives, is one of the most useful tonics that can be applied to decadence in public life, no matter from what aspect viewed. It is unpalatable at times, but so, as a rule, is doctor's medicine applied for the alleviation of our physical sufferings. Not infrequently it keenly hurts those to whom it is applied, but so does the surgeon's knife, even where an operation is inevitable. We had a good deal of this sort of criticism in the 

Courthouse on Monday evening, when the Rev. Jas. Fielding,

President of the Gaelic League of Chicago, delivered a lecture under the auspices of the Kilkenny branch of the Gaelic League. 

The Rev. Lecturer is a Kilkenny man, and from the parish of 

Mooncoin, where he was born. He, when his ecclesiastical studies were completed, left the old land like so many others, lay and cleric, for the Big Ireland across the water. We mention this fact as one which serves to give particular point to his remarks the other evening on the question of emigration from Ireland and the causes which lead to it. Like all important statements made by a responsible authority on vital issues, there are, no doubt, points open to cavil, or should we rather say, susceptible of argument, and from this point of view Father Fielding's lecture was no exception. But in the main it was a most healthy comment upon the existing situation in Ireland, viewed either from a political or a social standpoint. There was a good deal of bitter criticism about professional politicians, the press, and for that matter the clergy, and there was a good deal of inveighling against rameis. Now, we want something of that in 

Ireland at present, and we want a bit of the brake put on, as cyclists would say, on rameis, and Father Fielding contributed a good deal by his lecture in stemming the tide that was fast flowing in this direction. In reading over the Rev. Gentleman's lecture we were somewhat impressed by the antithesis that it was to a speech delivered a short time ago in Kilkenny by Mr. John 

Dillon. After his graphic sketch of all the Irishman suffered 26

abroad and at home, Father Fielding asked, "Must all these abuses remain in the country until we get Home Rule?" Now, this, to our mind, is the whole kernel of the situation, and passing from this hurried complication of speeches that follow the lecture, we find the Very Rev. Dr. Doyle, in proposing a vote of thanks, saying that he thought the time had come when the vast tracts of land of England were broken up and the bullocks expelled, and the people should be put in possession of those lands from which their forefathers were dispossessed. And then passing along hurriedly to the speech made by Captain the Hon. 

Otway Cuffe to which we commend attention as an utterance as important as Mr. Cavanagh's recent talk on the Irish question, we must take the proceedings in the courthouse on Monday last as rather of an epoch-making character. Hurried as we have been in striving to present a full report to our readers this morning, we cannot do justice in a brief comment to the excellent speeches delivered, but we feel sure that those who read the lecture will find in it food for thought, and that those who read the speeches of Dr. Doyle, Mr. Kennedy, and Captain Cuffe, will find that whatever else we are deficient in it is not in saying the happiest thing at the opportune moment.

VIRTUTIS PREMIUR HONOR

From the Waterford Star, Saturday, Sept. 27, 1902.

IRISH MUSIC 

Interesting and Eloquent Lecture in the City Hall by the Rev. J. 

K. Fielding.

On Friday evening last the Rev. J. K. Fielding (a native of 

Mooncoin), President of the Chicago branch of the Gaelic League, delivered a highly instructive and interesting lecture on "Irish Music" in the Large Room, City Hall. The Mayor, Ald. R. 

Hearne, J. P., presided, and amongst those on the platform were -Rev. Thos. F. Furlong, Adm. the Cathedral; Rev. James Mockler, De la Salle College; Professor Ahearne, Messrs. J. J. O'Mahony, Murray, W. Queally, etc., etc. 

The Mayor, who was received with applause, said that it was his pleasing duty to introduce to them the Rev. lecturer of the evening, the Rev. J. K. Fielding, who had come to speak to them on Irish music. 

Father Fielding, who was very warmly received, said: Speaking on Irish music I find at the beginning two difficulties staring me in the face. First, to avoid rameis, which is the duty of every public speaker in Ireland today; and, secondly, to say nothing that has not already been said on this subject. The Gaelic League has put the brake on rameis in Ireland, but so terrific had been the momentum that the machine is still running, but now at reduced speed. Those oratorical motor machines have played havoc with the nervous system of our people, and almost torn down the galleries of our public halls. Waterford has been so accustomed to the rant, cant and screech of those oratorical motor machines that a little genuine Irish music may help to soothe its nervous system. So much has been said and written on Irish music that it would seem difficult at this hour to say anything that has not already been said, but the difficulty vanishes when we consider that, with few exceptions, those who wrote on this subject knew absolutely nothing about the real nature of Irish music, and as 28

in the case of our language, but to a far greater extent, the densest ignorance prevails on the subject. Fortunately, perhaps, those who are our only authority on Irish music, as we shall see later on, wrote nothing on the subject. When a people lose their national language they lose their Nationality for with the loss of their language, go their national characteristics, as their Na. tional music, dances, songs, etc. When we lost our language, or rather when we became too "dacent" to speak it—(laughter) -we also became too "dacent" to sing Irish songs, consequently we sang the songs of the nigger that were made to order in 

Chicago and New York for the Irish in America. 

We sang the vile, vulger, vandiville songs of the Sassanach that were specially manufactured in the slums and music halls of London, for the Irish market at home. Those songs ridiculed us and all that we stood for, still in our ignorance we sang them, and paid to hear them sung-aye, even paid to see and hear ourselves ridiculed. I have been in parts of Ireland this summer where the Irish language is the language of the home, and I found that in such places those indecent foreign songs are unkonwn, and the demoralising effects that follow in their track are conspicuous by their absence (applause). Last Sunday I was at an 

Irish pattern on the Welsh Mountains. The fiddlers and the pipers were there, as they were perhaps 500 years before. The boys and the girls of the little villages were there in great numbers, dancing their own national dances. When I appeared on the scene I noticed confusion all round, and the proverbial stampede was threatened. On inquiring I found that the people had mistaken me for their own priest, who, they said, would horse-whip them if he found them dancing. I found that foreign dances or foreign songs were unknown on the Welsh Mountains, that the boys and girls danced nothing but Irish dances, "that they came to the cross-roads at two o'clock, and went home to the cows"; that life in the little villages along the Welsh Mountains was very happy, and that few go from there to America. I encouraged them to dance their own beautiful Irish dances, that were danced by their Catholic ancestors, who above all people on earth were remarkable for their morality and unadulterated Catholicism (hear, hear). I told them that no Theologian had ever condemned 

Irish dances as such, and that had they ever seen or understood the nature of Irish dance, they would have numbered it among

But our Theologians were not of this country, consequently, when they wrote condemning the objectionable dances of the Continent and of America, they did not have the moral dances of Catholic Ireland in their mind, consequently did not condemn them. If our own priests condemned Irish dances it was because of abuses-which was very illogical, or because they did not understand the nature of them, consequently included them in the general catalogue of foreign indecent dances. In America we give every encouragement possible to Irish dances, that they may serve as an antidote to the vulgar indecent dances of the day (applause). The stampede on the Welsh Mountains reminded me of a tale told in America a few years ago by an Irish missionary priest in connection with this matter. He was insisting on the necessity of the Irish people dancing their own dances and singing their own songs. He related that he was on one occasion travelling in this country, the object of his tour being to hear the grand salutations of the peasantry, and as he expressed it, to see Irish Catholicism unadulterated. Travelling through an Irish district near the sea coast he happened, to his great delight, to come upon an Irish dance on the cross-roads. The moment he put in an appearance the alarm was given and a general stampede commenced. Hats, wraps and shawls were left on the roadside, and, unfortunately, the poor old piper, who was blind, was left there, too. Moved to pity by his appeals for help, two brawny young men lifted him from his seat on the road-side, and threw him body and bones over the fence and then made good their own escape. As a result of this crusade against Irish daylight dances the young people now, who must have some amusement, come out at night-time and dance foreign, indecent dances in the dark. I mention those facts to show the mistaken ideas that still exist in Ireland with regard to the true nature of the Irish dance. Of course the same mistaken ideas prevailed a few years ago, and unfortunately in some quarters still prevail with regard to the Irish language. 

University men said it had no literature because they knew nothing about it. The convents and colleges sneered at it because they long since had educated themselves out of a knowledge of that thing. The "National" schools abhorred it because they were built for that purpose. The farmer wouldn't speak it because he had so many cows. The shopkeeper smiled when he heard

it spoken because he was fearful lest it might injure his beautiful English "brogue." The Land Leaguers didn't speak it because the "Laders" who were the "gintlemin" of this country in those days didn't speak it. The newspapers didn't publish it because it wasn't profitable. The itinerant beggars of this country boasted that they knew not the Irish language, though the boast was sometimes worded in the same language. God help us, we were all too “dacent" to talk it. We educated ourselves into ignorance concerning our own things and then boasted of our illiteracy. Ah! but what a change in a few years! We are now one and all educating ourselves out of that ignorance. Thanks to the Gaelic League and to the men behind the guns. Eternal thanks to the Dublin "Leader." The change is conspicuous, too, in America, thanks to the champion of our race-the uncomprising defender of Patrick's Faith and Patrick's people in America, the fearless Father Peter C. Yorke, of San Francisco (applause).

Now, if such ignorance prevailed with regard to our language, dances, songs, and as I showed some short time ago with regard to our National games, is it surprising, then, that our music, which cannot be divorced from our language, is surrounded by a barrier of prejudice and ignorance. Will you be surprised when 

I tell you that I was reprimanded for playing Irish music in an

Irish College some years ago, the professor assuring me that Irish music was stable boy music, and should not be practised within the precincts of the college. Said professor, who was supposed to know all things, could not distinguish the difference between the "Rogue's March" and "St. Patrick's Day," still he knew that 

Irish music was stable boy music. I have met my friend the professor since I came to Ireland, and found him a staunch Gaelic Leaguer. I had the pleasure of assuring him of the success of the Gaelic movement in Ireland, "because," I added, "it has reached you." Truly, a wonderful change has come over this country in a few years. Our music is an integral part of our civilization, bound up with our history and traditions. Where the language failed to convey the thought of the brain, the word of the mouth, or the sentiment of the heart, the music came to its assistance and carried the thought home. It lulled the child to sleep, it soothed the sorrows of the sick, it infused new blood into old hearts, it strengthened the arm and nerved the heart of the warrior. It is the unwritten history of this country, showing

the culture and civilization of our ancestors. It was composed by Irishmen a thousand, perhaps two thousand, years ago, and appeals today to their hearts as no music ever did appeal to the hearts of any people. In a word, it is the National music of 

Ireland, therefore it should be preserved. In our attempt to preserve Irish music, we must never lose sight of the fact that Irish music is not modern music, and cannot be played on the modern scale. The reason is that the two scales are fundamentally different. Our music was composed, not written, and handed down traditionally from one generation to another. It was composed before the modern system of notation was invented, and practically escaped the ruinous effects of that system until Bunting's time, which was a little over a century ago. When we lost our language we lost our Irish songs, then English songs were invented and set to airs still preserved by the fiddlers and pipers. 

In the endeavor to adapt the old Irish air to English words and then commit it to modern notation, the air lost that blas or “brogue,” as the Chicago pipers call it, peculiar to Irish music.

This process of mutilation was practiced by Tom Moore, so that his melodies, as written, are not Irish music. We may say of them that they are lovely even in death, but by no means Irish or as beautiful as when sung by the woman at the spinning wheel, who is utterly ignorant of modern music, or the milk maid on the mountain side, milking her cow. Hence you may have heard it said in country places that the fiddler or the flute player who plays by note is not as sweet as those who play by ear, or, as they say, the note players cannot put the "bearls" on it. We must remember, however, that perfect Irish music can be made from those printed collections by an Irish piper or a fiddler who plays by ear, or in the traditional way. The piano, and the do, re, me, fa college professor (laughter) are irreconciliable with Irish music. A modern musician cannot play Irish music, neither can 

Irish music be played on a modern instrument. The reason, of course, is that the two systems of music are totally irreconciliable. 

These are facts not generally known. From this then it follows that our only authority on Irish music is the Gael—the old woman at the spinning wheel, who never heard a college professor lecture on Irish music, or even sing "Dolly Grey" (laughter)—the fisherman or the ploughboy whistling-the milkmaid sitting on the mountain side--the mother at the cradle-the old pipers and fiddlers on the cross roads who were thrown over the ditches-these

The school teachers, the scholars, and the professors must go to the peasant's cabin for a knowledge of Irish music, and there, too, they will find their Irish grammars (laughter)! After all, there is some truth in the assertion made by my old professorial friend who told me that Irish music was the music of stable boys. We present to our readers today a full report of the brilliant lecture on Irish Music, which the Rev. J. K. Fielding, of Chicago, delivered to a highly appreciative audience in the City Hall on Friday evening. The reverend lecturer we can claim as a countryman, for he first saw light near Mooncoin, the birthplace of another Irish priest, Father Dollard, “Slievenamon," whose writings breathe all that is true, patriotic, and noble. In the latter-day history of this country, in the noblest of all noble projects for the dragging forth of our ancient language from the dark recesses to which a spirit of senseless pride and an idiotic endeavour to ape the ways and the fashions of our Saxon foes relegated it, the names of three local men stand out in bold relief, and they are these Father Henebry, of Portlaw; Father Dollard and Father 

J. K. Fielding, of Mooncoin. One cannot but feel gratified that when, almost, all others had apparently forgotten the existence of the Irish language, or at least passively ignored it, three natives of this ancient county held aloft the flag of an Irish Ireland, and by their writings, their speeches, and their personality made, with the ever-lamented Father O'Growney, and Father

Yorke, the revival, not alone practical, but pushed it well on the road to success. Father O'Growney lies in his flower-strewn grave far away from the plains of his native Meath; but the work for which he gave his life, is, thank heaven, in the hands of men of the right caliber. The Gaelic League in Waterford was launched under great difficulties. At one point, the few determined men who raised its banner were confronted by the old barriers of prejudice, and a deep ignorance of, if not aversion to, the 

Gaelic tongue. Irishmen and Irishwomen, who would see the country freed from the demoralizing influence of the low comic song and the equally abhorrent, indecent dances of the English type, who value the fair fame of our daughters, and who would wish to see preserved, unbroken, our record for unfaltering and unswerving fealty and loyalty to the Chair of Peter, will from their hearts cordially thank, as we do, the gifted young missionary lecturer. Father

Fielding's services to church and country in the Greater Ireland across the water, we need not dwell upon. The Gaelic revival and the industrial revival go hand in hand and are the most wonderful in the history of any country. The bishops, priests and people have taken up the movement in earnest. They believe that the English Parliament cannot make an 

Irish nation. The people, and only the people, can make or rebuild the nation, and they can do it only by encouraging their own national industries, which are the life of every nation, and fostering the national characteristics; their language and their literature; their music and musicians; songs, dances, games, etc. 

Last year the Gaelic League publications sold in Ireland amounted to 200,000, and happily took the place of the English "penny dreadful" and "shilling shocker" influence that has not been unfelt in Ireland. 

Within the last few years branches of the Gaelic League have been formed in nearly every town and village in Ireland. In connection with them are reading rooms, recreation halls, bowling alleys, gymnasiums; also technical institutes and athletic clubs for the encouragement of national games such as hurling and football and other manly exercises. There are now about 800 such athletic clubs in Ireland.

The Irish language is taught in primary and intermediate schools, all seminaries, colleges and convents and is required for admission to some institutions of learning. In 1900 it was taught only in thirty-nine schools, today it has a place in the curriculum of studies in 15,000 public schools. It is on a par with English in all those schools and the teachers are paid by the government to teach it just the same as English and they obtain the same "results fees."

People from English speaking districts of Ireland go into Irish speaking districts to learn the language, and business men, clerks, etc., from Dublin, Belfast, and other cities spend their

Summer vacations in Irish speaking districts. Excursions through the country are made by members of the league, and literary and musical festivals are held to the delight of the young people. 

Songs and addresses are all rendered in the Gaelic tongue. Pip-

ers and fiddlers playing the ancient Irish music are now much sought after, and they give much pleasure by their services at the "crossroad" dances.

EMIGRATION IS OPPOSED 

There is a growing sentiment against emigration in Ireland today. This has been inspired by the Gaelic League. If that which Irish girls and boys seek in foreign lands can be furnished them at home they will stay in Ireland. Work, happiness, salvation, the best possible in life for them they should find in their own land. By buying none but Irish manufactured goods millions of money are kept in the country which formerly went out for foreign shoes, foreign clothes, foods, machinery, foreign labor, etc. Even though the home product was much superior and cheaper the foreign found a market.

The passing of the Irish politician is another marked event in the Irish history of today. The politicians, who at one time were supposed to be the leaders of the Irish people and the infallible custodians of Irish nationality, bought their shoes marked "made in America" and their clothes marked "made in England" and their "national press" advertised free grants of land in Canada, America and Australia, and published advertisements for young Irishmen wishing to join the British army. Their "national papers" were printed on paper made in England, with ink made in England, and by compositors and others imported from Scotland. 

They spoke to the Irish people in English, advocated education in English schools and went to England themselves for a "change of (h) air."

Their speeches were all about England and the English House of Commons denouncing England for robbing the Irish people of their language, killing their industries and causing emigration!

Thanks to the Gaelic League the people now pay little attention to speeches on the floor of the British House of Commons. 

They believe in practical work, not words and vain promises. 

They believe that their battles must be fought in Ireland, not in England.

First of all, efforts are made to revive Irish domestic commerce and keep Irish money in Ireland. They believe, above all, that when Ireland was Irish Ireland was holy, and that their country must be built up from within on Irish lines; that an act of parliament cannot make a nation; that politics, agrarianism,

or even prosperity is not nationality. They believe that the 

Gaelic movement is their only salvation; that through it, and it alone, can this nation be saved from becoming a big, Godless, vulgar English colony. 

These are the facts though they are not generally known in this country, for care is taken to conceal them. The old leaven that once made Ireland the wonder of Europe is again working in her. Her people are being taught to think and work for themselves. They are returning to their birthright, becoming selfreliant, cultivated, educated in the true way, prosperous and happy.

The Irish in America fully understand the significance of the Gaelic movement, and in spite of the old slogans and platitudes of the politicians are awaking to a realization of the true state of affairs. 

Dr. Douglas Hyde, president of the Gaelic League of Ireland, is one of the most charming men I have ever met. Dr. Hyde is a man deserving of and possessing the best love of the Irish people. He said to me: "We found the country drifting into a vulgar imitation of what was vilest and lowest in England. English expressions and music hall songs of the most degraded type were becoming common in Ireland. The 'stage Irishman' was rampant. The fact that we were an inferior English country was veiled or cloaked. We were made to appear one. A word of Irish put one outside the pale of decency. An Irish air or song was scarcely heard in a concert hall. "Now it is different for we have established Feisanna all over Ireland of which Irish songs and dances are the features. The 

Irish harpers and pipers have displaced vulgar English minstrels. 

But the battle is a hard one and is not half fought yet. I am not exaggerating when I say the next ten years will decide whether Ireland is to remain Irish or become a big and vulgarized English county-English, not only in government, but in sentiment." 

Cardinal Logue of Armagh, whom I met at Archbishop Croke's funeral in Thurles, is an active leader in the Gaelic movement. 

He said: 99 "It is a well-known fact that nowhere in Ireland is faith stronger, religious feeling deeper, innocence of life more conspicuous than in those districts where the Irish language is spoken."

The Cardinal said he had been accused of giving all his attention to the Gaelic League and neglecting other movements, but he replied that there were enough others to do the other work, and that he would stick to the Gaelic League and thus serve his country best.

At Archbishop Croke's funeral I also met my boyhood's friend, the great Cardinal Moran of Sydney, Australia, and reminded him that I was confirmed by him (a Cardinal) and ordained by a 

Cardinal (Gibbons).

Cardinal Moran is one of the ablest ecclesiastics in the Englishspeaking church. He expressed great regret at the death of Archbishop Feehan. He spoke of him as "the great representative of the mother church of Ireland in America" and pointed out to me the spot where he was born-a short distance from where we then stood Killenaule, County Tipperary, and compared his mission to the Irish race in America to that of Archbishop Croke's to the Irish in their own country. 

Later I met Archbishop Feehan's relatives who live at Hedenpark, Killenaule. They expressed their delight at meeting some one from Chicago. All the people of Killenaule who knew the 

Archbishop as a boy on "the plains of sweet Tipperary" seemed to feel his death very much. One old man referred to him as "the big Bishop in America who taught me my catechism at the 

Holy Well of Hedenpark years ago.' 99 The death of the Archbishop of Cashel following so closely that of Archbishop Feehan added sorrow to sadness in their native homes. Many incidents of the modest, gentle and saintly boy, Patrick Feehan, were related by the old people.

"THE NATIONAL IDEA EXPLAINED" (To the Editor of the Kilkenny Journal) 

Dear Sir: On seeing in your paper some time ago the announcement of a coming lecture from such an accomplished Irish scholar as Dr. Henebry I naturally promised myself a treat in the reading of it. Well, I have read the addresses delivered by 

Dr. Henebry and Fr. Fielding, and I must say that on leaving down the paper my feelings on the subject were, to say the least, "a bit mixed."

I felt disappointed that there was no account of Dr. Henebry saying at least a few words in the Irish language. No doubt the great bulk of his audience would not have understood what he said, but would they not have been glad to admire the soft cadence of the language when used by a good speaker? And I saw the name of at least one fluent Irish speaker among those present.

Fr. Fielding, as I take it from his reported remarks, seems to think that there would be less emigration if we had more dancing in the country, and would seem to imply that the cultivation of dancing is the principal object of the Gaelic League. 

Let us take the first point. Are not our young people a little too much given to seeking after amusement? Would it not be well to try and give their thoughts a more serious turn? If we could persuade them to cultivate those habits of activity in handicraft and practical knowledge that they so sadly lack, would not such knowledge keep them from continually casting their eyes and thoughts across the sea? As far as I am aware, and I know them fairly well, they are quite content to spend one day in the week as a day of rest.

To my mind the great cause of emigration is the returned American or Australian; within the past three years I've known one returned Australian to be the direct cause of 13 young people emigrating from the neighbourhood where they were living contentedly and had intended to remain were it not for the blighting shadow of those foreigners falling across their path. 62

If Fr. Fielding is in the habit of reading the "Claidheamh Soluis," the organ of the Gaelic League, he must know that it is the constant complaint of writers in that paper that the cultivation of dancing in excess is dwarfing the work of the language revival throughout the country, and I have the best of reasons for knowing that the President of the Gaelic League shares the opinion. 

According to Fr. Fielding, it is appalling the little that has been done for our Catholic boys and girls. Is not Irish being taught in every convent school in Kilkenny? Westminster? THE RECENT LECTURE OF THE REV. R. HENEBRY, D.Ph. 

Fr. Delaney's Criticism 

(To the Editor of the Kilkenny Journal)

Sir: I notice that my lecture in Kilkenny is producing a certain amount of discussion, a rather hopeful sign, as it proves that the thought may be provoked if only it has the initial impetus; and a thinking Ireland means an Irish Ireland. 

But let us keep the discussion on the straight road. Fr. Delaney admits that when he had read the addresses of Fr. Fielding and myself, he felt to say the least, "a bit mixed." Now that is a healthy symptom. Let him take a rest on it and it will settle, mayhap clarify and then the late Gaelic League crusade in Kilkenny will have accomplished something, even if it did not succeed in knocking a single one of the 113 public houses out of business.

He felt disappointed that there was "no account of Dr. Henebry saying at least a few words in the Irish language." Well, I did, and more than a few words. I am not responsible for the account. I finished my address by speaking for about five minutes in Irish. Afterwards about eight persons spoke to me in that language. If the number in the hall be divided by eight it will be seen that the Irish speakers got more than their proportion. 

Fr. Delaney asks: "Is not Irish being taught in every convent school in Kilkenny?" For the sake of argument let us say it is, and well and effectually. But even if it is, when will the convents have atoned for their virulent propaganda of foreignism carried on as long as they have been in activity? Who was it taught manners and "accent" to our children and flouted everything of ours as unclean, to the extent that now poor, broken farmers and hucksters deport their children to England for education? But are they teaching Irish? My information gleaned in Kilkenny led me to believe that in one such school having 64

the enormous attendance of 750 children Irish was taught to some 40. I will admit that if you teach Irish to one child out of 750 you are teaching Irish in that school. But how much is it and what is it worth? Fr. Delaney knows as well as I that convent schools were established for quite another purpose than the teaching of Irish. He also knows that if they now teach Irish to 40 children out of 750, that they do so as a concession to public opinion, because Irish Ireland is knocking at their doors. 

But let it be here understood that for what they did in the past and for what they do now, I, at least, completely exonerate the nuns from all blame. They acted according to their lights, as we all strive to do, I hope; the fault lies elsewhere. 

Fr. Delaney also asks: "Is it (Irish) not taught in practically all the national schools of the diocese?" Well, the whole question is one of fractions. What is the ratio of Irishism to foreignism in the whole mass inculcated in all the schools of the diocese? Or will somebody give us an exact return of the number of schools in the diocese in which Irish is taught that we may compare it with the whole number of schools and thus determine accurately what strain Fr. Delaney is willing to impose on his use of the word "practically."

I must refuse to discuss with Fr. Delaney in a newspaper controversy the relations of Dr. McHale with the "National" Board. 

He asks finally what is the jarrying jangle of a multiplicity of religious sects? Is not Ireland a Catholic country? That is also a question of fractions. Do his non-Catholic fellowcountrymen count for nothing with Fr. Delaney? I will inform him again that the jarrying jangle mentioned is a phenomenon. 

I noticed it in all the English-speaking countries I lived in, whereas it was not noticeable in the many non-English-speaking peoples amongst whom I lived or whom I visited. It exists to a noticeable degree in Ireland. I classed it as an English-speaking curse and think I was plain enough. Fr. Delaney admits feeling “a bit mixed" on reading my address. I am afraid he got mixed before he felt it.

RICHARD HENEBRY.

THE TAILTEN GAMES 

Gaelic Park, July 6, 1913, Chicago, Illinois.

Organized athletics were held in Ireland when all that was known about athletics in ancient Greece was showing speed getting away from the prehistoric bear.

Runners and jumpers, weight men and vaulters were establishing records in Erin two thousand years before Troy fell into the hands of the Greeks.

The Tailten Games were hoary with age when the Olympic contests of the Hellenic peoples were in their infancy, when in fact the first winner of the first Olympic was not yet alive.

The Tailten Games flourished longer than the glory of Assyria, Egypt, Greece or Rome.

More history was made and forgotten at the Tailten Games than Rome or Greece ever were responsible for. 

The Tailten Games, established two thousand five hundred years B. C., flourished in all their glory until 1169 A. D. or two years before Richard de Clare, alias Strongbow, began the Norman conquest of Ireland.

Since 1169 they were carried on for a time in a desultory way at what is now Teltown (a corruption of Tailten) in Ireland. 

But they lost all their ancient glory and it is the purpose of the 

Gaelic Park Athletic club, headed by Father J. K. Fielding, to revive these famous games and restore them to their old-time fame. The restoration will take place at Gaelic Park, South 

California Avenue and West Forty-seventh Street, on Sunday, July 6.

Father Fielding has been one of the leaders in the Gaelic movement for many years. Always an athlete himself and with the inherent love of the Irishman for athletics he has always been interested in the supplying of healthy vigorous pastimes for the amusement and benefit of his native race. 

Gaelic Park was the result of his work along these lines and 75

the revival of the Tailten games will cap the climax in the Gaelic revival which has swept from America to Ireland and back again. 

Thousands of young Irishmen and Irishwomen in Chicago who had no place to go on Sunday afternoons have now been supplied with a park where they can watch the games and the dances to which they were accustomed at home. Chicago does not now seem so big and so lonely and so inhospitable to the exiles or to our American Catholic boys and girls. 

It is the desire of the Gaelic Park Athletic club to establish a permanent park for the use of the young men and women of this part of the country and it is believed the Tailten Games will aid in this project.

Every one who has at heart the interest of good clean athletics and who would see track and field games saved from oblivion should lend his support to this project. Athletic authorities throughout the country are bemoaning the loss of interest in athletics and they look to the Kelt and the Tailten Games to hold the fort until the wave sweeps back this way again. 

The Tailten Games, according to the best authorities, were begun about 2500 B. C. by Lewy of the Long Hand, king of the 

Dedannan tribe and one of the most powerful monarchs of his time. When his stepmother, Queen Tailte, daughter of Maghmor, king of Spain, ruled in Ireland, she was a great and well loved monarch. When she was about to die and her subjects were mourning the expected loss of their great sovereign she called her stepson, Lewy, who was to be king, to her side and told him she did not wish to have her people so downcast. 

She asked that when she was buried a great celebration be held in her honor and that the peoples from all parts of the country be called together in the cemetery in which she was to be buried and there hold their games and feasts in her honor. 

Lewy of the Long Hand carried out her wishes and so well beloved was Queen Tailte her feast day became the greatest festival in all Ireland. Beginning about the sixth of July the celebration lasted until along in August, and even after the Milesian conquest, held in great repute.

There the contracts of marriage were made between the parents for their children and there were the great hurling games and tests of strength, speed and skill decided. Even after St. Patrick came the games were continued under Christian auspices.

The games to be held at Gaelic Park July 6 will embody all the features which made the Tailten Games famous.

CARRYING THE MESSAGE 

Rev. J. K. Fielding and Rev. W. F. Cahill arrived in Washington early on Tuesday morning with the letter to the President.

Their information was that he was inaccessible to all delegations. 

No word had been received from Senator Lewis in reply to Father Fielding's request that the letter should be presented by him to the President. On arriving at the Raleigh Hotel the first acquaintance whom they met was Mrs. Mary McWhorter, president of the Ladies' Auxiliary of the A. O. H., who informed us that she was there on the same business, and had procured it that Sec- 88

retary Tumulty would present their petition to the President, late in the afternoon. Father Fielding, not having heard at a late hour from Senator Lewis, thought it might be as good a plan as any to present the letter from the Chicago clergy at the same time with that of the A. O. H.

Signatures were obtained in four hundred personal letters all of which had to be collated and put in alphabetical order and typed to make the historic document fit for presentation. In the meantime Father Cahill taxied to the National War Council Building on 14th Street, where they had learned from the Catholic University, Bishop Muldoon was staying. The signature of the 

Rt. Rev. Bishop was very readily secured and in addition he made the remark that he and his priests and laity were about to draw up another such petition. Important also, it was that he promised to lay the matter of an indorsement of the principle of self-determination for Ireland before the National Council of the Knights of Columbus, of which he is Chaplain. 

At the time appointed by Secretary Tumulty for the presentation, the document had not been half completed as to signatures and typing. Father Fielding decided that it would never do to let it go in an unfinished state, and it was 9:30 P. M. before the final word was added.

In the meantime Father Fielding had telephoned the White House and learned that the President and Mr. Tumulty were still there.

At 10 o'clock the White House telephoned him that the President had left for the train and that Secretary Tumulty would meet him there later.

Now the question was whether it were better to go to the White House to see Mr. Tumulty and give him the letter to the President for a later presentation, or whether it might not be best to meet him at the Union Depot in the possible event of his already having left to follow the President. 

Father Fielding decided that the latter course would be best.

They accordingly taxied to the Union Depot and on inquiry learned Sec. Tumulty had not yet arrived, but was expected at any moment.

The depot is about twice as large as any we have in Chicago and fully two hundred soldiers were outside the gates. The presidential train was located at track 20, and Father Fielding and his companion went forward until they were stopped by the

gate man with the polite but determined inquiry, "What is it you wish?" Father Fielding replied, "We have here a letter from the archbishop of Chicago, which must be delivered at once." "Well," said the gate man, slowly, “all right.”

FATHER FIELDING ON SINN FEIN ABLE DEFENSE OF THE GREAT IRISH MOVEMENT 

Address Delivered at St. Anne's Auditorium, Chicago,

December 22, 1918 I do not know what I have done wrong that this sentence should have been passed upon me-sentenced to explain the doctrine of Sinn Fein. Perhaps it is because of my Sinn Fein activities during the past fifteen years, or it may be due to my starting all this trouble here a few weeks ago relative to the self-determination of Ireland.

Well, I plead guilty to both charges and cheerfully accept the sentence.

Full well do I remember having received many a shell shock from good Irishmen and women in this city and elsewhere for my activities along these lines. When it was neither profitable nor fashionable to advocate Sinn Fein principles I dodged neither the shells nor the shocks because I had faith in the movement from the beginning.

Since America's entrance into the world war I kept on a gas mask and a muzzle of silence lest my feeble voice in behalf of Sinn Fein might in any respect embarrass the men at the helm of the ship of state.

During all that time I had to listen in silence and patiently to a vile avalanche of falsehood in the form of a bitter British propaganda against Sinn Fein over here, over there and everywhere. But the truth which is great and shall prevail is coming up from the grave where Britain had buried it during the past four years; and if I may judge from the rapid turn the Sinn Fein tide has taken during the past few weeks in this country, it is not only out of the grave, but in the air ready to sting its enemies. 

The truth has given Sinn Fein a court and a hearing. The people of America will be its judge and jury. Sinn Fein then is the watchword of the hour. 93

SINN FEIN MEANS OURSELVES OR SELF-DETERMINATION 

Now a sane question to ask even at this late hour is, what is the meaning of Sinn Fein? There are people who know full well how to denounce it, but do not know how to pronounce it. Some call it "Sign Fin," others call it "Sin Fine." Those are the fine sinners; but the worst sinners of all are those who say it means “Bolsheviki.” They might as well say it means "Ishkabibble."

Those who say those things about Sinn Fein think by proxy and as a rule say everything except their prayers. 

Sinn Fein is pronounced "shin fane." Translated into English it means "WE OURSELVES." SINN FEIN, SINN FEIN Amhain-WE OURSELVES AND OURSELVES ALONE. OUR COUNTRY FIRST, LAST AND ALL THE TIME. 

I would have you remember that Sinn Fein does not mean country right or wrong," for error has no rights. 66 our

A free translation of the expression is-and this is the sense in which it is commonly used-Self-respect, Self-reliance, Selfdetermination. In American English Sinn Fein means Americanism, pure and unadulterated. It stands for what George Washington stood for. It stands for the same principles the American people stand for. It holds that a just government must come from the consent of those governed, and that no people have a right to govern other people by force or against their will. It maintains that there is not unanimity of opinion regarding politics in any country, and that the majority rules in every civilized country except Ireland.

IRELAND NEVER LOST HER NATIONHOOD 

Sinn Fein also holds that an act of parliament cannot make a nation; that God gives to every country differentiating characteristics which if lost its manhood is lost, its soul is lost and the nation itself is lost.

Ireland never lost her soul for she kept the national characteristics that God gave her in the beginning. A glance at the early history of England and Ireland will show that Ireland was a nation when the Britons, to whom she brought the first knowledge of letters and Christianity were painting their bodies with blackberries.

Sinn Fein holds that nationhood is a sacred thing and cannot

be destroyed at will, or even by force; that Ireland is a distinct nation from England today in spite of centuries of unmentionable English atrocities, persecutions and other futile efforts to destroy her national characteristics and make her a west British shire. 

CHARACTERISTICS THAT DISTINGUISH HER FROM ENGLAND 

Sinn Fein never acknowledged England's right to rule her, nor did she ever abandon those characteristics that she inherited from beyond the dawn of history and that are still entwined with her very heart strings, namely, her language, her music and songs, her national dances, games and pastimes, her folk lore and fairy tales, her industries, ancient laws, art and literature, her mode of thought, her world outlook, her morals, her customs and habits, her honor, her fidelity, her bravery in battle, her spotless flag, her high sense of justice and fair play.

Those things she has never forgotten, nor has she ever forgotten the prayers that Patrick brought her from the cradle of Christianity.

OBSTACLE OF IRISH INDEPENDENCE REMOVED 

Sinn Fein also holds that Ireland's voice must be heard from within her own shores and not in a foreign parliament; that the presence of British paid Irishmen in England's parliament is an acknowledgment of that parliament's right to rule Ireland and of 

Ireland's acceptance of that yoke.

There was only one obstacle standing in the way of Ireland's fight for freedom, and that was the presence of the Irish parliamentarians in the British house of commons. But that obstacle was removed recently by the people of Ireland. 

I was there at the rocking of the Sinn Fein cradle fifteen years ago when the parliamentarians were requested to come home and try other methods in Ireland, but with few exceptions, they refused. They were asked in the sacred name of liberty not to continue toying with the destinies of the Irish nation. They did not have faith in their country, nor confidence enough in their countrymen to stand aside and heed the solemn warning. Meantime they accepted the crumbs that fell from England's table and refused even those crumbs to the brave Irishmen in the north of Ireland who have fought on their backs for their creed and their country.

Sinn Feiners have now compelled them to come home and have

assigned them to permanent seats, not in the parliament of the Irish Republic, not at the peace conference, but at the back of behind. 

IRELAND'S FREEDOM INTERNATIONAL QUESTION 

When the prime little minister of England now meets President Wilson he cannot conveniently say to him, "This is a domestic question and we shall settle it without any outside interference to the satisfaction of the Irish people through their representatives in our parliament." You shall do no such thing, Mr.

George. You didn't settle it when you could have settled it as a domestic question. It is now an international question and shall be settled without the fear of your hand or the favor of your face.

ORANGEMEN IN IRELAND ALIEN ENEMIES 

Mr. Lloyd George says the orangemen must not be coerced. I have never heard of anybody who wants to coerce the orangemen. But how about coercing for centuries the people of Ireland who were not orangemen? Must they continue to be coerced and enslaved? The Irish people never drew the line on a man because of his religion. You are aware that many of the leaders of the people of Ireland in the past were protestants, and not a few of them from the north of Ireland. May we not in the future under the flag of freedom look for loyal leaders among the protestant men from the north of Ireland? It isn't just nor fair to say that the orangemen are the only obstruction in the way of 

Irish freedom. I believe they will yet make good Irish citizens, and that they will be proud to have a flag and a country. Their present standing in Ireland would class them in any other country as alien enemies, without a flag to fly or to follow, or a country to love-like mules among the nations, without pride of ancestry or hope of posterity.

Will President Wilson permit such a motley mob, deceived and cajoled by a professional, rebel politician, Sir Edward Carson, to stand in the way of the freedom of a nation? If he should his name and all the noble principles he stands for shall spell failure, and he shall fall as Satan fell.

IRISH QUESTION SETTLED NOW IN PEACE OR LATER IN BLOOD 

If God Almighty blesses England with brains, with which she has never been overburdened, she will be glad somebody settles

the Irish question for her, for if it isn't settled now in peace it will be settled later in blood, and the settlement may cost her her dominions and the friendship of her former allies and America.

CARSON ENGLAND'S HIRED MAN IN IRELAND 

The poor orangemen are simply being used as a buffer by British interests in Ireland, and as an excuse for not letting loose their grip on that country. Sir Edward Carson is the hired man in the case. You will remember that when that gentleman imported into Ireland arms and ammunition purchased from the Kaiser in Germany, with the avowed intention of breaking every British law, resisting with all his strength the parliament of England and actually causing mutiny in her army, the government, instead of taking him to London Tower and hanging him as a rebel against legitimate and acknowledged authority, promoted him to a seat in their cabinet. 

Had they then hanged "King Carson" as a rebel and enforced the law passed by the parliament and signed by the king, regarding home rule for Ireland, they would have come out of the war with at least a little honor; but Carson, "the man of destiny," unconsciously saved Ireland.

SINN FEINERS THE REAL PATRIOTS-CARSON THE REAL REBEL 

Now witness the contrast in the treatment of the Sinn Feiners that Easter week in Dublin. They rebelled against illegitimate authority; against an authority they never acknowledged; against a so-called right that they denied should exist—the right of a big bully to beat a little boy-yet they were shot like dogs in the capital of their own country when they had surrendered as prisoners of war after making an honorable fight for freedom. Some of them were killed or rather murdered in cold blood, and others of them, wounded in battle, were propped against the wall and shot without the rights of their religion. Here is a sample of the democracy of the avowed "protector of small nations." If this be democracy, I ask, where are thy jewels?

Carson was the real rebel. The Sinn Feiners were patriots after the fashion of George Washington. Their fight like unto his was for freedom.

I knew most of those men personally who died for Ireland that Easter week in Dublin. I met them in their homes, in public

and on the same platforms. They were men of great intellectual attainments, deeply religious men; pure souled patriots; men who would be an ornament and an honor to any country. Bravest of the brave were those men of Easter week, for they knew full well that they were going to certain death and that they could not have won then; but they have won now. The martyr is victor in his death.

THE JEWELS OF DEMOCRACY 

We talk about democracy today and what it means, but to Ireland we must go to learn the real meaning of that Greek word. 

The English jails in Ireland are full today of liberty-loving Irishmen and women. There are more than one hundred Sinn 

Fein leaders-men of means of education and fine breeding-in prisons in England without a charge being preferred against them. They were deported and imprisoned there for advocating self-determination for Ireland.

Many of those men with prison stripes upon them were elected recently by the people of Ireland to sit in the parliament of the Irish republic; and twenty-five of them, according to cable dispatches, were elected to said parliament unopposed. 

Here, oh, democracy, we find thy jewels.

GOLD AND GREEN AND WHITE BETWEEN 

The Sinn Feiners demand liberty, not for a part of Ireland, but for all Ireland and for all Irishmen in Ireland; this the colors in their flag indicate "Gold and Green and White Between."

But at length the voice came, not in a whisper, but with a roar from the city of the violated treaty, and the voice was that of O'Dwyer of Limerick. A STRAIGHT TALK ON A STRAIGHT ISSUE POWERFUL ADDRESS IN THE THEATRE. 

Speeches by the Mayor (Ald. P. Deloughry), Ald. Upton, Etc. 

Despite the persistent inclemency of the weather, there was a big attendance of the general public at the Empire Theatre, Kilkenny, on Sunday night to hear the Rev. Father James K. Fielding, of Chicago, review the present and recent position of Irish affairs in his lecture entitled "Some Recent Events in Irish History."

Father Fielding, as is well known, is a native of Mooncoin, and one of the most popular and brilliant of Ireland's priesthood in the U. S. A., who is with the active vanguard working today for the emancipation of his native country. In that cause Father 

Fielding has been a persistent worker since his earliest years and it is not surprising to find him occupying such a high place in the esteem of his countrymen in both hemispheres. He was prominent with the Rev. Father Yorke and the late Rev. Father Fielding holds a warm corner in the hearts of the Irish people who only know him by repute for his worth and work as an Irishman in the cause of Ireland, and the rousing reception he had on Sunday night was but the expression of the warm appreciation of those who are proud to claim him as a Kilkennyman.

From San Francisco Leader, March 23, 1929 ST. PATRICK ORATOR PRAISES FR. YORKE 

Following is the oration as delivered by Father Fielding: 

Ladies and Gentlemen: I should like to make a brief announcement which I trust will set you all at ease. I am from Chicago, but I have no machine guns with me. For almost thirty years I have lived and labored as a priest in that great city, so I feel I can say with truth this evening that Chicago would be a paradise were it not for that un-American monstrosity called prohibition. 

I am not here to speak on prohibition, but may I not ask the question in a whisper now which may be asked with a roar later: "Is there any danger that prohibition will disrupt our beautiful country and finally put her on the rocks?" 

EVILS OF PROHIBITION 

Thus far it has paralyzed the strong arm of government. It is opening up old wounds. It is putting religion against religion, politicians against politicians, nationalities against nationalities, households against households, and encouraging disrespect for all law and order.

It has recently torn up the Constitution of the United States, trampled on the flag of our country, and still intimidates the leaders of our people, and treacherously proclaims itself one hundred per cent American.

A nation's greatness is not measured by its commercial progress, or by the size of its army or navy, but rather by the manner of men and women the country turns out. No people who trample on their past can hope to build for the future. Our ship of state is drifting from her original moorings. The mother of the future men and women of America, who will be the guardians of her rights, her honors and liberties, is on the housetops today proclaiming her political masculinity and her religious bigotry. 

IRELAND STANDS WELL 

It is a grand and glorious feeling to come from Chicago at the invitation of the United Irish Societies of San Francisco to ad- 118

dress the children of St. Patrick on the Pacific Coast. You will perhaps ask the old question: "How is dear old Ireland and how does she stand?" She stands where she always stood, though sometimes ankle-deep in blood-she stands for justice and right, for faith and fatherland. Should St. Patrick come again in the flesh and visit San Francisco, he would find his people different in language, different in dress, different in manners and customs, but one thing he would find unchanged and unchangeable, namely, the true faith of Jesus Christ which he brought to Ireland from Pope Celestine fifteen hundred years ago. He would also find them as loyal to the flag and constitution of America as they are to their faith.

STEADFAST FAITH 

For centuries she fought and suffered for her faith, and though she again and again passed through the furnace of persecution, she steadfastly refused to swap the doctrine of Jesus Christ for the new religion of the sixteenth century. 

Notwithstanding the persecution she suffered at home she sent out her children to the ends of the earth who, though England had educated them into ignorance and put the cloak of the clown upon them, carried the cross of Christ and planted it on the five continents of the globe.

Ireland did too much for God to abandon her in her most crucial crisis. Hence today she is coming into her own as a great 

Catholic nation. The cloak of the clown put upon her by England is off forever. She is now building up her native industries, teaching the Irish language, Irish music, art and sciences in all her schools; encouraging her national games, songs, music, her chaste dances and other national characteristics that were inherited by her from beyond the dawn of history and that are still entwined with her very heart strings.

NO FEAR FOR FUTURE 

Even today the world loves the sweet music of Ireland which puts a soul under the ribs of death. You need have no fears for the future of Ireland. She is in the hands of God Who will never let her sink or become absorbed, and with the assistance of her children around the world she will soon take her rightful place among the nations of the earth-independent and free from the center to the sea.

When coming to San Francisco a week ago I met a very intelligent old Irish lady on the train. In the course of our conversation I asked her if she had ever heard of Father Yorke. A. P. A.'s into the ocean as St. Patrick drove the snakes out of

Ireland, and if he was alive today the Ku Klux Klowns wouldn't throw so much dirt and mud at our holy Church."

His pen was mightier than the sword. His voice pulled down the thunder from the clouds.

Great men like great mountains must be seen at a distance to realize their mighty proportions. We live too near the time in which Dr. Yorke lived to appreciate to the full his wonderful qualities of head and heart. He loved San Francisco and San

From The Munster Express, Waterford, Ireland, Oct. 10, 1930 GALA NIGHT AT MOONCOIN 

Address by Rev. J. K. Fielding. 

Wednesday night (October 1st) will live long in the memories of those inhabitants of Mooncoin who were privileged to be present at the Band Hall on that never-to-be-forgotten night. The occasion was the visit of the Rev. J. K. Fielding (a native of Rathkieran and now on the foreign mission in Chicago) in response to an invitation from the Mooncoin Band Committee to address a few words of encouragement to the members of the band. 

As will be remembered, in a letter published in the "Munster 

Express" a fortnight ago and addressed to Mr. James Comerford, Mooncoin, Father Fielding enclosed a generous subscription of five pounds. On Wednesday night last, so impressed was he with the earnestness and efficiency of the members, that he contributed a further five pounds, making a total of ten pounds. 

Long before the scheduled time the hall was packed to overflowing, and the time was pleasantly whiled away by selections of music from the band.

Father Fielding arrived at about 8:30 p. m. and was received at the door by Mr. Wm. Kirby (Chairman), Mr. Joseph Allen (Secretary), and Mr. T. Cantwell (Treasurer), Band Committee.

As Father Fielding was being escorted to a seat on the platform the band played "God Save Ireland," and later, at Father Fielding's request, played "The Wearing of the Green.' 99 Others on the platform included Mr. Dick Doherty (one of the greatest Gaels Mooncoin has ever produced), Guard Joe Cody, Mr. John McDonald, Mr. Maurice and Paddy Comerford, Mr. 

Wm. Allen, Mr. T. Davis, Mr. N. Cantwell, and Mr. T. Dunphy (bandmaster). Mr. John McDonald, Nicholastown, and Mr. M.

Comerford acted as joint honorary secretaries to the meeting. 

CHAIRMAN'S SPEECH 

The Chairman (Mr. Wm. Kirby) said it was scarcely necessary for him to introduce Father Fielding to the meeting, because 124

Father Fielding was known and loved by every man, woman and child in the parish of Mooncoin.

Continuing, Mr. Kirby said the object of this meeting here tonight was to thank Father Fielding, firstly for his generous donation of five pounds and his splendid letter of encouragement (published in last week's papers), and, secondly, not alone for the keen interest he has taken in the Mooncoin Band, but for the Herculean services he has unhesitatingly rendered to the Irish cause. As you are all aware of the part played by Father Fielding, it would be superfluous for me to recount the magnificent work achieved by him during Ireland's struggle for freedom. 

Suffice it to say that his voice and pen and purse were used unsparingly to advance the cause. Mr. Kirby said no words of his could express their thanks, or could adequately convey to Father Fielding the deep sense of gratitude he felt they owed him for all he had done for the band, and he hoped that Father Fielding's generosity would point the way to others to help them.

In concluding the meeting, Father Fielding said-Always remember you are Irish; there is nothing to equal it. I would ask the secretary to report this meeting of comicality and joviality in the local papers so that it may catch the eye of every exiled Mooncoin man. My advice to you is to live in Ireland, die in 

Ireland and go to heaven from Ireland, for, as I remarked in my letter, the distance is shorter from Ireland to heaven than from 

America or any other country.

At the conclusion of the meeting the band played "The Minstrel Boy" and "O'Donnell Abu." Afterwards Father Fielding was an interested spectator of a "bout" between two local youths of the newly formed Boxing Club. Father Fielding was delighted with the exhibition, and has suggested that the club be called the "Tunney-Dempsey Club."

Before leaving the hall Father Fielding shook hands with each of the band boys. The scene as Father Fielding was leaving baf-

fles description. The band struck up "Come Back to Erin," while the huge throng present shouted themselves hoarse. The cheering was distinctly heard in villages two miles away. 

Father Fielding sailed by the S.S. Laconia on Sunday morning from Cobh. We join with his many friends in wishing him "Bon voyage."

From the "Scranton Republic" and the "Scranton Times" March 17th, 1931. 

ST. PATRICK AND THE SNAKES 

Address by Very Rev. J. K. Fielding, Chicago 

Your Excellency Most Rev. Bishop O'Reilly; Hon. Mayor Derby;

Mr. Chairman; Ladies and Gentlemen: 

It is a privilege and a pleasure to me to come almost a thousand miles to Scranton to enjoy your great celebration this evening in honor of the National Apostle of Ireland. To the Ancient 

Order of Hibernians and the Ladies Auxiliary of Lackawanna 

County, I am thankful for their kind invitation.

Honored, indeed, am I to speak from the same platform with your own illustrious and beloved Bishop O'Reilly, also the distinguished Mayor of your great City, Hon. F. K. Derby, and the genial State President of the Ancient Order of Hibernians of the great Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, Hon. John F. Buckley.

You will admit that I am in good company tho' I did come from Chicago.

I have always kept a cozy corner in my heart for Scranton, because when I came to America, a green little lad from the Emerald Isle, more than thirty golden years ago, it was my good fortune to be associated with students from Scranton at the great University of St. Mary, Baltimore, Md. Those students from 

Scranton with whom I associated then made me forget that I was an exile from Erin. Sure Scranton must be Heaven for your 

Chaplain, my classmate, Father O'Boyle, came from there.

By this time you will notice by my accent that I am from 

Chicago. Poor Chicago is blamed for everything. All the sins of politics and prohibition are saddled upon her. For more than 30 years I have lived and labored as a priest in that wonderful city and feel tonight I can with truth say that 90 per cent of the crimes you read about are due either directly or indirectly to the un-American monstrosity called prohibition. It is wrecking homes once happy, making numberless orphans and widows, filling the jails, the lunatic asylums, and the divorce courts, and worst of all, demoralizing youth. Young boys and girls, who are 129

destined to be the guardians of America's rights, honors and liberties are losing respect for their parents, their teachers and for all law and authority. 

Advanced thinkers today are seriously asking themselves the question, "Is there danger that prohibition will disrupt our beautiful country and finally put her on the rocks?" Thus far it has paralyzed the strong arm of government and defied law and order in every State of the Union. Three American governments have made an honest effort to enforce it and have failed. Ten years of failure to enforce it should be sufficient proof that no power on earth can ram a penal law down the throats of the American people and thus deprive them of personal liberty. 

Why should I further waste time or insult your intelligence by talking about the beast? The patriotic men and women of America are rounding him up now, and in due time will get him out of the country, and when he goes out the rejoicing will be like that on the occasion of the Armistice. The powerful press of 

America especially the world's greatest-"The Chicago Tribune" —is at the helm steering the Ship of State through this crucial crisis. 

You have now noticed by my so-called Irish brogue that I am a native born Irishman. Surely I am one of them, for even my speech doth betray me. Some years ago I wrote to an omniscient bureau of information in Washington requesting the origin and meaning of the word "brogue" as applied to an Irishman speaking English. Here is the reply:-"In ancient times, the Irish wore big, coarse shoes, which were called brogues, hence their coarse manner of speech in English was called the Irish brogue." 

Some ingenious explanation! The correct origin and meaning of the word, which I am about to give you, is not found in our dictionaries, encyclopedias, or Bureaus of Information. 

When Ireland was a free country, like America, speaking her own language and making her own laws the foreigners who came in were in time absorbed and became more Irish than the Irish themselves. This was particularly true of the Normans. But when the English came for conquest and plunder, they refused to learn the language of the country or observe its laws. They so persistently refused to speak the Irish language that the native Irish ridiculed their foreign accent and said they had the "Barog." The Irish word "Barog" means a weight on the tongue, or tongue-tied. In other words a badge of foreignism and in-

feriority. But when the English finally forced their language on 

Ireland, they referred to the natives as speaking English with the "BROGUE," which is a corruption of the Irish word "BAROG." It has acted as a boomerang from that day to this. 

The word "BROGUE" today is used as a term of approbrium and reproach by the English in England and in her colonies. Even the uneducated in America have inherited it from Colonial days. 

Of course, every educated man in America knows that the English spoken by the peasantry of Ireland today, is the classic English of Shakespeare which came into Ireland in his time. A glance at his original writings will confirm this assertion. The schoolmen of England at the time, who were second class educators, changed Shakespeare's English just for the sake, as they said, of euphony. For instance, door, floor, cold, old, etc., etc., sound better than dure, flure, cauld and auld. The English language spoken by the peasantry in Ireland today underwent no change since it was introduced there in Shakespeare's time, except idiomatic phrases from the Irish language, which like the Latin is the antithesis of English language. Those Anglo-Irish idioms, the 

English have dubbed, "Irish Bulls."

The world's greatest philologists, whom we find in Germany, tell us that the purest Anglo-Saxon English is spoken in Ireland today, especially in Dublin. Use, of course, is the law of language, but is it not distressing to listen in London to Englishmen refer to English as spoken in America as, "Yankee twang and nasal slang. ." Thus far regarding the origin and meaning of the Irish "BROGUE." 

The subject of my address this evening is entitled, "St. Patrick and the Snakes." I don't like snakes whether in animal or human form. If all that has been said and written on this subject were collected and strung together into one big carpet, the aggregation would cover the sidewalks of Scranton. And what an interesting carpet that would make. 

Before I left Chicago a very estimable lady called to see me regarding a story for her society's paper about St. Patrick. She introduced herself as a member of the Woman's Christian Temperance League, and then began her interview by asking me who is this St. Patrick about whom so much fuss is made at this time every year. "Is he related to the Pope?" she added. "No," said 

I, "But he is a first cousin to St. Michael, the Archangel.” “Well, that sure will be interesting news for all our sisters. "Isn't that 22

the man who drove the snakes out of Ireland?" she continued. "So they say," said I. "And where did they go when he drove them out?" "Over to England," said I. "But how did they come to this country," she inquired. "They came over from 

England in the Mayflower," said I: "Oh! that's the ship my folks came on," she proudly asserted. "I thought so,” said I. "That sure was some naval bus." 

My folks came over on the Cauliflower about the year 500 A. D.

The expedition was directed by St. Brendan, the famous Kerry navigator, who was the first to discover this country. The voyage lasted, from Kerry, Ireland, to Plymouth, Mass., 280 days and 

218 nights. When your folks landed at Plymouth Rock about 

1620, my folks came down to the beach to meet them and greet them, and extended to them all kinds of shelter and hospitality. 

Your sisters in Evanston will be interested to know that the kiddies of the descendents of the Cauliflower, whose parents were waiting several weeks for the Mayflower to arrive, amused themselves on the beach by picking cockles and that, that is the origin of that beautiful Irish Reel which you hear so often on the Irish hour over station WCFL at 7 o'clock each Sunday evening. The tune is called "Jenny Picking Cockles." "Please tell me, Mr. Priest, where can I get that song.' You can get it in 

Captain Francis O'Neill's "Music of Ireland," Number 1347, or from Mr. Maurice Lynch, care of broadcasting station WCFL, or one of the following Bards of Erin: Mr. Mullaney, Mr. Billy 

McCormick, or Mr. Frank Murnihan. Write in care of WCFL, Chicago. "Well, I hate snakes," she added, "and I do wish that that snake-charmer would come to this country and drive them all out as he did in Ireland." "Don't you know," said I, "that George

Washington," and she continued to take notes, "drove them back to England and some over the border to Canada, but he was so busy at the time cleaning house after them that several escaped his notice."

There is still a big fellow down in Alabama of the rattle species, but his poisonous fangs have recently been extracted so that he is harmless now. There is another big Bertha of the profiteering variety down in Virginia, whose fangs have also been drawn which renders him more or less harmless also. The fangs of the others and their followers will be pulled later by the American people. Many of those snakes are of a religious turn of mind. 

They like the odor of the divorce court, companionate marriage,

birth control, race suicide and the childless cradle. They love to push a pug dog in a perambulator. They are called “Strippers" or "Mabels."

This species of snakes mate but do not produce for God or country. They are like the mules among the equines, without pride of ancestry or hope of posterity. It is a terrible thing, madam, to be born without ancestors. But in time all those snakes, like the Mayflower ones of New England, will become extinct; for as the Irishman with the big family says, they will commit suicide on themselves and that will be the end of them. 

When the feeding appears good in Washington, they wiggle their slimy way down there. They may be seen crawling around the lobbies of Congress and the Senate, trying to intimidate the duly elected representatives of the American people. They frequently burrow, like rodents, beneath the back doors of the White House, even when President Hoover is home. They say the female of this species is more deadly than the male. "But are there not snakes in Ireland now," said my learned lady friend, as she still pushed her pencil. "No," I answered, "except in a few counties in the northeast of the country which were imported by Oliver Cromwell in 1650 from London, Glasgow and Edinborough. They were his camp followers called yellow snakes or alien enemies. They have neither a flag to fly nor a country to love. They were despised in the country they left and abhorred in the country they live. The natives of those counties who escaped Cromwell's cruel sword were driven by him, as he said, to hell or to Connaught. Connaught in those days, meant into the wilderness. On the left bank of the river 

Shannon he asked an old Irish woman whether she wished to go to hell or to Connaught. She replied, "I prefer to go to Connaught, sir, for I don't want to meet you again." The most withering malediction that could be pronounced in Ireland today is "the Curse of Cromwell on you." 99 Without fear of contradiction, I now assert that St. Patrick did not drive snakes out of Ireland, for there were none there when he came from Rome commissioned by Pope Celestine in the year 432. Further, I assert that this was English propaganda purposely put up to ridicule the Faith of Peter and Patrick that England had lost and Ireland had kept. 

Solinus, about A. D. 238, was the first to tell us that Hibernia has no snakes. This, as the learned Dr. Douglas Hyde says, robs

St. Patrick of his so-called laurels. Solinus adds that, "warlike beyond the rest of her sex, the Hibernian mother places the first morsel of food in her child's mouth on the point of a sword.” I presume we have inherited that fondness for fighting since pagan times.

The Venerable Bede, who first brought bees to Ireland, tells us that there never were any snakes or poisonous reptiles of any description in that country. He was born in England about the year 672 A. D., and was considered the most learned man of his day. It is true, the fake snake story is pictured in monuments of marble and paintings in many of our churches in America, but the monuments are modern, all dating from the so-called Reformation.

My Dear Father Fielding: 

This thing which I forgot in my previous letter to you I now subjoin in answer to your request.

Whatever is known as certain regarding Saint Patrick, his life and labours, is found in the following authentic monuments: His "Epistle to Coroticus," Patrick's OWN “CONFESSIO.”

Those hymns which are found in Vol. I of Windishe's

Now, you may ask me when and how did this fake snake story originate. It first came unintentionally from an English historian, named Jocelin, who wrote a life of St. Patrick in the twelfth century. Not being familiar with the Irish language, he translated the Irish words for demon or serpent by "snakes." St. Patrick found several persons in pagan Ireland possessed by the devil, as our Divine Lord found them when He was on earth. 

He used the form of exorcism, which every priest has in his Roman ritual today. There was little notice taken of Jocelin's translation until England swapped the true faith of Augustine for the new religion established by law, by the renegade Monk, Father Martin Luther, and the notorious king of England, Henry VIII. 

After her religious flop, England considered it her duty to protest against the faith of her fathers. It was then she began to put the cloak of the clown on the Irish and tried by every diabolical means to win them away from the true Faith of Peter and Patrick. She ridiculed their language, their music, their songs, and all their national characteristics, especially their religion. She created the Stage Irishman, the Paddy and the Pig in the Parlor for American stage consumption and she tempered this joke by popularizing the Blarney Stone myth. She ridiculed the national saints of Ireland to the extent that many Irish mothers in America today are ashamed to call their child after the "Mary of the Gael," St. Brigid. "I don't wish to handicap my child in life by calling her Brigid,” said an Irish mother to me when I suggested to call the baby after the great patroness of 

Ireland on her feast day. Where persecution fails, ridicule wins.

England put the cloak of the Clown on the Irish race around the world, but native education has thrown it off today, and forever. And what she trampled upon in Ireland, has now risen to sting her in America. 

As a reward for the valor of 50,000 young Irish men, who were not drafted, but who laid down their lives on Flanders fields, at the Dardanelles and Gallipoli, for her so-called freedom of small nations, Mr. Lloyd George sent 100,000 Black and Tans into Ireland, who put the country on the dissecting table, but failed to break her spirit of freedom. In 1920 I was an eyewitness to English atrocities in Ireland, compared with which the Germans, at their worst, as then reported, were but novices. The

may ocrite of the World's War, as follows: "You may build sufficient jails to imprison all this nation's people and hang those of us who be left, but not until the grave closes over the dead body of the last woman of Erin can you say with truth that you have conquered." Soon after Mr. George, representing the world's greatest empire, ran up the white flag and asked for a truce. At his conference in London later, he put the gun to the heads of the Irish delegation and insisted that they either sign his Free State stipulations, or else "immediate and terrible war in Ireland.” 

Some of the delegates signed it, but some, all honor to them, including Eamon de Valera, did not sign it. Then the civil war began, with some for England, and some for Ireland. England threw her bullets, bayonets, and cannon-balls against those who repudiated her fake Free State Treaty. Though the country was partially wrecked in the encounter of might against right, the brave boys of the Irish Republican Army showed the world that 

Ireland didn't take Lloyd George's "Freestate" medicine lying down.

His Free State pact partitioned the little island, giving Ireland an English parliament and an Irish parliament, which is still under English control. The pact also made provisions for the retention of his battleships in her harbors and his armies and fortifications all around her shores, so that they may be used against America or any other country in the time of war. And then, mind you, told the world that Ireland was free. England would rather lose in the World's War and go down below the coal regions than give Ireland total independence, for she knows that an Irish Republic at her door would mean that her other colonies, that she had won by Bibles, bayonets, and rum, would pull away from her.

It is true that Uncle Sam in recent conferences with England, gave his coat and vest away, but his pants and shirt still remain. 

He can fight best in those garments. Should England ever force a war on America, which I feel she will not, for she "knows her onions,” Ireland, whether free or not, will fight to the last man, to the last dyke, and to the last ditch for America; and the Germans will be with us. Though her colonies are drifting away from her gradually, she still hangs on to the words, Britain and 

British, instead of England and English.

When Ireland is prepared to demand an unpartitioned and independent Republic, she may count on America to stand by. 

The present naval conference in London will nerve America's

heart and strengthen her arm, for when her delegates return they will tell us how the trained diplomats of Europe tried to pull the wool over the eyes of the Yanks.

THE OLD CHURCHYARD By Rev. James K. Fielding. 

(The churchyard of Rathkieran is situated in the parish of Mooncoin, County Kilkenny, Ireland. It occupies a most delightful site, commanding a magnificent view of the Suir Valley. About two hundred yards from the east end of the church itself is pointed out the site of St. Kieran's monastery, also a rath from which the place takes its name. Written in June, 1894.)

In a verdant Leinster valley 

Where a river running pure, Glides by hamlet, cot and castle 

Till it joins the lordly Suir,

Stands a churchyard grim and hoary, And around it like a guard 

Are the trees that Kieran planted 

In an old churchyard. 

And the pilgrim as he passes 

Wonders what may be the tale 

Of that ruin once the glory

Of the land of Innisfail: 

Comes an answer from the gable 

Towering high above the sward— 

Ah! it tells a thrilling story 

Of that old churchyard.

Centuries have passed and many 

Since o'er lea and grassy dell,

Clust❜ring came the faithful people, At the call of vesper bell.

There they vowed to fight for Erin, And the priceless faith to guard- 

Ah! they kept it pure and spotless 

In that old churchyard.

Soon the tyrant's hand passed o'er it- 

Henry's bloodhounds mad with ire

Desecrated shrines and altars, Set the holy church on fire. 

There the dust of saints and sages 

Rests beneath the verdant sward- 

Hallowed with the blood of martyrs— 

In that old churchyard.

Tower of Erin's ancient glory,

Well may Oss'ry's sons today 

Look with pride upon thy ruins- 

Crumbling, falling to decay!

Death and exile, fire and famine- Such, the Saxon laws' award 

To thy children faithful ever

In that old churchyard. 

Ah! my weary spirit wanders 

To the graves by kind hands blest,

Where in springtime grows the primrose 

Fertilized by those loved best. 

Hallowed spot, I long to slumber 

With the loved one's 'neath the sward,

Where the daisies deck the green sod In that old churchyard.
