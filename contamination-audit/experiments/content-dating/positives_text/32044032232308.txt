Washington Lead and Zinc 

(September 1960) 1/ After investigation and report to the President under section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended, the President, effective October 1, 1958, modified the concessions on unmanufactured lead and zinc granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and imposed absolute quotas on imports thereof (Proclamation No. 3257 of 

Sept. 22, 1958; 3 CFR, 1958 Supp., p. 39).

The annual quotas are equal to 80 percent of the average annual commercial imports during the 5-year period 1953-57. Import duties were not changed. The quota is subdivided by calendar quarters, by tariff schedule classifications, and by principal countries exporting to the United States. Table 1 in the appendix shows the rates of duty for unmanufactured lead and zinc articles originally provided under the Tariff Act of 1930 and the reduced rates now in effect as a result of tariff concessions granted by the United States. Table 2 shows the quarterly quotas that were imposed and the actual quantities of unmanufactured lead and zinc subject to quotas that were imported in each calendar quarter beginning on October 1, 1958.

Mine production of lead in foreign countries increased in every postwar year except 1959 (when U.S. imports were restricted by import quotas), but the output in 1959 was only 1 percent below that in 1958, which was the highest on record. Whereas mine production of lead in the

Employment and hourly wage payments in U.S. lead and zinc mining

Employment in the lead and zinc mining industry in the United States after 1957 declined even more sharply than did mine output. The total number of employees dropped from an average of 15,900 during January- September 1957, the last period for which data were available at the time of the Commission's escape-clause report, to 10,800 in the first 9 months of 1958, and to 9,800 during the corresponding period in 1959, the last period for which such data were compiled by the Tariff Commission (table 7). According to data compiled by the U.S. Bureau of Labor 

Statistics, the average number of employees at lead and zinc mines in the first half of 1960 was about 3 percent less than the average number in the first 6 months of 1959.

Average hourly wages paid per man-hour actually worked by production and related workers at lead and zinc mines, as reported to the Tariff Commission, increased from $2.27 in the first 9 months of 1957, to $2.33 in the first 9 months of 1958, and to $2.38 in the corresponding period 1/ of 1959.

New York-London market price differentials

The United States is by far the world's largest consumer of lead and zinc. It is also the world's largest importer of these metals. In the past 5 years (1955-59), a third of its total industrial consumption of lead and almost half of its consumption of zinc were supplied by imports. Under these circumstances, any increase in import restrictions tends to result in higher market prices for lead and zinc in the U.S. market

than in foreign markets. Average monthly market prices in the United States and those on the London Metal Exchange for the last several years are shown for lead in table 10 and for zinc in table 11.

The differential between the New York and the London prices necessary to equal the U.S. import duty plus the cost of transportation and insurance from London to New York City amounts to about 1.9 cents per pound for lead and 1.6 cents per pound for zinc.

Lead prices.--The New York-London price differential for lead averaged about 2.6 cents per pound in 1957, 3.0 cents in 1958, 3.3 cents in 1959, and 2.7 cents during the first 8 months of 1960, the last period for which data are available (table 10). 

The differential has exceeded 1.9 cents per pound in every month since the import quotas have been in effect. However, the differential was more than 1.9 cents in most months of 1957 and 1958--before the import quotas were imposed--indicating the influence of other factors. The average price differentials in the last months of 1959 were unusually large (4.2 cents per pound in September and October) and probably reflect the effect of the previously mentioned shutdown of U.S. lead smelters and refineries. Testimony before the Tariff Commission in January 1960 suggested that a differential of this magnitude, if it were to continue, might give rise to large imports of lead in manufactured forms not subject to import quotas. The differential of 4.2 cents was of short duration, however, and actual imports of lead in forms not subject to the import quotas remained small. During the first 8 months of 1960 the U.S. price of lead was probably influenced, among other factors, by the large

stocks of lead ores and concentrates which had been accumulated during the shutdown of smelters and refineries in the last several months of 1959. 

Zinc prices.--The New York-London price differential for zinc averaged 1.7 cents per pound in 1957, about 2.6 cents in 1958, about 1.7 cents in 1959, and 2.1 cents in the first 8 months of 1960.

Since import quotas went into effect, average New York prices of zinc have exceeded average London prices by more than 1.6 cents per pound in 16 of the 23 months for which price data are available. 

New York-London price differential was less than 1.6 cents in the last half of 1959, reflecting principally a more rapid increase in European market prices than in U.S. prices; the consumption of zinc in the 

United States was reduced as a result of the steel strike while consumption in Europe was at a high level. The average price differentials for zinc during 1958--before import quotas became effective--were generally higher than those during the subsequent period, despite the fact that industrial zinc consumption in the United States in the first three quarters of 1958 was low owing to the business recession. During the first 8 months of 1960, the high rate of zinc consumption in Europe in relation to the rate of zinc consumption in the United States probably kept the price differential from being greater. 

For zinc, as for lead, a large increase in the differential between United States and foreign market prices might give rise to the importation of zinc in a variety of forms not now subject to quota. However, the import quotas have resulted in little increase in the price differential, and imports of zinc in forms not subject to quota have remained small.

Consumption and U.S. Government acquisitions 

Part of the recent changes in the market prices of lead and zinc in the United States are attributable to changes in industrial consumption of these metals. The unusually low prices in 1958 coincided with unusually low industrial consumption of lead and zinc in that year. 

Industrial consumption of lead in all forms in the United States declined from 1,138,000 tons in 1957 to 986,000 tons in 1958. It amounted to 1,091,000 tons in 1959, but it was 3 percent smaller in the first 6 months of 1960 than in the corresponding period of 1959 (table 3). The changes since 1957 in industrial consumption have been accompanied by the decline and eventual cessation of acquisitions of lead by the U.S. Government, principally for stockpiling. These acquisitions of lead are estimated from trade sources to have amounted to 158,000 tons in 1957, 78,000 tons in 1958, and 44,000 tons in 1959, after which they ceased. 

Industrial consumption of zinc in all forms in the United States declined from 1,232,000 tons in 1957 to 1,142,000 in 1958 and then increased to 1,269,000 tons in 1959. However, consumption in the first 

6 months of 1960 was about 8 percent smaller than in the corresponding months of 1959 (table 4). The changes in industrial consumption of zinc after 1957, as in that of lead, were accompanied by the abrupt decline and eventual cessation of acquisitions by the U.S. Government. These acquisitions of zinc are estimated from trade sources to have amounted to 267,000 tons in 1957, 72,000 tons in 1958, and 34,000 tons in 1959, after which they ceased.

On the basis of partly estimated data, the consumption of primary lead metal outside the United States increased from 1,618,000 short tons

U.S. producers stocks

The quantity of refined pig lead and antimonial lead held by primary smelters and refineries in the United States on September 30, 1958, the day before import quotas went into effect, amounted to 171,000 tons. 

Inventories of such metal at the end of 1959 were 120,000 tons, and at the end of July 1960, the last date for which data are available, they were 135,000 tons. However, stocks of lead contained in ores and concentrates held by primary smelters and refineries have increased substantially. 

As a result, total stocks of lead (both in ores and in metal) held by these producers were 279,000 tons on September 30, 1958, 230,000 tons on December 31, 1959, and 280,000 tons on July 30, 1960--about the same quantity as on the day before the import quotas were imposed (table 12).

Total stocks of lead in ores and in metallic form combined have increased almost steadily since the end of August 1959. 

Much of the buildup of stocks of lead in ores and concentrates was an indirect result of the existence of the import quotas. During the last several months of 1959, when the lead smelters of the American

Smelting & Refining Co. were shut down because of labor disputes, that company continued to receive lead ores and concentrates both from domestic mines and from foreign sources so as to assure itself of continuing supplies of ores that were limited by import quotas. The smelting of such ores, estimated to contain about 66,000 tons of lead, was deferred

1. until after the strike was settled near the end of 1959. Not all of the ores were initially stocked at the smelters; some of the stocks were held at U.S. mines or at warehouses not at the smelters. 

Producers stocks of slab zinc in the United States declined from 238,000 tons on September 30, 1958, to 154,000 tons on December 31, 1959, and then increased to 203,000 tons on August 31, 1960, the last date for which data are available (table 13). Stocks of slab zinc rose substantially after the end of March 1960, when they amounted to 137,000 tons. Total stocks of zinc held by producers on June 30, 1960, including stocks of zinc in ores and other zinciferous materials as well as slab zinc, were almost equal, to such total stocks held on September 30, 1958. 

This is due primarily to the growth in stocks of zinc in ores and other zinciferous materials between the end of 1959 and the end of June 1960 (table 14).

As domestic zinc smelters adapted themselves to operation under the import quotas, a practice developed of accumulating inventories of ores and concentrates in bond before the beginning of each new quota period. 

Such accumulations, depending upon their size and the particular country quota considered, provided each owner with greater ability to obtain a share of each new quarterly quota at the beginning of each new quota period. The zinc content of foreign ores and concentrates held in bond by domestic smelters increased from about 20,000 tons on September 30, 1958, to 76,000 tons on September 30, 1959, and to about 119,000 tons on

Although these large inventories enabled their owners to bid more successfully for the limited amounts permitted entry under quotas, they involve substantial bonding and storage costs. Moreover, the owners are obliged to tie up capital and risk losses if the price of zinc should fall before they are permitted to withdraw and market the 2/ metal produced from the imported ores and concentrates.
