SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 

This committee, appointed by President Thomas A. Byrne in 1949, was charged with the responsibility of studying the problems surrounding the administration of the general property tax as it applies to household furnishings. 

The committee was further instructed to present its findings, together with appropriate recommendations, to the General Appraisal Committee for consideration. 

In its study, the committee had the initial problem of defining the term "household furnishings" because there are such great variations among the states both as to the terms used and as to the items included under those terms. term "household furnishings" is used here to designate all the items of furniture and appliances that go to make up the furnishings of any private household. 

It was found that in statutes, lists, and returns the items which fall logically within the meaning of this broad term are often split up into different Because this practice of multiple classification presents classifications.

The problems to both the taxpayer and the assessor, the committee recommends that, for valuation purposes, all household furnishings should be classed together and treated alike.

Investigation of the tax status of household furnishings revealed such property is by statute either fully or partially taxable in all but thirteen states, but that the law is unenforced for all practical purposes in many jurisdictions.

Consideration of the problems involved in administration of the tax brought to light the tremendous difficulties surrounding the discovery and valuation of

-2- household furnishings difficulties caused by:

(1) the necessity of invading 

homes and questioning owners, in order to discover and list the property; (2) the number and variety of furnishings, which almost defy any attempt to list and value them accurately; and (3) the question of the basis on which the value of such furnishings is to be determined. Moreover, the mobility of people and the difficulty of distraining household furnishings make the collection of taxes. levied on such property unusually difficult. The committee found that almost all of the taxes actually collected are on "secured property, while there is considerable delinquency among persons who neither own nor have a long term lease on real estate. 

Further findings of the committee were: (1) that attempts to enforce the tax--i e., to discover and value the property and to collect the revenue--involve expenditures of considerable time, money, and manpower and (2) that, even with large expenditures, equitable assessment is scarcely ever achieved and much of the revenue remains uncollected. 

With regard to revenue, study revealed that the total assessed value of all household furnishings constitutes a very small percentage of the total assessed value of all property subject to the general property tax, and that the total revenue collected from the tax on household furnishings constitutes an even smaller percentage of the total revenue collected from the general property tax From these facts it follows that eliminating household furnishings from the general property tax base would result in no significant change in property tax rates or in the revenue derived from the property tax. 

Moreover, if assessors were relieved of the responsibility of assessing this type of property, their offices would be in a better position to administer the tax on those properties which make up the major portion of the annual assessment rolls and which are really productive of revenue.

-3- It was also found by the committee that the legislative trend is definitely toward more and larger exemptions of household furnishings--not only for administrative reasons. but also because of the belief that a certain amount of such furnishings are essential for decent living and should, therefore be exempt.

The committee is, however, convinced that, regardless of what it may think of the justice or administrative pract cability of a tax law, the assessor has the obligation to administer it to the best of his ability as long as it remains on the statute books. In view of this conviction, the committee makes the following recommendations for improving the administration of the tax on household furnishings so long as, and in those jurisdictions where, they remain a part of the general property tax base (1) Partial exemptions of household furnishings should be granted, so that the assessor can ignore small holdings of such property. The time, money, and manpower saved by this means should be devoted to securing more equitable assessment of substantial holdings of furnishings and of other property subject to the general tax. (2) Schedules should be used for assigning values to household furnishings, so as to provide more uniform assessment and assessment at an established value.

Scope of Study 

In assuming its responsibilities, the committee realized that it should, properly, extend its study to embrace a consideration of the problems relating to the assessment of household furnishings in each of the states where such property is still taxed. The limitations of time and personnel precluded such a comprehensive study, however desirable. Nevertheless, it is the committee's considered opinion that, although the laws might vary from state to state, in general the problems encountered in the administration of the tax on household furnishings are much the same everywhere.

A Definition Of Household Furnishings 

In this study, the term "household furnishings" is used in its broadest sense, to include all the items of furniture and appliances that go to make up the furnishings of any private household. This includes all household goods that are of a permanent nature and are usually held and enjoyed with a home. 

The term is not employed to designate items of a strictly personal nature, such as jewelry, clothing, and the like.

11 It should be noted that the term "household furnishings" has no precise meaning throughout the country, nor is this particular term found in the laws of all the various states. Often the statutes refer to "household goods, "household property, "household furniture and effects," and such, but mean the same items of property which are here called "household furnishings. 11 

There is, moreover, virtually no agreement among the laws of the several states as to the items to be included under the category "household furnishings" for assessment purposes. In many instances, the law refers to family portraits, libraries, and musical instruments as distinct from household furnishings, while in other states, jewelry and wearing apparel will be included under household furnishings.

Frequently the law refers to "household furniture," and, by implication, this appears to be a less inclusive category than "household furnishings. example, in the laws of several states, a limited exemption is prescribed for "household furniture" and another for private libraries, or family portraits, or musical instruments, all of the latter presumably being household furnishings but not household furniture. On the other hand, one section of the Wyoming tax law mentions "household furniture..., including gold and silver plate, musical instruments, watches and jewelry"--thus suggesting a concept of household furniture that is somewhat broader than the concept of household furnishings in most other states and in this report.

The practical effects of splitting up into different classifications the items which fall under the broad term "household furnishings" can be seen from a glance at those states where such different classifications are made and where separate assessed values are required for each. For instance, in Iowa, it is required that the taxpayer list and declare the value of his household furniture, on which he is allowed an exemption of $300. Since there are separate and complete exemptions granted to family pictures, kitchen furniture,

and beds and bedding requisite for the family, the taxpayer must not include them or their value in his return. He must declare the full value of his Since musical instruments and the value of his library in excess of $300. Such differentiation often seems unreasonable and confusing to the taxpayer. the tax assessor has no legal right to access to the home--and does not find assessment by personal inspection very practical in any event--there seems little sense in making him split up into different categories property which he is not permitted to view or does not view. 

It is, therefore, the opinion of this committee that for valuation purposes--i.e., in statutes, lists, and returns relating to their taxation, assessment, or exemption--all of these items should be classed together under the one broad term "household furnishings".

Household furnishings are fully taxable in only ten states. In each of these, except North Dakota, a constitutional amendment would be required before such property could be exempted. Some thirteen states completely exempt this class of property from taxation, while the other twenty-five states grant some kind of exemption--in one amount or another--which applies more or less directly to such furnishings.

In two states--New York and Delaware--there is no taxation of personal property. In three states--Pennsylvania, Ohio, and New Hampshire--taxable (rather than exempt) property is enumerated in the tax law. 

Pennsylvania does not include any tangible personal property in its enumeration, and neither New Hampshire nor Ohio includes household furnishings in its enumeration of taxable property.

The other states have general property taxes which extend to all real and personal property except that which is specifically exempt by constitutional or statutory provision. Household furnishings fall within the category of specifically exempt property in eight states: Connecticut, Louisiana, Maryland, Mississippi, Oregon, South Carolina, Washington, and Wisconsin. (In Maryland, household furnishings and effects for family use are fully exempt, but county and municipal taxes may be levied on such property in excess of $100 in value. 

According to recent reports, six counties in that state allow only this $100 exemption; one county grants a $500 exemption; and sixteen counties and the city of Baltimore completely exempt such goods.) 

Éight states provide for an exemption of a certain number of dollars' worth of property which may be applied to household furnishings and to no other class of property. Most of these exemptions are phrased broadly, the exemptions being given to "household goods," or "household furniture and effects," or "household property," without reference to particulars. Texas makes the specific statement that within the $250 worth of household and kitchen furniture which it exempts "may be included one sewing machine." Maine exempts "household furniture, excluding radios, "to the value of $500. 

According to the broadest use of the term "household furnishings" Iowa's exemption is somewhat more than the $300 for "household furniture" indicated on

the map, it provides further exemptions for all family pictures, all kitchen furniture, and all beds and bedding requisite for the family--and it has another exemption for private and professional libraries to the taxable value of $300. 

Vermont's exemption can also be considered to be larger than the $3000 indicated (which applies, according to the statute, to "household furniture and equipment"); 

Vermont law fully exempts all private and professional libraries. 

In another group of eleven states, there are exemptions of a certain number of dollars worth of property applicable to household furnishings and/or other specified classes of personalty--usually personal effects, household provisions, domestic animals, mechanics' or farming tools, or some combination of these classes. Some of these states, however, also have broader household furnishings exemptions than would appear at first glance, because, like the states mentioned in the preceding group, they have separate exemptions for items which would be included under the term "household furnishings" as defined in this report.

Oklahoma has a separate exemption of all "family portraits," while Michigan exempts not only "household furniture, provisions and fuel to the value of $5000," but also "the library family pictures, schoolbooks, (and) one sewing machine." 

In addition to its $300 exemption of "the household furniture and family stores of a housekeeper in the whole, including beds and bedding,' "Rhode Island exempts "the bibles, school books, and other books in use in the family, not exceeding in value the sum of $300."

Of all the states in this category, Alabama has the broadest exemption policy with regard to those items which can be classified under "household furnishings. Besides its $500 exemption which applies to "household and kitchen furniture" and/or specified livestock, Alabama exempts all family portraits, one sewing machine, and "the following items of personal property when owned by · individuals for personal use in the home or usually kept at the home of the

owner....libraries; phonographs, pianos, and other musical instruments; paintings; ....; ornaments and articles of taste; watches and clocks;.....; radios; mechanical and electrical refrigerators."

Attention should be called to Idaho's "vanishing" exemption, under which "household goods and furniture, sewing machines, musical instruments, wearing apparel, private libraries and jewelry in actual use in the private home or on the person of the owner, (are exempt) to the amount of $400 of full cash value when the full cash value thereof does not exceed $1000." Owners having over $1000 in such property are legally taxable on the full value of their holdings. 

Finally, there are six states where an exemption of a certain number of dollars' worth of property may be applied to household furnishings and/or any other class of personal property (or even, in the case of New Mexico, real property). Of these six states, only Kansas has an additional exemption which applies specifically to property included under the definition of "household furnishings"; in Kansas, "family libraries and schoolbooks of every person and family not exceeding in value...$50" are exempt. 

Rate of assessment.

With regard to the rate of assessment of household furnishings, the laws of most states make only the general provision that this property, like most other tangible personalty, shall be assessed at its "true," or "actual, or "full" cash value, or at some such standard.

Non-enforcement of the law. One very important point with regard to the taxable status of household furnishings is this: in many of the jurisdictions in which such property is fully taxable or only partially exempt, assessors have adopted the policy of complete non-enforcement of the tax. This is especially true of assessors whose jurisdictions contain a great deal of commercial, industrial, or public utility personal property, the taxation of which far outweighs the importance of attempting to tax household property.

This non-enforcement, and also the prevalence of at-least-partial exemptions for such furnishings, are largely due to the difficulties encountered in adminis tration of the tax, the costly and time-consuming character of its administration, and the proportionately small amount of revenue to be derived from taxation of this sort of property.

Problems of Administration 

There are three major steps in completing the assessment of any property; (1) Discovery, (2) Valuation or Pricing, and (3) Spreading the assessment on the tax rolls. The third step need not be discussed in this report, as the assessment of household furnishings presents no special problems in this regard. To determine the feasibility of the tax on household furnishings, however, it is necessary to discuss the possibility of discovering, listing, and valuing all the items which fall into this category of property, and to consider as well the probability of collecting taxes levied on such property. Therefore, these two subjects have been given special study by the committee. 

Discovery and Valuation 

To the inexperienced, the discovery and valuation of household furnishings might appear to be very simple administrative tasks. It is reasonable to assume that every home that is occupied must have some furnishings. 

It seems to follow logically that the assessor has only to inspect these homes and at the same time list and establish a fair value for the furnishings. 

There are, however, really great difficulties involved in "simply" inspecting homes, discovering furnishings, and ascertaining their value. Possibly the most important problem is presented by the household furnishings themselves. It is well-known that there are many different categories of such property--e.g., furniture, appliances, musical instruments, books, works of art and other decorations--and that, even in the most scantily furnished home, there are a large num ber of items which fall into these categories. A complete inventory of the

average home would necessarily include countless numbers and a tremendous diversity of individual items embracing a wide range of prices, of varied qualities, styles, and ages, and in various stages of repair. The characteristics of household furnishings make such property utterly incapable of classification for quick listing and appraisal.

A large and specially trained staff would be necessary for identifying and listing, to say nothing of pricing, all the heterogeneous property of this type in any assessing jurisdiction, especially if the work is to be done at the frequent intervals required by good assessment practice. Apart from the immensity of his undertaking, the number of men and of manhours necessary for listing and valuing household furnishings is clearly out of all proportion to the small amount of revenue derived from the taxation of such property.1 This time and manpower might be used to far greater advantage to secure more equitable assessment of those other types of property which make up really substantial parts of the tax base.

It is commonly accepted that where it is feasible to actually view the property being appraised, this should be done. 

Household furnishings should not be an exception. It cannot be assumed that two dwellings in the same immediate neighborhood--even if they are identical in all physical respects--actually have equal amounts of furnishings or furnishings of equal value. There is no standardization or uniformity in the furnishings of homes. It is a fact, therefore, that invasion of the home is essential for the fair and equitable assessment of household furnishings. (Of course, even then some portion--and not necessarily the least valuable portion--of household goods may escape assessment, for many items of this type can easily be concealed.)

Important as invasion is, however, in no state is the assessor given any 

See pages 19-20 of this report.

legal authorization to enter homes--and it is questionable whether he will ever get such authority as it might be considered a violation of both the state and the federal constitutions. Although, on request, the owner usually grants the assessor permission to enter his home, he does so very reluctantly. The American householder is jealous of the privacy of his home; he resents--and properly so-the intrusion of any governmental agent to search his home, room by room. He likewise resents the necessary inquiries about the nature, value, age, and ownership of items which he considers of a strictly personal nature. Such invasion is, of course, distasteful to the assessor--and is among those aspects of administration which have been most responsible for non-enforcement of the tax laws on this type of property. 

Without permission to enter homes, the assessor is forced to rely on findings secured in other ways. Recognizing this fact, most states have provided the assessor with alternative methods whereby an assessment can be effected. 

As a rule, the filing of a return by the owner of such property, showing the quantity and value of his holdings, is still a statutory requirement in states which tax household furnishings. In countless jurisdictions, however, this rule is honored more in the breach than in the observance. When a return is filed, no matter how scanty the furnishings are represented as being, the absence of inquisitorial powers and of a staff to exercise them practically forces the assessor to accept the owner's statement. 

Some states provide that, in the event a taxpayer fails to divulge the necessary information concerning his property, the assessor is to make a "doomage" assessment--i.e., an assessment in accordance with his best judgment on the basis of the best information he has at his disposal--and to add to this assessment a penalty for failure to comply with the law. When the assessor makes a doomage assessment, it becomes the duty of the taxpayer to present evidence

showing that this assessment is not correct. In attempting to do this, the taxpayer must reveal the quantity and value of his property. Still further authority is sometimes granted the assessor to subpoena a taxpayer or his agent with all his records, and, at this time, the taxpayer is obliged to disclose under oath such information as the assessor may need to effect an assessment. 

While some revenue may be derived from taxes levied on the basis of these alternative assessment methods, the fact remains that assessment by viewing the property affords the only sure way of assessing household furnishings. Then, too, the use of these methods--and the need for inspection--may well cost more administrative expenses than the revenue warrants. 1 In appraising household furnishings, the statutory measure of value must be kept uppermost in the mind. As has been pointed out, the statutes generally require the assessment of such property at true, actual or full cash value. As soon as household furnishings are purchased, the true or actual value represented in the purchase price most generally begins to diminish. The furnishings.do, however, retain a certain value--possibly their "intrinsic" value; possibly their "use" value--which is quite different from the price which they might bring at sale. By what method and on what basis is the assessor to make his valuation of household furnishings?

Secondhand furniture sales. There are a limited number of sales of household furnishings in the secondhand market, which the assessor might study, but they would be of little use in establishing the true value of such property. 

Because of the usual circumstances surrounding such sales, a large percentage of them must be regarded as forced sales and, therefore, not true measure of actual value.

Auction sales. Although the proceeds of an auction sale are generally 

¹See page 9 of this report.

higher than those of secondhand sales--because competitive interest may bid up certain items, these sales cannot be used by the assessor as conclusive evidence of value either. The very competition which frequently make the proceeds higher, also make auction sales an unreliable index to the actual value of the furnishings.

Insured value. Although some taxpayers are willing to disclose the amount of fire or theft insurance carried on their household furnishings, it must be realized that such insurance is usually and purposely carried at an inflated valuation to assure the owner a good settlement in the event of a loss. The insured value should, therefore, probably be discounted heavily before it is used as a basis of value for assessment purposes. 

Original cost less depreciation.

There are two major objections to the use of this method for determining the value of household furnishings; the difficulty in ascertaining the original cost, and the element of depreciation. If, as is usually the case, the owners of household furnishings are unable to recall the purchase price of numerous items in their possession, it certainly seems doubtful that an assessor can arrive at such a figure. Even when the original cost is available, however, there remains the necessity of applying a certain factor of depreciation, either observed or derived from some schedule. Deter- mining the percentage of depreciation is an extremely complex operation, since depreciation varies from item to item in proportion to its age, the type of use to which it is put, the amount of use, the repair or maintenance, etc. For these reasons, the original cost less depreciation method is highly impractical for valuing household furnishings, and its result is of questionable value for assessment purposes.

Replacement cost less depreciation. Although this method is used most successfully in measuring the true and actual value of real estate--as well as of some few items of tangible personalty, it cannot have practical application in

assessing household furnishings. To use this method, it would be necessary for the assessor to establish uniform replacement cost schedules on hundreds of items from which to price the inventory of furnishings in each dwelling. The very nature of the furnishings themselves, their number and diversity, make such a schedule almost impossible to make up. Moreover, vast quantities of household furnishings in American homes today cannot be replaced, while the very value of many other items is actually predicated on the fact that they cannot be replaced, i.e., antique furnishings. Then, too, there is the additional problem--much the same as was described above--of making reasonable allowances for depreciation. 

These difficulties rule out the use of this method for assessing household furnishings.

From the preceding discussion of methods and bases by means of which the assessor might value household furnishings, it appears that there is no satisfactory way to determine the actual cash value of such property even on view. 

It is for this reason that taxpayers' declarations and uniform pricing schedules are so widely accepted as evidences of true value in assessing household furnishings. 1 Taxpayers' declarations. As was stated above, most states which still tax household furnishings require each taxpayer to file with the assessor a declaration setting forth the value of his holdings. Since such returns are generally filed by only a minority of taxpayers, the assessor has to find another means of assessment in the majority of cases and the expenditures made to service declarations for this majority prove to be wasted. Moreover, there is, understandably, considerable confusion in the mind of the taxpayer as to the basis on which he should make his declaration. 

Some owners declare the minimum value that they feel will comply with the law; more conscientious taxpayers 

¹See page 12 of this report.

declare maximum values based on purchase price, replacement cost, or insured value. None of these figures necessarily represents the true and actual value required by the statutes.

Taxpayers' declarations are generally accepted by the assessor, because the task of assessing the property himself is almost impossible to perform well. 

But the acceptance of these figures amounts to self-assessment and necessarily results in many inequalities. It puts a premium on dishonesty and a penalty on honesty. The honest taxpayer is further penalized in those jurisdictions where the assessor is allowed to raise a taxpayer's declaration which he regards as low, but is forbidden to reduce the declaration which he regards as too high. 

Restrictions of this sort are highly unfair and incapable of producing equality of assessment.

Although such schedules have been employed with apparent satisfaction to the majority of taxpayers and, to the belief of the assessors, in reasonable compliance with the law, their use is a far cry from an ideal method of valuation.

The use of either of the first two schedules, for example, presupposes that there is a definite direct relationship between the assessed value of the dwelling and the value of the furnishings. It also presupposes that two dwellings of the same assessed valuation have furnishings of equal value. Both of these assumptions are clearly erroneous. It is highly speculative to argue that one house with an assessed value of $10,000 has household furnishings valued at $1000 and another at $15,000 has $1500 worth of furnishings. The reverse relationship might, in fact, be true. And the assessor's concern is with the furnishings that are actually in a house, not with the furnishings which are suitable for a house. 

Again, it is quite possible that one of two dwellings both of which are assessed at $10,000 might be furnished with valuable antiques and the second might well contain low cost modern furniture in a poor state of repair. A $1000 assessment of the furnishings in each instance, as called for under this type of schedule, is far from equitable and certainly does not comply with the intent of the law. Much the same criticism can be made of the use of the other typical schedules.

From this discussion of methods and bases by which the assessor tries to determine the value of household furnishings, it is clear that there is none which can produce assessment at true value or even equitable assessment. It

- should be emphasized that the cause of this condition lies mainly in the nature of the property itself and its complete inability to be classified and in the total absence of standardization in the furnishing of homes. 

So long as household furnishings remain taxable, however, the assessor is obliged to make a valuation of them. Under the circumstances, he is practically forced to allow self-assessment or to adopt some more or less arbitrary rule of thumb. In terms of good assessment practice, neither of these alternatives is really to be endorsed. In a subsequent section of this report, the committee deals with improving the administration of the tax in those areas which do not exempt household furnishings and until such furnishings are exempted. There, the use of schedules is recommended to assessors, but only as the least of the unsatisfactory methods of assessment. The point made here is that there is not any completely satisfactory means for effecting the evaluation of household furnishings; this is a cardinal point in the argument for complete exemption of such furnishings.

Collection of the Tax The probability of collecting a particular tax is an important element in tax policy-making, for a tax that is in large part uncollectible can hardly be said to be feasible. And the collectibility of a tax also influences the administration of it. Although the collection of taxes is not the business of the assessor as such, the probability of collecting taxes levied on the basis of his assessment certainly does affect the spirit in which he approaches the task of assessment.

The difficulties presented by the taxation of household furnishings do not stop with the completion of the assessment function, for there are further problems in collection of the tax. These additional problems are sufficiently great not only to make the feasibility of the tax highly questionable, but also to demoralize the assessor (and other tax officials) who face such great diffi-

culties in performing their own functions with regard to household furnishings. 

In some states, the tax on household furnishings is virtually uncollectible because such property is, by statute or by custom, exempt from attachment or distraint.

It is, nevertheless, true that a considerable part of the levy against household furnishings is collected year after year, but almost all these collections are of "secured" taxes--that is, taxes levied against persons who are anchored to a definite spot by the ownership, or occupancy on long-term lease, of real estate. There is a great deal of delinquency in the payment of such taxes not only when they are "unsecured," but also when they are levied against persons in the lower income brackets. majority of household furnishing tax accounts.

Tax Revenue 

An analysis of the assessment rolls of several tax jurisdictions discloses that the assessed valuation of household furnishings represent an extremely small percentage of all property subject to the general property tax. A particular study of the 1950 assessment roll for the entire state of Connecticut indicates that the assessed valuation of this class of property for the 169 cities and towns was

substantially less than 1% of the total. 

The committee is confident that this ratio would not be materially greater in those other states in which household furnishings are still taxable. The administrative difficulties encountered in assessing this type of property, together with the non-enforcement of the tax which results from them, account for this relationship. 

Not only is the total tax levy on household furnishings a minute fraction of the total property tax levy, the total revenue collected from the tax on such property is an even more minute fraction of the total revenue collected from the general property tax. The special collection problems which this tax presents, and which have already been outlined, provide an explanation for this fact.

It is, therefore, axiomatic that eliminating household furnishings from the tax base would result in no significant change in property tax rates or in revenue derived from the general property tax. 

The fact that the tax on household furnishings does bring in some revenue explains the continued existence of this tax, but it is not a justification for the tax. This committee is convinced that the amount of revenue now derived from the household furnishings tax is grossly disproportionate to the time and effort involved in its administration and that the revenue available from property of this type is too small to permit the sort of administration which would assure a fairly equitable distribution of the tax load over the owners of such furnishings.

It is significant that the demand for exemption of household furnishings from property taxation is ever growing, and that the number and size of such exemptions are on the increase. Although there have been a few cases in which exemptions have been granted and subsequently withdrawn, these have been exceptional and have not obscured the tendency for exemptions of this type to increase. It should be noted that there have been no withdrawals of such exemptions within the past decade. All changes in the taxable status of such property within that time period have been extensions of exemption. 

In 1945, Maryland dropped its $500 exemption for household furnishings in favor of complete exemption for such property, though it retained the provision that county and municipal taxes could be levied on such property in excess of $100. The number of counties and municipalities taking advantage of this provision is constantly on the decrease. Prior to the 1947 ratification of a constitutional amendment, South Carolina had only a $100 exemption which could be taken on household furnishings, or on other specified types of personalty, now, that state grants complete exemption to all household goods and furniture.

Alabama increased its exemption from $250 to $500; Massachusetts, from $1000 to $5000; Michigan, from $1000 to $5000; and Vermont from $500 to $3000. In view of the findings of this study with regard to problems in administration of the tax on household furnishings and with regard to the amount of revenue derived from this tax, the logic behind the movement to exempt such property is readily apparent. And there are additional arguments for exemption. Some of the partial exemptions, for instance, have been granted on the theory that a certain limited amount of furnishings are essential to maintenance of a decent standard of living and that, therefore, at least this amount of furnishings should be exempt. Another reason for granting such exemptions has been that ownership of such property, within reasonable limits, is not necessarily a measure either of ability to pay or of benefits received from governmental services. A number of exemptions accorded for one or both of these reasons are, however, of many years standing and consequently their limits are often no longer realistic. Our entire economic system has changed so completely in recent years that whereas $500 might have purchased a respectable amount of household furnishings some years ago, today the same sum would often fail to provide furnishings for one room. In view of this, it would seem that, if household furnishings are not made completely exempt, at least exemptions granted on these bases should be materially increased. 

This committee is convinced, however, that household furnishings should be made completely exempt from property taxation. This study has brought to light the following facts: that the difficulties and the administrative expenditures involved in discovery and valuation of this type of property and in collection of taxes levied on it make taxation of household furnishings infeasible; that it is difficult, if not impossible, to enforce this tax equitably; that the amount of revenue derived from taxation of such furnishings is not only small in itself.

On the basis of these findings, the committee recommends that complete statutory exemption be provided for household furnishings. The committee believes that elimination of the tax on such property will redound to the better administration of those elements of the property tax which are most important and most productive of revenue, for the savings in time, money, and manpower which will accrue from the exemption of household furnishings can then be used to good advantage in securing more equitable assessment of those other properties. 

While it is true that the retention of a tax on household furnishings reduces the tax burden on real estate, it must be remembered that most of the taxes actually collected on holdings of such furnishings are paid by persons who have substantial equities in real estate. As a group, therefore, real estate owners provide their own tax relief by paying a tax on personalty which is almost equivalent to what they save in real estate taxes. 

It can be argued, too, that any increases in taxes on real estate, merchants' stock, etc., which may be necessary to compensate for the loss of revenue through exemption of furnishings, will be passed on in the form of higher rents and higher prices which those relieved of taxes on household furnishings will have to pay. While this type of shift is not a very precise process, neither is the assessment of household furnishings, precise. Ordinarily in most states the increased burden in taxes would be so small as to be negligible; but if in some states, the matter assumed some importance, certain other forms of taxation, such as a sales or income tax could be used to absorb this part of the burden that would otherwise be borne by real estate. 

The availability of these forms of compensation is mentioned by the committee as an additional argument in support of the contention that the exemption of household furnishings is necessary and practical.

Improving Administration 

The preceding sections of this report have outlined: the present tax status of household furnishings; the administrative difficulties encountered in attempting to enforce the tax on such property; the relatively small amount of revenue which can be derived from taxation of these furnishings; and the grounds on which this committee recommends the complete elimination of the tax on household furnishings.

Until such time as the tax on household furnishings is repealed, however, it is the duty of assessors to enforce the tax to the best of their ability. 

This committee affirms its conviction that so long as these furnishings are part of the tax base, the assessor must make every effort to see that they bear their just share of the tax burden. 

In view of this conviction, the present section of this report contains definite recommendations for improving the administration of this tax in those jurisdictions where it is still levied. 

Most people who have given much thought to the problem of administering a tax on household furnishings believe that administration would be improved by (1) providing partial exemptions so that the assessor could ignore all but substantial holdings of such property; and (2) by using a schedule for assigning values to such remaining property. 

Partial exemption. The major arguments for at least partial exemption of household furnishings have already been set forth. So far as the improvement of administration of the tax on such property is concerned, however, this point should be highlighted; even partial exemption of these furnishings substantially reduces the expenditure of administrative time, effort, and money in listing, appraising, recordkeeping, billing and collection. The higher the exemption, the more obvious it becomes--especially to one accustomed to assess "on view"--that some people have no taxable property of this class and that

their homes need not be processed in any way for purposes of taxing such property. 

Thus, the assessors can devote more time, money and manpower to administering the tax on more substantial holdings of such furnishings in such a way as to secure as equitable a distribution as possible over the owners of these holdings. 

As a result of partial exemption, then, assessment costs will decrease not only absolutely, but also in comparison with the amount of revenue derived from each account. The tax yield per account will rise materially while the administrative cost per dollar of tax yield will be substantially decreased. 1 Schedules. As was pointed out above, the personal inspection and individual valuation method of assessing household furnishings--ideal as it may be in theory--proves both expensive and unsatisfactory in practice. The acceptance of taxpayers' returns, even in that minority of cases in which they are filed, amounts to self-assessment and also puts a premium on dishonesty. 

In an effort to overcome these difficulties, many assessors have adopted the technique of making a tentative assessment on the basis of a schedule, and then giving the taxpayer an opportunity to appeal from this assessment by filing an itemized return which may be followed up by a personal recheck. 

Most of these schedules are based on a theoretical relationship between the value of a house (or the rental of an apartment) and the value of the furnishings therein. The committee does not accept the theory that there is a definite direct relationship between these two values. It does recommend the use of schedules, however, for the following reasons: (1) the value of the house will almost invariably affect the assessor's judgment of the value of its furnishings, even when he is assessing on view; (2) the use of a schedule eliminates guess-work, thus promoting uniformity; (3) a schedule provides for assessment at an established value; (4) the use of a schedule may help to 1

- reduce the number of dissatisfied taxpayers; (5) the application of a schedule reduces time, money, and effort spent in assessing household furnishings. 

While the schedule is by no means a good assessment practice in this field, it offers the only hope of achieving any degree of uniformity and it is far better practice than self-assessment.

It is clear that few, if any, assessors are expert appraisers of each of the several types of property which fall under the category of household furnishings. If left to his own resources, almost every assessor would find himself forced to guess at the value of such furnishings in each home. And his guess would be influenced, in almost every case, by his judgment of the value of the house in which the furnishings were located. Because of the haphazard character of guesswork, it is almost inevitable that different assessors would assign different values to the same furnishings. It is highly improbable, therefore, that the assessment of household furnishings in a jurisdiction would be effected, if done on view, either at the statutory standard of value or with any degree of uniformity.

While it does not assure assessment at the statutory standard of value, schedule does provide for assessment at an established value. And while the use of a schedule does not assure uniformity in assessment, it does go far toward eliminating both guesswork and differences of opinion among assessors, which are major obstacles to uniformity. 

The use of a schedule has another advantage over the exercise of judgment by each individual assessor; the assessor can show the property owner not only the basis on which he arrived at the value he assigned to the holdings, but also that the property has been valued on exactly the same basis as other property in the jurisdiction. This procedure reduces substantially the number of dissatisfied owners and, consequently, the number of complaints. The administra tive work and expense required for handling complaints is likewise appreciably decreased.
