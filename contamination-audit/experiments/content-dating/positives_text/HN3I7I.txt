The north wind raced and tore along the coast-line and across the tundra like a mad thing on a rampage, driving and drifting the frozen snow before it. All of Nature's agencies seemed launched on a bitter crusade to annihilate every living creature from that part of the earth.

The barrabaras, or earth-covered dwellings, of the little Eskimo settlement presented a bleak and desolate appearance. There was not a sign to indicate that families of human beings were 3

huddled in the cave-like interiors beneath the round tops. 

Here and there was a dog-sled almost buried. in the snow. The dogs themselves, curled up beside the stakes to which they were chained, were drifted over like so many little hummocks of tundra moss. Not a single thing seemed brave enough to stand up and defy the storm.

The past autumn had been the poorest hunting season in many years. Few of the old hunters could remember when caribou had been so scarce on the tundra. The seals, generally to be found in the bay at any and all times, had been as difficult to find as black foxes during the trapping season. Even the salmon, usually swarming in hordes in all the streams throughout the summer, had seemingly forgotten to come in from the sea. Some of the families in the village had hardly been able to catch enough to feed themselves from day to day, and no one had put by enough supplies to last throughout the winter. Everything upon which the natives depended for food had apparently been swallowed up by some evil spirit. And now that the arctic winter was here, starvation faced the community.

In the largest of the barrabaras, which stood close to the beach, were gathered a number of the men-folk of the village. Nagooruk, the old chief, had been addressing them, and they were all very serious. "My people," the chief said solemnly, in conclusion, "it is a bad time. But we must face it bravely. You are all good hunters. I ask you to go out and get game. It is a request, but it is also a command. And may the gods of our ancestors be with every hunter."

With that, the men pulled the big fuzzy hoods of their parkas up over their heads and one by one went silently out through the low doorway and plodded thoughtfully through the blizzard to where their families were awaiting their return.

When they had all gone, the old Eskimo turned to where his young son had been sitting, in a far corner, listening to every word that was spoken. "Iglaome," he began, going over to lay a weathered hand upon the lad's jet-black head and gaze solemnly down into the frank brown eyes, "I fear you are soon to learn how unpleasant life sometimes can be."

Going to the sled which he was in the habit of using, he turned it over to dump off the snow; and then unearthing the harness, he shook it and beat it against his leg. The lines were like so many pieces of wood.

Iglaome had his own team of five dogs. They had been given him by his father when they were puppies, and he had raised them and trained them himself. Their mother was the old chief's favorite lead-dog and their father had been an untamed wolf; for it was a custom of the Eskimos to send their dogs out to mate with wolves, those malicious though hardiest creatures of the wilds.

One by one Iglaome had to drag his dogs snarling from the beds they had made for them-

selves under the drifted snow and fasten them into the frozen harness. The last to be hitched up was Azeeruk, the leader of the team and most prized of Iglaome's worldly possessions. This was a monster of a creature, with a big shaggy head nearly as broad as a half-grown grizzly's, short pointed ears that stood up so stiff and straight that they appeared to be made of bone or ivory, and a heavy, bristly coat almost as yellow-white as an old polar bear's. He was different in color from the rest, which were blotched black and gray. "Azeeruk" is the Eskimo word meaning "bad," and the huge dog was all that his name implied. From the time he was a wee ball of fluff, he had been a fighting demon. And with the strength and power implanted in his bulk and weight he was a menace to both man and other dogs. Iglaome, who had fed and trained him from earliest puppyhood, was his only master.

He knew that to get back to the village alive, in his present condition, was entirely out of the question; and he also knew that it would be almost impossible to build a fire of alders that would dry his fur clothing. 

He had a vague recollection, however, that there was the hull of a wrecked schooner on the beach not far from the mouth of Bear River. To reach this and set fire to it seemed his one and only chance.

Desperately he tried to regain his feet, but it seemed as if every muscle in his body were paralyzed.

It was an ordeal for him to travel, but travel he must-or die. At last they came to the beach.

It extended unbroken from the beach to the big volcano at Bear River Lake. To a trained eye a caribou would be visible, on a clear day, a long distance off. On the opposite side of the river, however, the country was more hilly; and it was in this direction that Iglaome gazed. 

Every little valley opening that they passed, he scrutinized. But nowhere did he see even a track in the snow.

In generations past, Iglaome's ancestors had prayed to this same burning mountain as to a god. Even to-day some of the older men regarded it with awe and reverence. Regularly, every season or so, some great power that lived down inside it would go on a rampage and blow the entire summit of the mountain high into the sky, covering the whole country for many miles around with a blanket of gray odorous dust. And regularly the earth trembled, as any human being discovered, who stayed for any length of time in the neighborhood. This mysterious mountain was also supposed to be the home of a great shaman grizzly bear which, when he traveled at night, shone with a light as bright as the north star. Iglaome's own father had told him of seeing this radiant bear, and the boy had long ago made up his mind to set out sometime to slay the phantom creature. 

But caribou meat for hungry mouths-and to make good a thoughtless boast—was his object now.

Here is the eagle quill from which the guiding feathers on the end were stripped. Here is some of the same thread used underneath the sinew wrapping. I found these in the work-bag of Pamassuk."

A buzzing murmur rose from the audience. "Where is Pamassuk? Bring him out!" shouted several.

There was an excited movement at one side of the crowd. All eyes were turned in that direction. Some one was making a mad rush to escape. "Pamassuk! Catch him!" a voice cried out. 

But away Pamassuk sped in the dark. A number of men raced after him. Every one became excited. Many people ran in the direction in which the youth had fled. It was like the smell of blood to a pack of wolves, scrambling

to tear to pieces one of their own kind who had gone down beneath their feet. Through the village they ran, then down toward the beach. 

Pamassuk fled frantically before them.

In a little while word was brought back that the culprit had attempted to cross the margin of broken-up ice along the beach, to the solid ice in the center of the bay and in his mad haste, in the darkness, had gone through into the freezing water some distance from the shore. 

The men were trying to get him out, but it was doubtful if he could be saved before he was so chilled that he could not hold on to the floating cakes of ice, and would sink beneath them.

All of the people hurried down to the beachexcepting the old chief and the wrinkled medicine-man. When they were left alone, old Nagooruk motioned to Meteglarook and they walked slowly toward the chief's big earthcovered lodge. The old chief walked erect, with face lifted up to the sky, and with a determined, dignified tread; and the medicine-man followed, almost reluctantly.

A tremendous effort was made to get Pamassuk out of the water of the bay. Iglaome even brought his small kayak and pushed it out over

the jagged ice. But this was very slow and difficult work, and before he could get to where Pamassuk had fallen through, the unfortunate young native, overcome by the cold, had sunk, and the ice had closed above him. 

Saddened, despite the momentary wrath which Pamassuk's misdeed had roused, the people returned to the dance-place. 

Meteglarook stood rigid and silent beside the glowing embers of the dying fire. His face was as stern and as immobile as though it were an ancient mask carved of wood or of whalebone. 

When the people were gathered about him, he spoke. What he said was brief, but they all knew the meaning. "Nagooruk, Chief of Igagik, has gone to join his fathers in the spirit world."

As daylight was fading in the southern sky, the caravan of dog-teams and fur-clad visitors began to leave. Gradually they strung out along the icy beach. The people of Igagik stood by and quietly though sincerely called friendly farewells as they left. There was sadness in every heart, yet in not a single one was there a feeling of enmity or hate.

"We grieve for your sorrows; but long live your new chief!" was the sentiment expressed by the departing visitors. 

When the last of the sleds had gone, the people of Igagik returned to their own dwellings. The chained dogs tucked their noses under the long gray-and-black hair of their shaggy coats and went to sleep. Everything became quiet.

Iglaome came out and stood before the cavelike entrance to his father's barrabara. The cold gray dawn showed tears upon his cheeks. There was no one else in sight in the village. Slowly he walked out toward the cape at the entrance of the bay-to be alone with his thoughts. 

He stood on the high rocky point that looked off across the great frozen sea. His face was lifted up to the morning sky, though his eyes saw nothing before them. His lips occasionally moved, as he besought the guidance and help of the Great Spirit. 

He had not stood there long when a girl clad in neat fur clothing, who had been following him from the village, came slowly and shyly up behind him. For a few moments Marmaseena stood and gazed sympathetically, as if half in-

clined to embrace him and half inclined to run away. Then, becoming aware of her presence,

Iglaome turned around. No words were spoken. 

But he smiled at her through his tears, and 

Marmaseena smiled back at him. There was a great deal expressed and understood in those smiles-sympathy, appreciation, and the asking and answering of questions that meant much to them both. 

Then Marmaseena brought out a bundle which she had been holding behind her. It was not a small bundle, though neatly covered with a new piece of calico. Shyly she unwrapped it, displaying a beautiful fancy parka made of tundra squirrel-skins, fringed with a wide band of patchwork. "It is my present for you," she whispered. "I made it all myself." 

Iglaome's face brightened as he gazed at the attractive garment-which was also a symbol of more than friendship. But before he took it, he pulled a tightly wrapped bundle out from under his own parka and unrolled two beautiful, big, black, silver-tipped fox skins, which he handed to her as he received her gift.

crunching snow and gazed in silence out across the sea. The pale sun crept up over the white rim of the earth far to the south, and smiled upon them. The ice out on the sea and in the bay thundered and boomed as the incoming tide broke it and ground it against the shore. Ribbons of gray smoke began to curl lazily into the air above the dome-like tops of the earth-covered dwellings in the village.

Iglaome at last rose to his feet. He wiped the tears from his eyes. Then straightening up to his full height, with all the dignity befitting a chieftain and a Nagooruk, he beckoned to the girl beside him, and they started back to the village together.
