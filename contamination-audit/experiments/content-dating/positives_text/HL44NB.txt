LETTER OF TRANSMITTAL 

To the Honorable James A. Rhodes, Governor; Martin A. 

Janis, Director of Mental Hygiene and Correction; and Members of the Legislature:

Complying with Section 5149.12 of the Ohio Revised Code, we submit herewith the Annual Report of the Adult Parole Authority for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1968. 

The report will show, we believe, that the Adult Parole Authority served the people of Ohio well by a prudent management of its allocated resources. As evidence we point to the fact that 9 out of 10 of our 4300 Ohio parolees work and earn on the average of $400 per month. Combined, they put over $23,000,000 into the economy annually. In addition, the penal population continues to decrease; our probation services continue to increase; and we now have a corps of well-trained, productive officers. 

The charts and graphs we have incorporated in this report serve as supplementary evidence. Although they require a degree of technical sophistication to interpret exhaustively, they do provide the general public with a comprehensive view of our efforts. 

In short, the achievements of the Adult Parole Authority are chronicled in this, our annual report, and we commend it to your attention. 

M. C. Koblentz, Chief

Introduction ADULT PAROLE AUTHORITY ANNUAL REPORT 

Ohio has approximately 10,300 inmates in its seven penal institutions. Eventually, about 98 percent of them will be released, almost all (94.6%) through parole. When they are released, they become the responsibility of the Adult Parole Authority. 

Parole has become a part of our system of criminal justice. 

By controlling an offender's behavior while he is in the community, the parole authority effectively protects the public while applying rehabilitative measures. 

Sometimes an offender is given a suspended sentence instead of being sent to prison. He is then said to be on probation and remains free under supervision with good behavior. The APA has a Probation Development Section and by its judicious use of probation it can avert the needless expense and stigma of incarceration in many cases.

Parole Supervision and Probation Development are two sections in the APA. There are two others in the form of the Parole 

Board and the Administration and Research Section. All four sections are administered by the Chief, George F. Denton.

The APA was created by a special act of the Legislature on March 18, 1965. In its three-year history it has innovated, experimented, changed and researched. By the studious application of the latest concepts in corrections, it has compiled an enviable record of achievement. Recorded in this report is an accounting of these achievements.

Board Conducts Parole Hearings 

Ohio has a seven-man Parole Board, one of whom serves as chairman. Regularly each month, the board makes the circuit of 

Ohio's seven penal institutions, conducting interviews of "parole eligible" cases. This year the board interviewed a total of 6,818 cases. Of these, 6,228 were parole hearings; 566 involved technical parole violators; and the rest were clemency cases.

Paroles granted by the board averaged 302 per month. 

Of the 6,228 cases considered by the board during the year, 3,628 were granted parole and 2,600 were denied parole. This means that 58.3 percent of all inmates eligible were granted parole.

The remaining 41.7 percent (2600) were denied parole and their cases were continued. (See Table I)

Jail Time Credit 

In some instances the Parole Authority may reduce the time the offender must spend in prison by granting jail time credit.

The jail time is that period of time the prisoner spent in jail between his conviction and his commitment. For example, he might have pleaded guilty in December, and for a number of reasons, been kept in a city or county jail until January, when he was formally admitted to begin his sentence. The APA is authorized to give credit for this time if a Judge so recommends. 

The APA had 267 such requests in 1968. Of these, 232 were granted in total, 18 were partially granted, and 17 were denied. 

Parole Supervision - A Complex Job 

The APA's Parole Supervision section maintains custody, control and supervision of paroled persons throughout the state. 

It performs this task with its corps of parole officers organized in units of 5 or 6 officers each. Each unit is headed by a unit supervisor who is accountable to a regional supervisor. 

During the year, two new units were established in Youngstown and Lima, making the total number of units 15. Altogether we now have 118 field parole officers, 15 unit supervisors and 3 regional supervisors. Combined, this corps of officers supervises close to 4,500 parolees at any given time. Caseloads vary but for this year they averaged 54.4 — 55.8 for male officers and 36.3 for women officers compared with caseloads of 66.3 and 51.2 respectively just two years ago.

In addition to supervision, officers perform investigations of various kinds. For example, nearly 6,000 parole plans were thoroughly examined. There were also 5,532 placement investigations and 7,489 miscellaneous investigations. 

Parole officers also keep careful records of their contacts with or about parolees. For instance, contacts with the parolee himself totaled 57,470 or an average of 4,789 per month. Contacts with others (e.g. family, employer, neighbors, etc.,) amounted to 93,408 or 7,784 per month. During the year Ohio parole officers supervised a total of 9734 offenders. Of these, 8484 were Ohio cases and 1250 were compact cases. (see table 2)

Ohio is signatory to the Interstate Compact. This means that 

Ohio agrees to supervise offenders from other states upon proper application. Ohio is accorded a similar reciprocal privilege. During the year Ohio sent 555 offenders out of state for supervision and received a similar number in return.

Return of Violators Provided

When a parolee absconds supervision or ends up in trouble in another state, the APA makes provisions for his return as a parole violator. In fiscal 1968, $24,667 was spent to return 143 such violators from other states, or an average cost of $172. The bulk of this expense (71 percent) was for mileage costs incurred by the officers when they picked up the violators by automobile. Other costs in this return process include food, lodging, and parking. 

Number of Inmates Paroled Each Year 

More than 8,000 were on parole sometime last year. (See

Table 2). During the year, 967 of these reverted to criminal behavior or violated parole and were returned to continue their sentences. This represents only 11.4 percent of the 8,484 on parole. 

The majority succeed.

Final Release-Restores Citizenship 

In Ohio, a convicted felon loses his citizenship -the right to vote, to serve on juries, and to hold public office. While he is on parole, these rights are still denied him. However, if his behavior on parole is satisfactory for at least one year (in most cases), he is awarded a "final release" certificate. This restores his rights and terminates his parole.

Last year in Ohio, the Adult Parole Authority granted 2,949 final releases. This figure represents 34.8 percent of the parole population. Overall, in a given group of freshly released inmates, more than three-fourths succeed on parole and regain their rights. 

Administrative Release Not Leniency 

From time to time a parolee will abscond supervision or be committed to an institution for another crime. If the absconded parolee is not heard from for ten years or more he may be given an administrative release. Similarly, if he is reconvicted, he may also be granted such a release. This is not leniency, however. 

Rather, it permits the APA to terminate jurisdiction over a case for reasons of administrative convenience. Such a release does not restore the civil rights of the parolee. Releases declined from a high of 1918 in 1965 to a low this past year of 572. To date, 3,475 such releases have been issued with an incalculable but large saving in needless administrative costs.

What Does Parole Cost? 

In 1967, the cost of keeping a man in prison for one year was $1,521. How does this compare with the cost of parole? One way of determining this would be to divide the average monthly parole caseload into the amount spent on parole supervision. Caseloads averaged 4,680 per month and the amount spent on parole supervision was $1,515,210. Thus, the average cost of keeping a man on parole is $324, a savings of over $1,000 per case compared with imprisonment. 

Probation supervision saves money, too. Calculations show that the cost of annual probation supervision in the APA is $462. 

When probation and parole combine caseloads and expenses in measuring cost, the average comes to $374. (See Table 3). 

How Much Do Offenders Earn?

Most parolees and probationers work! A survey by the APA shows that almost 9 out of 10 (88 percent) hold jobs that average $400 per month. Some earn as high as $12,000 per year. Combined, they contributed at least $23,000,000 to the economy during the year.

Parolees and probationers become taxpayers, too. In the period covered by this report, they paid an estimated $3,661,000 in income taxes alone. In addition, 27 percent of these offenders are supporting dependents who might otherwise be on the welfare rolls.

There is one other financial saving worthy of note. If these offenders under supervision were still imprisoned, the cost of the state for maintaining them would come to $7,442,000! This is on a yearly basis, using the average monthly caseload mentioned earlier. 

Probation Development Grows 

Probation service has been a county responsibility for over 40 years. In 1965, however, the State Legislature sought to improve probation services by creating a state Probation Development Section. Presently, the APA has 10 probation officers serving 16 counties.

In Ohio, 36 percent of persons convicted of felonies are sentenced to prison and 41 percent are placed on probation. 

While most probationers are supervised by the counties, some counties lack such services. Where they are lacking, the APA is empowered to set up a probation system if the county courts so request. By June of 1968, on request, the section set up probation 10

systems in 16 counties and staffed them with 10 highly trained professional officers. This is in contrast to last year when the section had just two officers in 6 counties. 

One of Probation Development's major contributions to the administration of criminal justice is compilation of a report known as Pre-sentence Investigation. This report, structured by a well-trained probation officer, gives vital information about an offender. It also helps select offenders for probation, serves as a guide for the judge, and is used by institution staffs, the Parole 

Board and parole officers.

What is "Shock Treatment" 

Is imprisonment so distasteful that only short terms are necessary in some cases? Ohio is finding out through a unique "shock treatment" law. This permits a court to release a man into the community after he has served at least 30 days of his felony sentence.

Table 4 reveals the extent of the use of this law this year. 

It shows that 258 felons were recalled from prison after a brief stay of about a month. Only 27 of this number had to be returned to the institution for misconduct or further criminal behavior, a failure rate of only 10.4 percent.

What Kind of Parolees Do Murderers Make? 

The APA has an Administration and Research Section. This section is responsible for maintaining records and files, publications of the APA and preparation of budget requests as well as research.

One of the studies conducted by the A. and R. Section involved released murderers. From 1945 to June of 1968, the State of Ohio released 307 first degree murderers on parole, 19 of them in the past year. All of them had served at least 20 years. To date, 166 of these men have been given a final release and been discharged from parole.

At the beginning of the year there were 84 murderers on parole. Of this group, only 5 violated parole. One committed a new crime and was resentenced; the other four were returned to prison.

Of all murderers released on parole in Ohio to June of 1968 (307), only 22 violated parole (7.2 percent). Three of these 22 committed a new crime. Thus, more than nine out of ten murderers made a successful adjustment while on parole. In fact, they do better than any other classified group of offender. 11

Parole Data by Institution 

There were 5,513 parolees in Ohio at the start of fiscal 1968. 

Table I gives a breakdown of the data from each of the seven institutions. It shows the cases received during the year, the returns and new sentences, and final releases. Of the cases on parole in fiscal 1968, 33.8 percent were given final releases, including compact cases.

Future Plans, Objectives of the APA 

National attention is focused on crime and crime problems. 

The Adult Parole Authority intends to meet it's responsibilities. 

There will be more parole officers, more clerical staff and more research. As expansion of service takes place, the feasibility of using automated and technical improvements will be explored. 

Innovation, too, will continue with such specialized services as the employment officer, the review officer, halfway houses, and special caseloads.

The Adult Parole Authority intends to continue and to intensify its program of staff development. In addition to our regular parole officer training school we expect to organize seminars, institutes, and special projects. Much of this in-service training will come about from federal grants that are now being structured. 

Conclusion 

This report of the Adult Parole Authority has high-lighted the major achievements of the past year. This is not an exhaustive summary, however, but a comprehensive survey. While we are proud of many of our accomplishments, we realize there is still much to be done. While we proceed with the task, we never lose sight of the objectives: protecting the public, eliminating criminal careers and making citizens of offenders. 12
