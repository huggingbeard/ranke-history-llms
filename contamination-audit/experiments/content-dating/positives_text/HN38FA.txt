ECONOMIC EFFECTS OF A DISMEMBERMENT OF GERMANY INTRODUCTION 

Among the ideas offered for the treatment of Germany after Hitler's final defeat proposals for a partition of the Reich have found considerable able attention, which has in part been due to the experience and standing of their authors. The underlying idea of such proposals is that influential German groups are already at work preparing for another World War. We do not minimize the danger of such renewed aggression and recognize the need for drastic action to prevent the Reich from making preparations for a third world war; but the policies proposed should be subjected to careful scrutiny, for fear they may fail or even bring about wholly opposite results from those intended. paper deals with the economic aspects of the partition program. First of all, we need to define the proposals more exactly than has been done by some authors. We must not fail to distinguish clearly between administrative centralization and totalitarian dictatorship. There is no lack of examples for centralized administration in a democracy, such as France, the Low Countries and Great Britain, in varying degrees. There are also examples for totalitarianism originating in local centers under a decentralized system of government, such as in Austria during the early 'thirties. Moreover, Germany itself showed a parallel and fairly even growth of National Socialism all over the country after 1929. Decentralization and regional self-government represent, above all, a state of mind and a way of life, and not a mere administrative arrangement.

In the second place, we need to clear up a somewhat similar confusion in some of the economic arguments which have been cited in support of a partition program. This applies, in particular, to the idea that partition is necessary in order to eliminate the huge industrial and financial combines. Everybody agrees that they should be controlled in some way to render them harmless, but there is no reason to believe that

Athey cannot be eliminated without a political dismemberment of Germany. At the same time, such German combines as the I. G. Farben have only too often expanded economically into other countries; and experience teaches that political frontiers do not necessarily preclude international capital links or other types of economic influence abroad, especially if the governments concerned do not discourage such influence. In fact there is a serious danger that the fallacy of partition might weaken our watch for a revival of expansionism from the new "independent" states by the same economic and social groups which were responsible for it in the past.

THREE GERMAN STATES?

In summary, none of the three zones would be either entirely agricultural or completely industrialized. Each zone would harbor great war-essential and civilian industries, though in a varying degree. This is not, however, identical with the complete economic balance which is supposed to result somehow within each of the three states. The mere fact that a country has a mixed economy with both industrial and agricultural output does not necessarily make its position superior to that of an agricultural or industrialized nation, unless we assume that each of the new states should embark upon a policy of selfsufficiency. Even then the break of the customary market links, the possible division of numerous companies into three geographic units, and similar factors would cause grave dislocations in the industry of each zone. The mutual economic integration of the various German regions has always been far reaching. 

The automotive and engineering industries of Stuttgart and Munich, for instance, have in the past depended on the coal and steel of the Ruhr; the supply of fertilizers for the farm areas of the East came largely from the chemical plants around Frankfurt, and so forth.

In other words, there is absolutely no reason to believe that any of the three states could achieve self-sufficiency, which appears to be one assumption of the program under discussion. In addition, the establishment of new trade barriers and a strengthening of self-sufficiency trends would certainly be a most undesirable policy for the United Nations. If, on the other hand, the economic interplay among the three states should be left unrestricted, then the erection of new political boundaries would not change anything from the economic point of view, especially if both the three state governments and private business should be bent on continued cooperation.

ECONOMIC IMPLICATIONS OF INDEPENDENT STATEHOOD 

It is true that a number of economic changes is implicit in the very process of establishing several independent states in an area which was formerly occupied by one political system. The purpose of the discussion which follows is to explore whether or not the technical automatism of such separation can

31

be expected to outweigh the probable endeavor of new state governments in a divided Germany to establish the closest possible cooperation in their actual. economic policies.

Perhaps it is advisable to remind first of the historical separation of national sovereignty from property. The establishment of a new state today involves no automatic dispossession of land-owners or other social groups, particularly those responsible for the aggressive policy of a nation. It is true that national independence may give a government the power either to encourage or to obstruct economic development; and that dismemberment may impair communications and may lead to inequality in the access to raw materials, investment, taxation, use of seaports, etc. However, state independence does not establish any automatism but a mere opportunity for such restrictions--an opportunity which may or may not be actually utilized, depending on the philosophy and relations of the governments concerned. In other words, if 

Western Germany, for instance, should prohibit the exportation of iron ore to the two other states, then Southern Germany's industries might suffer from such prohibition until other supply sources of equal value can be found. But this does not mean that the government of Western Germany will actually issue such a decree just because it is independent. Any such measure would have to come from Allied control authorities, directly or indirectly; and these authorities can issue compulsory measures concerning the German economy just as well if Germany is not dismembered.

2. Financial Policy. Partition would prevent an equalization of regional budget deficits, provided public or private loans to the government of another zone are prohibited. In this respect, therefore, separation might be relatively effective, but the main effect would be the inability of Eastern Germany, for instance, to cover its possible deficits from the surpluses of Western or Southern Germany. Here again it is very doubtful whether this is the kind of effect which the United Nations want to achieve. 

We can also take it for granted that partition would involve a compulsory separation of the currencies and the creation of three separate banks of issue. An official pegging of the three currencies could, of course be prohibited; and if there should be a free exchange market---which is very doubtful--- then the rates of exchange would be exposed to fluctuations which might conceivably hamper the economic inter course and cooperation among the three states (always assuming that this should really be the aim of United 

Nations policy).

Yet there is little reliance on an official separation of currencies unless the governments and populations concerned really want it. What kind of foreign control can prevent citizens of a state from accepting at a stable rate the currency of another state, especially if the government and population of the latter are partners to such a practice? Even if the interstate exchange rates should fluctuate, the new setup would not be altogether different from the Nazi exchange system with its numerous types of marks which have actually represented a multiple-currency system during the 'thirties. At any rate, from the financial point of view partition, in order to be effective, would involve an amount of restrictive regimentation which would be out of tune with the principles which are expected to guide the post-war policy of the Allies.

3. Trade Policy. During the last few decades national sovereignty has often led to a strong tendency toward trade restrictions, but this effect is by no means automatic. After all, the free-trade spell during the nineteenth century was basically a period of tacit agreement on liberal trade principles among a number of independent nations. The establishment of three independent governments on German soil would not, therefore, guarantee in itself the erection of tariffs and other trade barriers, if indeed the victors should really find such a course desirable. Can the new states be compelled by foreign action to establish trade barriers against their own will? How effective would such involuntary trade barriers be? And do the United Nations really wish to set an example for a strengthening of protectionist tendencies in the post-war world?

Similar considerations apply to restrictions upon interstate travel and migration. An enforcement of such restrictions by foreign troops or officials over hundreds of miles of new frontiers would impose a terrific burden upon the Allied control machinery, on top of the far reaching supervision which is planned for the domestic and foreign policies of Germany as a whole. Moreover, the reflux of soldiers and war workers into their home towns will make any tight restriction of travel or migration impossible during the transitional period after the defeat of Germany.

It would, of course, be possible to for bid the creation or continuance of a unified system of communications or transportation, and perhaps to force the new states to reload or control all freight cars and trucks at each state boundary; but this is again a course which the United Nations will hesitate to adopt in an era of fast transportation and expanding transcontinental communi cations.

This leaves the possibility to restrict or forbid capital export from Western to Eastern Germany, for instance; or even to forbid any investment in either one of the other two states, As has been mentioned, the prerequisite would be a prohibition for private companies to have branches or subsidiaries outside their own state without a special license; and a compulsory dismemberment, into three parts, of every nation-wide concern now in existence. Such restrictions would clearly mean a grave prejudice for large-scale enterprise and industrial efficiency, and such a distortion of legitimate opposition to monopolistic combines would find few supporters. 

Such restrictions would also endanger civilian: employment and industrial production in each of the three states. In addition, in the absence of strictly enforced regimentation with heavy penalties such capital links could largely be replaced by an unwritten coordination of company policies in and among the three states. American experiences since 1890 in trying to enforce the Sherman Act teach how complex this problem is even where it is purely domestic. 4.

Social Policy. As for indispensable social reforms, the division of the Reich into three new states can be of little help, to say the least. What really matters is the power of the social groups behind the Nazis and the General Staff; the Junkers and industrial magnates. The expropriation of the 

Junkers will be primarily a task for Eastern Germany. However, lack of a coordinated social policy might give the Junkers, as a group, a refuge with possible financial compensation in one of the other states. Western Germany is the foremost center of industrial empires which have in the past actively fostered German nationalism and aggression, such as the Vereinigte Stahlwerke combine; but the concentration of industrial power also applies to the other two proposed states, with the center of the I. G. Farben combine in the Southwest.

Any effective expropriation or division of these industrial empires will have to proceed according to economic rather than geographic units. Unless a unified policy toward the chemical combine, for instance, is adopted, reforms in its southwestern sector might be frustrated by increased footholds in Central Germany, or vice versa. Similarly the Krupp armament combine, who se ? old center is in the Ruhr, established in 1941 a new center in Eastern Germany, the Schlesische Industriebau GmbH. which has gained in importance since the partial destruction of Essen. Many other industries from the West have moved or transferred their production to the East or South; a policy which was for a while encouraged by the Nazis through preferences for raw materials and labor, aimed at the creation of a great industrial center in Eastern Europe. 

The geographic range of many concerns is, therefore, far greater now than it was before the war. A uniform policy throughout Germany of social reform in both industry and agriculture will be in the vital interest of the United Nations and of lasting peace. A division of the Reich into three states would only make this task more difficult and complicated. 

The same applies to labor policy after Germany's defeat. A NEW REGIONALISM? 

15 195 to

As has been mentioned, certain authors attribute a partition of the Reich effects far-reaching enough to take the sting out of German politics and to permit the admission of the new German states as members of various custom unions to be created after the war. Others have expressed the view, much along the same lines, that the formation of regional economic groups on the Continent is impossible as long as one great power exerts an overwhelming influence based on its central location, huge population and industrial resources. They believe that a dismemberment of Germany would make it possible to balance the membership of each regional group, since it would then consist of states with comparable size and resources. Sometimes this idea is extended to German membership in a European or world federation of economic or political character. 

The first condition of a fruitful discussion about these ideas is a clear distinction among the various possible meanings of regionalism. To begin with, regionalism can potentially mean a substitute for international cooperation or the first step toward it; but: it can also mean economic isolationism and the erection of trade barriers on a regional scale. Regional groups can be cooperative members of one international organization, or they can be engaged in bitter rivalry and in a race for expansion. In short, regionalism can involve either decentralized self-government within a major federation, or a network of hostile alliances, Finally, from the viewpoint of pure economics and in the hypothetical absence of political barriers, regional groupings would change little, if anything, in the setup of interregional trade and in the location of productive facilities, which would still depend primarily on price factors. It is only on the assumption of effective political barriers to trade that the question of economic changes through regional reorganization arises.

One author submits an extensive scheme of world organization under which regional councils would establish a link between the individual states and the supreme world agency. Regionalism would: thus serve to reduce the impact of national sovereignty without sacrificing the democratic principle of local self-government to a super-state under the centralized domination of a few great powers. Others have pointed out that regionalism may be used as an expedient in order to reconcile the atavistic survival of national sovereignty with the requirements of growing interdependence and integration in the world. 

What few, if any, supporters of regionalism held in mind until very recently, was to increase the number of independent states just to improve the internal balance of each regional association. Such a procedure seems indeed to mean jumping from the frying pan into the fire. What contemporary Europe needs least of all is a more numerous and strengthened system of sovereign states, even if such growth should partly be equalized by closer cooperation on a regional scale.

Little attention has been paid to the possibility that such a program might make three independent states on German soil partners in all the major regional associations on the Continent. If the evil forces of aggression should retain their influence in the German nation--and this is obviously the underlying assumption of the whole partition program--then the new German states will find means to conspire with a view to fostering in each regional group discord and resentment toward the others, and to achieving a renewed di sruption of European and international unity. 

The relative importance of each of the three states in a limited regional group would be at least as great as the role of the Reich in a united Europe. 

No detailed information about concrete plans for regional organization in Europe has been made public, but we can imagine hypotetically the possibility of an association of Eastern Germany with Poland and Czechoslovakia; of Western

Germany with Holland and Denmark; and of Southern Germany with Austria, Switzerland, and perhaps with France, Belgium and Luxembourg. These three countries would have no common frontier with Western Germany under at least one of the schemes offered.

This mere enumeration of possible groupings makes clear immediately how little realism is involved in this whole program. Above all, two great questions arise; first, will the various nations mentioned, to the East, North, West and South of Germany, be willing to enter into a regional association with the new German states at any early date? It reflects a basically sound reaction on the part of the smaller European nations, especially those bordering on the Reich, to feel that a continental system of sovereign states with the German colossus in its very center will always be a precarious state of affairs: and this is true regardless of whether the Germans are organized politically in one state or in three, for the total of German population and resources will remain the same even in case of a partition. It is time that the right conclusion from European experiences with Germany be drawn: to change the basic significance of statehood by a far reaching international organization with "teeth", to such an extent that the means of external power and aggression would increasingly be taken away from the individual nations. As long as this is not the case such nations as the Netherlands or Czechoslovakia will probably remain about as suspicious toward Western or Eastern Germany as they are toward the Reich.

And secondly, will the Soviet Union tolerate any regional group on the Continent, in which she is not a partner? This latter question has in fact been answered already: the Soviet Union has made it perfectly clear that its attitude toward any such scheme would be absolutely negative.

However, even if we assume that these political obstacles could be overcome it remains very doubtful whether this type of regional organization would bring economic advantage to anybody. The London Times of February 29, 1944, in an editorial devoted to criticism of plans for a dismemberment of Germany pointed out that the resulting crippling of German production and trade would have "desperate repercussions" all over Europe and concluded, "Freedom from fear and want will be won not by breaking down what forms of economic organization are left but by building them up into the broader structure of European unity."

If we try to obtain a concrete picture of possible regional associations of the three German states then the only information available is that about past patterns of production and foreign trade. It is clear that this picture is in the nature of an illustration rather than a prediction: 

Eastern Germany will be the one state with an appreciable surplus of food, especially grain. It is true that much of this surplus will disappear if agricultural subsidies cease, if the Junker estates are split up, and if East Prussia is detached. However, the result need not be any increase in effective demand for foreign foodstuffs, especially if the loss in farm production cannot be made up by new industrialization. Poland and Czechoslovakia cannot, therefore, expect to find any sufficient market in Eastern Germany for their food exports. On the other hand, their own import of German industrial products in the past consisted largely of products of Western and Southern Germany, such as chemical goods, automotive equipment and specialized machinery. In 

Eastern Germany it will be chiefly the electrical and engineering plants of the Berlin area (as far as they will have survived the air raids), which can produce types of equipment that are needed regularly in the two countries mentioned. The textile industry of Saxony, on the other hand, will meet serious competition from both Czechoslovak and Polish factories.

Both of the two countries mentioned are huge importers of coal, of which Southern Germany has much smaller deposits than have the two other zones. The chemical works of Southwestern Germany could indeed cover a large part of the import needs of Austria and Switzerland in this field. 

Since one author makes Southern Germany border on Belgium and Luxembourg, the possibility of a regional association among these three countries has also to be considered, although it would constitute something of a geographic monstrosity. It must be admitted that the coal and steel of the two countries mentioned would meet certain raw material needs of Southern Germany and that Belgium could absorb some of the mechanical and chemical products of that state. The main difficulty would remain in the field of foodstuffs, since all of the areas in question have a deficit in this respect. There is a theoretical. possibility that France might be included in such a regional combination, which would then be of very large size. This possibility seems indeed to be too unrealistic to require any detailing. Incidentally, the new customs union between Holland, Belgium and Luxembourg seems to exclude any possibility that these nations join different regional groups. or shall we assume that Southern 

Germany would be in the same group as the Netherlands?

Many more examples could be given in order to demonstrate the unrealistic character of the borderlines just mentioned.

Western Germany, finally, would face some competition from Holland for its coal, but would provide an important market for both Dutch and Danish dairy surpluses; assuming that the latter would not be absorbed by British needs. However, these two countries would not represent a sufficient market for the coal, steel, machines and textiles of Western Germany, and exports from this zone

to the countries east and south of Germany--and, of course, to Southern and Eastern Germany--would probably suffer from the regional division under discussion. Generally speaking, Western Germany is likely to suffer most from a partition of Germany and it is difficult to imagine any regional combination which could provide a compensation, especially if the Western Rhineland should become a buffer between that zone and Belgium and France, as one author suggests. At the same time, a disruption of the industrial production of Western 

Germany will particularly affect its former customers in Eastern Europe. 

Of course, this whole discussion can offer only a very rough and approximate picture of the problem which would be implicit in a regional combination of the three German states and their neighbor countries. Obviously the picture would change considerably if more than three regional groups in Europe should be established, or if the establishment of preferential trade relations within each group should meet serious obstacles. The possibility of regional associations on the Continent which would include the three German states is so slight that a more detailed discussion of their effects would be premature. To So much, however, can be said in general: regional groupings, if established primarily on the basis of geographic proximity, would often combine countries with similar resources and in a similar phase of industrial and agricultural development; countries, therefore, which have relatively little to offer each other save the advantages of neighborhood and low cost of transportation. this extent a close association is quite justified, especially if it is based on links of tradition; but it is difficult to see any reason for preferential treatment of members of the same regional group--which would seem to be its only raison d'être--at the expense of such non-member countries as may offer much better opportunity for mutual trade relations.

About the only other motive which has been suggested for regional organ ization has been the specific necessity for Balkan countries to acquire a stronger trade position, especially in their negotiations on farm exports to and industrial imports from Germany before the war. 

Obviously no such considerations would apply to those regional groups which the new German states would be supposed to join. It is at least very doubtful whether what the supporters of such ideas have in mind is a uniform organization of the imports and exports of countries with related economic structure. This would be exemplified by the formation of a bloc of Eastern Germany, Poland and Czechoslovakia for uniform agreements about exports of grain and imports of chemicals and heavy machinery; a bloc which would deal directly with the regional bloc of Southern 

Germany, Belgium and Luxembourg, for instance. Such negotiations among regional blocks, whatever their merits and faults may be otherwise, would very probably invite renewed, though indirect, collaboration among the newly separated parts of the former Reich, and perhaps German economic leadership throughout Europe--the very effect which the authors of partition programs, as well as their opponents, want to see eliminated.

It is quite possible that the European problem cannot be solved at all o the basis of national sovereignty; and this applies equally to a Europe with or without regional groups, and with or without a dismemberment of Germany. Many of us realize that the immediate after-effect of war and oppression in Europe is likely to be a general revival of nationalism and of the wish for unrestricted national sovereignty, and some of us feel that regionalism might at least check such a tendency for the time being. But unless the revival of nationalism remains temporary and unless it shifts before long into a general willingness to cooperate in an effective international organization, even at the price of sacrificing some national rights, no real solution of Europe's troubles and no lasting peace can be expected.

At any rate, if national sovereignty must survive for the time being, then its. dangers should not, at least, be aggravated by the artificial creation of new under sized states, even if this should be the price for the establishment of regional associations. In the particular case of Germany, every student of 

German history, national character and social psychology will realize that renewed partition would stimulate nationalistic aggressiveness in Germany; and that it would render the cause of a genuine democratization of that nation hopeless for many decades to come--all this for the mirage of turning back history by more than a century. 

Experiences during the Ruhr occupation in 1923 and the Saar plebiscite over a decade later prove the hopelessness of separatism in Germany even if an association with other countries is offered. The whole partition program stands and falls with the assumption that the creation of new frontiers automatically results in the creation of a new national consciousness: that soon after the partition the Bavarian, for instance, would begin to feel loyal toward the state of Southern Germany only, and would no longer give his loyalty to the German nation as a whole. There is no indication whatsoever that such an assumption is realistic. On the contrary, the whole school of Geopolitik, headed by General Haushofer, has taught the German people for over twenty years that "artificial frontiers" and "boundaries contrary to nature" cannot last and that in the long run "biologically correct frontiers" always prevail. SUMMARY 

The real issue underlying the various partition programs is whether a di smemberment of the Reich would result, within a measurable space of time, in a shift of loyalty on the part of the German people from Germany as a nation to a mere part of it. Historical examples for the constitution of new nations during the last few centuries--such as Switzerland, Holland, Belgium--prove nothing in the case of Germany. In particular, religion in the twentieth century has much less state-creating capacity than it had in the past. short, there is no reason to believe that the German people, in their majority, would accept voluntarily any type of partition program and that they would cooperate with it.

In If this is not the case, however, then there is very little hope for a really independent policy of new German States either in the economic or in any other sphere. The whole concept of for cing states to exist against their will, and to abandon economic cooperation against their own wishes, is absurd 

In the words of E. H. Carr,

The world has plenty of experience of the difficulty of removing economic barriers, but little experience of the probably still more difficult problem of forcibly erecting and maintaining such barriers between people who desire to constitute an economic unit. 

A second reason to discount the partition program is its probable effect upon the economy of the Continent, especially of Central and Eastern Europe. We need not go so far as to claim that the three new states would be incapable of living, a statement which was often made about Austria after 1918, for example.
