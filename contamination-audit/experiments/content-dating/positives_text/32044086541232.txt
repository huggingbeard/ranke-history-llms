SINO-TIBETICA EDITORIAL FOREWORD 

This series of compact monographic articles is designed to afford publication to certain interpretative findings of study on the languages of Southeastern Asia. 

In 1935, the Government of the United States instituted the Works Progress Administration, an economic relief agency succeeding the Federal Emergency Relief Administration and State Emergency Relief Administration. 

Part of the problem of these administrations was to afford relief, through productive employment, to clerical as well as manual workers. 

65-3-2746 was authorized in 1935, for the preparation of a Comparative Dictionary of the Sino-Tibetan Languages, and renewed in 1937 as official project 165-3-6200.

The immediate direction of the work was in the hands of Robert Shafer, Supervisor of the projects. 

The plan called primarily for the compilation, in convenient form, of lexical material on the numerous languages and dialects of the great Sino-Tibetan family, spoken by one- 

The most pertinent or fourth of the earth's inhabitants. available data on each natural linguistic group or division

of languages in this family were brought together and transcribed, as nearly as possible in a uniform orthography; first, as a lexical compendium, and second in the form of phonetic tables collating data on the principal sound equivalents. 

Twenty parts or "volumes" of "Sino-Tibetan Linguistics" were undertaken, each for one such group of languages. 

Of these, volumes 3 and 4, on the West and West Central Himalayish dialects, have been completed in 296 and 63 pages; the others are in various stages of progress. The twenty parts will vary from about 25 to about 2,000 typewritten pages, according to fulness of available data. 

Full publication of "Sino-Tibetan Linguistics" is not within the scope of the Works Progress Administration project. For the present, therefore, eight clean typewritten copies of each part are being prepared, one copy being deposited in the University of California Library, one in the Congressional Library in Washington, one in the custody of the sponsor, and one in that of the supervisor and author; besides copies remaining in the hands of the Works Progress Administration. 

This ordered mass of data is a collocation which should greatly lighten the labors of all future students of comparative Sino-Tibetan philology. It will render possible the determination of sound shifts, of semantic as well as phonetic equivalences, the degree of likeness and unlikeness of the languages, and therefore of their relationship and presumable comparative history.

In the course of this underlying work, interpretations on certain points have already been formulated by the project Supervisor, and the initial numbers of Sino-Tibetica will contain Shafer's findings on Tibetan nasal prefixes, and on a link between Burmese and Lolo. Articles of similar scope are expected to follow. As sponsor of the Dictionary project, editorial responsibility for SINO-TIBETICA falls to me. 

The Sino-Tibetan Linguistics or Comparative Dictionary has proved to be more than a relief measure of a governmental agency. It is also an experiment in technique of scholarship. 

Traditionally, the comparative philologist works single-handed. 

He transcribes thousands of slips of extracted material, and recopies or reshuffles them. If he has the aid of an occasional student assistant or two, he is fortunate. That most of this laborious preliminary task of ordered assembling might be satisfactorily accomplished by a corps of workers not one of whom had had previous academic training in philology, was, I believe, scarcely conceived heretofore. So far as the experiment is adjudged reasonably successful, it will be due to Mr. 

Shafer's skill in selecting, training, and directing the personnel of the project. Appreciation is certainly due the 

Works Progress administration for its broad-minded willingness to undertake this experiment in scientific method, which may help point the way toward an increased freeing of productive linguists for the interpretative aspects of their work.

The prefixed or superscribed letters in Tibetan are, when arranged in vargas: g- d- b- m- h- I- 1- - S There are no prefixed surds in Tibetan because it appears as if practically all absolute initial stop consonants, in Tibetan, had become sonants. The exceptions found in the dictionary, if one eliminates words obviously borrowed from another language, consist largely of names of artifacts or of words apparently not well authenticated by Jäschke as occurring in the written language. In these cases, the words look as if they might be borrowed or words which Jäschke had heard in dialectic form but which he was unable to refer back to a written form. The written form, we may surmise, contained a prefixed consonant. 

That the absolute initial surd stops became sonants is indicated by the modern Ladakhi where initial surds which are not prefixed or superscribed tend to become sonants.

A. H. Franckel states: "The greatest difficulty of Ladakhi pronunciation rests with the unaspirated tenues, that is, 2 with k, t, p, ts, and c. The normal pronunciation of the same holds the mean between English media and tenuis but the tenuis is always inclined to be pronounced like a media, if it is not furnished with a protecting letter." By protecting letters, he refers to prefixed or superscribed letters, a y following a surd initial, and an occasional initial surd in the second part of a compound. As examples of the pronunciation of unprotected surd initials he gives 

Ladakhi gun <kun "all", gushu < kushu "apple", etc. If absolute initial surd stops become sonants, then prefixed surd stops would also become sonants.

I long ago decided from the description of the sound given by Jäschke that his h- represented an "indeterminate" nasal: one produced by lowering the velum, thus opening the nasal passage, but with the mouth, including the tongue, relaxed. In the relaxation of the mouth it would exactly agree with h- as an initial before vowels where it represents the easy vocalic ingress. The only difference seems to have been that in the prefixed h- the sound was nasalized and in the initial position it was not. Since the Khotanese alphabet from which the Tibetan was derived had no sign for either of these sounds, the Tibetans apparently were satisfied to let one sign serve for both closely related sounds.

SINO-TIBETICA is designed as a series of brief monographs dealing with the languages of Southeastern Asia. It has been established as the result of a 

Comparative Sino-Tibetan Dictionary in process of compilation as a project of the Works Progress Administration of the Government of the United States, and will include findings deriving from this project. 

Editorial responsibility for SINO-TIBETICA rests with A. L. Kroeber. 

It is not a publication of the University of California. 

Parts of the edition of each number respectively become the property of the author; are distributed among certain of the world's leading institutions of learning actively interested in East Asiatic philology; and will be set aside for needs of the scholarship of the future. The remaining copies are available at a charge designed to cover postage and expense of distribution, and fixed, uniformly for all countries, at one cent per page of publication; which may be re mitted by International Postage Stamp Order (Reply Coupons).
