Authority. --This report is in response to the following resolution adopted 16 August 1960:

Resolved by the Committee on Public Works of the 

United States Senate, That the Board of Engineers for Rivers and Harbors, created under Section 3 of the River and Harbor Act, approved June 12, 1902, be, and is hereby, requested to review the report of the Chief of Engineers on Southwestern Alaska, published as House 

Document numbered 390, Eighty-fourth Congress, second session, with a view to determining whether the recommendations contained therein should be modified in any way at the present time, with particular reference to dredging the entrance channel and constructing a smallboat harbor at King Cove, Alaska. 

2. Description. --King Cove is near the southwesterly tip of the Alaska Peninsula. The nearest ports of principal importance are 

Kodiak Harbor, approximately 450 miles northeasterly, and Dutch 

Harbor, about 175 miles southwesterly. King Cove is a natural bay approximately two miles in length extending inland between high ridges to a sandspit at the northerly end of the cove. The village of King Cove has been developed along this spit which separates King Cove from a 2-mile long lagoon extending further inland. The cove contains water depths of 70 to 130 feet and serves as an anchorage area during calm weather. The lagoon contains water depths of 3 to 60 feet, but is seldom used for anchorage due to the shoaled condition of the entrance. 2

3. Economic development.--The village of King Cove exists primarily because of the fishing resources of the area. The population was about 500 in 1964. The principal industry is a salmon cannery which was constructed around 1912. The harvesting and processing of salmon and king crab, and the services provided for the people engaged in these occupations, provide the major source of income of the area. 

Commercial fishing supports a resident fleet of approximately 46 boats. 

The village also serves as a trading and supply center for transient fishing craft and smaller communities in the immediate area. 

4. Existing improvements. --There are no existing Federal navigation improvements at King Cove. The Pacific American Corporation has constructed a multiple cannery wharf, oil dock, and marine railway. 

Local interests have provided piles, dolphins and single mooring piles for the use of boats not tied up to the cannery wharf. 

5. Improvements desired.--Local interests desire an all-tide access to a protected anchorage area sufficient in size to accommodate local fishing craft. They suggest deepening and widening of the entrance channel to the lagoon or construction of breakwaters to provide a protected anchorage at the head of the cove. 6.

Plan of improvement.--The District Engineer reports that the major navigation problem in King Cove is lack of protection for small craft anchored offshore. He finds that the most satisfactory plan of improvement would consist of: a dredged anchorage basin located between King Cove and King Cove Lagoon, with an entrance channel to provide an all-weather, all-tide access; a dredged earth-fill training dike to control tidal flows and sedimentation; and a rock groin to control littoral drift along the existing sandspit. His plan would provide a protected swing-anchorage for 75 fishing craft and a harbor of refuge for transient craft. 

7. Costs and justification.--Exclusive of preauthorization study costs, and based on April 1966 prices, the District Engineer estimates the first cost of the proposed improvements at $532,000 of which $527,000 would be the Federal cost including $5,000 for aids to navigation, and $5,000 would be the non-Federal cost for lands, easements, rights-ofway, and relocations of structures and utilities. Annual charges are estimated at $31,700. Average annual benefits from boat damage reduction, increased fish harvest, reduction in fish spoilage, and providing 77-000 0-67-3 3

Division Engineer concurs. Local interests have expressed their willingness to cooperate. 8.

Public notice.--The Division Engineer issued a public notice stating the recommendations of the reporting officers and affording interested parties an opportunity to present additional information to the Board.

Views and Recommendations of the Board of Engineers for Rivers and Harbors, 9.

Views. --The Board of Engineers for Rivers and Harbors concurs in general in the views and recommendations of the reporting officers. 

The Board notes the lack of safe anchorage facilities in the area and that the proposed improvements are centrally located with respect to fishing activities and will meet the existing and future needs for an adequate anchorage and harbor of refuge. 10.

The Board also notes that King Cove, Alaska, is a depressed labor area as designated by the Economic Development Administration under the Public Works and Economic Development Act of 1965. REPORT OF THE DISTRICT ENGINEER KING COVE HARBOR SYLLABUS 

The District Engineer finds that improvement of King Cove Harbor is urgently needed to provide sheltered anchorage for existing and prospective commercial fishing craft in the King Cove area. Annual benefits expected to accrue from construction of an entrance channel, training dike, and anchorage basin would exceed the annual costs sufficiently to warrant participation by the Federal Government. The local project sponsors state that they are able to provide the necessary features of local cooperation for justification of this project. Accordingly, the District Engineer recommends that improvements to King Cove Harbor be undertaken at the earliest possible time.

DESCRIPTION. 

a. General. The village of King Cove and its harbor are geographically located in latitude 55°03' north, longitude 162°20' west, at the southwesterly tip of the Alaska Peninsula in Southwestern Alaska. The harbor is located approximately 30 air miles southeast of Cold Bay. The nearest ports of principal importance are Kodiak Harbor, Alaska, approximately 450 miles northeasterly, and Dutch Harbor, Alaska, approximately 175 miles southwesterly.

b. Harbor Condition. 

(1) King Cove, the primary anchorage in this area, is a natural bay extending inland between high ridges which rise steeply from the shore. The Cove is approximately two miles in length and contains water depths of 12 to 22 fathoms, Vessels anchoring here find excellent holding ground during calm weather. 

King Cove Lagoon is a brackish body of water separated from the northerly end of the cove by a large sand spit. The village of 

King Cove has been developed along this spit. The shallow, twomile long lagoon contains water from 2 to 10 fathoms in depth; however, it is seldom used for anchorage because of its shoaled entrance. The village spit is slightly overlapped by a rounding counter spit extending from the westward shore of the Cove. 

Between these spits, a natural basin has formed adjacent to the narrow area of entrance to the lagoon. This basin of approximately 20 acres holds depths from 5 to 7 fathoms in which fishing

boats are frequently anchored. Entrance to this anchorage basin must be made at high tide stages to clear shoals separating the basin from deep water in King Cove. Extensive shoaling in turn separates the basin from the shallow waters of King Cove Lagoon further inland. (2) The extreme range of tide in the harbor is 14 feet; the mean range is 4.8 feet; and the diurnal range is 6.8 feet. With the exception of the protection provided by Deer Island located to the south of the village, King Cove is openly exposed to the Pacific Ocean. With this available fetch, winds have been observed to reach a velocity of more than 100 miles per hour. 

C. Harbor Use. The harbor bases a fishing fleet of small boats that rely on King Cove for supplies, harbor, and point of delivery for their catch. The Alaska Steamship Company also enters the harbor seasonally to load and unload cargoes. Mail and supplies are distributed from King Cove to the fishing fleet and to adjacent areas along the Pacific side of the peninsula. 

d. Map and Chart References. King Cove Harbor and vicinity are shown on U. S. Coast and Geodetic Chart No. 8703, and on U. S. 

Geological Survey and quad map, "Fort Randall," 1:250,000. 

4. TRIBUTARY AREA. 

a.

General. 

(1) King Cove is an important fishing community in 

Southwestern Alaska. Southwestern Alaska is defined as that area of Alaska lying west of Cook Inlet and south of Kuskokwim River drainage, including Bristol Bay, Kodiak Island and the islands adjoining the Alaskan Peninsula. The entire southwestern area is severely handicapped by climate and geographic features. The area is mountainous, with low lying plains along Bristol Bay broken and divided into numerous bays, islets, peninsulas and fjords by an elaborate system of waterways. Most of these waterways are navigable by small boats, and many are navigable by ocean-going vessels. 

Partially compensating for the severe topography are the ready avenues of water transportation. However, this produces a dependence of the area on navigation. 10

(2) King Cove, with a 1964 population of 350 permanent and 150 seasonal residents, was founded about 1912 when a salmon cannery was built on the Cove. It serves as a trading and supply center for resident and transient fishing craft, and smaller communities in the immediate areas. At the present time mail and supplies are received by sea and air from the lower states for local distribution. area. b.

Industries. The settlement of King Cove exists primarily because of the fishery resources of the area. IMPROVEMENTS DESIRED. 

a. From informal meetings held with the townspeople, it was determined that they desire construction of or access to a sheltered water area for anchoring their commercial fishing boats. There is no protection for boats anchored offshore or during the winter season layover when these waters are in their roughest state. From this information, a preliminary plan for harbor construction was developed and presented at a public hearing held in King Cove on 26 August 1964. 

Hearing testimony revealed that local boat owners desire harbor improvements which would assure full protection to their craft from storms. Little protection is afforded by Deer Island, located five miles offshore to the south of the harbor; consequently, winds from this quadrant generate storm waves of damaging size over this fetch. Although a natural anchorage lagoon lies to the west of the town, its shallow entrance and limited area do not provide adequate protection to the resident craft. Local interests suggest deepening and widening of the entrance channel and the lagoon to provide all-tide access to a protected anchorage area sufficient in size to accommodate all the local craft. As an alternate, local interests requested that consideration be given to the construction of rock breakwaters in King Cove to provide a protected anchorage area at the head of the cove. Also, local interests suggested development of entry into King Cove Lagoon; however, this plan was not strongly advocated or stressed because of excessive channel dredging, ice conditions and wave problems. b.

As justification for the requested improvements, local interests cited boat and dock damage due to storms, loss of fish revenue and incomes, high marine insurance costs, and lack of repair and layup facilities. They also noted that the needs are becoming more acute because of the expanding crab fishing industry; the increased use of the cove for supply and servicing, and the increasing costs for boat repairs and maintenance.

a. General. The principal difficulty faced by small boats operating in the King Cove area is a lack of sheltered anchorage. Prior to the development of the recent king crab industry, most fishing vessels in the King Cove area were hauled out on ways for winter storage. Craft of shallow draft occasionally were taken into the lagoon where they were afforded some degree of protection from the southerly exposure. Because of the shallow entrance to King Cove Lagoon only smaller craft are afforded anchorage there. Larger craft are forced to moor to exposed piling in the open cove or tie up to the cannery dock. 

The exposed cove and dock are particularly vulnerable to winds during winter months. At this time of year, the major crab fishing season, severe and sudden winds develop over a maximum fetch from the southsouthwest. Any vessel caught at mooring or dockside is hard pressed to get away upwind, and if not underway before winds reach high velocity, is certain to suffer damage or cause damage to the dock. Fishermen find it impossible to reach unmanned boats at anchor; tie lines strained taut at the dock are impossible to release; mooring piles and lines are broken and boat operators are forced to stand helplessly by while boats run awash or are battered against the dock and each other. Bearing piles are broken, deck and dock structures battered or crushed, and valuable navigation and electronic equipment lost. Because of the remoteness of the King Cove area, repair costs are excessively high. Only minor repairs are possible locally, and major work requires a costly tow to a Seattle shipyard. Cost of this tow service averages $12,000 for a single craft. 

Because of the recent trend in damages, insurance costs are high with recent insurance premiums quoted at the rate of 5.7 percent of depreciable value for the seven month season. A rate advance is predicted, because of consistently high losses. With the current trend to crab fishing and its attending costlier gear, larger vessels and increasing fleet size, marine losses are predicted to escalate under existing navigation conditions.

15. PLAN OF IMPROVEMENT. 

a.

Proposed Plan. The proposed plan provides for an entrance channel 125 feet wide and about 950 feet long leading to an 11-acre anchorage basin and maneuvering area. The King Cove resident and transient fleet is composed of seiner-gillnetter craft averaging 6.0-foot draft; seiner-crabber craft averaging 7.8-foot draft; and tender-crabber craft averaging 10.3-foot draft. During the possible occurrence of the lowest estimated tide of minus 3.8-feet, the 10.3-foot draft vessels will require a navigable depth exceeding 14.1-feet. Accordingly, a project depth of 15 feet below mean lower low water has been designed 16

for both entrance channel and anchorage basin. An essential element of harbor construction, 17,000 cubic yards of dredged earth fill would be placed as a training dike to divert tidal flows into the channel extending from the cove to the lagoon. This dike would be approximately 

800 feet long, would have a top width of 10 feet, and would have a top elevation of 12 feet above mean lower low water, A rock groin would be located south of the entrance channel to divert littoral drift along the spit to deep water. The rock groin to have a top elevation of 10 feet above mean lower low water and an overall length of 200 feet. A public landing and necessary boat service facilities will be provided by the wharf complex operated by the Pacific American Cannery. This plan of improvement is fully acceptable to local interests and will fulfill the needs of local navigation at the lowest project cost. b. Basis of Design. Analysis of basin dimensions is based on data representing past damage to boats anchored in the cove; the number of fishing boats operating in the area; prevailing winds and tide levels, and natural configuration of the harbor. Excessive wave conditions are prevalent during onshore winds from the southwest; low tides prevent free access over existing gravel bars; and an exposed fetch in King Cove Lagoon generates choppy waves with williwaw winds from the north. Several locations for the improvements were considered; however, the location selected is the most economical; develops a natural shelter area presently used for anchorage; is most readily accessible to the cannery dock; and is free from encroaching structures or conditions which would hinder construction or operation of the harbor. c.

Tidal velocities in the channel reach a maximum of 6 feet per second. This velocity is insufficient to move tabular beach cobbles averaging one inch in least dimension.

ESTIMATE OF BENEFITS. 

გი General. The proposed improvement at King Cove would earn benefits by prevention of vessel damage to the present fishing fleet; reduction of excessive maintenance costs of the present salmon seiner fleet; reduction in repairs to dock facilities; reduction in miscellaneous losses to boats, lines, antennas, and outrigging equipment; increased fish harvests; and provision of a harbor of refuge from storms. Increased fire protection has not been included because of the present dispersal of the fleet in time of storm. Analysis of past, present and future projected use requirement indicates that the project should provide swing-anchorage for 75 boats in the proposed basin. Approximately 

50 boats will use the basin immediately for home port, with a possible future increase to 100 boats. Indications are that these additional craft will be made up of approximately 10 salmon seiners and 40 crab fishing boats. On this basis it is expected that the harbor, although presently sized for 75 craft-equivalents at swing-anchor, could adequately accommodate future short duration demand of a larger number of boats in periods of heavy storms. The additional 25 craft would be provided short term space by more regulated (double anchor) anchorage of the vessels occupying the basin. It is expected that these 50 additional craft will come with harbor improvement and they will be one-half vessels new to Alaskan fishing and one-half vessels attracted from other Alaskan fishing fleets. No recreational craft are considered for present or future use of the project as there is no recreational boating in this remote area of Alaska. b. 

Prevention of Vessel Damage. During the 10 year period from 1954 to 1964 a total of 29 vessels were damaged to the extent of $102,928. It is estimated that an available harbor would have prevented or reduced this cost by $87,250 or $9,975 annually. Benefits from a small boat harbor to the 57 boat-equivalents from damage prevented or reduced in severity would amount to about $10,000 annually. Co 

Reduction in Boat Maintenance Costs. The present practice of beaching salmon seiner vessels during eight, to nine months of offseason allows the wooden hulls to shrink excessively which then requires excessive amounts of recaulking. This maintenance could be reduced to normal maintenance by keeping boats anchored in wet storage.. Estimated reduction of the excessive annual maintenance by provision of a harbor is $5,850. 22

d. Reduction in Repairs to Dock Facilities. Because of the lack of anchorage at the present time, it is common practice for four to six fishing vessels to tie abreast at the cannery while waiting to unload or service. Sudden storms frequently catch these boats unaware, resulting in excessive damage to dock, dock facing, and piling. A general benefit of $10,000 annually would result from reduction of these damages by provision of a small boat harbor. 

e. Miscellaneous Preventable Boat Damage. Under an un protected anchorage pattern, fishing vessels show losses to hawsers, lines, fenders, railings, antennas, and fishing equipment in times of severe weather. A small boat harbor would provide protection and prevent an estimated $7,700 yearly in small miscellaneous losses. £o 

Increased Fish Harvest. Improvement of a small boat harbor will improve the harvest of salmon and crab by increasing fishing time, by reducing the spoilage of fish and crab, and by attracting new fishing craft to the King Cove area. Estimated annual benefit from these sources is $91,800. g. Harbor of Refuge. During 1961 and 1962 ten vessels valued at an estimated $245,100 were lost in the vicinity of King Cove but not included in previous benefit estimations. It is estimated that 15 percent of these losses could have been avoided had a harbor of refuge been available at King Cove. Annual benefits of a harbor of refuge are conservatively estimated at $18,400.

DISCUSSION. 

a. 

The area tributary to King Cove is generally an undeveloped, highly remote sector of the Alaska Peninsula-Aleutian Island portion of 27

the State of Alaska. There are no large cities, and no major industries such as manufacturing, mining or agriculture. Fishing and fish processing remain the sole income resource for the area. These industries are vir-' tually dependent on safe waterways and protective navigation improvements benefiting water transportation for their continued existence. b. Small-boat harbors in Alaska are the hub of a transportation system similar to the Federal-aid secondary or "Farm-to-Market" road system in other states. King Cove is an essential link in this transportation system, as a fish marketing and supply distribution center. c.

Local interests feel that increased operations at their harbor would give impetus to the local state and national economy. Improvement of King Cove Harbor is urgently needed to relieve crowded navigation conditions, by providing protected anchorage for the commercial fishing boat fleet, resident and transient craft, and for future expansion, The provision of an eleven-acre dredged anchorage basin at

King Cove would afford protected anchorage to fishing craft in times of storm and off-season layup. Local boat owners would benefit from the proposed improvement by virtue of a protected anchorage area. d. e.

It is determined that local navigation would benefit from decreased boat damage and maintenance, increased useable lifespan of fishing craft, and a general increase in the economy of the King Cove area, King crab fishing, while new to Alaska waters, is profitable. 

It is expected to increase, with newer and larger boats and ever increasing fleet size, by provision of improved navigation facilities at 

King Cove. A 50-boat basin to harbor the existing fleet would be economically justified. Provision of additional anchorage area will permit expansion of the fishing fleet, and it is estimated that the fleet will increase to approximately 100 boats. Satisfactory protection of the expanded fleet can be accomplished by provision of space to accommodate 75 vessels at full swing anchorage with short duration accommodation of the additional 25 vessels by more regulated anchorage during time of need. It is expected that one half of the additional fishing boats will be attracted from other Alaskan fishing fleets and therefore no increase in fish harvest is credited as a benefit to the improvement. The remaining 25 boats are expected to be new to Alaska fishing, and the benefit from increased fish catch from their operation is credited as an annual benefit. KING COVE HARBOR, ALASKA 

Information called for by 

Senate Resolution 148, 85th Congress

Adopted 28 January 1958 

1. Existing Project Description. 

There are no existing Federal

Navigation projects at King Cove. The settlement of King Cove exists primarily because of the fishery resources of the area. The harvesting and processing of salmon, king crab, and halibut, and the services provided for the people engaged in these occupations, provide the major income of the area. The town is heavily reliant upon the single cannery which is owned and operated by the Pacific American Corporation. 

Terminal facilities of the port of King Cove are privately owned, consisting of twin wharves with a small berthing area between them, marine rail and slipways flanked by catwalks and oil terminal facilities, mooring buoys, dolphins and piling. Pacific American Corporation, the owner, also operates marine radio station KMS. Emergency repairs, minor maintenance, limited fuel oil, coal and water are kept on hand for emergencies. All wharves are of open-pile, timber construction. 

Least depths alongside docks exceed 35 feet below MLLW datum. Facilitities are considered adequate for commerce handled by larger ships, but provide no protection for the local commercial fishing fleet of 

46 boats. 

2. Navigation Problems. A lack of sheltered anchorage space is the principal difficulty faced by small boats operating in the King Cove area. During storm periods major damage has occurred to about 34 (74 percent) of the fishing fleet in the harbor. Areas available for anchorage outside the harbor are many miles away and difficult to reach in time of storm. Because of the exposed position of King Cove, the shallow entrance to King Cove Lagoon, ice conditions in winter months, high tidal ranges, and uncertainties of weather in the southwestern Alaska-Aleutian Island area, the use of this anchorage carries a high rate of risk. 3. 

Alternate Plans Considered. 

a. General. Four seperate plans of harbor development were investigated and rejected at King Cove before all aspects of the proposed plan were finalized. A brief resume of the pertinent features of each plan and the reasons why each was rejected follows below. b. Breakwater Plan 1. Local interests suggested that a 2800 feet long breakwater be constructed to inclose the upper 800 feet of King Cove. This would provide shelter for approximately 200 boats. 

Because of the relatively deep water at the location (up to 75 feet below MLLW) and the associated high cost of rubble mound breakwater construction, the plan was rejected as impractical. 42

c. Breakwater Plan 2. An alternate configuration for harbor development suggested by local interests included an "L" shaped breakwater protecting an area south of the existing dock facility. While the inner portions of the breakwater would be constructed in shallow water, some of the outer extremeties would be placed in water approximately 

50 feet deep. In this case, as in Breakwater Plan 1, the depths of water involved and the associated high cost of rubble mound breakwater construction dictated that further investigation of the plan be abandoned. d. Dredging Plan 1, An investigation was also conducted into the possibility of connecting King Cove with King Cove Lagoon via a dredged entrance channel. Alternate configurations of channel layout were studied, but the study was concluded after the investigation revealed that substantial dredging would be needed in the lagoon to provide an anchorage area, the anchorage area would be open to winds over three mile fetch in the lagoon, and that substantial maintenance would be required. e. 

Dredging Plan 2. Also considered was a plan involving the dredging of an entrance channel through the lower end of the counter spit; the construction of an earthen dike across the present channel; and the enlargement and deepening of the harbor thus formed. This plan, though simple in scope, is at variance with observed topographic conditions. 

It is believed that the natural forces that presently maintain the spit would rapidly shoal and fill any breach in the spit. Accordingly, the plan was given no further consideration. 

4. Plan of Improvement. The recommended plan of improvement consists of a dredged anchorage basin approximately 11 acres in area capable of accommodating 75 craft at swing-anchor, and a dredged earth training dike 800 feet in length to control tidal flows and sedimentation. Access to the basin would be provided through a 125 foot wide dredged entrance channel commencing in deep water of King Cove. The proposed depth of the basin and entrance channel is 15 feet below mean lower low water to accommodate deeper draft fishing vessels now plying the Alaska waters.
