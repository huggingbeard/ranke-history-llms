"HELLO MAC! How's every little thing with our future 

Attorney-General?" Closing the door of the unpretentious law-office he had entered with studied breeziness, Jed Soules, the haberdasher, thus jauntly saluted his friend, Hal H. 

McCleary, the most successful, and hence the most soughtafter lawyer in Western, who sat in the midst of a pile of law-books which were open, and placed one on another in several piles. Evidently he was hard at work on one of the knotty legal problems he loved so much to solve. "Morning Jed," smiled the lawyer, yet looking his visitor up and down with that disconcerting directness of gaze of his under which scores of shifty witnesses had squirmed and truculent ones quailed. "What's under this veneer of exuberance?"

The extraordinary insight into the hidden purposes of men, natural to McCleary, was one of the things which made him so greatly feared by opposing counsel.

The lawyer's question provoked Jed Soules; but he concealed the fact so well that only a man such as McCleary would have observed his annoyance. He had come to employ the lawyer in an important suit he intended to bring; but he knew his cause was not a righteous one. He knew, too, that before McCleary would permit himself to engage in any litigation, he must be convinced that his client's cause was just. Any client desirous of having the benefit of McCleary's services, had first to "sell" his case to the lawyer, who was a most discriminating buyer. Knowing all this, Mr. Soules, as a part of his salesequipment, had put on very skilfully, as he thought, an

appearance of easy good humor. To have this carefully practiced disguise completely penetrated in the first few moments of his interview, was both disconcerting and annoying. Nevertheless, he smilingly played his second card. 

He drew out and laid carelessly down under the eyes of the man of law a crisp new one hundred-dollar bill, remarking casually: "A little retainer, Mac." "Sit down, Jed," said the lawyer making no move to take possession of the bill, but settling himself to listen. "Now let's have the facts." By the time the story was told, many questions had formulated themselves in the lawyer's mindquestions, too, which his acute sense of justice forewarned him would, when answered, deprive him of all right to pocket the bill lying before him. Notwithstanding this prescience, and regardless of the results, he fixed his deep-set, cold, blue eyes, with their most penetrating gaze on the face of Jed Soules, and relentlessly asked him many most disturbing questions. His cross-examinations were always a terror to evildoers. It seemed that nothing in the mind of a witness could escape the lawyer's searching, pitiless questions.

As it was the ultimate truth he was always after, his questioning, whether addressed to an unwilling and hostile witness in court, or to a good friend and client in his office, was the same. Under his examination Jed Soules, as many before him had done, reluctantly but nevertheless completely revealed the truth. As soon as McCleary was satisfied that he had really got to the bottom of the matter, he touched the hundred-dollar bill for the first time, but not in the way men usually touch bills laid down for them. "You'd better settle that case, Jed," he said, as he pushed the bill in the direction of his would-be client, "without further ado."

Jed Soules made no move to take possession of the bill, but said: "Look here, Mac, I'll give you a check for two thousand dollars to represent me in this case." 

He was playing his last card. He knew the fee named was larger than McCleary would have charged and, by making it so large and offering to pay it all in advance, he

Hal H. McCleary was a man of the most exalted ideals.

VIII. 

HOWARD GATES never attended church. He had no objection to his wife and children's going; as for himself, he usually had some interesting trip or excursion planned for Sundays, and, quite often, his family were willing to share with him the pleasure such trips afforded. Possibly one Sunday the shining big car would be at the door at nine o'clock, ready for a jaunt of a hundred miles to the beach or the foothills; the next Sunday, with a slightly earlier start, they would be off for a sail in Mr. Gate's yacht on the shimmering waters of Mahogany Bay.

The morning after the events described in the preceding chapter, the Gates family sat down to breakfast, and the maid handed Gates the Daily News. It was not his custom to do more than scan the headlines at the breakfast table, unless something of unusual interest appeared, in which case, reading as he ate, he shared the news with his wife and older children.

Although he had little use for the church, per se, Howard Gates insisted on a standard of living both in his business and social relations considerably higher than is often found among people who are considered good church members. Recalling the blameless life Tom had lived since the day he attended the prayer-meeting in the office, Jed Soules felt a deep satisfaction permeating his being. Outside the bar of the court many members of Old Calvary waited with deep and sympathetic interest to hear the trial of the young man for whom concerted prayer had been made for weeks past. Among them was one whose interest was probably not less intense than that of Mr. and Mrs. 

Soules. She was sure that God would do the thing that was best for all. Even if Tom should actually have to go to prison, she was resolved that none of these things should move her. When, finally, Tom should be free, he would find her waiting for him at the prison gates. 

Sitting in the hotel lobby the night before, Howard Gates had drawn the prosecuting attorney into conversation. "I understand that it is the law," remarked the former, "that the prosecuting attorney although he draws a salary, gets a fee besides for every conviction."

"That is the way it is," answered the attorney, not at all averse to friendly conversation with the rich and influential Howard Gates, "and the practice is intended to put a little more pep into prosecutions than attorneys would show perhaps, if they got the same pay, win or lose." "Do you ever have men on trial who you feel are innocent of the crime with which they are charged." "Occasionally," the attorney replied, "and the fee-for-conviction system might with some attorneys, in such cases give them an interest in a conviction which the law does not warrant their having. They are officers of the court, and presumably should be as thoroughly interested in an innocent man's acquittal, as they are in a guilty one's conviction. In such cases the fee system is, in my judgment, not a good law." "I should think a straight salary would be better at such times," Mr. Gates replied, "but, of course, human arrangements can never be perfect.
