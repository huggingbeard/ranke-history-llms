INTRODUCTION For some years past Reform Temples throughout the country have been raising the Confirmation age from thirteen to fifteen or sixteen. Since this movement became widespread, a real need has been felt for a new Confirmation Manual. The older manuals were adequate in their day, but at the present time they are two or three years below the mental age of most Confirmation students. It has been our experience in teaching Confirmation classes that the boys and girls soon become dissatisfied with the over-simplification and the dogmatic manner of approach which characterize the older manuals and which perhaps were necessary when the material was being presented to younger children.

It was this feeling of dissatisfaction encountered in our students which prompted us to attempt the preparation of a manual which may better meet the needs of confirmants of the age of fifteen or sixteen.

We begin with the assumption that the boys and girls can and do think for themselves, and that, therefore, they ought to be reasoned with rather than indoctrinated. We have tried to think through the subject vii

INTRODUCTION 

with our students, and, wherever possible, to avoid being dogmatic. The material of this text was used for two years in our Confirmation classes before any attempt was made to put it into writing. 

We begin our book with a brief section devoted to a general discussion of religion. This is, in our estimation, an important departure from the older manuals. We believe that Confirmation students are old enough to be given a rational and truthful account of the origin, nature and development of religion, as well as some insight into the aspirations and problems which are common to all of the great religions of the world. A study of what religion is should be the preface to a study of what Judaism is. 

In our survey of Judaism we have tried to present the material which we felt was most vital and essential for Confirmation study. Much has been omitted and some topics have been only sketchily presented. We are fully aware of these omissions and defects. We can only plead that a Confirmation Manual, being necessarily brief, cannot possibly hope to be comprehensive. We have tried to be as fair and impartial as humanly possible. We willingly grant that there may be many valid disagreements with us on questions of emphasis or interpretation. That is one of the glories of Judaism, that it admits of such wide latitude of opinion within its confines.

CHAPTER ONE THE ORIGIN AND DEVELOPMENT OF RELIGION The Origin of Religion 

What is the origin of religion? This is a question which surely must run through your mind over and over again. For many years you have been studying the religion of Israel and without a doubt know that our tradition places its beginning many thousands of years ago with Abraham, the father of our faith.

However, we also know that many different types of religion flourished among the peoples of the earth long before Israel took its place in the arena of history. The Sumerians, the Egyptians, and the Chinese, for example, had religions of their own much before our own system of beliefs was introduced to the world. But these facts still do not answer our original query as to the actual beginnings of religion itself. When we face this question, we are confronted with one of the most profound and perplexing mysteries of the universe. Just when and where this 3

mighty force in human life first made itself felt we cannot determine. But this much we do know. As far back as we delve in history, the recorded life of man, we discover that every people had some kind of religion which influenced its life and shaped the goal for its strivings and aspirations.

Our wonder grows when we learn that the many peoples of the earth, very often different from one another in color of skin and language, and many of them separated by thousands of miles of mountains, plains, and water, have passed through similar stages of religious development. We are reasonably sure that religion is as old as the first human being who lived, labored, and died on our planet. This important aspect of his life was one of the outstanding qualities which distinguished him from the lower animals. It was an inseparable part of his mind and heart and filled one of the great needs of his earthly existence. Therefore, the evidence at our disposal compels us to believe, although we do not know when and where the first spark of divine faith was kindled, that religion was, is, and, in all likelihood, will remain a universal and an influential force in the development of mankind.

Religion is an Evolutionary Growth 

When you first commenced the study of religion, you were naturally taught the one you believe in, the 4

Jewish. From what we have already learned in this lesson, we of course know that our faith was first taught by Abraham who lived several thousands of years ago in the Mesopotamian valley. To him came the great discovery of the one God and he was impelled to go forth from his ancestral home to spread this wondrous truth among men and also to found the community of Israel. (Genesis XII, I ff.) 

When we read the Bible, however, we learn that certain types of belief and religious practices were in existence which did not harmonize with the lofty and sublime sentiments taught by Abraham and the teachers who came after him. We know that most of the people in his day were idol worshippers and did many things in the name of religion which would now appear to us as reprehensible. For example, when we read the attacks made by the teachers of Israel on child sacrifice (Leviticus xx, 2-5), we must come to the conclusion that at one time people used to burn their first-born children as offerings to the gods. This inhuman practice is far removed from the magnificent summary of religious duties voiced by the prophet Micah who said, "It hath been told thee,

O man, what is good and what the Lord doth require of thee, only to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God." (Micah vi, 8.)

So we see that in religion, as in everything else, there have been a number of successive stages in its 5

development. Religion has grown from very humble beginnings. Its steady and healthy growth is due to the fact that important and ofttimes revolutionary discoveries were made in the realm of the spirit just as they have been made in the field of science. For example, there is a long period of development that may be traced in history from the time when people were compelled to make long journeys from one place to another on foot or in animal-drawn vehicles to our present day when an aeroplane, guided by human hands, flew from one coast of the United States to the other in less than twelve hours. The secret of the aeroplane was locked in the fastness of nature until it was opened by man himself. So in like fashion the revelation of the one God and His eternal truths were ever present. But not until the great religious geniuses discovered Him in their own hearts did He become known among men. Before this thrilling discovery was made, mankind groped its way blindly through dark valleys of ignorance and superstition. Today men and women may easily make their way along the trail of spiritual light and truth if their hearts are attuned to the teachings of their religious writings and leaders.

Some Early Stages of Religious Development 

It should be of interest to you to learn some of the developments of religion from humble beginnings to

ORIGIN AND DEVELOPMENT 

its present advanced state. Through the careful and thorough studies that have been made of primitive man, we have been able to learn something about the earliest type of religious practice. This humble stage of religion is known as Animism. Animism is the belief that all the inanimate objects of nature possess life even as human beings are endowed with life. 

When primitive man bathed in a river and then became ill, he was convinced that it was an evil spirit lurking in the water that was responsible for his ailment. When a raging tempest swept through his frail dwelling, accompanied by the rumbling roar of thunder and the forked fire of lightning, he was certain that the spirits of the elements were consumed with wrath because of something which he had done, or failed to do. When the sun did not shine, when the rain did not fall, when his crops failed to grow, when his cattle perished, he was sure that he had committed some grievous fault. Unable to account for all of these bewildering whims of nature, he was driven to the belief that he was at the mercy of a host of spirits that must be appeased. So he worshipped them, sang hymns of praise to them, and offered them gifts even to the extent of his most precious possessions. 

Fetishism is the name which describes another stage in the religion of primitive man. Certain objects, such as sticks and stones, were regarded by him as sacred and precious and worthy of his worship. These

objects were sometimes the personal possessions of dead ancestors whose souls were considered to be present in them or they might belong to some outstanding leader of the tribe and hence possessed special value. Strict rules were enforced regarding the treatment of these sacred objects. They could not be profaned by the ordinary contacts of human beings. 

This attitude of respect and awe was described by the word "taboo." When an object was taboo, it meant that it was sacred and must not be profaned. 

Another interesting form of religion which developed among certain groups of people in early times is known as Totemism. Various animals, such as lions, wolves and tigers, were claimed as blood relatives by a tribe. They named their tribe after the animal from which they believed they were descended and worshipped it with many elaborate rites and ceremonies. 

The North American Indians are an example of a folk which developed this type of worship. 

In connection with the various types of religion which we have just described, many magical practices were introduced. People attempted to induce the clouds to give rain through pouring water on the ground. Charms and spells were employed to heal diseases. Medicine men, sorcerers and witches were familiar personalities in the practice of religion in the early days of mankind. These people were supposed to

have extraordinary supernatural powers which enabled them to do things beyond the ken of ordinary man. Their range of activity included everything from the casting out of demons from insane people to communicating with the souls of the dead. 

Religion Advances 

However, like the beautiful butterfly which rises above its origin in the humble and lowly caterpillar, so religion emerged from the layers of superstition, fear and ignorance which enveloped it for so many thousands of years. Myths and stories which grew around the gods worshipped by the many peoples of the earth were handed down by word of mouth from generation to generation and were finally put into writing. When this happened the literary history of religion began and we are able to watch its developments more closely. During the last six or seven thousands of years, many religious documents were written and some of them, including our own Bible, have been preserved until this day. As we examine them, we are able to behold the steady progress of religious ideas through the ages. We are able to see primitive man controlled to a great extent by his quest for food, clothing and shelter, and how out of his many needs and fears he created and worshipped hosts of gods.

As he became more at home in the world and as he

commenced to think of matters other than those surrounding his physical needs, his religious views advanced accordingly. In the course of time he asked himself pertinent questions about the origin and destiny of the universe, about the problems and purposes of life and upon what basis his relationship with his fellow men should be established. It was out of this kind of self-examination and through such searching questions that his religious beliefs were purified. 

Thousands of years have elapsed to mark his slow but sure rise from superstitious service of many gods to his discovery and worship of the one God. Time was when he offered up his children as sacrifices to the gods and later, when he abandoned this practice, birds and animals. Today he strives to reach God through prayer and good deeds. There was a period in his history when he could not distinguish good from evil, right from wrong, but now if he chooses to do so, he is able to make these important distinctions and to use them as guides in the fulfillment of his duties and responsibilities to his fellow men. Are we not all filled with wonder when we fully sense the significance of these great advances in the religious thinking of man? 

In the next chapter we shall turn our eyes to the religions of our own day and try to analyze the many important essentials which they all possess in common.

CHAPTER Two ESSENTIALS OF RELIGION 

The great religions of mankind have differed in many of their doctrines and teachings. Each has reflected the general atmosphere of that part of the world in which it originated. An occidental religion is different from an oriental religion. Each religion has borne the individual stamp of the people which created it. The religion of the Greeks differed very widely from the religion of the Hindus. And each religion within itself has revealed the influence of the economic conditions, the historical events, the political system and the social organization of the various ages through which it has passed. For example, after the beginning of the Dispersion in the year 70 C. E., Judaism had to undergo a number of transformations in order to adapt itself to the new conditions and circumstances of world-wide exile. Christianity today is very much different from Christianity of the Middle Ages.

Religions not only differ in their ideas and teach-. ings, but they also differ in many details such as cere- 12

ESSENTIALS OF RELIGION 

monials, holidays and prayers. In other words, they express themselves differently. The symbols that they use are different. These things, too, depend upon the individualities of various peoples and upon differences in environment. Each religion has its own type of sacred scripture. Each has distinctive types of prayers. 

Each has its own holidays. Each has a unique set of ceremonials. Religions vary, too, in the manner in which they are organized. Each religion during the course of its development changes its manner of organization. At one time our own religion had one central sanctuary, the Temple in Jerusalem, ministered to by a priestly class whose authority was hereditary. After the destruction of the Temple, each community had to organize its own religious institutions and select its own spiritual leaders.

From the above we can readily see that the religions of the world have always differed in very important respects; also, that each religion has changed during the course of its own history. Yet there are very important similarities which characterize all religions. 

Religion is universal in two senses. It is universal in the sense in which we discussed it in the first chapter, namely, that it exists everywhere and among all peoples. But it is universal, too, in the sense that it gives rise to certain fundamental ideas or beliefs which sooner or later are embodied in all religions. These fundamental beliefs are the foundation upon which 13

all religions are built. We may term them the Essentials of Religion. All religions have them just as all structures have foundations. Of course, they receive different interpretations in the various religions just as foundations may be of various sizes, shapes and manners of construction. But they exist and we can recognize them no matter what religion we study, whether it be our own or that of another group of people. 

A Belief in God

The first and perhaps the most important of these Essentials of Religion is the belief in a Supreme Being or beings, in God or in gods. This is the cornerstone of the foundation. Most religions possess this belief in some form or other. What that belief is depends largely upon how advanced in its development the religion is and how civilized are the people that follow that religion. Very early and primitive religions worshipped numerous gods; so that our fardistant ancestors thought that there was a power or spirit or god who was responsible for each thing that happened or existed in the world of nature, a Sun 

God, a Mountain God, a Storm God, a God of Thunder and other gods too numerous to list. Religions which believed in many gods we call Polytheistic. 

The next stage of development is known as Henotheism. This is the belief in one deity for each tribe 14

ESSENTIALS OF RELIGION 

or nation. But the highest point of development is reached in Monotheism, which is the belief in one God for the entire universe. Judaism justly claims for itself a position of leadership in religious thought because it was the first great religion to insist upon the absolute universality of God.

But no matter how primitive or advanced it may have been, practically every religion has been built around some idea of God. If we had the time we might study some of the very interesting ideas of God which religions have held in the past and hold at the present time. For the most part they have looked upon God as being good and wise and as ruling the universe for some good and wise purpose. Almost every religion in the history of mankind which has influenced the thoughts and actions of human beings has made a belief in God its central and most essential idea. 

The Moral Law 

The second of these Essentials of Religion which is shared by all religions grows directly out of a belief in God. This is the belief in moral law. The moral law is based simply upon the conviction that there is a way of living which is pleasing to a good and wise God and that human beings ought to follow that way of living. Just as God governs the material world in an orderly fashion through the laws of nature, so He governs human life in an orderly fashion through the 15

moral law. Just as there would be utter chaos in the physical world if there were no natural law, so there would be complete confusion in life if there were no moral law. The planets would collide and crash and bring annihilation to the entire universe if there were no law of gravity to hold them in their courses. Life would cease to exist if men would take each other's lives at will. Upon this principle all the great religions have agreed: that it is man's prime duty to live and think nobly in his personal life and to do good and practice justice in his relations with his fellow men. 

Of course, religions have formulated the moral law in different ways. They have understood it differently.

Some religions have emphasized the personal aspect of the moral law. Others have stressed the social aspect. But no matter how it has been formulated, personal purity or social righteousness or both have formed the kernel of the moral law for all religions. We shall go into a further analysis of the moral law when we come to study it in our own religion.

Life has a Great Purpose The third of these Essentials of Religion common to all the great religions of mankind is the belief in Purpose or Destiny. We may state this belief simply and broadly as follows: God has some definite plan 16

ESSENTIALS OF RELIGION 

or scheme in accordance with which the universe came into existence. This plan or scheme is working itself out in life. We can glimpse part of it in the history of our own world and mankind. And finally, this plan or scheme leads ultimately to some goal. All religions have some sort of belief in a "far off divine event toward which all creation moves." All religions believe that there is a definite program for life. But again they have differed in their interpretations or explanations of just what God's purpose may be. Some religions have described this purpose in terms of individual human happiness. In a general way this is true of the ancient religions of Greece. Others, like 

Judaism and some of the religions of India and China, have said that the goal will be reached when justice, righteousness, peace and brotherhood prevail upon earth, in other words, when there shall be happiness for the entire human family. Some religions have rejected the idea that there could be a goal in this life and for this world. They have set the goal in the hereafter, in some world to come, and have made this world merely the place of preparation, the antechamber to the future world. To a large extent, Christianity has always held this view. But however they have described it, all religions hold firmly and uncompromisingly to the belief that there is a goal for existence and a purpose to life. They have, with a very 17

few exceptions, vigorously opposed the materialistic view which regards the universe as a blind machine moving without aim or purpose and life a mere accidental product of that machine. In fact, we may almost say that this has been the great difference between the religious man and the non-religious man, that the religious man sees a purpose in life and the non-religious man does not.

Freedom of the Human Will 

Two Essentials of Religion still remain to be discussed. Although they may not have received as much emphasis in all the religions as the ideas we have just discussed, they are nevertheless important. The first is the doctrine of the freedom of the will. We may state it briefly thus. Although God has established a definite purpose for his creation, he has granted man the freedom either to put himself in harmony with that purpose or not, as he wills. Since we assume that 

God's purpose is good, we must also assume that man may not only have a knowledge of the difference between good and evil, but that he actually has the power to make a choice between good and evil, between truth and falsehood, a choice that he makes with his reason and executes in his actions. Man may will to think good or to think evil, to do good or to do evil. 18

ESSENTIALS OF RELIGION The Spiritual Elements in Life are Eternal 

The last of the Essentials of Religion is the doctrine of the Immortality of the Soul. Man is something more than flesh and blood, more than physical being. He is not only a creature who eats and drinks and walks and talks. He is possessed of an inner essence which causes him to think, to dream, to aspire, to hold ideals, to love, to sympathize with his fellows and to exercise all those noble qualities which he alone of all creatures possesses, which make him something more than an animal. This essence we call the Soul. 

And the Soul is immortal. It lives on after the physical body has decomposed into the dust which was its origin. Of course, each religion has its own unique conception of what immortality is. But that there is something in man which lives on after individual men and women die, nearly every religion has believed. 

Summary 

To summarize then, there are five fundamental ideas which we usually find no matter what religion we study, a Belief in God, Obedience to God's moral law, Purpose, Freedom of the Will, and Immortality of the Soul. Each religion has expressed these ideas in some original and different manner. But the ideas are present both in the religions of the past and in the re- 19

ligions of today. So basic and universal are these ideas that we may safely conclude that they will continue to be present in the religions of the future.

CHAPTER THREE THE  The Need of an Appreciation of Religion 

We usually take our most precious and cherished possessions for granted. For example, none of us would deny or deprecate the tender ties that bind us in love and devotion to our parents. And yet in our daily lives we rarely pause to take stock of the countless sacrifices that they have made and are ready to make for us. Nevertheless, we constantly derive a sense of safety and security through the ever present realization that we can always count upon their sustaining strength whenever we are in need or in trouble. Now this description of parent-child relationship may be applied in many respects to our connection with religion. We attend religious school once or twice a week, we participate in Divine services, we listen to sermons and lectures on religious themes, but withal we rarely, if ever, take pains to bring before our mind's eye a clear and complete portrait of 21

religion's many and valuable services to man through the long procession of past centuries and in our own times. And so it is our purpose in this chapter to sketch briefly for you some of the ways in which we have become heavily obligated to religion. Let us then examine what we shall call, for the sake of simplicity, the major and minor achievements of religion. 

Major Achievements of Religion

I. IT CULTIVATED THE MORAL LAW IN THE INDIVID- 

UAL. 

As far back as we can turn the pages of history, we find religion vitally concerned with the welfare of human beings. Even in its most primitive form, it made an effort to make the lot of the individual happier and more secure. As it developed on higher levels, it realized this aim more successfully. From religiously minded men came the first stern denunciation of the law of the jungle which permitted man to regard all people outside of his immediate family and tribe as his rightful prey. Robbery and murder were banned by the early teachers of religion and every type of exploitation and unfair advantage employed by one man to enslave his brother was severely censured. As the nature of religion grew more refined, it became at the same time more and more concerned with the moral nature of man. 22

ACHIEVEMENTS of RELIGION 

Religious teachings therefore endeavored to suppress the brutal nature of early man and to cultivate within him a sense of responsibility toward the other members of society, whether they were a part of his group or not. They attempted to make him a reliable person and to equip him with loyalties which could be appealed to in the name of the higher and better things of life. They implanted within him a spirit of sacrifice and taught him to value the welfare of others over and above his own selfish needs and desires. In a word, what religion did for the individual was to impress upon him this fundamental truth: that only when others are happy, he too may gain some measure of happiness. 

2. A HAPPIER AND BETTER WORLD FOR ALL MANKIND. 

It is evident, then, that religion made every effort to cultivate the moral nature of the individual. In addition, very early in its history, it also became vitally concerned with the status of society as a whole. It was through religious preachments that men became sensitive to and were able to identify all kinds of wickedness, injustice, hatred, oppression, and the other evils which keep humanity from making the progress which it should. It made men and women feel responsible for the sufferings of others even though they were not directly the cause of their plight. The presence of disease, of poverty, and of crime, was held 23

up as a reproach to all members of society by religion which sought to fill them with a burning desire to remove these blotches from the face of humanity. All our present-day movements in behalf of social justice and righteousness have their original sources in religious teachings. Our charitable institutions, such as orphanages, homes for the aged, and all types of relief agencies, came into existence because of religion's regard for the lot of the unfortunate and the suffering. This yeoman service which religion has been attempting to perform for mankind marks one of its major contributions to the world in which we live. 

3. HOPE, FAITH, AND COURAGE FOR THE INDIVIDUAL. 

Moreover, religion not only contributed to the welfare of the group and provided the individual with ideal and righteous standards of conduct, but it also inspired him with hope and courage to face the many and varied problems of life itself. It provided him with true and worthy goals for his earthly plans and labors. It strengthened him with comfort and consolation in the darkest hour of sorrow and bereavement.

It ever sustained him during those critical times when the misfortunes of life seemed ready to drag him into the very depths of hopelessness and despair. The man who accepted the gift of faith provided by religion was always able to live with a stout heart in the face of the greatest trials and tribulations. This type of 24

faith reached its sublimest expression in the life of 

Job who cried out in an hour of almost unbelievable suffering: "Though He slay me, yet will I trust in 

Him." (Job XIII, 15.)

Each one of us, if we but do a little serious thinking, may easily gain a greater appreciation of the enormous debt of gratitude which the world owes religion. 

The world would have remained a cheerless, hopeless, cruel and savage place to live in, if religion had not entered the hearts of men to direct their thoughts upward and onward to better and happier goals.

Minor Achievements of Religion I. ARCHITECTURE. 

The earliest forms of architecture which had definite design were religious structures such as sacrificial altars, sacred monuments, and temples of worship.

If we were to pay a visit to Palestine, Egypt or Greece, and examine the oldest architectural remains to be found in these countries, we should surely learn that they were originally built because of the religious needs of the first civilized people who dwelt in these countries. One need only look at the pyramids in 

Egypt to be convinced of this. Moreover, the finest types of architecture to be seen in Europe today are churches and cathedrals. Gothic architecture, for example, traces its origin to and receives its grandest 25

and completest expression in religious structures. 

One is overpowered by the majestic beauty of such cathedrals as are to be found in Paris, Chartres, Rheims, Rome and London. The builders of these edifices were inspired by their task and one almost feels that their pious emotions were blended with the very stone from which these places of worship were built. 

2. PAINTING. 

The art of painting had its beginning in the religious nature of man. When archaeologists uncovered the caves of men who lived thousands of years ago, they were able to discern crude drawings and paintings on the walls of these humble dwellings. Most, if not all, of this primitive art dealt with religious ideas and subjects. And all through the ages since those early days of man's life on earth, the greatest and most beautiful murals and pictures have been inspired by religious themes and personalities. The most famous masterpieces which decorate the walls of Europe's art galleries and salons bear testimony to this truth. How many have seen the originals or copies of paintings by Rembrandt, Leonardo da Vinci, Titian, and

Rossetti, with their beautiful treatment of sacred subjects? Have you a copy in your school of Michaelangelo's immortal portrayal of the "Prophets" which 26

may be seen in the original at the Sistine chapel in Rome? 

3. SCULPTURE. 

The development of sculpture to a great art is due for the most part to the fact that it became a medium in the hands of religious people for the expression of their desire to worship through the spirit of beauty. 

Idols, totem poles, and the many vessels connected with the rites and ceremonies of religion are among the earliest specimens of sculptural skill. A visit to the British Museum, where are housed some of the ancient specimens of sculpture, will assure us of this connection between religion and sculpture. Probably the finest specimens of religious art in ancient times are the sculptures to be found at the entrance to the Temple of Ninip. The many decorated figures and icons which still may be seen in the churches of the world are additional evidence of how the religious impulse was the original source for the plastic arts. 

4. LITERATURE AND POETRY. 

It should not be difficult for us to understand the fact that the earliest forms of literature were sacred writings. The first documents ever to be written by the hand of man were inspired by religious thoughts. 

In fact, if we study the literary history of the many 27

peoples of the world today, we shall find that the incentive behind their earliest written compositions was religious in nature. The greatest literary masterpiece ever composed is our Bible, which was written thousands of years ago. The Vedic scriptures of the Hin- 

Idus and the Koran of the Mohammedans are additional illustrations of the influence of religion on literary composition. In addition, the most ancient forms of poetry were praises of the gods in blank verse. And much of the subsequent development of poetry from the days of religious scriptural writing to the present grew out of man's desire to express his religious emotions and ideas in poetic form. 

5. MUSIC AND DANCING. 

The earliest songs known to man were hymns of praise to their gods. Primitive man found expression for his religious emotions in the composition and rendition of sacred melodies. In Bible times the 

Psalms were composed so that the priests might sing songs unto their heavenly Father in the Temple at

Jerusalem. Church and synagogue music eventually became the foundation for our present great development in that artistic field.

Dancing also traces its origin to religious worship. 

Sacred dances accompanied by the rhythmic beat of the tom-tom can be traced back to the very beginnings of human history. Most of the religious festivals that 28

were celebrated in ancient times were marked by dancing of one kind or another. In our own day many of the tribes of the North American Indians solemnize the coming of certain religious festivals with song and dance. 

6. DRAMA. 

The first plays ever to be written were designed as dramatic spectacles which exhibited religious ideas and events. Both comedy and tragedy began with the people who observed the rites and ceremonies of early Greek religions. In medieval times, plays, pageants and tableaux were regularly conducted by the church in connection with religious ceremonies. 

7. MEDICINE. 

The art of healing began as an inseparable part of religion. The ancient medicine man who advised the members of the tribe how to cure their ailments and diseases was at the same time a religious priest. Since it was believed that all bodily ailments were caused by demons and spirits, it was necessary for a specialist in religion to cast out these evil influences through charms, counter-charms, incantations, and through the application of certain herbs. In the book of Leviticus of our own Bible, we find many injunctions given to the people in the name of religion for the safeguarding of their health. Among the many interesting 29

medical facts which are to be found there, is the one dealing with the principle of quarantine as a protective measure against the spread of communicable diseases. 

Moreover, it should be of interest to us to know that the first hospitals were built and operated by religious orders. Medicine is of course no longer associated with religious practices, but it is to religion that we must go back for the earliest beginnings of the art. 

8. LAW AND GOVERNMENT. 

The whole science of government and law upon which our civilization is based rests upon religious doctrines and teachings. If one were to remove, for example, from the laws of the United States government those ideas which grew out of religion, the government would collapse and the people of the country would be thrown into chaos. The very structure of law is the result of religious thinking. What would the world do without the Ten Commandments and the many implications to be derived therefrom?

Many more achievements of religion could be mentioned, but enough have been set forth to enable us to see why religion has earned the title of "Queen of the 

Arts and Sciences." Of course most of the minor achievements of religion are today no longer classified under the heading of religion, although they were originally sponsored by religion. However, we would gain a true appreciation of the place that religion has 30

occupied in the lives of men if we should stop to consider that religion has been the fountain-head of inspiration of most of the beautiful and useful activities and institutions which we see about us today.

Origin of the Hebrews-Abraham 

Our remote ancestors who became the founders of our religion were members of a great language group known as the Semites and when we first meet with them in history, their home is in the vast Arabian desert. There for many centuries they pursued the life of nomadic shepherds. They roamed from place to place looking for fertile areas amid the arid bleakness of the sand where they could find water and fodder for their cattle. Untouched by the temptations and distractions of settled and organized civilizations, their lives were marked by an austere code of conduct. 

Their religion consisted mainly in the worship of those spirits whom they believed to dwell in stones, trees, oases, and the sun, moon and stars, under which they travelled and slept. In a word, they were worshippers of nature and their type of religion is called Animism. 41

In the course of time, food and water for their herds became less and less abundant and they were compelled to push farther and farther north in their quest for these vital necessities. Gradually a number of these primitive desert tribes penetrated into the territory which lies to the north, the northeast and the northwest of Arabia. There they found conditions easier and began to live more settled lives. Some of these tribes moved into that part of Asia Minor which is known as the Mesopotamian Valley, a very fertile strip of land which lies between the Tigris and the Euphrates rivers. It was among one of these tribes in Mesopotamia that our first great Hebrew personality, Abraham, was born.

It is evident from the records which we possess that Abraham rebelled against the type of religion which was practiced by his family and neighbors. He did a great deal of thinking and he finally came to the conclusion that the idolatrous practices which he saw about him were foolish and vain. He discovered for the first time that there is only one God in the universe who cannot be found in one particular place or worshipped through images and idols. CHAPTER Two THE JEWISH IDEA OF GOD 

We have already learned that the idea of God is the first and most essential idea in Religion. In fact, we can say that the greatness of any religion may be judged by the kind of God which it worships. We 

Jews may be justly proud of the fact that early in its history our religion already had developed a very lofty idea of God. This is all the more remarkable when we consider that our ancestors were numerically always a very small people living in the midst of great and warlike nations. These nations possessed beliefs and carried on practices which were quite contrary to those that our fathers held. They worshipped the powers of nature such as the sun, the moon, the wind and the storm. They represented these powers of nature in images of metal, wood and stone. They practiced methods of worshipping these deities which were ofttimes terrible and cruel.

Our fathers sometimes were disloyal enough to follow the practices of these idolatrous religions. It is only human to want to imitate our more powerful 49

neighbors. Then prophets would arise to chide them for their backsliding and urge them to return to their God. The Unity of God 

First of all and most important of all, Judaism insists that God is One, absolutely One. That is the basis of our religion. It is gloriously expressed in the grand watchword of our faith, "Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One." But as if this were not emphatic enough-and in the ancient world it could not be made too emphatic-the fact that God is One removes the possibility of the existence of other gods or divine powers of any sort. "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." (Exodus xx, 2.) Judaism thus became the arch enemy of Polytheism and Idolatry. God alone may be worshipped and He only is worthy of worship. "I am the Lord, that is My name and My glory will I not give to another, neither My praise to graven images." (Isaiah XLII, 8.) 50

God is its creator and He governs it according to the laws which He has established. These laws are of two kinds, the law of the physical world and the moral law which governs the life of man. The Unity of 

God also implies that He is the source of all that exists. He is the source of life, and life is sacred to Him. The Spirituality of God

The beliefs of Judaism concerning the brotherhood of man flow naturally from its central conviction that God is One. CHAPTER FOUR THE MORAL LAW IN JUDAISM 

In our discussion of the Essentials of Religion we found that the second most important element in every religion is the belief in a moral law. This belief is second in importance only to a belief in God and depends upon it. Let us restate briefly what we mean by the moral law. The moral law governs the lives of human beings and regulates their conduct toward each other. It is comparable to natural law which rules the physical world and regulates the various parts of nature and their adjustments to each other. We believe that God established the laws of nature. Sometimes we are arrogant enough to think that we have discovered these laws just because we state them in human language. But in reality we only have an imperfect knowledge of how these laws work. The same is true of the moral law. Man formulates moral laws but they are only an attempt to express in human terms the type of life which we believe is pleasing to God who is our ideal. 

Our moral insight has grown and developed and 63

changed from age to age, just like our scientific knowledge. Nevertheless, there are some fundamental rules of morality which seem to be constant and eternal just like the basic axioms of mathematics. 

Holiness, the Ideal of Jewish Morality

With this review in mind, let us turn now to a consideration of the moral law in our own religion. 

We shall expect to discover that our moral law grows directly out of our belief in God. And so it does. God is the moral ideal in Judaism. He is morally perfect. Therefore man, who is spiritually formed in God's image, should strive to make himself morally as godlike as it is humanly possible to be. That is what we mean by Holiness. Holiness is the attempt to pattern our lives after what we believe to be the Divine Model. "Holy shall ye be, for 

I the Lord your God am Holy." (Leviticus xix, 2.) Thus in Judaism, religion and morality are closely bound together. They go hand in hand. Living a moral life is the finest way that we have of serving God.

How We Learn to be Moral 

Now let us examine a bit more closely the moral law of Judaism. We have already seen that it is based upon the conviction that there is a definite kind of life which God wants us to live. But that 64

MORAL LAW IN JUDAISM 

is not all. How can we know the kind of life that 

God wants us to live? Judaism maintains that there is a real difference between what is right and what is wrong, just as there is between truth and falsehood. By constantly practicing the moral principles of our religion, we may learn to distinguish fairly accurately between right and wrong, between good and evil. There is nothing mysterious about it. It is just another illustration of the truth of the old maxim, practice makes perfect. Through long experience, Judaism has developed certain rules of morality which it believes are right and in accordance with God's will. We become moral, first by learning those rules, and secondly and more important, by habitually carrying them out in our daily lives. We might elaborate the above quoted proverb intomoral practice makes for moral perfection. One of the later Jewish sages put it succinctly when he said, "Not only the learning, but the doing, is important."

The Ten Commandments 

The basis of Jewish moral law is to be found in the Ten Commandments. We may best think of the Ten Commandments as the Moral Constitution of Judaism, from which all of its other laws are derived. In fact, the importance of the Ten Commandments is not confined to Judaism. We may almost think of them as universal moral laws. Through 65

experience humanity has found the Ten Commandments to be such an excellent code of moral guidance that they are recognized as fundamental by practically all civilized people of modern times. Morris 

Joseph, a well-known Jewish writer truthfully says, "And today the Commandments still continue to search out each heart, but each heart in a yet mightier company. Jew and Gentile alike confess their power. 

They are written on the walls of synagogue and church: they are engraved on the conscience of mankind. They are the world's law for all time. Never will their empire cease."

Suppose now that we analyze the Ten Commandments. We will discover that in a general way they deal with two subjects. These are the relationship between the individual Jew and God, and the relationship between the individual Jew and his fellow man. The first three commandments deal exclusively with our duties toward God. The first commandment asks us to recognize His existence as well as the debt of gratitude which the Jewish people owes Him. The second commandment is a prohibition of idolatry or the representation and worship of God in any material form. This is of extreme importance in 

Judaism. In fact, it distinguishes Judaism from all ancient religions as well as from some modern religions. The third commandment, which forbids our taking God's name in vain, is merely another way of 66

MORAL LAW IN JUDAISM 

teaching us that when we approach God we ought to approach Him in the spirit of reverence. 

The next commandment, enjoining us to observe the Sabbath, emphasizes the importance of days of rest. Thus it points out to us the value of setting aside certain days, not only Sabbaths but Holidays as well, upon which we can obtain physical relaxation. This gives our minds an opportunity to dwell upon higher and nobler thoughts than we are daily accustomed to think. Not only we, but persons who work for us, even the animals who help us in our labor, are entitled to such regular periods of rest. 

Then comes the injunction to honor our parents.

Parents take the place of God upon earth and we owe them some of the same reverence and respect which we owe to God. Upon this fifth commandment Judaism bases its insistence on that purity and unity of family life for which our people has always been noteworthy.

The last five commandments are concerned with our conduct toward our fellows. They are very properly worded as prohibitions. There are certain rights which every human being possesses which we may under no circumstances transgress. Every human being has a sacred right to live as long as he can. Therefore it is wrong wilfully to deprive any individual of life. Nor dare we consciously take away from any other person that which rightfully 67

belongs to him, his just possessions, the happiness of his family life, his honor and his reputation. The last commandment is very significant. It furnishes us with an excellent rule by which we may guard ourselves against violating the four preceding commandments. It warns us not to covet anything which belongs to our neighbors. If we discipline ourselves not to be envious of another, we will rarely fall victim to the temptation to kill or to steal or to gain wrongful possession of what does not belong to us.

Some Other Jewish Moral Principles 

The Ten Commandments are the general moral principles of Judaism but they are by no means all of the moral laws of the Jewish religion. The Ten 

Commandments themselves are elaborated upon and applied to specific cases in other laws of the Torah, in the teachings of the Prophets, in the maxims of the Book of Proverbs, in the laws of the Mishnah and Talmud, in the ethical writings of the later 

Rabbis and sages. There is the Law of Holiness which we have already discussed. Judaism also teaches us to be chaste in our thought and clean in our personal lives. It tells us specifically what we must do in order to fulfill completely our social obligations to other human beings, to care for the widow and the orphan, to provide for the poor, the aged and the ill, to champion the cause of the oppressed and the 68

MORAL LAW IN JUDAISM 

downtrodden. Jewish morality is thus not only negative but positive as well. It not only provides us with moral rules for our daily lives, but it also lays upon us as Jews the moral duty of laboring together for the constant improvement of the world in which we live.

CHAPTER FIVE THE PLACE OF SYMBOLS AND CEREMONIES IN JEWISH LIFE All Religions Possess Them 

As far back as we can go in the history of man, we learn that he invariably attempted to express his sentiments and beliefs in concrete form. Ideas and thoughts are in themselves not capable of stirring the hearts and minds of men unless they can be presented in such a way as to appeal to the imagination. Only in this way can the loyalties and interests of people be preserved and strengthened in matters which have been designed for their best interests. 

Thus we find every group possessing numerous customs, symbols and ceremonies which portray in concrete and dramatic form the history and content of its religious life. Through such means religion was always kept fresh and vital in the lives of succeeding generations of its adherents. As a result, we find that the individual from birth until death was surrounded by ceremonial institutions common to the people with whom he made his home. 70

SYMBOLS AND CEREMONIES Their Significance in our Daily Lives 

Now this fact which we have just described is not only true of our religion, but may also be applied to many significant aspects of our lives. In our home life we observe many customs which are primarily the concern of our dear ones and ourselves. Birthdays and anniversaries become the occasions for annual joyous celebrations. The ties of home life are bound closer about us because of these observances. 

Another important illustration of the rôle which ceremonialism plays in our lives is to be seen in our national life. 1. THEY PRESERVE OUR RELIGIOUS HERITAGE. 

A little reasoning on our part should convince us of the great importance which we should attach to the proper observance of the Jewish festivals and Holy Days. Among the many reasons which may be advanced for this attitude are three that deserve most careful consideration. In the first place we must be ever mindful of the fact that without its festivals and Holy Days Judaism would lose much of its meaning and inspiration. For it is through the setting aside of certain days upon which we recall outstanding events in the history of our people that we retain our desire to rededicate ourselves to the purposes and ideals which inspired them and to strengthen our allegiance to the religion which made them possible. 

Our festivals and Holy Days, therefore, cultivate 76

within us a sense of piety, which is a loyal and reverential attachment to the sources of our faith. 

2. THEY CULTIVATE OUR RELIGIOUS NATURE. 

In the second place, faithful observance of the festivals and Holy Days helps us stimulate the religious side of our natures. We all know that, in order to retain our bodily strength, we must take regular physical exercise. If we fail to do so, our limbs and vital organs become weak through disuse. In a like fashion our spiritual faculties require expression at frequent intervals if they are to remain strong and alive. Those of us who do not avail ourselves of the opportunities presented by the Sabbath, our festivals and Holy Days, for spiritual exercise, will find that their interest in religion will grow less and less because of their neglect. If we are to remain staunch followers of Judaism, we should take advantage of these additional occasions in our religious life for attendance at Temple and for the performance of religious duties in our home. 

3. THEY HELP PRESERVE THE UNITY OF OUR PEOPLE. 

In the third place, throughout Jewish History our festivals and Holy Days have played a prominent rôle in keeping our people together as a religious community. No matter how scattered the Jews were in the various countries and among the many nations 77

of the world, they were all united by the same thoughts and the same emotions on their festivals and Holy Days. The Jews of the world today are divided among many countries where they speak the native language and have become an inseparable part of the national life in the places where they now make their homes. What is it then that unites, for example, the Jews of France and the Jews of Germany, with the Jews of America, if it is not their common historical past and religious heritage? So we can see once again why it is so important that we keep alive those memories which help us preserve our existence as a people.

We shall now turn our attention to a brief explanation and summary of the principal Jewish festivals and Holy Days.

The Sabbath 

The fourth of the Ten Commandments, "Remember the Sabbath Day to keep it holy," admonishes us to observe and preserve the Sabbath as a day of rest and prayer. Since the Sabbath is the only Jewish holiday which is mentioned in the Ten Commandments, we can well understand why it has always been considered the most important occasion in our religious calendar. The Sabbath has been looked upon by the Jew as a day set apart from the other days of the week for rest and religious assemblage. It is 78

through the Sabbath that Jews throughout the ages learned to recognize the fact that they are not only toiling creatures interested in making a living, but that they also possess spiritual aspirations which need expression.

In our modern age it has become increasingly difficult to observe the Sabbath as a day of complete rest. Nevertheless, every effort should be made to mark the day as one different from the rest of the week. Children who are attending religious school and who have the opportunity of participating in the Sabbath service should do so willingly. Only in this way are they able to develop temple-going habits which will remain with them in the years to come. 

The Jewish calendar reckons the day from sunset to sunset in accordance with the tradition set forth in the Biblical story of Creation: "and there was evening and there was morning, one day." (Genesis 

1, 5.) The Sabbath therefore commences on Friday evening just as soon as three stars are visible to the eyes and continues until the same time on Saturday evening. The Sabbath is observed in our places of worship with special services which include many beautiful and inspiring prayers. Each Sabbath takes its name from the Hebrew name for the particular section of the Torah which is read in the synagogue on that Saturday morning. The Torah or Pentateuch is divided into fifty-four sections. These are read 79

in regular order during the year at the Sabbath morning service. The annual cycle of readings begins on the first Sabbath after Succoth, known as Shabbath Bereshith or the Sabbath of Beginning. Each section is called the Sidra. In addition to the Sidra, selections are read from the prophetic writings of the Bible. This additional selection is known as the Haftarah.

In addition to attendance at Divine worship, the Sabbath should be fittingly observed in the home. 

Friday evening should be one period of the week set aside for family reunion and communion. The 

Sabbath candles should be kindled by the mother of the household. The father should recite the Kiddush service over the wine and bread. The parents, as well as their children, should participate in a brief service before partaking of their Sabbath meal and thus give thanks unto God for blessings which they have received at His hand during the week just past. Only through these ceremonies can the traditional beauty and sanctity of the Sabbath be preserved as ennobling influences in the life of the Jew. 

Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur 

On the first day of the Hebrew month of Tishri we are commanded to solemnize the Hebrew New Year or Rosh Hashanah in accordance with the Biblical injunction, "In the seventh month, in the first 80

day of the month, shall be a solemn rest unto you, a memorial proclaimed with the blast of horns, a holy convocation." (Leviticus XXIII, 24.) Rosh

Hashanah is a most significant and important occasion in Jewish life. It is a day set aside for selfexamination and for self-consecration to a better and nobler life during the year to come. As we read our prayers and sit in silent meditation in the synagogue, we are able to view more clearly and more honestly our past purposes and deeds and to judge which of them are cause for joy and which of them are reason for self-reproach. The day is most solemn but at the same time it brings a message of hope and faith in man's ability to make himself more worthy of God's guidance and love. A special ceremony connected with this Holy Day is the blowing of the 

Shofar or ram's horn, which represents a symbolical call unto man to prepare himself for a better way of living.

Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, is the most solemn day in the Jewish year. It always comes ten days after Rosh Hashanah. The ten days between 

Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are known as the

Return or Shabbath Shubah. The observing Jew re mains throughout the Day of Atonement in his synagogue, where, through fasting and prayer, he makes sincere atonement for his sins and implores the Father of Mercy to grant him forgiveness. 

Pesach, Shabuoth, and Succoth

Pesach, Shabuoth and Succoth are the three important agricultural festivals celebrated by the Jew. 

They are days of joyous assemblage in the synagogue. In ancient times, they were celebrated by all 

Jews through pilgrimages to the Temple in Jerusalem where they brought the best products of their farms and vineyards.

The Feast of Passover, or Pesach, begins on the evening of the fourteenth day of the Hebrew month of Nisan and lasts for seven days, with the first and seventh days observed as days of religious convocation. This festival has a double significance for the 

Jew. It is the Jewish feast of liberty because it reminds us of those stirring days when our ancestors were mercifully delivered by God from Egyptian bondage. At the same time it heralds the coming of 

Spring. The outstanding features of the celebration of Passover are the eating of unleavened bread or Matzoth and the beautiful Seder service in the home. 82

The Feast of Weeks, or Shabuoth, begins on the evening of the sixth day of the Hebrew month of Sivan and is observed for one day. This festival reminds us of the giving of the Ten Commandments at Mount Sinai, which according to tradition took place on Shabuoth, and of the bringing of the first ripe fruits to the ancient Temple in Jerusalem.

The Feast of Booths, or Succoth, is observed five days after the Day of Atonement and begins on the evening of the fifteenth day of Tishri. It lasts for eight days with the first and eighth days as days of religious assemblage and worship. It reminds us of those early days in our history when Israel dwelt in booths in the desert under God's benign protection and it also heralds the gathering of the later harvest in Palestine. Many Jews throughout the world today eat their meals in frail but beautifully constructed booths or Succoth and in this fashion attempt to relive the experiences of their forefathers. Many synagogues also construct booths on their altars so that the congregation as a whole may participate in the Succoth spirit. Among the beautiful symbols employed in the observance of Succoth are the branches of palm trees or Lulab, the citron or Ethrog, the boughs of thick trees or Hadasim, and the willows of the brook or Arabit. 83

Chanukah and Purim

Through the festival of Chanukah we are reminded of the physical and moral victory which the Israelites, under the guidance of the heroic Hasmoneans, gained over the pagan forces of Syria, who attempted to destroy their religious faith and substitute in its stead their own idolatrous practices. 

Chanukah is primarily a festival which is observed in the home through the lighting of candles for eight successive days and the recitation of blessings over them in thankfulness to God for His kindness and providence in guiding the destinies of Israel through dark and fateful hours.

CHAPTER SEVEN THE LITERARY SOURCES OF JUDAISM The Bible 

The most important source for the ideals and teachings of any great religion is to be found in its 

Sacred Scriptures. Sacred Scriptures, as we remem ber from our study of the Essentials of Religion, are a basic part of all the great religions of mankind. 

The Bible constitutes the Sacred Scriptures of Juda ism and is therefore the basis and source of the teachings of the religion of Israel. If we want to know most authoritatively what we Jews believe about God, Brotherhood, the Moral Law, Justice and other religious ideals, we must go to the Bible. 

We can, however, define the Bible more explicitly than by the use of the phrase "Sacred Scriptures."

The Bible is really the written record of the development of the Jewish Religion from its earliest beginnings to the time when it reached a high peak of very advanced religious ideas such as the Unity of God and attained the lofty ethical ideals of universal 88

peace and justice. Since, as we have already noted, there is such a close connection between the religion of Israel and the people of Israel, the Bible is also the historical record of the experiences of the Jewish people during a period of almost a thousand years. 

Since the Bible does cover such a long period in the history of our religion and our people, it is obviously not one book. In fact, the Greek word "biblia" from which the English word Bible comes, means "books." The Bible is a literature, a collection of books. Nor were these books written at one time or by any one person but in different centuries, between 1200 B. C. E. to 150 B. C. E. It was written by many persons and it dealt with many different phases of Jewish life and religious thought. There are historical books like Samuel and Kings, books of law like Leviticus, a book of religious poetry like Psalms, prophetic books and so forth.

Finally, these books which had been written during the early periods in Jewish History were collected and placed in an authorized edition usually known as the Canon of the Scriptures. This was done some time during the second century B. C. E. by the Men of the Great Assembly, who were the religious authorities of that time. There were many more religious books in existence, but only those which were most worthy and considered most important were included in the Bible. 89

We can see that the editors of the Bible went about their task very intelligently. They tried to give us a thorough account of the history of Israel up to their time. They included laws representative of every period in that history. They told us what the religious ideas of the people were at the time of the earliest beginnings of the religion. Then, by way of contrast, they gave the most exalted and inspiring religious teachings that mankind has ever known, showing to what a high point the religion of Israel had developed in a comparatively short time. Thus the Bible has become a true and complete reflection of the life and thought of our forefathers.

Other Jewish Writings 

Although the Bible is the primary source for our knowledge of our faith, we must not imagine that it is the only source. There is a vast treasure of 91

Jewish literature which has accumulated through the ages since the Bible. In these other Jewish writings, great Jewish teachers, sages, rabbis, philosophers, commentators and poets, have tried to explain and interpret the spirit as well as the teachings of the Bible to their own and to succeeding generations. 

In each age there have been leaders and thinkers who have attempted to adapt the beliefs and practices of Judaism to the needs and conditions of that age. 

Judaism grew and developed not only during the period of the Bible. It continued to develop after the 

Bible and down to the present time. We hope that it has not ceased developing even now. The record of that later development we will find in the Jewish Literature which came after the Bible. We cannot completely understand the religious life and thought, the culture and tradition of Israel, unless we know something about that later literature. We shall here be able only to set down a few descriptive facts about the more important Jewish literary products. If we really want to come to appreciate the greatness and richness of Jewish Literature, we shall have to read it for ourselves.

The Apocrypha 

In discussing the Bible, it was pointed out that there were other religious books in existence among the Hebrews in addition to those which were in- 92

Some were regarded as being too mystical to be understood by the people. Still others were influenced by foreign, particularly Greek, thought and therefore not in accord with the true spirit of Judaism. In addition, there were books which were written after the Canon of the Bible had been definitely fixed.

A large number of these books, however, have been preserved in a collection known as the Apocrypha. Apocrypha is a word of Greek origin meaning "hidden writings" and refers to those books which were not included in the Bible for one reason or the other. Although the Apocrypha is not as important as the Bible, it nevertheless contains some fine and interesting books. For example, there is I and II

The Mishnah 

The Mishnah is a very important part of Jewish Literature. After the Bible was completed, it was 93

necessary to explain and interpret its teachings, especially the laws of the Torah, to the people. These laws of the Bible were general principles which had to be applied to specific instances or cases in daily life. Furthermore, some of the laws had to be changed or elaborated upon to meet changing conditions. The Constitution of the United States or of any other country is constantly being interpreted and expanded and adapted to new conditions through the laws passed by legislative bodies. 

Each law and teaching of the Torah was thus considered and many new laws and explanations derived from it by our sages and rabbis. These were at first taught orally and transmitted from age to age. Finally this material became so vast that it was no longer possible to preserve it as oral tradition. It had to be edited, classified in systematic form and written down. After several attempts, a final and permanent edition was made by Rabbi Judah Ha Nasi about the year 200 c. E. This collection is called the 

Mishnah, which means "The Oral Tradition." The 

Mishnah is thus a commentary on the Torah. Although its content consists largely of laws, it contains a great deal of illustrative material. It also includes ethical instruction like the section known as the "Sayings of the Fathers." The many teachers whose opinions, explanations and teachings are found in the Mishnah are known as Tannaim. 94

The Mishnah later became part of a more comprehensive work, the Talmud. The Mishnah, too, soon became inadequate to meet the changing social, political and economic circumstances forced upon the Jewish people by the Dispersion. The Mishnah itself had to be explained and expanded. So in the succeeding centuries there grew up a larger body of laws, interpretations and traditions known as the Gemarra, which means "completion." That is, the 

Gemarra completed the work of the Mishnah. Mishnah and Gemarra were finally edited together about 500 c. E. in the collection known as Talmud, which means "teaching."

The work of developing the Mishnah was carried on simultaneously in the rabbinical academies of

Palestine and Babylon. So that we have two versions of the Talmud, the Palestinian and the Babylonian Talmuds. The Babylonian Talmud, however, has always been regarded as the more complete and the more authoritative. The Talmud contains a complete code of Jewish Law. But it is much more than law. 

In discussing various laws, it branches out into a discussion of almost every aspect of human life and knowledge. It is a veritable ocean of human wisdom. 

It contains almost every type of literature, fiction, fable, poetry, philosophy, discussions on ethical and 95

moral problems, treatises on medicine and other sciences and even humor. The purely legal parts of the Talmud are called Halacha. The non-legal parts are called Agada. They are not separated, however, in the text of the Talmud but intermingled. The hundreds of teachers who were responsible for the development of the Talmud and whose work we find quoted in it are known as Amoraim.

The Talmud has wielded a tremendous influence on Jewish life throughout the centuries. It was the guide by which our fathers for almost two thousand years regulated the conduct of their daily lives and affairs as well as their religious customs and ceremonies. Next to the Bible, it is our most important source for a knowledge of Judaism. 

The Midrash 

Another very old literary product is the Midrash.

The Midrash, like the Mishnah and Gemarra, is also an attempt to explain the Bible to the people. But it differs somewhat from them. It is not quite as legal in its style although it does contain some law. It represents an effort on the part of the Jewish teachers to capture the spirit of the Bible rather than its letter. It is like a collection of sermons. In fact, we may best think of the Midrash as being the sermons which the ancient Rabbis preached on texts from the Bible. 96

Prayer, both private and public, is an important and essential part of the Jewish religion. Every good 

Jew does and should pray regularly. All of us have felt and throughout our lives will feel many times the necessity of reaching out for help to some Power greater than ourselves. Prayer is the only way that we know of communicating with God. As the Psalmist puts it, "I lift mine eyes unto the hills. Whence cometh my help? My help cometh from God." 

(Psalms CXXI.) Prayer as Help in Time of Need 

Prayer can be of real help to us at those times of sorrow, difficulty and illness which come at some time or other to all human beings. Prayer gives us courage with which to bear our hardships, strength against temptation and guidance in making vital moral decisions. We feel better after we have prayed, after we have poured out our hearts. Judaism has always believed that God gives heed to our petitions ΙΟΙ

if they are worthy and unselfish. That faith sustained our fathers in their times of greatest stress. 

Prayer makes us braver. It ennobles us. Honest prayers of repentance help us to overcome our sense of guilt when we have sinned.

Prayer as an Expression of Gratitude 

However, prayer is more than a petition for blessings that we need. It gives us an opportunity to express our reverence and love for God. It enables us to show our gratitude to Him for blessings that we have already received, to voice our thanksgiving for 

His past mercies both to others and to ourselves. "O give thanks unto the Lord for He is good, for His mercy endureth forever." (Psalm cvII.) Prayer also provides us with utterance for our fondest hopes and highest aspirations.

Our Attitude Toward Prayer 

We must remember though that prayer, if it is to be most helpful, should be carried on in a certain frame of mind. Prayer should be sincere. There is a difference between repeating prayers mechanically and really praying. Prayer should be reverent. “Remember before whom thou art standing" is a good rule to keep in mind. Prayer should be intent. "He who prays must direct his mind intently to Heaven,” 102

says the Midrash. Prayer should not be selfish. We should never ask for aught that will benefit us but which at the same time may bring injury or sorrow to some one else. Prayer should not be unreasonable.

We cannot expect God to set aside the laws of Nature to perform miracles in our behalf. That might help us but would harm countless others. 

We ought to cultivate the habit of praying, privately, individually, whenever we feel the urge to do so. The pious Jew prayed thrice a day as well as on all special occasions of joy or sorrow. It can mean much to us also if we come to look upon God as a 

Great Friend to whom we can turn in confidence during the important or critical moments of our lives.

The Importance of Public Prayer 

It is our duty as Jews to participate in public prayer. No Jew can claim to be a good Jew unless he attends divine services regularly. The Jewish people is traditionally a religious people and a praying people. We cannot be loyal to our religion unless we are willing to join our people in prayer. In addition, public prayer is good for us both as individuals and as Jews. It is good for us as individuals to unite with other individuals in prayer. More than anything else, it helps us to realize how closely we are all related 103

as human beings when we observe that other people have the same needs as we have, that they too have hopes, sorrows and joys just as we do.

In addition, public prayer is good for us as Jews. 

When we pray in company with other Jews, we are expressing together not only individual hopes and aspirations, but Jewish hopes and aspirations. We are proclaiming the majestic ideals of Israel. That helps to cement our loyalty to Judaism. The Jew who takes part in worship in the Synagogue not only unites himself with his God but with his people as well. He not only feels himself a part of his own congregation, of the people around him, but he comes to realize that he is member of a larger congregation, the whole Household of Israel the world over. Thus, through prayer, Israel becomes one just as its God is one.

The Prayer Book I. THE NEED FOR A PRAYER BOOK. 

A discussion of prayer naturally leads us to the subject of the Prayer Book. The Prayer Book is necessary both for private and for public prayer. 

The question might well be asked, why is a Prayer 

Book necessary? Ought not prayer to be fresh and spontaneous? Should it not express the deep emotions that we feel at the very moment that we are 104

praying? The answer is yes. We ought to pray spontaneously whenever it is possible. Many people do. 

Many thousands of men and women have uttered simple prayers in their own language, prayers which came directly from their hearts. But very often it is not possible. There are times when we are so full of feeling, either of sorrow or of joy, that we are unable to find the right words in which to express that feeling. There are thoughts and aspirations, longings and hopes which we find difficult to put into language. Then the Prayer Book helps us to find the language that we need. It provides us with the simple and beautiful prayers which our fathers used in praying for the very same things that we may be in need of.

But if the Prayer Book is helpful for individual prayer, it is absolutely essential for public prayer in the Synagogue. By introducing order it upholds the decorum and dignity of the service. The very Hebrew name for Prayer Book, Siddur, means the orderly arrangement of the prayers. Think what confusion there would be in the service if all of us were to pray independently and in our own words.

Furthermore, the Prayer Book stimulates ideas. It suggests to us the things for which we ought to pray and which ordinarily we might overlook. Then, too, it draws us closer together as human beings when we pray together in the same language. Finally the 105

Prayer Book is a bond of unity between Jews. It reminds us of our debt of gratitude to God for having preserved us as a people. It recalls to us the ideals of our religion. Just as we pray together for their fulfillment, so we are encouraged to labor together so that their fulfillment may come more speedily.

Having completed our discussion of prayer and worship, it is only natural that we should want to know something about the Synagogue, the Jewish House of Worship. The Synagogue is the central and most important institution in Jewish life. Ever since the final destruction of the Temple and the beginning of the Dispersion in 70 C. E., the religious and educational life of every Jewish community throughout the world has revolved about the Synagogue. 

The Synagogue, therefore, has an ancient and honorable history of some two thousand years. Although it is impossible for us to review that history here in detail, there are a few important facts with which we ought to be familiar.

As we all know, for a long time the only recognized place of worship for the Jewish people was the Temple in Jerusalem. There the people would go to

They were devoted to the Temple. They missed it sorely and constantly prayed for its rebuilding.

Today we find Jews living in almost every country of the world. Economic necessity and persecution are the chief factors responsible for their widespread distribution. According to the latest estimate there are about 15,050,000 Jews residing on the five continents of the globe of whom 4,058,000 live in the United States. It is significant to note that wherever the Jew made his home he attempted to adapt himself to the larger environment about him and at the same time preserve his Jewish identity. 

This problem has confronted him ever since he was driven from Palestine after the destruction of the first Temple. From the time of the Babylonian exile until our own day he has been compelled to change the outer aspects of Judaism on many occasions in order to conform with new conditions presented by frequent change of residence. A great mass of literature representing interpretations of and commentaries on the laws of the Bible, the original source of Judaism, is eloquent proof of the Jew's determined effort 116

to preserve his existence despite almost insurmountable obstacles. We shall now turn to a brief examination of the effect of this effort upon the status of 

Judaism in the modern world. 

Reform Judaism and the Causes of its Origin 

In our studies of Jewish history we learned that the majority of Jews lived for many centuries in Europe. During much of that time they were the victims of severe restrictions which prevented them from participating in the general social and civic life of the countries where they made their home. 

Occasionally, as in Spain during the Golden Period, they were afforded the opportunity of absorbing the culture of the country without sacrificing their own identity. But privileges of this nature were the exception and not the rule. Accordingly, the Jew developed a social life of his own which enabled him to observe the customs, traditions and laws of rabbinical Judaism without any difficulty. However, at the end of the eighteenth century a radical change took place in the life of a substantial portion of European Jewry. 

On September 27, 1791, the National Assembly of

France passed an act granting full rights of citizenship to all Jews who would take the French civic oath. 

This act marked the beginning of the emancipation of European Jewry and their enjoyment of rights and privileges which until that time had been denied them. 117

Country after country in Europe gradually liberated their fettered Jewish population. 

With the dawn of Emancipation, the Jews for the first time in centuries were thrown into direct contact with the language and culture of the peoples among whom they lived. Eager for knowledge that had been so long denied them, they quickly entered the fields of general literature and scientific learning. But their passing from the ghetto walls created a grave problem. Many of them found it increasingly difficult to observe all of the old customs and traditions of Judaism. In addition, they became dissatisfied with the old system of religious worship. It was out of this condition of discontentment that there arose a group of thinkers who attempted to formulate a new interpretation of Judaism. To them customs and ceremonies were not fixed for all time but were to be adapted and changed according to the needs of successive ages in conformity with a practice in Jewish life since the earliest days of its history. In Germany we witness the first attempts to put into practice the beginnings of what was later called Reform Judaism. 

Israel Jacobson (1768-1828) made the first attempt to adapt religious services to the conditions and needs of the times. He introduced the sermon in German, German hymns with organ accompani- 118

ment, and also adopted the ceremony of confirmation for children. Leopold Zunz, in 1819, founded the

Society for the Advancement of the Science of Judaism which "worked toward a scientific investigation of Jewish history, literature, and religion." He and his associates formulated the intellectual basis of Re form Judaism. Abraham Geiger and Samson Raphael 

Hirsch continued their work by emphasizing the prophetic ideals of Judaism as being more important than ceremonial laws. Through these Reformers many 

Jews were enabled to continue as followers of Judaism who might otherwise have been lost to its cause.

Orthodox and Conservative Judaism 

While the Jews of Western Europe were beginning to enjoy the fruits of Emancipation, the Jews of many other countries continued to suffer the sad and bitter effects of persecution. This was particularly true of our co-religionists in Poland and Russia. Some Aspects of Jewish Life Today 

The twentieth century has witnessed a number of radical changes in the status of Jewish life throughout the world. Economic hardships and unsettled social and political conditions resulting from the World War as well as new philosophies of life, have done much toward weakening the integrity of Jewish religious life in many European countries. Russian Jews.

Probably the most outstanding phenomenon in Jewish life since the World War has been the tremendous impetus given to the Zionist movement. 

The modern Zionist movement was initiated largely through the efforts of a Jewish journalist by the name of Theodore Herzl (1860-1904). He and a number of other Jewish leaders met in Basel in July, 1897, for the first Zionist Congress, where they adopted a platform which read in part: "Zionism aims at establishing for the Jewish people a publicly and legally assured home in Palestine." It was not until 1917, however, with the issuance of the Balfour Declaration which expressed Great Britain's approval of the establishment in Palestine of a national homeland for the Jewish people, that Zionism 122

took on the color of a hopeful reality. On April 25, 1920, the San Remo Conference of the League of

Since their occupation of the country they have done much to improve sanitary, economic and cultural conditions. A fine Jewish university has been erected there and many schools, hospitals and charitable institutions have been established.

The outstanding center of Jewish life today is in the United States. Jewry all over the world is looking to this country for inspiration and leadership. More favorably situated than most of their co-religionists, the responsibility rests upon the Jews of America not only to help their brethren abroad but to solidify their own ranks and to continue to practice and perpetuate the faith of their fathers.

CHAPTER ONE THE MEANING OF CONFIRMATION The Ceremony of Confirmation 

The ceremony of confirmation is very old indeed. 

The records of man show that most peoples from earliest times have had some ceremony for their young people corresponding in one way or another with the purposes of our own confirmation exercises.

However, confirmation as we understand it today is of comparatively recent origin and the ceremony which it displaced was better and more aptly known as initiation. For example, in ancient times, when a boy reached the age of thirteen or fourteen, it was considered by his elders that he had reached the period of his life when he could be accepted into the group as a full-fledged member. However, before he could achieve this new dignity, the boy was compelled to pass a test of his fitness. Now in those days the outstanding quality stressed in a man was physical courage. The young candidate for membership in the group was therefore submitted to a number 135

of ordeals to determine whether he had a brave heart. 

Some groups made their youth pass through fire. 

Others caused them to engage in single-handed combat with wild beasts or pass the night alone amid the perils and terrors of the jungles. If they endured these ordeals successfully and without any show of cowardice, they were then admitted into the group amid many elaborate rites and colorful ceremonies.

Jewish Confirmation 

Jewish Confirmation serves the primary purpose of preparing the young Jewish boy and girl for full membership in the faith of Israel. These boys and girls must pass a test also. But unlike their primitive forbears, they are not compelled to pass a test for physical bravery but must rather demonstrate their possession of moral courage and show their spiritual preparedness to assume the sacred obligations of Jewish manhood and womanhood. To meet these challenges successfully, the candidates for confirmation must be equipped with a knowledge of the truths of Judaism and have their hearts set to put them into practice in their daily lives. For when they are confirmed, they become additional links in that great chain of tradition which had been handed down from one Jewish generation to the other since the days of Moses and the prophets. 136

MEANING OF CONFIRMATION Bar Mitzvah and Confirmation 

Before the rise of Reform Judaism only boys were confirmed. This type of confirmation is called "Bar Mitzvah," which is the Hebrew for Son of 

Duty. A boy became Bar Mitzvah at the age of thirteen and on the Sabbath nearest his birthday.

On that day he came to the synagogue where he read in Hebrew the scriptural selections assigned for that particular Sabbath and also the Haftarah for the same day. In addition, he usually made a speech in which he pledged himself to abide by the teachings of Judaism and also expressed his thanks to his parents, teachers and rabbis. A little over a century ago the service of confirmation somewhat as we have it today was held in Seesen, in northern Germany. As Jews we constitute a very small minority of the population of the world. PRIVILEGES OF BEING A JEW Our Birthright as Followers of Judaism 

As Jews we have inherited the privilege of being included among the followers of one of the great religions of mankind. Through the compelling force of our religion's teachings our people have been dedicated to the realization of the noblest principles and ideals so far revealed to man. The Glory of Jewish History

CHAPTER THREE THE RESPONSIBILITIES OF BEING 

A JEW Privilege always entails responsibility. Since we possess the privilege of belonging to an historic people which has made great religious, social and ethical contributions to the world's thought, that membership places upon our shoulders as individual Jews and Jewesses some very definite obligations. We must make every effort to bring honor to the name of our people. At the same time we must be doubly cautious to avoid any act which might reflect disgrace or discredit upon that name. Our Responsibility to Humanity 

Judaism is not only a religion of individual morality. It is also a religion of social morality. In fact, some of the most important teachings of Judaism, those of the prophets of Israel, are social teachings. 

To be a good Jew or Jewess, one must be more than moral in personal living. We must also be sociallyminded. A good Jew or Jewess will make every effort to live up to the ideals of the religion and will observe, if possible, its holidays, customs and ceremonies. 

A good Jew or Jewess will support, as far as he or she is able, the philanthropic and educational institutions of Jewish life, its hospitals, schools, orphan asylums, and homes for the aged, and will respond generously to appeals for succor from Jewish communities in other parts of the world. 

A good Jew or Jewess will be interested in knowing something of the language, history and literature of Israel and in preserving this cultural heritage for the future.

A good Jew or Jewess must feel the obligation to enrich the content of Jewish idealism and culture. 

It is not enough that we use the material created by our ancestors in the past. Each succeeding generation must add something to the sum total. Our aim should be to hand down to coming generations an even nobler and more meaningful Judaism than we inherited from our fathers.

Conclusion 

These are the responsibilities of being a Jew. They are not really as difficult or as complicated as they may appear in writing. Certainly the task of fulfill- 151

ing them will not be as difficult as that which confronted our ancestors who sometimes had to go to the extent of sacrificing their lives as well as their means of livelihood for their faith. Nonetheless, they are serious obligations and we must face them seriously. Confirmation Day should find us filled with an earnest resolve to live from that moment on as loyal sons and daughters of Israel to the end that God's name may be sanctified and Israel's name be blessed among the peoples of the earth.
