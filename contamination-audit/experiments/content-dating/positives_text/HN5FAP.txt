PREFACE 

The historical horizon of the American people has perceptibly widened since the World War. Every day brings with it a growing appreciation of the close relation between the past and the present. Closely linked with this is the desire for a better perspective on our complicated present-day social order. There is a demand for the "long view" of history as contrasted with the narrow provincial outlook of earlier days. 

It is not easy to attain to such a concept of history. 

School history properly presented means a contact with a number of segments of man's development. This broader outlook depends essentially on presenting any portion of history in its relation to the larger whole of which it is a part — conceiving history as a single great adventure, a never-ending movement of mankind. 

This is the purpose of the present series, "Westward March of Man." In Our Past in Western Europe we follow the fortunes of our European ancestors from the break-up of the Roman Empire to the close of the sixteenth century. Then it was that the eyes of Europe were turning more and more toward the West. 

This book is designed to provide a better background for the understanding of the history of our own country. This ever "Westward March of Man" is a

phenomenon of the greatest significance for the American of today. It begins with primitive man and his civilized successors in the Ancient East. They are followed by the Greeks and the Romans, and they in turn give way to the peoples of central and western Europe. Although the emphasis is always on man's ultimate habitat, the North American continent, the young American is not permitted to lose contact with the cultural stream of which he is a part. The books of the series, both severally and collectively, present the vista which lies behind him, and point the way to the inviting prospect just ahead.

TO THE TEACHER 

Today there is an increasing tendency to teach children only those phases of history which can be made concrete and definite enough to come within their understanding. Through the use of pictures, slides, historical motion pictures, and vivid word pictures, the child can appreciate something of the social and economic aspects of life in the past. Pictures and maps are an integral part of this text. Through the use of questions based on illustrations the child is encouraged to learn from these as well as from the text itself.

TO THE TEACHER 

vii A simple vocabulary and many details help the child to visualize the life of the people. 

The subject of Part One is "War and Destruction." 

It seemed best to include a general view of Roman civilization before discussing the break-up of the Western Roman Empire. Any study of the achievements of Charlemagne and Alfred necessitates for its understanding some knowledge of the preceding period of confusion. Otherwise the background for Part Two -"New Nations in an Old World" is lacking.

Part Two tells of the progress of the Germans in building up a new civilization in the territory formerly occupied by the Romans. In Part Three "The 

Way People Lived Seven Hundred Years Ago" - life in a typical castle and in a monastery of the Middle Ages is discussed in detail. By means of an imaginary trip the child is encouraged to identify himself with the life of the time. The Crusades are discussed in 

Part Four. This organization is used because one of the most important results of the Crusades was the impetus given to trade and to the growth of towns in western Europe. The Commercial Revolution is but one of the many phases of the period of activity called the Renaissance -true "Days of Great Adventure."

Part Five-"Finding a New World" - tells of the explorations which naturally followed the increased interest in geography and in trade. The pupil should not be allowed to lose sight of the relationship between the separate topics and the subject of each part,

INTRODUCTION 

What a wonderful world we live in! Did you ever stop to think how strange our world would seem to a Roman boy who lived 1800 years ago? How many surprises he would have if he could visit you today!

He lived so long ago that he would hardly believe that the world could be so full of things which seem to work by magic. For, of course, he would never have heard of electricity, gasoline, or radio. 

No doubt the Roman boy would be badly frightened if you took him for an airplane ride. The speed of the plane would seem terrific to him. It will be fun for you to name the many things that we use daily which you think would surprise this boy of long ago.

You have already learned how the Romans lived. 

Now in this book you will read the very interesting story of the many changes in the world after the days of the Romans.

The people who lived north of the Roman World in those far-off days were very different from the Romans. They did not have comfortable homes nor any of the comforts that the Romans enjoyed. 

Their customs were very different. You can easily understand then that the beautiful buildings of the Roman cities and the many things that the Romans used must have seemed very splendid and strange to

place to place, them. But these people from the North were great warriors. And we shall find that after many years of fighting they took much land away from the Romans. 

During the fighting between the Romans and the people north of them, many were killed, buildings were burned, and roads and bridges were destroyed. 

These people—you know they are called the barbarians came into the Roman land. But instead of settling peacefully, they wandered from fighting and destroying beautiful things. These fighters took many long years to learn how to live together in a more orderly way. It took a long time for them to learn to work together in order to keep peace and to make their new home a better place in which to live. 

In time, the people from the North learned many things from the Romans. Many of them became Christians. 

Slowly, groups of people from the same lands, speaking the same language, and having similar customs began to live in one place. They learned to make laws for the benefit of their people. And then, gradually, the nations which we know as England and France grew up. The people of those countries. worked hard. They fought hard too. They built great stone castles and beautiful churches. But, at the same time, every one enjoyed the gay tournaments in which the brave knights took part.

Although many years had passed since the days of the Romans, the English or French people, who followed them and who lived about the time of the

Other countries also grew up in the lands which had once belonged to the Romans. After a time the people who lived in the lands of western Europe began to travel farther away from their homelands. They learned about the people of far-away China. You can imagine how interested they were to learn about this very new and very different country with its wealth and luxuries. And, as time passed, they learned more and more about the world. 

The greatest voyage of all took place over four hundred years ago. A new world, America, was found. 

What a change that made! The Spanish, English, and French people found many marvelous things in this unfamiliar world. The brave explorers who sailed from Europe to America were amazed at the new land. 

America at the time it was discovered was very different from the America of today. The first people who came from Europe to America had many exciting experiences. Some of them were the first European men and women to live in what is now the United States.

As you read these stories of the way in which the world has changed, you will learn how many things which today make our world so wonderful were discovered.

3. HOW THE ROMAN WORLD SLOWLY FELL TO PIECES 

But the Romans began to change slowly, and the Roman World, which seemed so strong, gradually became weaker. Finally, the Romans were conquered by the men of the North. These conquerors from the 

North were our parents many centuries ago. The story of how they gradually conquered the Romans is full of adventure. Winning from the Romans in battle became fairly easy as time went on, but it was not as easy to learn to do the great things which the Romans had done. It was many years before the people of the North learned to coöperate as the Romans had. It was many years before the men who took over the land within the Roman World learned to make great buildings, write great books, make good roads, and keep order in their lands. 

You remember that in the earlier days many Romans owned their own farms. They were strong, sturdy men who worked hard and who made the Roman army strong. In time many of them lost their farms

or left them because of hard times. They moved to the cities where life was easier. After a while the land was owned by a few rich men instead of by many Roman citizens. Very often the land that was deserted by the farmers was overgrown with weeds.

Many of the farmers who moved to the cities did not get work. They could not be allowed to starve. So the Roman government began to furnish them with free bread and meat. Soon many men in the city no longer wanted to work. They spent most of their time at the chariot races or at the gladiatorial games. 

As you can guess, feeding so many people in the cities cost a great deal of money, and as the government had to pay for the food which it gave away, it had to increase taxes. But, as time went on, people 

Idid not have as much wealth to tax.

As long as there were prosperous farmers in the Roman World, the workers in the cities could sell their goods to the farmers. But a change took place. The farmers moved to the cities, and they no longer had any money to buy goods from the traders. 

If men who could not get work in the city wanted to go back to their farms, they found it very hard indeed to make a living. Slaves often did the work on the lands of the large landholders. So most of the men decided to stay in the city where food was free rather than to try to work in the country where they were paid very little.

The Roman army was not as strong as it had been. Romans no longer wanted to serve in the army. 

They were no longer the brave men of old who were willing to fight and work hard for their country. The 

Roman emperors began to hire the people who lived in the North for their army.

THE PEOPLE WHO DESTROYED THE ROMAN WORLD 

The people who conquered the Romans lived north of the Rhine and Danube rivers. Do you remember why we call these people barbarians? It is because they were uncivilized. They had not learned to work together in order to make a strong, powerful nation. 

The Romans were civilized. They coöperated in building their roads and cities and in keeping order in their land.

These people who lived along the northern boundaries were called Germans by the Romans. Many people who now live in Germany, Holland, Denmark, England,

Sweden, and Norway are the descendants of these early Germans. So also were many people who came to this country from Europe to become Americans. The

The German girls and the young men were very strong. The women often went to fight with their husbands, and they encouraged and cheered the men to victory. 9. THE GREAT WORK OF THE CHURCH 

During the years of war between the Romans and the Germans, many cities were destroyed. Beautiful homes were laid in ruins, and many were killed in battle. Men could not care for their farms or protect them from plunder. It was a very sad time for the many people who only wished to be left in peace. 

Most of the people lived by farming. They were very poor and lived in mud huts or in wooden cabins without floors. They were always in fear of being

robbed. Many died of disease, for health rules to prevent sickness were unknown in that day.

During the many years that the Germans and Romans were fighting, many of the Germans became Christians. The Bishop of Rome became the leader of all the Christian people in the West. 

Among the great Christian leaders of this time was a man named Benedict (běn'ê-dikt). He, like many other Christians, was not interested in war. He wished to work, to help the poor people, and to live according to the teachings of Christ. Other men accepted his leadership and agreed to spend their lives in the service of God. Many men followed the plan of 

Benedict. These men lived together in groups, or communities, which with their dwellings were called monasteries. The men living in the monasteries were called monks. The monks explained the life and the teachings of Christ to the Germans. 

The monks served God with their prayers and their good works. Men who were writers or artists had no place in the changed world in which fighting was the rule. Many of them, therefore, came to find peace and safety in the monasteries. The monks helped the poor people who lived near them. They took care of the sick in those days when there were no hospitals.

During the years of war, the schools in the Roman World had been closed. Few men could read or write, and books were written by hand. We would know very little about the Greeks and Romans if some of their

books had not been saved for us by the monks. Besides saving many of the old books, they copied some of them.

So the Christian Church was able to keep from entire destruction many of the great things that the ancient people had learned and put down in books. The 

Church also tried to stop the fighting and to prevent robbery and destruction.

His health was excellent, except during the four years preceding his death, when he was subject to frequent fevers; toward the end of his life he limped a little with one foot. 

Even in his later years he lived rather according to his own inclinations than the advice of physicians the latter he very much disliked, because they wanted him to give up roasts, to which he was accustomed, and to eat boiled meat instead. "In accordance with the national custom, he took frequent exercise on horseback and in the chase, in which sports scarcely any people in the world can equal the Franks. He enjoyed the vapors from natural warm springs, and often indulged in swimming, in which he was so skillful that none could surpass him; and hence it was that he built his palace at Aix-la-Chapelle (ěks-la-shȧ-pěl') and lived there constantly during his later years. (The German name for Aix-la-Chapelle was Aachen (äʼken.) "His custom was to wear the national, that is to say, the Frankish, dress - next to his skin (he wore) a linen shirt and linen breeches, and above these a tunic fringed with silk; while hose fastened by

bands covered his lower limbs, and shoes his feet. 

In winter he protected his shoulders and chest by a close-fitting coat of otter or marten skins. Over all he flung a blue cloak, and he always had a sword girt about him, usually one with a gold or silver hilt and belt. He sometimes carried a jeweled sword, but only on great feast-days or at the reception of ambassadors from foreign nations. He despised foreign costumes, however handsome, and never allowed himself to be robed in them, except twice in Rome, when he donned the Roman tunic, chalmys (cloak), and shoes; the first time at the request of Pope Hadrian (hāʼdri-ăn), the second to gratify Leo, Hadrian's successor. On great feastdays he made use of embroidered clothes, and shoes adorned with precious stones; his cloak was fastened with a golden buckle, and he appeared crowned with a diadem of gold and gems; but on other days his dress differed little from that of ordinary people. 66 Charles was temperate in eating, and especially so in drinking, for he abhorred (hated) drunkenness in anybody, much more in himself and those of his own household; but he could not easily abstain from food, and often complained that fast injured his health. 66 'He gave entertainments but rarely, only on feast-days, and then to large numbers of people. His meals consisted ordinarily of four courses, not counting the roast, which his huntsmen were accustomed to

bring in on the spit; he was more fond of this than of any other dish. While at table, he listened to reading or music. The subjects of the readings were the stories and deeds of olden time. He was fond, too, of St. Augustine's (ô-gus'tin) books, and especially of the one entitled 'The City of God.' "He was so moderate in the use of wine and all sorts of drink that he rarely allowed himself more than three cups in the course of a meal. In summer, after the midday meal, he would eat some fruit, drain a single cup, put off his clothes and shoes, just as he did for the night, and rest for two or three hours.

While he was dressing and putting on his shoes, he not only gave audience to his friends, but if the Count of the Palace told him of any suit in which his judgment was necessary, he had the parties brought before him forthwith, (he) heard the case, and gave his decision, just as if he were sitting in the judgmentseat. . . "Charles had the gift of ready and fluent speech, and could express whatever he had to say with the utmost clearness. He was not satisfied with ability to use his native language merely, but gave attention to the study of foreign ones, and, in particular, (he) was such a master of Latin that he could speak it as well as his native tongue; but he could understand Greek better than he could speak it. He was so eloquent, indeed, that he might have been taken for a teacher of oratory. He most zealously

cherished the liberal arts, held those who taught them in great esteem, and conferred great honors upon them. He took lessons in grammar of the deacon Peter of Pisa, at that time an aged man.

Another deacon, Albin of Britain, surnamed Alcuin (ǎl'kwin), a man of Saxon birth, who was the greatest scholar of the day, was his teacher in other branches of learning. The king spent much time and labor with him studying rhetoric, dialectic, and especially astronomy. He learned to make calculations, and used to investigate with much curiosity and intelligence the motions of the heavenly bodies. 

He also tried to write, and used to keep tablets and blanks in bed under his pillow, that at leisure hours he might accustom his hand to form the letters; however, as he began his efforts late in life, and not at the proper time, they met with little success. "He cherished with the greatest fervor and devotion the principles of the Christian religion, which had been instilled in him from infancy. Hence it was that he built the beautiful basilica at Aix-la- Chapelle, which he adorned with gold and silver and lamps and with rails and doors of solid brass. He had the columns and marbles for this structure brought from Rome and Ravenna, for he could not find such as were suitable elsewhere. He was a constant worshiper at this church as long as his health permitted, going morning and evening, even after nightfall, besides attending mass. He took care that

all the services there conducted should be held in the best possible manner, . . . . He provided it with a number of sacred vessels of gold and silver, and with such a quantity of clerical robes that not even the doorkeepers, who filled the humblest office in the church, were obliged to wear their everyday clothes when in the performance of their duties. 

He took great pains to improve the church reading and singing, for he was well skilled in both, although he neither read in public nor sang, except in a low tone and with others. "He was very active in aiding the poor, and in that open generosity which the Greeks call alms; so much so, indeed, that he not only made a point of giving in his own country and in his own kingdom, but when he discovered that there were Christians living in poverty in Syria, Egypt, and Africa, at Jerusalem (jé-roo'sȧ-lěm), Alexandria, and Carthage (kärʼthaj) he had compassion on their wants, and used to send money over the seas to them." *

16. How CHARLEMAGNE RULED HIS PEOPLE 

Charlemagne was a great ruler as well as a great warrior. He had trusted men called counts living in different parts of the Frankish kingdom. The counts helped Charlemagne to rule, and they made sure that Charlemagne's laws were carried out. Several times a year, two men, called the lord's messengers, visited these counts. Then the two men reported to Charlemagne what they had seen and heard. So Charlemagne had eyes" and ears everywhere in his land to make sure that his people were ruled well. When any question arose which a count did not feel that he was able to settle, Charlemagne himself decided the case. 66 99 

17. A PLAN TO EDUCATE THE FRANKS 

Charlemagne was one of the wisest men of his time. 

He realized that the Franks needed schools very much since few of them could read or write. They did not, of course, know many of the things which the Greeks and Romans had known.

Charlemagne planned to have a school in each village

under the charge of the village priest. Those who did their studies well were to go to a higher school at one of the monasteries. Last of all, a student was to attend the School of the Palace. 

Even today we can see the results of Charlemagne's efforts. The books written in the Frankish kingdom before Charlemagne's time were very difficult to read.

So Charlemagne ordered that all books used in the church service should be carefully written. We can see some of these books today. They are often beautifully made and can be easily read. 

Charlemagne often visited the schools in his land.

He said that others might admire them because they wasted their time and did not study, but that he, Charlemagne, would never do anything for any of them until they changed their ways and did their work well.

The last days of Charlemagne were days of peace.

In this Louis made his promise in the French language while Charles made his promise in the German language. This is the first example of the use of the written German and the written French. 

The German tribes which settled in Italy, Spain, and France mingled with the Romans. They learned to use many Latin words. As a result, the languages used by the Germans in these countries were more like the Latin language of the Romans. For this reason the Spanish, French, and Italian languages are called the Romance languages. The Germans farther north in Europe did not mingle with the Romans. We call their languages, which were influenced very little by the Latin, the Germanic languages. As early as 842 some of the Germans were using the German language, while others were speaking French. In the years that followed these languages gradually changed.

26. ALFRED AND THE DANES 

When Alfred became king of part of England in the year 871, he was twenty-two years old. The same

people, the Northmen, who had raided France, had been fighting Alfred's people as well. The Danes, as the people in England called the Northmen, were better warriors than Alfred's people. The Anglo-Saxons wanted to be left alone to carry on their farming and their herding. So the Danes had already been able to take some land from the Anglo-Saxons and also to destroy London. 

What was he to do? Imagine what he thought when some Norse ships like that in the picture on page 73 appeared once again off the shores of England. But 

Alfred, a brave and clever leader, led an army against the Danes. He got back some of the land that the 

Danes had taken in England.

Alfred had an army trained to fight and forts built along the southern coast of England. Towns were protected from the Danes by walls and ditches. He employed masons and builders from other countries to rebuild London which had been destroyed by the

27. HOW ALFRED ESTABLISHED ORDER IN ENGLAND 

In time of war, men often become careless about obeying laws. In Alfred's reign, Anglo-Saxon England was

divided into districts called shires, about the size of modern counties. Each shire was made up of smaller districts known as hundreds. The people in a hundred met in their folkmoot or folk meeting to decide important questions of various kinds. They selected their own officials and through the folkmoot obtained valuable practice in self-government.

Sometimes Alfred's people would not accept the judgment of the local judges. They then went to the king in order that he might decide the question. The king always carefully questioned the men who came to him. 

No one who was guilty of doing wrong wanted to go before the king, for Alfred was always just. If the king found that the judge was not doing his work well, he tried to find out why the case was decided incorrectly.

If a judge decided unjustly because he either liked or disliked the man being tried, the king would not allow him to be a judge any longer. He made the judges who did not know the laws study them, and soon the courts of law were much better.

No longer were the forests filled with so many outlaws. 

The laws of the country were obeyed better than they had been obeyed before. Alfred had the laws written down and he included the best laws of the past. The most important members of the noble class advised the king about new laws, taxes, and treaties. This group of wise men, chosen not by the people but by custom and the king's will, was called the Witan (witʼăn).

Alfred used his wealth to improve his country, and

employed workers from every nation. Among these were smiths, builders, carvers, and jewelers. Alfred not only planned and dreamed of great deeds, but he worked very, very hard to make his dreams come true. 

No sacrifice was too great for him to make. It is now a thousand years since his death, but he is still remembered as one of the greatest men of all time.

31. CHANGES IN ENGLAND UNDER THE NORMANS 

The Normans did not take over the manners and customs of the Saxons. Instead they spoke French and lived as nearly as possible as they had lived in France. The Normans considered themselves better than the Saxons. The children of the Normans went to France and studied the French language there. For three hundred years after William conquered England, the king, the nobles, and the judges spoke French. 

Later, when English became the language of all the people, some Norman (French) words were added to the English language. In this way the Normans helped to make the English language better by adding new words to it.

We have already learned that the Normans took the land from the Saxons. Gradually the land in England was divided into more than nine thousand parts. 

These parts were called manors. The manor was a large farm, and at the head of each one was a lord.

32. THE DOMESDAY BOOK 

William gave good laws to his people, and he employed men to see that these laws were obeyed. He needed money to do this and to do other things. In 

1085 King William sent his men over all England.

They wrote down the amount of land owned by each person in each part of the country. Each ox, cow, or pig was put down. They counted the number of plows that a man owned. Mills, fishponds, woods all were added to the king's list. Even the beehives on each manor were counted.

The lists of the property which the people of England owned were used to make up a book called the Domesday Book. Through this book William could decide how large a tax should be paid by every man. No other king at this time knew as much about the property of his subjects as did William of England. You will agree that William was a very good business man.

34. A RED-HEADED KING 

In the year 1154, Henry II came from Normandy to be king of England. This was about one hundred years after the Normans had conquered England, and of course there had been other kings before Henry II.

He had short red hair, his eyes were gray, and on his face were many freckles. Henry II did a great deal for England. He made his mistakes when he became angry. His eyes were said to flash like a ball of fire

when he was enraged. At one time he became very angry at Thomas Becket, the Archbishop of Canterbury. An archbishop was then, as now, a high official in the church. In his rage the king rolled on the floor and cried out, "Is there no one who will rid me of this priest?" Four knights, who believed that the king wanted the archbishop killed, murdered him. The king lived to regret his terrible anger and his words. 

Henry II gathered learned men around him at his court. The king himself spoke both French and Latin. 

He was a hard worker. He put down a revolt of the great lords, and he was able to increase the power of the king because as a result of this the lords had been weakened. The common people found the rule of a strong king better than the rule of many great lords. 

Henry II did also two other things of great importance. 

He introduced trial by jury in the courts, and he helped to establish the system of rules and customs called the common law.

39. SAFETY FIRST 

You can imagine what happened to the poor farmers when the Vikings or Huns came marching through their country. These poor men, without any protection, saw their homes burned and many people killed. They 109

They wanted to know that the crops which they planted in the spring would not be destroyed by invaders in the autumn. They wanted to feel sure that they themselves would not be killed.

The most important men in any community were the strong, powerful fighters. The men who could make the community safe were the leaders of all. 

Weak men were willing to serve a strong leader in return for his protection. The small farmers might still live on the land and raise their crops, but the land belonged to the lord or strong leader who protected the people living on it.

41. THE COMMON PEOPLE 

The real farming was done by the common people, who were the poorest class of vassals. They gave part of their crops to their lord in return for a small plot of land. They also worked a certain number of days a week for the lord. Although these people were poor they were not slaves. They were more fortunate than the poor people in Greece and Rome who lost their freedom and were owned by their masters. Some of the poorest farmers were not allowed to leave the land on which they lived. These men were called serfs.

42. THE MANY RULERS OF THE MIDDLE AGES 

During the Middle Ages, there were no strong, powerful countries in western Europe as there are today. 

At this time each feudal lord ruled his own land. He made laws for the people living on his land and punished those who disobeyed his orders. When he decided to go to war, his vassals and serfs had to help him. 

Some of the clergy held land under the feudal system. The clergy were the priests, bishops, and W. O. P.-9

other officials of the church chosen to rule the church and to conduct the church services. These leaders of the church might also be lords and have vassals under them. Sometimes members of the clergy were themselves vassals to the king or to a great noble.

Because of this system the countries of western Europe were divided into many parts. Each part had its own ruler. Each local ruler was expected to obey the king of his country. But you can see that a weak king might have difficulty in getting all of the feudal lords to do as he wished. 

You remember that a lord could ask his vassal to help him in case of war. The many lords found cause to quarrel very often. As a result, wars among the many small rulers were very frequent.

Then the first joust, or combat, of the day starts.

Two knights sit erect on their horses at opposite ends of the field. Their squires stand beside the horses. 

The signal is given. Each knight rides forward and bends low, defending himself with his shield. They hold their lances tensely. Then we hear the sound of

Then other knights, two by two, fight. After the joust is over we attend a merry banquet at Roland's castle.

The knights are divided into two sides. At the signal each man fights some one on the opposite side. Some men are hurt. There are several broken ribs, but only one is seriously injured. 54. LOOKING BACK 

Seven hundred years ago life was going on everywhere in western Europe in very much the same way as it did at Raymond's castle. The lords of the feudal manors were really the rulers of the people who lived on their land. Many of the lords were knights who were interested in warfare and in hunting. They gave protection to the poor people who worked on the land of the manors. The many small communities of the time were self-supporting. The armor, building material, clothing, and food which were used on the manor were made there.

Many of the feudal knights knew little about the world beyond their own community. They were not interested in books, art, or in gaining new knowledge. 

They took sickness as a matter of course and showed no interest in trying to find ways to prevent it. Although men of this time were courageous fighters, most of them did not realize that learning about the world in which they lived might be the greatest adventure of all. 

The monasteries of the Middle Ages served the people of the time in important ways. The monks cultivated their land, cared for the sick, copied old Latin books and wrote new books in Latin, taught young men, and welcomed travelers who needed shelter as they journeyed about. The devotion of the monks to the teachings of Christianity furnished an example to all Christians of the time.

63. THE TOWNS IN EUROPE GROW LARGER 

During the years which followed the Crusades, many changes took place in Europe. We know that during the time of Charlemagne, and for many years after, most of the people in Europe lived in the country on

71. LEARNING TO PRINT Books 

We know that after the Crusades people everywhere in Europe were eager to learn more about the East. 

The people living in the towns were building beautiful cathedrals. Men were anxious to study with the great teachers and to learn about other lands and peoples, but only the very wealthy people could afford to buy the valuable manuscripts or books which were written entirely by hand. There was little reason for the common man to learn to read, because books were far too expensive. He could not hope to own even one book. It is hard to imagine a world without newspapers and many books to read, is it not? 

At this time there were men in Europe who were as anxious to find a way to make books cheaper as Marco Polo had been to find out about the wonders of the 

East. When they discovered how to make a printing press, they gave to the world one of the most important gifts of all time. After the printing press was invented books could be made quickly and for very much less money. Then the common people wanted to learn to read. A man could read of the travels of

Marco Polo and of the things that the great men of his own time and past time had said and done. Traders and sailors could read of other lands.

The first step in learning to print books came about when some one cut out a block of hard wood which was about the size of a page. On this block the words of a page of the book which was being made were cut. The wood was then cut away from the sides of each letter. 

This left the letter raised above the rest of the block. 

The letters were inked and pressed down hard upon paper. Books could be made faster this way than by hand. After the blocks were finished, as many books as were desired could be made. 

In about 1450 John Gutenberg (goo'těn-běrk), who lived in a German town, was using this way of printing. 

But, by this method, a separate block had to be made for each page. It was necessary to make seven hundred blocks in order to print the Bible. It would have taken thirty years to make the seven hundred blocks!

So Gutenberg worked to find an easier way. Finally he decided to cut out of wood the separate letters of the alphabet, and he invented a frame to hold these letters. 

Then when he wanted to print a different page all he had to do was to move the letters in the frame about to make new words. It was much easier to do this than to make a new block for each new page. 

No discovery of this time was more important to the people of the future than Gutenberg's invention.

The printing press looked very different from one used

today because many improvements have been made in printing since Gutenberg's day. Some people think that a man named Laurens Coster was the first man in Europe to use movable type or letters. The important thing to remember, however, is that the printing of books One of the first printed pages. made information very much easier to obtain. You remember, too, that when books were copied by hand it was easy to make mistakes in copying words. Now that books were printed, all copies of books made from the same type were certain to be the same. As you know, the valuable manuscripts of the Middle Ages were made of parchment. 72. THE RENAISSANCE 

In books which tell of the things which were happening at the close of the Middle Ages the word Renaissance appears many times. Generally this is the name given to the period of time from about 1200 to 1600. 

It was during these years that many of the customs of the Middle Ages gradually changed. These changes prepared the way for the very different customs and life of modern times.

At the beginning of this period of time known as the Renaissance, scholars in Italy became very interested in learning about the Romans who had lived in Italy so many years before. They searched for the old 

Roman manuscripts which had not been destroyed and learned to read and to speak Latin.

As time went on, scholars in other countries as well as Italy also became interested in studying the Latin language. Then these scholars of Italy, France, and

later England realized that they could learn much from the Greeks as well as from the Romans. They began to study the Greek language so that they could learn the wisdom of such wise men as Pericles (pěr'iklēz) and Socrates (sõkʼrȧ-tēz). 

For the first time since the destruction of the Roman World, many men in Europe were anxious to learn all they could from the great men of ancient Greece and Rome. This new interest in finding out about the 

Greeks and the Romans is only one of the important things to know about the period which we call the Renaissance. This was a time not only of looking backward but also of looking forward. The people of this time were not only curious about the past, but they also wanted to know more about the world around them. Because they were inquiring and always trying to find new ways of doing things, we find that they made discoveries in art, in science, and in geography, as well as discovering again the marvelous things which had happened many years before their own time.

Now we come to what is, for all Americans, the most interesting part of our story. In Part Five we shall learn how a Spanish king and queen agreed to help Columbus make the voyage which resulted in the discovery of America. Columbus sailed west across the Atlantic Ocean and discovered what he supposed to be a part of the Indies. He died thinking that the kingdom of Cathay would be found near the lands which he had discovered.

For one hundred twenty-five years after Columbus made his first voyage, Spanish explorers braved the unknown seas and the hardships of strange lands in their search, first for the riches of Cathay, and later for the gold and silver of the New World. You will find their adventures as amazing as any of which you have ever heard. 

You will be interested to learn if the Spaniards found the gold which they sought. Just as exciting as their search for gold was the way in which they learned to live with the Indians whom they found in America.

The stories of the fight which the soldiers and explorers of France waged against cold, hunger, and unfriendly Indians are just as remarkable as those about the Spanish.

86. LATER VOYAGES 

Columbus started out for a second voyage in 1493. 

But how different it was from the first! He had seventeen ships, some large and some small. And now every one wanted to sail with the great admiral, because every one dreamed of getting a part of the gold of the Indies for himself. Magellan was a Portuguese sailor. After Balboa had discovered the South Sea, Magellan believed that there must be a water passage, a strait, in the land already discovered which would lead to the Spice Islands which every one wished to reach. The king of Spain gave five ships to Magellan so that he could search for them.

A man named Pigafetta (pē-gå-fā'tȧ), who sailed with Magellan, wrote a book when he returned. From this book we learn of the many strange things which happened on the trip. Pigafetta, at times, may have stretched the truth a little in telling the story of this great adventure.

It was a dangerous place for sailors because ugly rocks were everywhere, and the water was rough. 

It has been named for Magellan, who was the first to discover it. Magellan sailed hundreds of miles through this strait into the great South Sea which Balboa had discovered, but he called this ocean the Pacific because it seemed so peaceful after the stormy strait.

93. PIZARRO FINDS GOLD IN SOUTH AMERICA 

The story of how the Spaniards took possession of several islands in the West Indies, how they settled on the mainland in Central America and in Mexico, and how they found their way around South America to 

Asia has been very exciting, has it not?

For many years the Spaniards, pushing on through heavy forests and over rough rivers, continued their search for gold. They believed the stories which they heard of Indians who drank from golden goblets and ate from plates of gold. Gold, which was scarce in 

Europe, had become more and more valuable as trade increased, for it was used as money. 

Not all of the explorers who were looking for gold found it. We are interested in their travels because they helped to make a great part of the American continent Spanish. For many years, wherever the 

Spaniards went or lived, Spanish became the language used by the people. The Catholic Church sent out missionaries to convert the Indians. 

But there was one explorer who found more gold than even the Spanish had dreamed of finding. This was Pizarro. You remember that he had been with

Balboa, who had never been able to follow the Indian chief's directions and so find his way to the great riches in the south. Where Balboa failed, Pizarro succeeded.

He fitted out ships and started to explore the west coast of South America.

96. A NEW SPAIN 

During the two hundred years which had passed since Columbus discovered America, the Spaniards who came to the New World had slowly brought about a New Spain larger than old Spain, their former home. 

Spaniards came to the West Indies, Mexico, South

America, and the southeastern part of what is now the United States. They mined the metals, farmed the land, and raised cattle. Spanish towns were built, and these became trading centers in the new land.

You remember that many Indians learned to speak

Spanish, which is still the language of most of the people in these countries. 

The outer walls of many of the early Spanish homes in Mexico City were stuccoed and colored. The houses were close to the street, and the windows were protected by screens of metal bars, or grills as they are called. The Spaniards in New Spain had balconies on their homes like the ones on the houses in their home country. The red tiles of the roof added to the beauty of their homes. The Spanish homes were built around a patio, or open court, because the people spent a great deal of their time out-of-doors. 

The city of Mexico was the most important city in New Spain. Like other towns there, Mexico City had a market. Here one could buy hats, beautiful silk, and velvet cloth which was made in the city. It was surprising how many things were made in New Spain soon after the Spanish settled there. Among these were cotton, woolen cloth, furniture, and wagons. Tables and chairs were made in California. This early style of furniture made in California was copied later and was called mission furniture. Have you ever seen mission chairs? In cities such as San Blas on the coast ships were built. Those ships carried goods from one port to another of New Spain or across the ocean to the homeland. 

The Spaniards raised maize or corn, beans, sugar, tobacco, cotton, potatoes, tea, grapes, and olives. New

Spain sent sugar, tobacco, and vanilla to Europe. 

Beautiful pottery was made by the Indians.

The Spaniards were interested in starting schools in the new country, and whenever they built a Catholic church, they built a school beside it. Many Indians learned to read and write, since the Spaniards were interested in teaching the Indian boys and girls. Over a thousand Indian boys attended a school in Mexico City. They were taught to be tailors, carpenters, and shoemakers in the workshops of the school. 

The Spaniards founded a university in Mexico City, and one in Lima in 1551. Boys who were born in New 

Spain could now be educated as well as if they were living in Spain. The people in New Spain did not have to buy all their books in Spain. We know that there was a printing press in New Spain by about 1536. 

These early settlers found time to write books even while they were building a new country. Some day, if you learn to read Spanish, you may want to read some of the books which were written three hundred years ago in this new Spanish land.

There were many more Indians in New Spain than there were Spaniards. These Indians were the ancestors of the Indians who still live there today. Often the Spaniards married Indian women, and so today you find many people in Mexico and in South America who are of both Spanish and Indian blood..

The Spanish were very anxious to convert the Indians to the Christian faith, so many Catholic monks

came to New Spain to bring their religion. You will remember that they went to all parts of this new country. They often gathered the Indians together in villages called missions. In the center of the mission village was a square around which were built a church, a school, and a home for the monks. The Indians lived in huts on streets just off the square. 97. NEW FRANCE 

But Spain was not the only country interested in finding a way to the Indies by sailing westward. While 

Cortes was gaining new lands and great riches for the king of Spain, hardy fishermen of France were making yearly journeys to the far north in the New World. 

Large quantities of fish were brought back to France from Newfoundland (nu-fund-land') by these venturesome sailors. For a time, the king of France was busy with wars at home and had no time to send out expeditions. But when things became more peaceful, the 

French king sent explorers to the New World who discovered great areas there which rivaled the Spanish lands in size.

Brave Frenchmen traveled many miles to find new lands for the French king. But New France, which was begun in Canada and along the Mississippi River, did not grow as rapidly as New Spain. The climate was much colder, and the Indians were not as friendly as those in the south. The French people were not anxious to make new homes in the cold American wilderness. 98. CARTIER SAILS UP THE ST. LAWRENCE 

It was an April day in 1534 when Jacques Cartier (zhȧk' kär-tyā') started for the New World with one hundred twenty men. He was to plant the French flag in the New World and start a French colony there, and he expected also to find a water route which would lead to China.

Cartier and his men sailed along the coast of Newfoundland and up the St. Lawrence River. As they sailed along, they saw forests of pine, maple, and ash. 

Find Cape Gaspé (gås-pa') on your map. It was here that Cartier landed and put up a cross, thirty feet high. On the front of it was a shield with the arms of 

France. But autumn had arrived, and Cartier decided to return to France. He took two Indians back with him.

There was great interest in France when the people learned about this river in the New World. Frenchmen were interested not only in finding a new way to the Indies, but also in teaching the Indians the Christian religion. So Cartier returned in 1535.

When Cartier returned to sail up the St. Lawrence, he had only the two Indians to pilot him up this unknown river. He had no knowledge of its channels or unseen rocks, but he was determined to succeed. 

Finally, the French came safely to the place where 

Quebec (kwe-běk') is now located. Above the mighty cliffs along the side of the river, they found a few

Indian wigwams and a few bark cabins. The Indians told Cartier of a much larger Indian village farther up the river, so Cartier decided to visit it. 

But the Indians tried to frighten the French into not going farther. Cartier paid no attention. Then three

Cartier that these devils were the servants of an Indian god, and Cartier was warned that if he went on up the river he would die from the ice and snow. Cartier only laughed and told the Indians of his God who would protect him.

99. THE MAN WITH THE IRON BREAST 

Because of the long, hard winters the French had found it very difficult to make settlements along the banks of the St. Lawrence River. But there was a great French soldier and sailor who was to be a leader in the founding of French colonies along its banks. 

This man was Samuel de Champlain (shăm-plan'). He was known by the Indians as the man with the iron breast. This was because the arrows of the Indians

made no impression upon his steel armor.

Cham- plain has also been called the "Father of New France. 

He loved the country where he was born, but he also dreamed of a New France in the wild forests of the New World. Champlain.

Champlain's greatest desire was to find a waterway to the Indies and to teach the Christian religion to the Indians. Though he discovered new lands for France, Champlain never found a way to the Indies.

He also brought priests to New France to teach Christianity to the Indians. He hoped that the teachings of Christ would make his 

Indian friends less cruel. 

One day, in the year 1608, curious Indians saw a strange sight on the shores of the St. Lawrence. Below the cliffs Frenchmen were cutting down trees. In a short time three wooden buildings appeared near the river. These were surrounded by a strong wooden wall, and the wall, in turn, by a moat. Champlain also had a garden laid out nearby. The most important town of New France Quebec - had been begun. 

It was a long time before Quebec became a prosperous town. The first winter after Champlain had erected the buildings, there were only twenty-eight Frenchmen left to protect them. They trapped fox and waited for the long winter to pass, but scurvy

broke out among them, and only eight men were left alive when spring came. With the spring new hope came, for another ship arrived from France. 

Quebec. From an old print.

This drawing shows you Quebec as it was about eighty years later. There was a home for the French governor, a cathedral, and a seminary. Here the priests taught young men and prepared them to go out for their mission work among the Indians. In their old age, the priests returned to the seminary to spend their last days. Along the river below the cliffs you can see buildings which were used by the fur traders. 

The furs which the Indians and French traders brought down the river to be shipped to Europe were very valuable. But the river was filled with ice during the long winter, heavy snow fell, and life was hard. Frenchmen did not like to leave their sunny homeland and go to this cold land, so it is not surprising that by 1690 Quebec had only between one thousand five hundred and two thousand people living there.

Most of these settlers were either fur traders, servants of the government, or priests who worked among the Indians. Here was built the first hospital in New 

France. Nuns acted as nurses. 

Champlain wished to go on exploring, and in order to earn the good will of the Huron (hūʼron) and Algonquin (ǎl-goŋ'kin) Indians who lived around Quebec, Champlain agreed to join them on the warpath against their enemies. Because he was always talking about this plan of reaching China, the friends of La Salle called his land La Chine, a name which it has to this day.

Indian prisoner tortured and killed. Although the

As time went on the Senecas did not seem so friendly. 

La Salle feared that they might attack them because some French fur traders had recently killed an Indian chief. Find Frontenac on your map. The king of France granted land here to La Salle for his services as an explorer. He cleared more than one hundred acres of this land and brought cattle and swine from Montreal. 

The little settlement consisted of a guardhouse, a mill, a bakery, and a small village of French families. Near the houses was a chapel. Fort Frontenac was in the midst of the forest, a week's trip from the nearest settlement. Here La Salle might have spent the rest of his life, trading furs with the Indians, but he preferred daring deeds to riches. He was willing to venture his life for the glory of France, so he started out on the greatest adventure of his life.

One of the men who went with La Salle was Father

La Salle joined them at Niagara, which the Frenchmen realized was an ideal spot for a fort and a French trading station. 106. SPAIN SENDS A FLEET TO ENGLAND 

King Philip of Spain did not like England for several reasons. For one thing Queen Elizabeth had refused to marry him, and too, she had helped the people of the Netherlands who were fighting for freedom from

108. HENRY HUDSON 

For many years the men of Europe did not give up the idea that they could reach the riches of India by finding a sea passage around America. Of course, they did not as yet realize how large the new land was which they had discovered. 

Henry Hudson was a brave English sailor who believed that he could find a northwest passage around America to India. Some merchants of London persuaded him to carry out his idea, and so he sailed in 1607 with a crew of only twelve men. After several months he returned to London since he had been unsuccessful. He sailed later to try again to find his northwest passage but he had no better luck.

At about this time men in another small country of Europe were becoming interested in the New World. 

In this country, called Holland, there was a group of men who were carrying on a profitable trade with the East Indies. These men made up the East India 

Company. They thought that a western passage to the Indies would increase their profits of trade. It

was natural, then, that they should turn to the experi enced Hudson as the man to find this western route for them. 

Hudson sailed on March 25, 1609. During his voyage the Half-Moon, Hudson's small ship, was battered by ice. Often it had to sail in fog, and so his small crew became discouraged and wanted to go home. 

The Half-Moon. 

When Hudson reached Newfoundland, he obtained codfish for his crew. You can imagine how much they enjoyed eating it. Then he sailed along the coast of 

Maine, passed Cape Cod, and finally entered Delaware Bay. He soon found out that this was not the sea

passage which he wanted to find, and now he turned north. Soon Hudson and his men entered what is now the harbor of New York City.

Hudson was encouraged and he sailed up the river which now has his name. Friendly Indians brought 

Hudson and his men oysters and beans for trade.

Hudson noticed that the Indians smoked tobacco in pipes.

The Half-Moon sailed nearly to the place where 

Albany is now located. What a disappointment!

There did not seem to be enough water to sail farther. 

So Hudson traded beads with the Indians for more provisions and furs and sailed back down the river. 

Hudson's discovery was to be of great importance to the men of the Dutch East India Company. They sent out other explorers, and they soon discovered that the Indians were glad to trade beautiful furs for beads, knives, and other Dutch goods. They had not found the spices of the East but the rich pelts of the beaver were well worth buying. So the Dutch East India 

Company built forts near what is now Albany and on 

Manhattan Island. As a result of Hudson's voyage the Dutch held the land along the Hudson River for nearly seventy-five years.

Later, the great English seaman decided to sail again under the English flag. Once more he tried to find a passage around North America to the East.

This time he sailed past Iceland and south of Greenland. Then his crew quarreled over the small supply

of food and fought with one another. Hudson was powerless to control them. It is believed that he and his son were put in a small boat and set adrift on 

Hudson Bay which he had also discovered.

The work of Hudson added to the knowledge of both the Dutch and the English concerning the New World.
