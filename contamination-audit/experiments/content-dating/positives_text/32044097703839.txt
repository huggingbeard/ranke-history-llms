People 

Ethnic composition.--The inhabitants of American Samoa 

Polynesians and are closely related to other people of the central a eastern Pacific. They form a part of the family which includes t 

Hawaiians, the Tahitians, the Tongans, and the Maoris of New Ze land. They are a friendly and generous people and their culture rich in ceremony and mythology. The Samoan language, which closely related to other Polynesian tongues, is believed to have be derived from Malayan origins at the time that the central Pacific w settled. The best evidence available to date indicates the ancestors the present Samoans arrived in the islands from a homeland in sout eastern Asia between A.D. 400 and 600. Although the Samoans ha had increasingly close contact with European institutions, they r main today an outstanding center of Polynesian culture. After mo than 125 years of contact with western influences, the fabric of th Samoan social organization remains intact and continues to be th dominant influence in the lives of the Samoan people. Samoa's bas economic and political unit is the extended family composed of relate kin tracing their origins bilaterally back through the generations mythological ancestors. Within their extended group, which is ad ministered by a matai or chief, a collective family economy prevail The matai is responsible for control of family lands and property an represents the family in the political affairs of village, county, and dis

ict councils. He is responsible for their welfare and is trustee of mily lands and property.

Since the middle of the 19th century, the people of Samoa have een converts to Christianity and indeed have been active in sending issionaries to other islands of the southwest Pacific. They have, owever, at the same time retained those customs and traditional cereonies which have remained meaningful within the context of the ontemporary island environment. To a large extent they have also tained the warmth and the grace of their ancestors and have prerved unspoiled much of the charm of the Polynesians.

Judicial. The judicial power exercisable in American Samoa is ested in the High Court and five district courts. The judicial. -ower, as in other American jurisdictions, is independent of the

executive and legislative powers. It is under the general supervisi and administration of the Chief Justice of American Samoa, who known as the Chief Judge. All courts are courts of record.

The High Court consists of the Chief Justice and four associa judges. Sessions are held at such time and places as the Chief Ju tice deems essential to the promotion of justice. The High Cou has an appellate division, a trial division, and a probate divisio The appellate division consists of the Chief Justice and four associa judges, the presence of the Chief Justice and at least two associa judges being necessary to constitute a quorum for the hearing an determination of an appeal. The trial division ordinarily consis of the Chief Justice and two associate judges, the presence of tl Chief Justice and at least one associate judge being necessary to co stitute a quorum for the trial and determination of a case or contre versy. For the trial and determination of a charge of treason, mu der, manslaughter, or assault with intent to kill, the trial divisio consists of the Chief Justice and four associate judges, two of who are temporary associate judges assigned by the Chief Justice from tl panel of persons so designated by the Governor. The presence of th 

Chief Justice and four other judges is necessary to constitute quorum for the trial and determination of any such charge. Th probate division consists of the Chief Justice alone.

American Samoa is divided into five judicial districts each o which has a district court. The district courts have jurisdiction, con currently with the trial division of the High Court, of all civil case of debt or injury to persons or property in which the amount in dis pute is less than $1,000 and does not involve the title to or right claimed in land. The district courts have original, exclusive jurisdic tion of all criminal cases in which the maximum penalty which may be imposed is a fine of not more than $10 or imprisonment for no more than 1 month. The district courts in Manu'a, which compris the fourth and fifth districts, in addition have original jurisdiction concurrently with the trial division of the High Court, of cases o divorce, separation, annulment of marriage, adoption, all probate matters, and all criminal cases of felony except treason, murder, manslaughter, and assault with intent to kill.

At least one Samoan judge sits in all cases except cases in probate in which the court is exercising original jurisdiction. 

During the year, 337 persons were tried on criminal charges. Of these 272 were convicted and 65 acquitted. Fifteen were sent to jail and 257 fined. Sixteen worked out their fines by doing labor for the public benefit under the direction of the pulenu'u (village mayor) or the county chief. During the fiscal year the courts disposed of 

247 civil cases.

Criminal cases are disposed of as promptly as circumstances ermit, due consideration being given to the defendant's rights under he Constitution of the United States and the bill of rights contained n the American Samoan Code. 

The fundamental human rights of the American Samoans are proected not only by the Constitution of the United States but also by he American Samoan bill of rights, which is patterned largely after he Bill of Rights in the U.S. Constitution. The courts are ever alert o see that these fundamental human rights are fully protected. 

Charges of felony are prosecuted by the Attorney General. If a efendant pleads guilty to a charge of felony, the Government is evertheless required to prove a prima facie case.

In the case of a plea of guilty to a misdemeanor, the court must be atisfied that the defendant clearly understands the charge and makes is plea voluntarily. If in any case it should appear that there is easonable doubt as to the defendant's guilt, he is permitted to withraw his guilty plea.

The Government of American Samoa provides at Government ex- -ense a public defender who is available to assist persons charged with crime. All court personnel are Samoans with the exception of he Chief Judge and Clerk of the High Court who are from the

Police.—The police branch staff consists of the Chief of Police, 1 captain, 3 lieutenants, 5 sergeants, and 47 patrolmen.

American Samoa has no narcotic problem, very little grand larceny, and, in fact, very little major crime of any nature. Most of the offenses against the code are occasioned by thoughtless acts of violence or passion. With this in mind, the Chief of Police, with the assistance of the Attorney General, continues to conduct training courses designed to cope with the type of problems presented here. 

All criminal investigations are under the direction of the police investigating division. Preliminary investigation and prevention is usually handled by the patrolmen who are sent to the scene by the watch officers and who furnish the investigating division with a list of witnesses and a general description of the offense. The investigating officers then complete the investigation by questioning witnesses, taking photographs, and, when necessary, obtaining laboratory and hospital reports. The information obtained is then furnished to the 

Attorney General who determines whether or not the matter requires prosecution. Reports forwarded to the Attorney General are usually in such form that no further investigation need be made before presenting the matter to the court.

Educational Conditions 

Objectives

The basic approach to education in American Samoa is to provide training which will enable the people to serve more effectively within their social, economic, professional, and political structure. At the same time, education must provide suitable background for those who will find it possible to take advantage of opportunities for higher education in the United States or other countries. The mastery of the English language must not be the sole subject goal, but also mas-

ery of basic skills related to science, medicine, law, business, the arts, and other varied fields must be accomplished. Therefore, it becomes the responsibility of the Government of American Samoa, as the Samoans are influenced more and more by contact with the Western civilization, to conserve the best of Samoan culture, to guide the Samoans in developing social changes which are to their advantage, and to bring them to a better understanding of what is best for their vell-being, health, and general improvement.

Additional aims of the Government through education are the reservation and development of their economic resources to enable the people to cope with the problems of an increasing population and increasing demands which accompany modern society and to become as self-supporting a government as possible. 

As English is an international language and the official language of the Government, it is essential that the children become literate in that language while maintaining the bilingual nature of the society. 

Policies.-In line with the above objectives, it shall be the policy of the Department of Education to maintain a program adapted thereto in the following fields: (a) Curriculum.-(1) Teach both Samoan and English as a language in the elementary schools; (2) the fundamental processes of reading, writing, and arithmetic; (3) science and health; (4) social studies, with emphasis on Samoan culture, Government, and environment; (5) vocational preparation; and (6) avocational pursuits. (b) School organization.-Continue the village elementary schools (grades 1 to 6), the junior high schools (grades 7 to 9), the senior high school (grades 10 to 12), and the vocational school (grades 10 to 12). The latter two schools are for a select group of junior high school graduates. Continue, in addition, the 12-year teacher-training college program. (It is proposed to further expand the teachertraining college program to a 2-year course.) (c) Compulsory education.-To continue the long-established and popular policy of compulsory education between the ages of 7 through 15. (d) Buildings and equipment.-An expanding population makes it essential that buildings and equipment are adequate and up to date. 

The general policy shall be that public school education shall be a cooperating enterprise between the local communities and the Government. As a school becomes more advanced in character, the Government shall be responsible for additional support. In the elementary school, the villages provide the school buildings and teachers' living quarters; the Government provides teachers, equipment, and supplies. In the junior high school, high school, vocational, depend-

ents' school, demonstration school, and teacher training college, th Government has sole responsibility of the physical plant. (e) Scholarships.-Provide for and promote scholarships an special training for qualified Samoans in needed fields. (f) Standards.-It shall be the policy to upgrade the entire schoo system as rapidly as possible, with initial emphasis being placed i the five junior high schools which will feed a better trained group o young people into the high school and community life. Special at tention will be given to the teacher-training college and relate demonstration school. This becomes essential in order to provid facilities whereby teachers can be better trained to do their job i upgrading the school system.

Appendix I AMERICAN SAMOA-STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES AND POLICIES 

Objectives

The objectives of the Department of the Interior in the administration of nerican Samoa are (1) To provide for the orderly and progressive development the people toward self-government and (2) To assist the people to attain the ximum possible self-support.

Policies Political advancement.—It is the policy to support the continuance of the rritorial government with separate legislative, executive and judicial departents, constituting a system of checks and balances. 

Within this framework, the political structure of the government shall be in ccord with the desires of the Samoan people in regard to such adaptations as ay be desirable by virtue of Samoan customs, traditions and land ownership. 

During the period of development of self-government, the people and their esources shall be protected against undesirable exploitation. 

It is also the policy to delegate to, and encourage the acceptance of increased uthority by local Samoan officials, including the district governors, county hiefs and village mayors. As the legislative advisory body demonstrates a ontinued ability to legislate effectively, increasingly greater measures of uthority shall be granted to it.

With respect to organic legislation, it is the policy to support such legislation when the Samoan people desire it and are ready for it. It is the policy to seek he views of the Samoans and consult with them as regards such legislation in order that it will contain adequate protection for the rights of the people, ncluding their historic land rights. In the meantime, the Samoans are encouraged to take such forward steps as may be consistent with organic egislation if and when such legislation is considered desirable. 

Economic advancement.-Economic policy in the territory shall be directed toward creating a sound basic economy that will (1) be suited to the needs and resources of Samoa, (2) will provide adequately for the food and monetary needs of the rapidly increasing population, and (3) will establish a firm foundation for self-government and maintain Samoa for Samoans. The territory's limited land resources and rapidly growing population make it essential that every effort be made to increase agricultural production, industrial development and tourist trade.

Since the economy of the area is basically agricultural, emphasis shall be given to encouraging and stimulating agricultural production. This includes the improvement of food crops, poultry and livestock and the increased production of copra and other exportable crops.

Protection of Samoans against the loss of their family lands is an important policy not only as regar the economy, but also as it may affect the Samoan "matai" system. It is the policy to maintain this protection. 

In view of Samoa's lack of resources, and the existence of a substantial labor pool previously accustomed to wage income, it is the policy to look with favor upon the establishment of processing and other minor industries which will bring a source of income to the people. In view of the need for this type of economic activity, favorable consideration shall be given to industrial incentives in the form of tax and other benefits.

As the principal employer of wage workers in the territory, the Government American Samoa shall maintain a wage structure and conditions of employm which are consonant with the advancing social and economic conditions of territory.

General advancement.—It is the policy to respect the Samoans' desire to p tect the Matai system and to consult fully with the Samoans, through th legally constituted representatives, on any question which may affect the pr ervation or destruction of the system. It is also the policy to encourage acceptance of such nonindigenous social concepts as would be beneficial and provide such social regulations and services as may be necessary in the light local conditions and dictated by precepts of common humanity and governmen responsibility. (a) Health. The Public Health program shall be administered so as to p vide necessary medical and dental services, to improve the health and sanita conditions of the people, to minimize communicable diseases and to train nati health personnel. (b) Education.-It is the policy to maintain a free public school system f the education of American Samoans and to provide in that school system a cu riculum which will (1) prepare students for life in American Samoa, and (2 establish a firm foundation for the political, economic, and social advanceme of the people and thus hasten the date when more Samoans can replace stat side employees in their government and Samoa can achieve a greater degree self-government.

Relationship with Western Samoa.-Close cooperation and consultation b tween the Governments of American and Western Samoa shall be encourage in order that action taken by the two governments will take into account th close ties and relationships between the peoples of the two areas. 

Approved: November 27, 1956.
