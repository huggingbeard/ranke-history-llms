The Jews of the World 

INTOLERANCE stalks again in the modern world. 

The Jews, always the early victims of its effects, are bewildered as they contemplate the latest scene. 

The Germany of the Third Reich has vented upon them the full measure of "spite, hatred, hatred and once more hatred," as commanded in Hitler's book, the new Bible of the land, and has precipitated a grave crisis in Jewish life everywhere. It is difficult enough to maintain peace and equilibrium in social relationships under ordinary circumstances. But when all means of invention are directed with passionate cruelty against a whole people, when every conceivable avenue is utilized to inform the world that Jews are the misfortune of Germany, that their race is inferior, that their ethics are dubious, and that they are parasites, it is inevitable that their problem should become a world problem. Objective students of history may wonder whether both sides of the case have been {{5}%

adequately presented. They may find it exceedingly difficult to believe that in the midst of developed industry and science, international progress and extensive travel opportunities, a nation has suddenly repudiated the rules of western civilization; that it has denounced the principles of humanity advocated by all religions; that it has contemptuously flaunted its desire for isolation in the face of neighboring peoples, and has turned its boundaries into an inferno that might beggar even the descriptions of the immortal Dante. But 

German Jews, unfortunately, cannot allow themselves the privilege of such musings. They are in a cauldron dedicated to mystical pagan gods, deprived of their rights, prevented from earning a livelihood, ostracized by their former associates, and humiliated in every possible way. They can hardly be blamed if they are not objective about it, for they face the loss of citizenship and equality of opportunity which they have so dearly prized in the century or more since their emancipation. The wounds of war are felt most by those who are attacked, and the Jews throughout the world know full well the meaning of the governmental policies of Hitlerite Germany, from the boycott of last April to the announcement that henceforth Jews in Germany will no longer be permitted to hold membership in the Esperanto Society of that country!

There can be little doubt of the far-reaching effects of Germany's campaign against the Jews. The countries of the world are now so closely knit by modern inventions that a sore spot in one part of the body of nations quickly affects the rest of the body.

Nazi supporters are not in Germany alone. They are in {6}

this country, in England, in Austria, in France, they are everywhere. The radio stations of Germany roar their blasts against the Jews so that the whole world may hear. The printing presses of Germany issue millions of leaflets and books against the Jews, printed in many languages that the world may read. The 

Jewish refugees from Germany who are now in France, in Czechoslovakia, in Belgium and in many other lands, have seriously complicated the economic situation everywhere. And the German leaders have openly announced their intention of making the Jewish problem a world problem. 

The acuteness of the situation forces the Jews to scan anew this anti-Semitism that has been so widely discussed since Hitler appeared on the German horizon. It is not the traditional kind of Jew-hatred that their ancestors knew and understood. It is something that is deeper-rooted, more devastating in character. Prior to the nineteenth century the anti-Semitism that existed was based upon religion. The Jews had rejected Jesus. They were infidels, unbelievers.

They suffered because their faith was different. The prejudice against them was thus directed in one field -the field of religion. Upon the abandonment of their religion they were free from that prejudice. 

The modern anti-Semitism so violently displayed in Germany and other lands leaves the field of religion to enter a much larger field—the world of economics, and since the economic factor in our civilization is generally linked with life itself, modern anti- Semitism thus becomes a matter of life and death to those who are attacked. The Germans may disguise it under such fanciful names as Aryan supremacy, {7}

racial tendencies, etc., but in reality these theories are rationalizations of the true motive behind the campaign against the Jewish people. They constitute the lever by means of which it is hoped to remove the Jew from whatever position he occupies, to strip him of whatever laurels he has won, to exclude him from the common enjoyment of labor that is his birthright as a free man. The Hitlerites themselves admit it; they frankly confess that the Jew is too difficult a person to compete with, that he is too ambitious, too clever, too resourceful. The unsuccessful in fair competition thus permits himself to believe that he has failed not because of his lack of ability, but because the Jew uses deception to gain his ends. It is a grim sort of naivete that prompts the Nazi party to force Jews into revolting scenes in the motion picture "S. A. 

Mann Brand", but it shows how an inferiority complex can lead people to destroy creative effort in the manner of the dog in the manger. Dr. Alice Hamilton, in her striking articles in the "Survey Graphic" Magazine, has discovered the German definition of a Jew to be a person whose job is desired. 

Such anti-Semitism is difficult to combat, for it is impossible to reason with it. It seeks every unfair advantage in order to eliminate the foe. It knows no pity. It is indifferent to religion and ethics. 

It wishes to remember nothing good of the object of its hatred. The Minister of Propaganda and Enlightenment, Dr. Goebbels, has expressed it in this manner: "Certainly, the Jew is also a human being but the flea is also an animal . . . As the flea is not a pleasant animal, we do not feel any duty to ourselves and our conscience to guard and protect it and to let it thrive ... {8}

so that it may bite, sting and torture us, but to render it innocuous. So it is with the Jews." Anti-Semitism of this nature is generally linked with the economic situation in a country, and it has invariably followed in the wake of economic depressions. Modern history has proved it many times. It was so in the years following the Franco-Prussian war when the German stock exchanges crashed and socialism spread among the masses. The Jews were blamed, even though most of the Jewish fortunes were lost in the crash. 

It was so in 1916 when the Germans were not so sure they would win the war, when an anti-Semitic member of the Reichstag accused the Jews of being slackers, despite the fact that twelve thousand of their number had died for Germany and sixty thousand were in the front line trenches. It was so in the bitter inflation years when the middle class was ruined and a majority of the Jews who were in that middle class were reduced to poverty. It was so in the United 

States when the Ku Klux Klan agitated against the Jews in 1924. It is so in the America of the present, in Greece, in Austria, in England, and in all lands where the economic depression is keenly felt. Blue 

Shirts in Ireland and Silver Shirts in America, Yellow 

Shirts in Poland, Black Shirts in England and Brown

Shirts in Germany all tell the same story - hatred against those vulnerable, the search for channels through which to express economic discontentment. 

It is not surprising that the majority of the Jews are utterly confused and dismayed by the anti-Semitism described above. They themselves have overlooked certain important psychological conditions attending their emancipation in Europe. They have not {9}

known their recent history, or else they have forgotten it. As a consequence, they considered themselves accepted on terms of equality by their neighbors, sure that anti-Semitism could not occur in a world which respects international laws and gives expression of its devotion to the principles of humanity. They confidently believed that modern cultural development could meet any aberration of social progress and handle it through the enlightened conscience of man. 

They built their lives on the assumption that if the gentile does not like the Jew, it is because the gentile has met the wrong kind of Jew. There are good people and bad people, they reasoned, and the sensitiveness of their Christian neighbors to the violation of the ethics of the Gospels would not permit the existence of anti-Semitism. Surely, their contributions to the civilization of their day would break down unjustified prejudices and hatreds. They remembered their scientists and artists, their students and their philanthropists, their professional men and women whose creative effort extends beyond the Jewish community to benefit their nation and world. They contemplated their patriotic devotion to the countries in which they had lived for centuries, their soldiers who had sacrificed their lives, their civilians who had given of their treasure and their energy in every national enterprise. 

How could the modern world overlook this? How could it forget? How could it repay love, sacrifice and devotion with coldness, suspicion and hatred?

The answers to these questions may be found in the attitude of the Christian peoples in the world of the eighteenth, nineteenth and twentieth centuries. 

Anti-Semitism is essentially a Christian problem, for {10}

people do not ordinarily endeavor to direct hatred against themselves. The Jews of the modern world have enjoyed periods when they were free from physical barriers to normal existence. But it is exceedingly doubtful whether they have ever been free from moral barriers to good fellowship and friendship. 

Thirty-six years ago Max Nordau, the eminent scholar, pointed out the neglected dangers of Jewish emancipation in a masterful address which he then delivered before the first Zionist Congress assembled at 

Basle, Switzerland. "You remember the old proverb," he told his audience, "if a man wishes to drown a dog, he says it is mad." And then he continued in the following vein: "I must give utterance to the painful truth; the nations which have emancipated the Jews have been self-deceived as to their true sentiments. In order for the emancipation to have been complete it must have been perfected in good will ere ever it found expression in law. The history of Jewish emancipation is one of the most remarkable chapters in the history of European thought. It is not come of the perception that a race has been shamefully sinned against, and that it is high time to atone for a thousand years of injustice; it is simply the outcome of the straight ruled geometrical manner of French rationalism of the eighteenth century. This rationalism was simply bare logic without the slightest reference to living sensibility; its principles were of the certainty of a mathematical axiom and consisted in efforts to realize these visions of pure reason and {11}

to make them of account in the world. . . Jewish emancipation became one of the indispensable signs of a highly civilized state; something like the piano which on no account must be missing in the drawing room though no single member of the family can play it." 

Dr. Nordau uttered a profound truth. It has been demonstrated time and again in Jewish. experience. That a Jew with objectionable external characteristics should be summarily dealt with by the non-Jewish world is fairly understandable. It follows somewhat the social law of gregariousness where humans look sharp askance of those who are radically unlike. But what of the cultured Jew? Why has he met with such prejudice in his general environment? Surely, not because of his dress, nor his carriage, nor his manner; not because of his iniquity, (he is known to be straightforward and honest in his dealings, and he contributes more than his share toward the upkeep of the larger community), but because genuine friendship cannot be legislated. Honest recognition of equality cannot be enforced by public decree. It must spring from within the human being. Cultured Jews who have drunk deep of secular knowledge have come to know this in some way and to feel it. They have experienced a peculiar discomfort in their position, finding no definite security in a world which they assumed to be their home. This is Jewish suffering in the moral sense. It is far more bitter than physical suffering because it deals with people of more sensitive nature and keener pride. Nordau, the gifted student who wrote "The Conventional {12}

Lies of Civilization," saw clearly the unwillingness of the national masses to accept the Jews who had lived among them for centuries. "At the time of the Black 

Death," he said, "the Jews were accused of poisoning the wells; today the corn merchants accuse them of beating down the price of grain; the artisans of destroying the small trades; and the conservatives that they are in their principles opposed to a constitutional form of government." And this was in the year 1897!

Thus far the general problem of world Jewry has been considered in the light of existing conditions. 

There yet remains the necessity of outlining some course of action that will meet the situation adequately and effectively. Whatever thoughts are offered in this connection follow the assumption that the Jews of Germany are completely ruined. Life is no longer tolerable for them in the land influenced by Hitler's teachings. They are economically throttled, cut off from their business and professional enterprises, and bereft of the opportunities they once enjoyed. Their children will find it increasingly difficult to grow in an atmosphere poisoned by unbridled hate. They must look elsewhere to their future existence and consequently they will create enormous responsibilities for the rest of the Jews of the world. Whether or not those responsibilities will be adequately met is something that even the most sanguine cannot answer at this moment. It becomes increasingly clear, however, that the Jews outside of Germany will have to bestir themselves mightily before any adequate adjustment to this unprecedented condition will be possible. {13}

I. First, the Jews of the world will have to show unity of purpose and action. They cannot afford to be divided and helpless, as they have been in the past. There is too much at stake in the crisis, and unless they offer a concerted defense to the ruthless attacks upon them, the present German rulers will increase their nefarious acts, secure in the knowledge that hopeless divisions among Jews make their task simple. A central clearing house for world Jewish opinion should be established at the earliest possible moment to make sure that the one test of Jewish leadership in the days to come will be the disposition to subordinate every problem to this world Jewish problem.

As a corollary to this step, the Jews of the world will have to live their lives more openly. Too many prominent Jews have been anxious to demonstrate how un-Jewish they were. Germany is not the only nation that has considered Jews as citizens when it took the fruits of their labor, and the same citizens as Jews when it came to the question of awards and honors, or when it came to a consideration of the value of such individuals to the national culture. Curiously enough, it is Max Reinhardt, long estranged from Judaism, who has expressed the belief that in the long run it would be better for Jews to let their contributions bear the hall-mark of their Jewishness without necessarily isolating themselves from the general environment. Reinhardt had learned his lesson from sad experience, for Hitler did not hesitate to call him a parasite despite his contributions to the German theatre. {14}

2. Secondly, the Jews of the world will have to develop the land of Palestine. Jewish refugees in lands bordering Germany are creating severe difficulties for their own in those countries. They cannot easily be absorbed therein because of the prevailing economic depression. Palestine, therefore, is not merely a possibility to the Jews of Germany; it is a necessity. There must be a haven of refuge for the thousands who have already left Germany, and for the thousands who will eventually be forced to migrate elsewhere to start life anew. 

3. Thirdly, the Jews of the world will have to enlist the aid of the League of Nations to save their own in Germany from worse trials than those they have thus far endured. The League itself may be in a precarious position, but so long as it exists, its offices must be used. The Bernheim petition has indicated that the present rulers of Germany are amenable to international pressure. The decision of the 

League Council in favor of the Jews was highly important, but far more important were the implications of the discussions on the subject. The members of the Council did not hesitate to oppose the German methods of dealing with minorities within that country. Mr. Joseph Paul-Boncour, the Foreign Minister of France called attention to the fact that when the peace treaties were being drawn up, Germany insisted strongly on the desirability of minority protection, and at that time she stated clearly that she would respect the right of minorities within her own territory. The League of Nations has clearly established the general principle that the rights of racial, religious {15}

and linguistic minorities are not matters of domestic policy, but constitute obligations of international concern. Germany, having put the Jews in the minority position, cannot well plead that they are not a minority.

Time alone will answer many of the perplexing questions that have risen to plague the Jews of the modern world. At the moment they know definitely that they have been thrown into the forefront of the stage on which the international events take place. 

They face a situation not altogether of their own making, but they can overcome their danger if they meet it properly. There are the usual alternatives open to them, and they must make their choice. The first is indifference. They may consider the wretched 

German Jews as charity patients in need of help, and remain complacent in the belief that Hitlerism cannot take root in other countries. Unfortunately, the German Jews, too, were indifferent to their situation at one time. They learned their mistake too late. Even those who thought their wealth and station guaranteed protection against destruction found that in the economic field of anti-Semitism there is no consideration of person, place or group. "What makes the Jewish situation so hideous (in Germany) is that it is inescapable," says Dr. Hamilton, "one can hide one's opinions, but not one's race; it involves people who have never concerned themselves with politics as much as the most ardent Democrats, and it strikes with special cruelty the children whose only crime is to have been born."

The second alternative is despair. The Jewish people are unstrung and shocked at the events in {16}

Germany. They had always considered that country the center of culture and civilization, and now, as they look daily in the newspapers to see what fresh indignities are heaped on their own, they grow more and more uneasy at the increasing tempo of anti- Semitism. Not even converts to Christianity are to be spared, they read, if their ancestry was tainted with Jewish blood as early as the year 1800. Their uneasiness turns to sadness and fear, and fear paralyzes them. 

In a crisis, the like of which has not been experienced before, they are incapable of thought or action that will bring relief to those in distress and avert the spread of the danger.

The third alternative is resistance. The Jews of the modern world cannot permit the loss of their hard won rights to citizenship and equality of opportunity without a struggle. They can and must fight, not with the weapons of their insane adversaries, but with the moral indignation that their prophets bequeathed to them, with renewed loyalty to their own, with boycott of those who would strangle them, with continued solemn protest before the bar of public opinion. 

They must realize that the progress of education is slow, the veneer of civilization is thin, the hold of religious ethics is feeble, and the promise of the golden age is no more certain today than it was centuries ago. They can easily recall that they were a problem in the days of the Greeks and the Romans because they wanted to live and let live, and because they refused to be regimented into dull conformity to a status quo. 

They are still a problem, but they will solve the problem by standing on their own merits and by assuming their burdens in the light of the advantages they 17}

have thus far won in fair and honorable competition. 

They can yet remind the world that they are an old people, a people that has known misunderstanding and scorn and hatred before, and that their present bit of history in Germany, while graver than any they have experienced in the past, does not by any means spell the end of Jewish life and ideals. {18}

II The Jews of the United States THE LIFE of the Jewish people in America strik ingly illustrates the late lamented principle of "rugged individualism." In the cities where they dwell they are burdened with competing synagogues that groan under mortgages and portray the religion of Israel as anything but an ennobling, inspiring cause about which to rally. The multiplicity of their institutions indicate how hard it is for them to agree on methods of relief for their poor, their orphaned and their sick. 

The lack of understanding and sympathy between group and group is sad evidence of their inability to recognize even those common aims and aspirations which are characteristic of the Jew everywhere and which constitute the traditions they have inherited from their fathers. It may be the inevitable result of the full freedom they have enjoyed under the American form of government, for democracy is a philosophy of individual expression and not a symbol of coordination. (19)

Such a state of affairs might not have mattered greatly in times past. But in this hour the Jews of the world are passing through a critical stage of their existence, and the American Jews, no less than the German Jews and the English Jews, are vitally affected by the repercussions of the events in Germany. So serious are the conditions facing them here in America they can no longer resort to the old rationalizations to justify their divisions, their quarrels, their misunderstandings and their mistakes. They are challenged to the very fibre of their being to analyze carefully their status in this great Republic, to marshal all their resources and their intelligence to meet the grave problems of their own in Europe and the lesser problems here in the United States with which they must deal wisely and well if they are to avert serious consequences to themselves in the not too distant future. A survey of the present status of the national 

Jewish life can leave no doubt of the stagnant condition of a great community of four million souls. 

The first thing that is quickly apparent to anyone who scans the American Jewish scene is that there is no genuine national leadership in Jewish affairs. 

There are many organizations, but none are truly representative of the complete sentiment of the Jewish people. The American Jewish Committee is perhaps the most prominent of the national Jewish bodies, but its effect upon Jewish life is negligible. This organization grew out of a New York social club known as the Wanderers, a group of cultured New York Jews which met at the homes of the members to discuss Jewish affairs. At the time of the Kishineff massacres in 1905 the little club decided to enlarge its member- <20>

ship with Jews selected throughout the country and to call itself The American Jewish Committee. The Committee did heroic work in behalf of the Jews in the difficult post-war rehabilitation years. In the present crisis, however, it has not shown itself capable of resolute action calculated to benefit the Jews abroad and to strengthen the status of the Jews of America. It may have contact with certain officials at Washington, it may hold private conversations with visiting diplomats regarding the Jewish situation in Europe, but it does not represent the American Jewish life. 

It is a self-created, self-perpetuating body, of which the Average American Jew knows very little beyond the fact that prominent names are associated with it. 

The American Jewish Congress is another national Jewish organization, the creation of Dr. Stephen 

S. Wise. It stands in much closer proximity to the average Jew because it is at least democratic in principle and because it seeks the mandate of the masses of the Jewish people for its utterances. But like the 

American Jewish Committee, its personnel and its influence are too little known by the Jewish people who reside west of New York City. Then, too, its representation of Jewish rights has been questioned by not a few of the Jews of the country who feel that this body has not always been judicious in publicizing its endeavors. As a consequence, it has not won the support that it so ardently desired. The quarrels between the "Committee" and the "Congress" have disheartened those who believe that there is no room for competing national Jewish organizations. Impartial observers who have longed for unity and cooperation and found only irreconcilable differences of attitude between {21}

these organizations have cried "a plague o' both your houses" and have remained aloof from their enterprises. 

The third national Jewish organization that is well known to American Jewry is the Independent Order B'nai Brith. Here the representation is adequate for there are lodges of the Order all over America. These lodges are organized into districts and the districts are under one general head-the national executive. But the policies of the B'nai Brith as enunciated by its international officers residing in America have been vague. 

Perhaps this organization has endeavored to take a position midway between the American Jewish Committee and the American Jewish Congress. At any rate, it has not crystallized American Jewish opinion through its public expressions. Having given tacit approval to the policy of the American Jewish Committee, the B'nai Brith stands in the same relationship to the Jewish masses as that organization. Its leadership has been neither far-sighted nor discerning of the thousands of members that it represents.

Some twenty years ago certain members of the B'nai Brith built within the larger structure a little organization that was destined to play a most significant part in the future Jewish life in the country. 

It was the Anti-Defamation League, created to fill a definite need in America, and because it circumscribed its activity, it succeeded as no other national Jewish organization could. At the time of its inception, vaudeville actors caricatured the Jew and thereby perpetuated the cruelty of centuries against a whole people. 

Through the quiet energies of this body the distorted portrayals ceased. At that time also the Associated 

Press of the country constantly used the term "Jew" {22}

in connection with the publication of a Jewish name. 

Again the organization labored for months, and finally won from the head of the Associated Press the admission of its mistake and the order which was sent to every newspaper office in the land instructing that the term "Jew" must not be used in connection with the publication of any Jewish name, for a man's group and his religion cannot and ought not to be associated with his individual acts. Since those early days the Anti- 

Defamation League has acted quietly and effectively for the Jews of the nation, staving off impending dangers that threatened them time and again. Utilizing the facilities of the B'nai Brith lodges the country over, it deputized Jewish citizens of the various localities to be its agents and to transmit to its headquarters the problems of the local centers as they would arise. 

It succeeded in amalgamating Jewish life throughout the country through one community of interest-the protection of the Jewish name against slander. It may be that its achievements are unknown and unacknowledged, for it has steadfastly adhered to its guiding principle that its labors must be accomplished quietly and attended by no publicity. In the present crisis the Anti-Defamation League is preeminently the organization fitted to carry out an organized program of defense of Jewish rights in America.

The second thing that is quickly apparent to the surveyor of the American Jewish scene is the shocking indifference to the menace of Hitlerism in America. So alarming is the situation that one wonders how 

Jewish people can avoid its implications. There is definite evidence to indicate that Hitlerite Germany is sending to this country printed matter in support {{23}

of its doctrines. The reappearance of the infamous 

Protocols of the Elders of Zion is not an accidental matter divorced from Germany's anti-Jewish policy. 

The menace has become serious enough to warrant the publication of a booklet by the Anti-Defamation League to counteract the slanders and lies of these freely circulated Protocols. Pamphlets printed in both 

German and English condemning Jewish life and influence can be found in almost any city of the country if diligent effort be made to discover them. 

That Hitler organizations exist in the larger cities of the United States is now well known. A typical meeting was reported in Chicago recently. The German consul was on the platform, as was the editor of a widely circulated German newspaper. The Nazi anthem, the Horst Wessel song, was sung by the audience. Twelve hundred persons paid twenty-five cents each to listen to speeches lauding the Hitler regime and denouncing the Jews in America. Brown shirts, swastikas and steel helmets were in open evidence at this meeting.

The universities of the world have established exchange fellowships to enable their students to study abroad and enrich the culture of the land upon their return. Those who have come to our shores from 

New Germany seem bent upon giving America some instruction. They are thoroughly indoctrinated with 

Nazi ideas, having been selected for sojourn in America because of their capacity to direct propaganda in favor of the Hitler regime. Their nefarious activities have already been discovered in several of our universities and sharply challenged.

The ways of propaganda are insidious and the <24}

Hitlerites are acknowledged masters of the art. There may be more than one meaning to an apparently harmless address delivered before a luncheon club, as the Jewish people have had occasion to learn recently. 

There is on file in the Anti-Defamation League office the record of an event in one of the smaller cities of the country. A speaker at a service club luncheon deviated from the subject of his address to offer his audience a few animadversions against the Jews. On the same afternoon signs appeared in several of the stores of that city bearing the legend "don't buy from the Jews."

Native Americans, too, are now inspired by Hitlerite achievements. They have formed an organization called "The Silver Shirts of America" headed by one 

William D. Pelley. Only a paragraph is here devoted to their activities, but it is sufficient to show the sinister motives of this new menace to Jewish peace in the 

United States. Mr. Pelley edits the magazine "Liberation," the official organ of the "Silver Shirts" which is to be found on news stands in many cities. Its contents are vile-as vile as anything the Hitler papers have printed about the Jews. The following excerpt from a recent number of the publication is typical of the attacks that Mr. Pelley has consistently levelled against American Jewry during the past year: "America's Jews certainly love trouble. They love trouble so much that when all is well with them, they go out of their way to make it for themselves. When they have made it, they set up a twentieth century Wailing Wall, protesting that they are being 'persecuted' because of their {25}

• • religion. It is an old trick, and in generations bygone it has met with success. Suddenly, however, gentiles by wholesale are commencing to perceive it. And gentiles by wholesale, are no longer being fooled If he were adroit, suave and constructive in this, he still might contrive to hold his gains. But he is anything but adroit, suave and constructive. The instant he suspects that he has the upper hand, he shows himself boorish, vulgar, arrogant and selfishly predatory. He is interested only in the tribe of Judah. 

Other races exist for Hebrew enhancement. By the documentary attestment of his own rabbinate, he is a nation unto himself. Automatically and inexorably, the Israelites are manufacturing their own racial reprisals. In this respect we are compelled to agree with the slogan of the Hitlerites: "The Jews are to blame!'"

The third thing easily apparent to the observer of the American Jewish scene is the great ignorance of things Jewish, the indifference to Jewish values and the lack of support of worthy Jewish enterprises manifested by those responsible for Jewish life today. Benjamin Stollberg and Dorothy Thompson have located the difficulty in the American Jewish scene when they have mentioned the Rabbis as professional Jews. 

The lay people have little attachment to Jewish literature, Jewish culture, Jewish creative endeavor and Jewish ideals. The cause of faulty national Jewish organization may well be found in the fact that few of those outside the "professional Jewish" group are qualified to discuss, to counsel and to labor in behalf <26}

of Jewish life. The responsibility for the upkeep of 

Judaism rests ultimately with the laiety. From time immemorial the life of the Jewish group has been guided by principles of democracy. The Jews have survived not because of their leaders, but because their lesser individuals have contributed vitally to the causes and needs of every generation of their life. 

American Jewry is now at the crossroads of its destiny. It must definitely decide upon some course of action. Despite the assertions of Elmer Rice, Martha

Gruening and others, it is not possible for the Jews to assimilate themselves completely. Even if they wished it, the present conditions would not permit them to do so. But they do not wish it. They desire to keep their Judaism. And since assimilation is unthinkable to the masses, they are faced with the necessity of adjusting themselves to the realities of their environment. For the three deficiencies mentioned above three remedies suggest themselves. 

1. First, the Jews of the land can consolidate their interests and their efforts to achieve maximum protection for their existence. To do so they must survey their equipment critically, take greater interest in their communal problems, study the nature of the conditions attending the American Jewish scene, realize their possibilities and their limitations, and demand of their leadership intelligent guidance and advice free from vainglorious publicity and void of individualism and its attendant evils. Certain of the characteristics of present day Jewish leadership may have been adequate in the pioneer days when each Jewish community lived independently. Today they {{27}

are not only superfluous and unnecessary, but decidedly dangerous to the national Jewish well-being. 

2. Secondly, the Jews can eliminate their overlapping organizations. Useless competition is harmful in national Jewish causes where so much is at stake, and irresponsible people can inflict great harm upon their own by speaking and acting without the sanction and wishes of the great Jewish body politic to whom the words and actions are inevitably attributed. There is no reason why Jews cannot be made aware of the efficiency of their separate organizations, so that they concentrate their support and thus obtain the maximum benefit from each. If the Anti- 

Defamation League can deal best with one phase of a given problem, it is not necessary for other organized national Jewish bodies to do the same work. The 

American Jewish Congress and the American Jewish Committee may have other tasks which they can handle with better results. These tasks should be definitely known and classified. We do not suffer from too many national organizations, but from the needless duplication of activity and from the overlapping of purposes that accompanies the life of these organizations. 

3. Thirdly, the Jews of the land can educate themselves. They can cease the practice of making the modern Rabbi the sole repository of Jewish knowledge, Jewish interests, Jewish concerns and Jewish responsibilities. They can give intellectual aid to their so-called "professional Jews." Their pathway to knowledge and participation in the larger Jewish life is unbarred. There are books printed in English if 28}

they wish to read them, organizations doing Jewish work if they wish to associate with worthy endeavors, and causes awaiting their help if they wish to espouse them. There is work to be done, earnest constructive work along all lines of Jewish endeavor. Our children are still told the story of the prince who lost his sword and found the blunt and broken weapon of a craven, to wield it as a finely tempered steel blade. 

The inferior organizations and institutions await the fire and devotion and sacrifice of the masses to make them strong, intelligent and meaningful in the larger community.

Changing America is now confronted with new problems in every channel of human effort. The old methods have been found wanting, the old policies have been found inadequate. Jewish life too must undergo change in order to meet the difficulties that inevitably accompany the shift in economics, politics and philosophy in the present American scheme of things. The tranquility and happiness of the great 

American Jewish household of the future will be in proportion to the healthy change and adjustment it is able to make in this period of crisis. {29}

III The Jews of the Local Community 

نند THE VISITOR to the large city who embarks on a sight-seeing tour in one of the many busses parked on the downtown corners can behold much that is bizarre and startling in the trip. The itinerary generally includes the Jewish ghetto. The stranger from Arkansas or Mississippi, or Vermont, where

Jews may be scarce, or from a distant city containing a ghetto which he has never seen, is filled with wonderment as he listens to the guide attempt to describe the human beings who crowd the thoroughfares, surround the stores and inhabit the houses of the area. 

There is bustle, confusion and shouting everywhere, albeit it is tinged with a spirit of friendliness and marked by good humour. The sounds of a distinctly strange language are heard to commingle with the spoken English. It is a jargon which, the guide says, is Yiddish, a European importation consisting of old German, Russian, Polish and Hebrew. The gentile sight-seer <31}

might nod his head in clear understanding of the situation. He had read somewhere that there are four million Jews in the United States, and that they are clannish. He was observing proof of the fact, for here they lived in a single community not unlike the Italians or Poles. The Jewish sight-seer might shake his head in bewilderment as he contemplated the scene, for he had never known Jewish life outside of his particular environment where it centered about a Temple and its Rabbi, and where it was expressed in an occasional religious service that he attended. 

If both sight-seers knew the truth they would be astonished. The gentile would, upon investigation, discover Jews that so resembled him in thought, outlook and pursuits that he could never recognize them alongside the dwellers of the ghetto. A very good example of such type could well be the person who sat next to him in the motor bus, perplexed at it all. 

The Jew would find that Judaism and Jewish life are not confined to certain precincts of any given community, and that the characters who now excite his interest and curiosity are most intimately linked with the people Israel.

We cannot deal with the gentile's casual estimate of Jews and Judaism, for it is not expected that he will interest himself in Jewish affairs any more than he would expect a Jew to delve deeply into the problems of his circle or of his church. Many who long for understanding, peace and good will between group and group little realize how difficult it is to take even the most preliminary steps toward such goals. People are hemmed in too closely by their environment and the cultures do not impinge upon each {{32}

other. Jews, for example, know next to nothing of the people who frequent the Greek Orthodox churches of the city. These Christians reside within a few miles of the synagogues, yet, so far as Jewish interest is concerned, they might never exist. And as Jews consider

Greek Orthodox churchgoers strangers to their lives and traditions, their holy days and their customs, so do they consider Jews a people foreign to their aspirations and sympathies. It is an odd phenomenon in this day of fellowships of faith and wide travel opportunities, but it is a problem that the science of Sociology must solve in some manner if the happiness of the city is to be assured. Unfortunately, the world of the average person is not wide; it is narrow and provincial. Sometimes the representatives of the different groups meet at civic functions, exchange greetings and make sincere gestures of amity and concord. But the masses are clannish. They huddle about their own institutions as much as possible, for they long for the sights they knew in childhood, the associations which they formed through the early years of development, and the mores to which they have become thoroughly accustomed.

In this disquisition we are concerned primarily with the Jew's estimate of the thousands of Jewish people who dwell in the average large center of population, who hold sacred the same holy days and holidays, respect the same memories and traditions, pray to the same God, share the same destiny, suffer the same fate, and yet are unknown to each other. The processes of Americanization have not made uniform advancement among them, for the periods when they or their fathers migrated to these shores are not iden- {33}

tical. Therefore they move in different social channels, not seldom oblivious to the fact that their heritage is a common one and that the difference between the so-called ghetto Jew and the Jewish sight-seer traveling on the comfortable bus through the densely populated district is a difference purely of time. It is a common ambition of all people to move from a poor neighborhood to a better one. In seeking to improve their station in life, the Jews tend to stress that which is most easily apparent to the observer-their material well-being. There is nothing distinctive about this. 

It is a typically American trait.

The first thing noticeable in an analysis of the Jewish milieu in an American city is that the people differ markedly in their approach to Judaism. The present generation cannot be held altogether responsible for the difference. Those who settled in the cities earlier adapted themselves sooner to conditions attending American life, while those who came later are perhaps still in the process of adjusting themselves to the larger environment. The conflicts and antagonisms that arose in the nineteenth century may have been inevitable in a land of unrestricted freedom, but they never permitted the formation of a balanced Jewish community. Liberal Jews were impatient with their Orthodox brethren and Orthodox Jews were distant toward their liberal co-religionists, until they reached the point where not even the common values inherent in Jewish life and religion could bridge the groups within the greater life of Judaism. The chasm became wider as the years went by, and today many a Jew in an average large community realizes the irony of such statements as those printed in the spurious 134}

Protocols of the Elders of Zion, that the Jews are united in everything.

The city of Chicago offers interesting proof of the many divisions in Jewish life. It is probably typical of other communities in varying degree. On the west side of this metropolis is a tremendous number of Orthodox Jews, conservatively estimated at one hundred forty thousand souls, living in one compact area known as the Lawndale district. They are for the most part unorganized and are led by countless Orthodox Rabbis, few of whom are outstanding and universally respected. There are groups of Conservative 

Jews scattered over the city who possess highly capable leaders, but their organization is weak and their active participation in Jewish communal life does not bring with it the full enhancement of the community. The dozen liberal synagogues do not have more than six or seven thousand members, and while these institutions are effectively organized and play an important part in the civic life of Chicago, they generally do so without the knowledge, the consent and the cooperation of the great masses of the Jews of the city. Then there are the 

Zionists who, with a maximum membership of twenty-five hundred, have, nevertheless, managed to cut through the strata of the Jewish people, at least in one instance, to obtain support for the tremendous "Jewish Day" pageant held in connection with the Century of Progress. Another group of Jews that it is difficult to classify is a unit that concerns itself neither with the synagogue nor with religion, and yet it has a feeling for the Jewish people and a strong desire for Jewish identity. It thinks in terms of Jewish life and creative 

Jewish culture, is radical in its political outlook and {35}

intelligent in the direction of its projects. It is a spiritual entity within the vast unorganized realm of Chicago Jewry, and it gave a striking demonstration of its virility and its strength when twenty-five thousand of its members marched in protest against the German government's inhuman treatment of the Jews. 

Last, if not least, is the Federated Charities group which counts on the larger humanitarian motives of its members to form its organization, and because of this it cannot be considered representative of the Jewish people of the city in the deeper sense of the term. 

The result of division in Jewish life in the average city of this country is chaos. There are petty leaders, but no great leaders. In the confusion, the unrespected and the unfit often assume positions of eminence. The capable and the intelligent shrink from contact with such a situation and leave the field to those who care to assume power. Faction rises against faction, group against group, the strong do not lend their strength to correct evils, and the majority merely fit into the picture, or remain aloof and indifferent. 

There are many consequences of the chaos in local Jewish life. Space permits the mention of but a few. The first is the gross neglect of the institutions that have been built over a long period of years by noble-minded and farsighted Jews. The Rabbis may deserve a share of the blame, but they cannot be held completely responsible for the lack of appreciation of what the synagogue has to offer to those whose lot it is to be willing or unwilling Jews. The synagogue is, after all, the most striking evidence of public dignity that the Jew can show to his Christian neighbor. The

Jewish educational institutions, in which the pre- {36}

ceding generations took such pride, are at the point of disintegrating. And even the most noteworthy of all the enterprises of the Jewish people, the Charities, are functioning in a manner that would cause wonder to the Jewish settlers of New Amsterdam, who are said to have promised Governor Peter Stuyvesant that never, so long as Jews lived on this continent, would they fail to take care of their own who were in need. In Chicago that magnificent institution, the Jewish People's Institute, is desperately struggling to keep from closing its doors. The effect of this remarkable cultural and social center upon the countless thousands of Jews who draw from it sustenance and knowledge in their leisure hours may perhaps never be realized until it actually ceases to function.

A second consequence of chaos in the local Jewish life is the noticeable lack of pride in the Jewish community. Jews often bemoan the fact that ordinary politicians are their best known representatives in civic life. Yet they do nothing to alter the situation. If those who are recognized as philanthropic, cultural and social leaders in the various cities only knew that a few poor Jewish politicians in public life can be and are accepted as the best that Judaism has to offer to the world, they would better understand why inadequate Jewish participation in legislative and executive functions of city government can in a very short time destroy the effect of their enormous financial and moral contributions to civic betterment. 

A third consequence of this chaos is found in the inability of the Jews in cities west of New York to assert themselves in national Jewish affairs. It is start- {37}

ling to discover how insignificant a role the inhabitants of these centers of Jewish population actually play in the American Jewish scene. They possess no voice, no spirit, no integrated life from which leadership might come. Where they should each contribute something vital to the national Jewish life, the overwhelming majority of the Jewish communities throughout the land are at the mercy of any so-called national Jewish leader that chooses to exploit them. 

Few ideas initiate within their corporate limits. Most are brought to them from New York, and they accept the domination of this eastern metropolis without resisting even those national leaders whose course of action may be inimical to the best interests of the Jews of the nation.

The feebleness of the Jewish public institutions and the lack of dignity and pride in local Jewish life are in strange contrast with individual Jewish achievement in all fields of endeavor. Such conditions cannot continue without bringing in their train great hardship and sorrow for the people who tolerate them. There was never any excuse for their presence even in the beginnings of Jewish communal life in our country. Now the acuteness of the problem the world over should not permit their existence. The Hitler organizations in the cities of America make unity in local Jewish life imperative; the poverty that abides among Jews makes necessary the maintenance of charitable institutions to fill the many needs; the ignorance that prevails in Jewish life demands the support of the educational institutions to raise the level of intelligence; the condition of the unemployed Jews who have time on their hands justifies the added ex- {38}

penditures in behalf of community centers where they may turn their idle hours to good use; the bewildered and confused Jews who seek understanding of their course of procedure in these difficult times indicate more strongly than ever the need of synagogues where they may find inspiration and strength.

But the responsibility of every Jewish community is enduring, not temporary. It must function in conformity to a well-conceived plan showing not only the pride of worthy endeavor, but the social philosophy for which its people has been noted throughout the centuries. Jewish differences, are, after all, not inherent differences, they are petty ones. Jews are akin even if they do not think alike, or talk alike or dress alike, and there is no reason why there cannot be mutual respect between group and group within the larger fold. It is not necessary for all Jews to be Orthodox to sympathize with Orthodox Judaism, nor for all to be Reform to sympathize with and appreciate the aims of Reform Judaism. It is, however, necessary to be open-minded and liberal in spirit. Nor can the 

Jews read out of existence the unorganized thousands who do not fit into their category and with whose aims and purposes they may not agree. They belong to the Jewish community, whether they are known or not, ofttimes contributing more to that community than do those who underestimate their importance. 

The Jews of wealth and station, the Jews who are more fortunate in their adjustment because their fathers succeeded earlier, or because opportunity came sooner to them, must assume responsibility for the humbler members of the great community. They must do so voluntarily and realize that if they do not un- 139}

dertake this responsibility themselves, they will eventually undermine their own position through such neglect.

Two courses lie before the Jews of the cities. 

The first is apathy. They can let things be as they are. They can be content to leave untouched the dangerous roots their fathers overlooked in the struggle to improve their position, the roots of misunderstanding, ignorance and prejudice between the German Jew, the Russian Jew, the Polish Jew and the

Roumanian Jew, the roots that were not of their own planting, but which are decidedly their concern today. They can regard complacently the poor specimens of Judaism that often represent them in civic affairs and constitute the standard by which the Jew is likely to be measured. They can be coldly aloof from those Jews not within their circle, their club, or their Temple.

There is another course. That course demands an interest in the local situation, a realization that those who belong to any given Jewish organization are not isolated, but are part of the life of the thousands of Jewish people whose interests are determined by events and literature and tradition that bear a common stamp. If they have the education, the refinement, the advantages that others lack, then they must use those talents in behalf of the less capable and the less fortunate within their ranks. When wrongs occur in 

Jewish life the best are blamed along with the worst by the outside world, and the sooner the individual Jew realizes this phenomenon which is peculiar to a minority group, the better able will he be to adjust himself to his world and to live calmly and creatively.
