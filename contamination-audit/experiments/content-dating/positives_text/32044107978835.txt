JAPAN in MANCHURIA 

Today the Manchurian question is absorbing the minds of our people in Japan because of the realization that it is connected with the national security and economic welfare of our country. Manchuria is the only barrier between Japan and Russia, which, in the former years under Czarist regime, was aggressively seeking for an ice-free port in Asia and which is now dominated by a social system so incompatible with the political and economic order prevailing in our country. 

During the past few months, newspapers have reported to youprobably in colorful manner-of the incidents which have been occurring in Manchuria. You have doubtless been perplexed by these reports of extraordinary events and have been entirely at a loss to draw satisfactory conclusions. As a matter of fact, the rapidity of events have thrown even the statesmen of the League of Nations, for a time, into a state of confusion. 

But, by this time, perhaps the world is at least sure of this much that the affairs in China cannot be judged according to the standards prevailing in other countries and, also, that the incidents in Manchuria cannot properly be appraised, unless they are carefully examined in the light of past history. 

What then is the historical background of the Manchurian situation? 

And in this connection, other questions will naturally come to our minds. 

Why and how did Japan come into Manchuria which is a part of the territory of China? What has been her policy and what has she accomplished in Manchuria?

Shortly after the middle of last century when Japan was compelled by Commodore Perry-very much against her will-to come out from her life of seclusion, she was amazed to find the powerful nations of the West vying with each other in expanding their territorial possessions. The hands of aggression were felt by practically every country in Asia. It was most fortunate for Japan that our leaders in those early days awoke to the necessity of zealously safeguarding our national independence. Our country, too, was compelled to swallow the bitter pill of unequal treaties by accepting extraterritorial jurisdiction; but our fathers were quick to see the inevitableness of the situation and, with determination, they set themselves to the task of removing the causes. Our country reorganized our political system, codified our laws, established unimpeachable judiciary, encouraged popular education, developed industry and commerce, and organized the forces of national defense. By shouldering squarely the responsibilities of a modern state, we were able to negotiate with the powers of the world to abolish extraterritoriality. 

Within the borders of our own territory, we were thus able to ensure security, but in the territory of our nearest neighbor Korea, we were confronted with dangers due to weak government and unsettled conditions. 

Korea in the hands of a hostile power would be a dagger pointed at our heart and it was to save Korea that we were compelled to engage in a war with China. By the Treaty of Peace signed at Shimonoseki on April 17, 1895, China ceded to Japan in perpetuity and full sovereignty the southern portion of the province of Fengtien, which later became known as Liaotung Peninsula, and which was considered essential to Japan for protection of peace in the Orient. Just 8 days after the signing of the treaty, however, 607598 3

Russia, Germany and France, took a joint action advising Japan to renounce the possession of Liaotung Peninsula on the ground that the possession of this peninsula by Japan will be a menace to Peking and a perpetual obstacle to the peace of the Far East. 

It is hardly necessary to tell you of the deep feeling of indignation caused in Japan by this joint intervention of the three European powers. 

This national resentment was intensified when, in the years immediately following, we were compelled to witness the scene of these same European powers collecting their rewards, as it were, from China. In 1898, Germany secured 99 year lease of Kiao chao Bay, and France secured 99 year lease of Kuang chou wan. 

As for Russia, she had far more ambitious and extensive designs in the Far East. When the crafty Chinese statesman, Li Hung-chang, went to 

St. Petersburg in 1896 to attend the coronation of the Czar, Russia concluded with China a secret treaty of alliance directed against Japan. The text of this treaty was never officially revealed and it was only at the time of the Washington Conference in 1921, that the Chinese delegation submitted a telegraphic summary. Under this secret treaty, Russia and 

China undertook to support each other by all the land and sea forces in a war against Japan; and further Russia secured the consent of China to construct a railway across the Provinces of Amur and Kirin in the direction of Vladivostok. This treaty was to be in force for 15 years and was therefore in full effect during the Russo-Japanese War. Just 3 months later, on September 8, 1896, the contract for construction and operation of the Chinese Eastern Railway was signed with Russo-Chinese Bank. This contract provided for the establishment of the Chinese Eastern Railway Co. 

By the 6th article of this contract the company was given "absolute and exclusive right of administration" in the railway zone. In this way, Russia not only extended her Trans-Siberian Railway straight through the Chinese territory to Vladivostok but also opened the way for the establishment of her political power in Manchuria. 

However, Russia was not to be satisfied with Vladivostok alone for her outlet on the Pacific Ocean. For more than half of the year, Vladivostok is an ice-bound port and is necessarily closed to the sea. An ice-free port in a more southern portion of the Asiatic continent must be secured at any cost. In 1898, only 3 years after the treaty of Shimonoseki, Russia signed with China a convention to secure the lease of Liaotung Peninsula, the very territory which Russia herself joined with Germany and France to wrest from the hands of Japan. This convention not only gave Russia the lease of Liaotung but it also gave her the right to construct a branch line through Manchuria to Liaotung Peninsula, as well as the right to exercise civil and criminal jurisdiction and administrative power in the railway zone.

Russia lost no time in strengthening her position in Manchuria. She immediately started construction of what was considered an impregnable fortification and naval station at Port Arthur. At the same time the work of building the commercial port of Dalny and of laying the railway was pushed with unparalleled vigor. To intensify the situation still more, the 

Boxer Uprising in 1900 gave Russia the pretext to occupy the whole of Manchuria, on the ground of protecting the Chinese Eastern and South Manchurian Railways. Moreover, with the completion of the direct rail-

way communication between Moscow and Port Arthur, Russia extended her aggression beyond the borders of Manchuria into Korea. 

To Japan, these activities of Russia had but one meaning. With 

Manchuria and Korea in her hands, there was no question as to the next Russian objective. Since no cooperation could be secured from China, Japan had no alternative but to approach Russia direct in an attempt to settle the question at issue in Manchuria and Korea. After protracted diplomatic negotiations, however, Japan realized the futility of her efforts and took her only recourse in breaking off her diplomatic relations. 

By the Treaty of Portsmouth, the Russo-Japanese War was brought to an end. As a result of this war, Japan delivered Manchuria from the hands of Russia and restored the land to China, retaining simply the lease of Liaotung Peninsula and the railway zone which covers in all an area of only 1,400 square miles out of the vast region of nearly 400,000 square miles. Her chief aim was to keep Manchuria safe from encroachments of aggressive powers. For this right to ensure the security of our national life, Japan staked her existence in a titanic struggle and sacrificed 120,000 lives and 2 billions of yen from her scanty coffers. Our people cannot for a moment believe that any nation on earth can fail to recognize this right of self-preservation.

It is true that Czarist Russia no longer exists, but to Japan the passing of the despotic government of the Czar has brought upon its wake a cause for even greater anxiety. For just beyond Manchuria, there exists a state, upholding the principles utterly incompatible with our ideals of government, social order, and human relationship. That state is vigorously pushing forward its programs and projects, and the energy and effectiveness that have been displayed cannot fail to sound the alarm in the minds of the thoughtful peoples of the other nations. We must again emphasize that 

Japan has a most vital concern in the affairs of Manchuria. 

Such being the position of Japan, it will not be difficult to understand why Japan has no territorial designs upon Manchuria. Our government has repeatedly made declarations upholding the sovereignty of China and this policy has consistently been endorsed by our people at large. In our actual undertakings in Manchuria, the fundamental policy from the outset has been to advance economic development. In Japan, itself, we have realized that the welfare of our nation can be fostered only by developing healthy mind and body and by providing for the economic needs of our people. As our land is poor in natural resources, it is necessary to seek abroad for sources of food supply to feed our great population and this must be paid for by the fruits of our industries and commercial enterprises. 

In Manchuria, developed under cooperative efforts of the peoples of China and Japan and of other nations, we shall find not only a source of food supply and raw materials but also a profitable market for our manufactured goods. It is to the interest of Japan both for economic and strategic reasons that Manchuria remain peaceful and undisturbed in the hands of China and there should be no ground for any doubt or suspicion concerning the aims and motives of our country. 

In opposing the Russian advance into Manchuria, Japan upheld the territorial sovereignty of China. Before the war, Russia utilized every opportunity to extend her influence from economic to political. Her military forces not only occupied many of the strategic centers but they even went so far as to take control of administration of such important

places as the cities of Harbin and Mukden. In great contrast to these actions of Russia, Japan, on succeeding to the Russian rights, took measures to withdraw from the occupied areas and to turn over the administration to the Chinese authorities. 

By the treaty of Portsmouth, Russia transferred to Japan her lease of Liaotung Peninsula, the railway between Port Arthur and Changchun and its branches, and the coal mines along the railway as well as the various rights appertaining thereto. By the Treaty of Peking of 

December 22, 1905, China approved these transfers. Moreover, by an additional agreement China gave to Japan "the rights to maintain and improve the railway line between Antung and Mukden" in order to make it "fit for the transport of the commercial and industrial goods of all nations." To this treaty was also attached a Protocol wherein China engaged herself "for the purpose of protecting the interests of the South Manchuria Railway, not to construct any main line in the neighborhood of and parallel to that railway or any branch line that might be prejudicial to its interests."

From the outset, Japan was not unmindful of the responsibilities of developing Manchuria along economic lines. In order that she may not deviate from her declared policy and to avoid commission of errors in promoting this great task, a special commission on Manchurian Post-Bellum Enterprise was officially appointed on November 16th, 1905, to make a special study of the subject. The Commission was headed by the Prime 

Minister, Saionji, himself and included Baron Kodama and other heads of the departments concerned. Premier Saionji personally visited Manchuria and the Commission laid down the fundamental principles which were:- 

1-to maintain Chinese sovereignty and equal commercial opportunity; 2to encourage joint enterprise of Japanese and Chinese; and 3-to supersede 

Japanese military administration as quickly as possible. This policy was approved in Tokyo on May 22, 1906, at a council of Cabinet Ministers and Elder Statesmen before the Imperial Throne.

In order to maintain peace and order in the leased territory and along the railway zone, an Imperial Ordinance was promulgated on July 30, 1906, to establish the Government General of Kwangtung. The military administration in the leased territory was converted into a civil administration. To develop the economic interests, South Manchuria Railway Co., was created on June 7, 1906, and the newly acquired railway was transferred to the management of this joint stock corporation. It should be noted that the Japanese Government desired the cooperation and participation of the Chinese people. In the ordinance sanctioning the organization of the South Manchuria Railway Company, it was provided that the shares of the company may be owned only by the Japanese and Chinese Governments and by the subjects of Japan and China. At the time of the floatation of the company, the shares were offered to the Chinese people as well as to the Chinese Government, but they declined to avail themselves of the opportunity.

In the years that followed, Japan and China entered into several treaties and agreements concerning Manchuria. In every instance, it may be said that the object was for the furtherance of economic progress. Even

the much blamed Treaty of 1915 was in essence no departure from this rule. As a result of this treaty Japan secured the rights:- 

1. to extend the term of lease of Port Arthur and Dairen and the terms of South Manchuria Railway and Antung-Mukden Railway to 99 years; 

2. to permit Japanese subjects to lease lands and to enter, travel, and reside in South Manchuria for trade, manufacture and agriculture; 

3. to open more towns in Eastern Inner Mongolia to international trade; 

4. to recognize joint enterprise of Japanese and Chinese in agricultural and auxiliary industries in Eastern Inner Mongolia. 

Some of the other provisions which savored of monopolistic privileges were either adjusted in the agreement creating the Four Power Financial Consortium or were withdrawn through the statements made by the 

Japanese delegation at the Washington Conference.

So far as the necessity of extending the lease of Liaotung Peninsula is concerned, no one can fail to recognize its inevitableness. To return the territory to China at the expiration of the 25-year period in 1923 would have meant to return to the situation which existed before the Russo- Japanese War. Such a step would have become the cause of another war on the fields of Manchuria.

As to the contribution of Japan in the economic development of Manchuria, we cannot help but look upon Manchuria of today with a deep feeling of satisfaction. In the course of a quarter century what was no more than a war-worn wilderness has been turned into one of the richest districts of China. While recognizing many forces which made important contributions, I am sure, it would not be considered immodest to claim that the efforts of Japan were by far the greatest factor; and to the South 

Manchuria Railway Co., must be accorded the credit for the wonderful service rendered by that corporation. 

In recovering the barren wilderness for prosperity and civilization, the first task of South Manchuria Railway Company was to carry out its extensive program of reconstruction and improvement of the railway lines- 

450 miles between Port Arthur-Dairen and Changchun and 160 miles between Mukden and Antung and various branches. These lines are now operated with the latest equipments and afford services comparable with the most advanced railroads of the world. The company also developed the modern harbor of Dairen which was made a free port in 1907. The comparatively obscure harbor of 20 years ago has won its place as one of the most important ports, not only of China but also of the world. In the returns of the Chinese Maritime Customs, it stands second in rank, being exceeded only by the port of Shanghai. The company has built docks and warehouses, established steamship lines, opened coal mines, developed steel works and other industries including numerous public utilities and hotels. 

Furthermore, in addition to these enterprises of economic nature, the company has been engaged in the works of advancing public welfare, such as educational and hygienic institutions, model farms, experimental laboratories, etc. Perhaps the most important service of this nature is the building of model cities at Mukden, Changchun, Antung, and many other important centers.

In the conduct of its stupendous enterprises, South Manchuria Railway 

Co., has made investments amounting to over one billion yen.

If to this amount is added the investments of Japanese government and individuals, amounting roughly to seven hundred million yen, and also the value of public and private properties belonging to our nationals, the total 

Japanese investments in Manchuria will reach the figure of 2,147,000,000 yen. All this could not help but redound to the benefit of China and the 

Chinese. In fact, the Chinese people are the chief beneficiaries. Manchuria has become the most popular section of China. Year after year, millions of immigrants have migrated in steady flow from all parts of China. In no way can we describe the development of Manchuria more vividly than by citing the figures concerning the increase of population and trade. In the course of the last 20 years, the population has increased by over ten million and the trade which was 52 million Haikwan taels in 1907 reached the sum of 755 million in 1929.

We have, thus far, considered why and how Japan came into Manchuria; also examined her policy and accomplishments. We should now give our thoughts to the present situation. ment.

Since the adoption by China of the national policy to secure abolition of unequal treaties, persistent and systematic efforts have been made by the Chinese Government to nullify the rights and concessions which have been extended to foreign nations, particularly to Japan. No one can understand more fully than we do, the sentiments of the Chinese people regarding the unequal treaties. We are sincerely in sympathy with the aspirations of 

China and are eager to extend every assistance in our power to hasten the day when their hopes may be realized. We cannot help but feel, however, that the aims of China can never be attained by resorting to arbitrary acts of nullification or by simple declarations on the part of the Chinese govern- This is a practical question and must be approached in a practical manner. The issues involved are not theoretical but deal with actual contracts and relationships. The experience of our own country in securing the abolition of extraterritoriality should furnish valuable suggestions to China in formulating her course of action. We believe the quickest way for China to attain her goal is to take the actual conditions in hand and to remove the cause by establishing peace and order, by perfecting laws and providing for their judicial enforcement, by affording adequate protection for life and property, by ensuring security for legitimate rights and interests, and by encouraging friendly relations with peoples of all nations. As soon as China sets her course in the right direction by taking practical measures and in a spirit of sincerity, I am sure all the nations concerned will not be found parsimonious in extending to her every cooperation. 

With good reason, the leaders of China are seeking to inculcate the spirit of nationalism in the minds of the masses. But it is a cause of serious alarm to all the civilized nations that, to attain this purpose, the leaders of the Nationalist party are engaged in systematic campaigns to arouse popular feeling of antagonism and bitterness against all foreign nations. 

Anti-foreign sentiments are being fanned and nurtured through the mediums of text books, posters, public declarations, and popular demonstrations. In this age of close contacts and interdependence among the peoples of the world, inculcation of hatred on the part of any nation. cannot but place serious obstacles in the way of peace and international cooperation.

Unfortunately, Japan has lately been singled out as the mark of antagonism on account of her position in Manchuria. The Chinese 

Nationalist Government has adopted determined policy of depriving Japan of all her interests and properties in Manchuria by ignoring treaties and international agreements. Attempts are made even to deny the right of 

Japan to hold the lease of Liaotung Peninsula and of the South Manchuria Railway zone. To bring about a complete ruin of South Manchuria 

Railway, the Chinese Government has not only built competitive lines in utter disregard of treaty obligations, but has also been seeking to operate the various railways, which were built for them with Japanese money and for which they have paid neither principal nor interest, in destructive competition against South Manchuria Railway. A hundred and one obstacles are placed in the way of the Japanese and Koreans engaged in rightful pursuits of agriculture, commerce, and other economic enterprises. Moreover, a boycott of most vigorous nature, enforced by severest measures, has been organized and directed against all trades of Japan. Every means conceivable has been utilized to imprint upon the minds of the masses a picture of Japan as the arch-enemy of China. The result has been the 

Chinese assaults upon Japanese civilians and Korean farmers, the attacks upon the railway lines, the murder of Japanese officers, and other incidents of similar nature.

The recent outbreak in Manchuria is a natural outcome of this campaign of antagonism against Japan. Strangely enough, Japan has been made to appear to the outside world as committing high handed actions of aggression. Before an audience far away from the barbarous conditions of disorder, it is easy to appeal to emotions by ignoring facts, or to create interests and even to arouse humor, by making cynical references to the actions of Japan as those of a selfish bully trampling upon a weaker neighbor. But the situation is too serious to be handled in such light hearted manner. The persistent efforts of the Chinese Government to nullify rights and concessions have resulted in innumerable violations of treaties and the bitter campaign of antagonism has caused untold cruelties, outrages, and attacks upon lives and property of rightful residents and legitimate enterprises. The actions taken by Japan are no more than the measures of self-defense. Confronted with a like situation I am sure that the United States would resort to similar measures and that you would not hesitate to give full support to your government. The object of the Japanese operations has been to bring order out of chaos and there is no intention whatever of waging a war against China. I have no doubt that the Japanese Government will be found faithful in its efforts to maintain the principles of the Nine Power Pact and of the Kellogg Treaty. 

I shall not now take the time to dwell upon the specific incidents in Manchuria. I would like only to say that the conditions in China are so different that it would be utterly impossible to form judgments according to the standards existing in other countries. Just outside the railway zone in 

Manchuria, there still exist conditions not altogether unlike those that prevailed in Europe during the feudal period when the barons and bandits harassed the peoples of peaceful communities. The Chinese authorities have been helpless to eradicate the bandits of Manchuria. 

Mounted banditry in Manchuria has been notorious since time immemorial. During the Russo-Japanese War, these bandits served useful purposes of both the belligerents. Chang-Tso-lin the former war-lord

of Manchuria, came to power because he was the biggest chief of the bandits. It is difficult to draw the line between the bandits and the regular army. The bandits are enlisted, as occasion may demand, in the regular 

Chinese army, and if the army fail to pay wages or disband the soldiers, officers and men join the original bandit-soldiers to become regular bandit groups. The number of these men before the outbreak last September were estimated at about 50,000—over 17,000 in Mukden Province, and over 10,000 each in Kirin, Amur and Jehol Provinces. Some of the bands have been armed with machine guns. Near the railway zone the raids are carried on in smaller groups but in the interior, bandits in large groups are festering the land. It is reported that within the Japanese railway zone alone there were 368 bandit raids in 1929. 

Moreover, even the actions of the Chinese war-lords and regular armies cannot be judged by modern standards. Although the civil wars of China have been kept out of its borders, Manchuria has suffered heavily because of the drains made to pay the expense of extensive military operations. Not satisfied with the spacious domain, the war-lords of 

Manchuria have become ambitious to participate in the military contests. of the China proper. For this purpose, an immense army of over 300,000 men has been maintained. This force has been used by both Chang-Tso-lin and Chang Hsue Liang to gain power at Peking and in the Central Government. This immense army and the military campaigns have been financed by the funds raised partly by various imposts but principally by issuance of paper money by provincial banks. In exchange for these paper notes, the inhabitants have been compelled to sell their valuable crops which turn, sold for silver or gold to pay for the exploits of the war-lords. were, in

As to the Chinese army:--at the time of the recent outbreak, the 

Chinese regulars in Manchuria were estimated at 220,000 strong; against this immense force there were only 10,400 Japanese scattered over the narrow strip of nearly 700 miles as railway guards. This colossal Chinese army has failed to make effective resistance against the small number of well organized Japanese soldiers. It would not be difficult for us to realize, however, that such ill-disciplined army has unlimited capacities for creating disorders. Against them, peaceful communities and inhabitants have little protection. In such a situation, a proper force to maintain order is essential for the safety of innocent and helpless inhabitants, whether they be Chinese, Koreans or Japanese. Under the existing conditions it would be a malicious crime against humanity to withdraw the Japanese forces to the railway zone until due provisions are made for protection of innocent lives and property.

This brief review of the subject leads us to the conclusion that the nature of the Manchurian question makes it impossible to hope for an immediate solution. As anxious as we are to arrive at an early settlement, I believe our goal of satisfactory solution can be reached only by advancing step by step. So long as the Manchurian question is made a tool of the 

Nationalist party in China and bitter feeling against Japan is allowed to subsist, it is useless to hope for a true solution. Our first step must be to create in the minds of the Chinese leaders an honest desire and disposition to find practical solution; and secondly, by dissipating all suspicions, it must be brought home to the Chinese mind that Japan seeks for no territory nor new privileges in China and that she wishes only a candid recognition and enforcement of her existing rights; and finally, the question must be 10

considered on both sides with practical mind animated by the spirit of cooperation and conciliation. 

The Japanese government and the Japanese people are prepared to take up the whole question with China in the most friendly spirit. It is our earnest hope that for the sake of Japan and for the sake of China and for the sake of their own peoples, the enlightened nations of the earth will study this question in a dispassionate manner and by the compelling force of intelligent public opinion of the world, they will aid in hastening the settlement, not only in the interests of Japan and China but also for the benefit of all mankind. 11
