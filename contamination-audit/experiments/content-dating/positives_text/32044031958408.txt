Aid to Dependent Children in Wyoming 

Of recent years the people of Wyoming have become increasingly concerned about the costs of public welfare programs. When Wyoming's public welfare effort is considered as a whole, it is found that the Aid to Dependent Children program is claiming an increasing percentage of the total welfare costs. 

As stated in the 1950 Biennial Report of the State Department of Public Welfare, "The Aid to Dependent Children program proposes to give a decent standard of living for those children who have been deprived of parental support because of death of a parent, continued absence of a parent from the home, or physical or mental incapacity of a parent. 11 The part of this program that has received the most criticism from the public in the past is the support of children whose parents are able but unwilling to furnish such support. Some of the public may believe that the granting of assistance in such cases has often encouraged a father to desert his family but this would be difficult to prove or refute.

Wyoming Legislatures have passed a number of laws which deal with enforcement of support of children. Feeling in the state is divided as to whether present statutes are adequate, whether they could be better used, or whether they need revision. This report will briefly point out the ADC problem, the present statutes and some viewpoints on possible changes in those statutes. 783272

The share of the total ADC costs that the federal government has been contributing has been increasing over the last four bienniums while the state and county shares of the cost have been decreasing.

These facts reflect an increasing dependence upon the federal government in the ADC program and an increasingly powerful hold on that program by the federal government.

The decline in the average monthly number of ADC cases from 1942 to 1945 may have been due to any or a combination of the following four reasons: 

1. Increased war effort with accompanying high wages. 

2. Women whose husbands were in the armed services even though divorced or estranged, could demand and receive a serviceman's dependency allotment. 

3. Some partially incapacitated fathers could obtain employment during the wartime labor shortage. 

4. Some of the older children were able to secure employment to help support the family. 

The present apparent decline in the average monthly number of ADC cases is perhaps due to the same reasons as above except for number two which no longer applies.

It should be pointed out that the ADC case load cannot be considered a static one. For example, the turnover in the last reporting biennium was 503 cases added and 578 cases closed. 

The 222% increase in the average monthly grant per ADC case since 1939 is partly due to a 88.7% increase in the national consumers price index during the same period. The amount of federal participation in the ADC program has been increased several times since 1939 and the state has increased the grants to be able to take advantage of the federal money offered. The average number of children per ADC case has increased from 2.5 in 1939 to 2.7 in 1952 or about 8%.

When the ADC programs of other states are viewed, Wyoming compares very favorably in the number of persons under 18 years of age being aided. In both 1950 and 1952 only two other states had a lower percentage of persons of this age group being aided. Wyoming's number per 1,000 under 18 years of age being aided in 1952 was 14 compared with the national average of 32. While Wyoming's problem may not be as great as that of many of the other states there is need for intelligent and effective work in Wyoming's ADC program in no less degree so that Wyoming may remain in that comparatively favorable position.

Therefore, any civil enforcement procedure to make parents fulfill their support obligations to their children would logically be directed toward this 45% of the ADC cases which are the result of desertion, separation, and divorce. 

This 45% of the ADC cases represents over $600,000 in a biennium and roughly $300,000 for a calendar year. It is not suggested, of course, that these amounts would be saved in public monies simply by a change of law or policy. One cannot assume that all abandoning parents have the means or ability to carry the financial responsibilities attendant to the care of their children. 

Also, the costs of many of the necessary civil actions would be an important factor. 

Costs and problems in dealing with out-of-state parents can be great and cumbersome. (In 1951 the Legislature passed the Uniform 

Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act, its purpose being to improve and extend by reciprocal legislation with other states the duties of support and to make uniform the law with respect thereto.) 

In a survey made in 1950 by the State Welfare Department it was found that in about 70% of the ADC cases due to desertion, separation or divorce the parent who should be expected to contribute to his children's support was out of the state or his whereabouts was unknown. 

It is quite probable that a similar percentage would be in that category today. From this can be understood the complexness of the problem confronting persons interested in action against parents who do not contribute to the support of their children. Freedom of movement of parents can make it extremely difficult to keep tract of them. Also, if a deserting parent works in certain occupations his income cannot be attached by a civil action without great difficulty.

The Statutes 

Section 9-801 provides that any parent deserting or failing to support minor children under 16 years of age shall upon conviction be deemed guilty of a felony. Punishment is one to three years in the penitentiary or three months to one year in the county jail. If the parent is committed to the penitentiary the state commission on prison labor may give parole and divert earnings of such parent to the use and support of the minor children. Such imprisonment, it has been determined by the courts, must be a course of last resort. 

In lieu of sentence to imprisonment the court may make an order directing the parent to pay a certain sum periodically to the wife, guardian, or custodian or to an organization or individual approved by the court as trustee. This procedure takes the form of a penal bond whereby if the parent fails to make proper payment he must show cause why sentence should not be imposed. (9-802 and 806) It is understood that this provision is seldom used.

Section 58-215 states in part that if any person shall abandon any child, so that such child shall become a charge upon the county, that person shall be liable to a civil action by attachment without undertaking, by the board of county commissioners, for all expenses that may be or have been incurred by such county in consequence of such abandonment. This course, it seems, is used comparatively little. As in any similar actions, locating the parent is not always possible.

Sections 58-213 and 214 of the statutes provide for the procedures for adoption of abandoned children. and children.

A so-called Wife and Child Support Bill passed the 1951 Leg islative Session as an amendment to Section 3-5919. This bill amended existing law to include cases where the husband is not reasonably and adequately contributing to the support of his wife The then existing law made no provision for a wife's recourse in cases where the husband is contributing something to the support of his wife and children but not sufficient for them to live on.

Under the amended law fathers who make only a token effort to support their families but have the means to bear more of the whole cost of such support may be forced to do so by court order "together with any remedy applicable in a court of equity." 

A law (19-202) passed in 1949 enables the courts to order parents of children committed to the State Children's Home to pay toward the care and support of such children. 

Some Viewpoints 

Whether the statutes just reviewed are adequate to cover the desertion or abandonment situations in aid to dependent children or are all necessary can no doubt be argued with good cause. Regarding changes in these laws, at this time there are five apparent and known viewpoints to be considered with regard to the present situation. They are: 

1. That county attorneys maintain any civil action brought against proper persons where a family is getting ADC by reason of desertion, separation, or divorce. Results might be possible with more extensive use of Section 58-215 but this course many times is not used because the parent is outside the jurisdiction of the court. 

This fact alone would make the procedure slow and complicated.

11. 

Some persons have pointed to the fact that county attorneys in Wyoming are not elected as full time officials and have said that the position should be a full time job with an accompanying adequate salary. But it has been pointed out that even if the county attorney was full time he would have little time for anything but ADC cases if he was to do a good job because of the large number of such cases that could be acted upon. 

2. That the county welfare board be authorized to employ private counsel where desertion, separation or divorce is involved in ADC cases. This would require amendments and could provide attorney fees similar to the way in which attorney fees are regulated in workmen's compensation cases. Under this procedure it is possible that politics could enter in with only certain attorneys being retained. parent, child or guardian involved hire private counsel and pay for same from the funds obtained in the action similar to the way funds are obtained by individuals in workman's compensation cases to pay doctors. Of course, this is possible at present but in many cases is not done because (1) the individual is not aware that it can be done, (2) the individual hopes that the erring parent will reconsider and feels nothing should be done to antagonize him, (3) a very great amount of litigation may be required if the parent is out of the state and the cost would be prohibitive to the recipient, and (4) a greater degree of security may be possible with public assistance than with the uncertain forced payment from an unwilling parent who may move his location thus requiring more action and new proceedings.

While this proposal may have merit, it would seem that such a person would only be able to spread his activities very thinly over the state and it would be extremely difficult for him to get all the information needed for all the potential criminal and civil suits that might demand his attention. 

4. That the Uniform Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act be amended to make it more workable. It is the opinion of some that the five dollar court docket fee should not be required of persons initiating action in support cases. The point has also been made that the 

Reciprocal Act should provide for payment of the sheriff's fees in cases where his services are required and should provide for or designate a definite person whose duty it should be to enforce the law. 

5. That present laws are adequate. Whether the laws really are adequate at present rests with the value attached to the above four viewpoints or other views to be advanced. 

A sixth viewpoint has been that any attempt along the lines of any of the first four viewpoints above would tend to be socialistic. 

This view cannot be considered with any seriousness since it is the "welfare state" and its attendant costs and burdens that would be avoided if all would shoulder their rightful social responsibilities.
