FOREWORD 

In the founding of each colony and state in our commonwealth, there lies much raw material for romance. The religious or moral zealot; the ubiquitous adventurer; the fugitive from justice or injustice; the sturdy builder of his own and his children's destiny; each had a part. But behind all, and dominating all, was the urge for material gain that hazarded all in the hope of economic betterment and security. No colony was settled, no territory organized without this motive of direct economic advantage to individual, class or group.

But the climax in territorial organization, the direct intermingling of the sublime and the ridiculous, broad farce and stark tragedy was reached when in 1854, the passage of the Kansas-Nebraska Bill repealed the Missouri Compromise and left the question of slave or free labor open for settlement by the voters of the territory itself. 

As the Southern slave states unquestionably controlled the political moves and dictated the policies of the National Government, the dice were loaded, and the contest seemed unequal, but the vastly different methods employed to seize the advantage ultimately determined the issue.

In the preparation of this work, the authors gratefully acknowledged the assistance of the Kansas Historical Society in supplying authentic data.

His bright blue eyes shifted from the tender green of the leafing trees in the tiny front yard, to the figure of a young man laden with heavy carpet bags, wearily toiling up the steps.

It augurs well for human progress, that youth, impetuous and intolerant though it may be, often, under stress of new and untried surroundings, leaves the path of prejudice diligently trodden by its progenitors, and blazes new trails for itself. That these trails are sown thick with new errors, new pitfalls, does not lessen the ultimate good wrought by the breaking of old shackles. 

So our young adventurers, blithely unaware of having in large measure liberated themselves from sundry preconceived sectional inhibitions, were conscious only of sympathetic companionship in a joint adventure; of common interests, hopes, anxieties, and possible dangers in the new life awaiting them beyond the Missouri's muddy trail, The wreck of the Commerce had, in one swift, unforseen stroke demolished barriers and dissipated prejudices arising from sectional discord and obsessions, for if, in the year of Grace, 1855, it was a far cry from the rugged hills, the fair green valleys, the busy striving, prosperous cities and towns of New England, to the wide sweeps, the vast forests and deep, broad rivers of the Gulf Slope, their youth had overcome their, sectional antagonisms by forgetting their existence.

The call of youth to youth, clean and unspoiled, knows no North or South, no opposing economic 52

interests; it simply knows its own, and meets it and greets it clear-eyed and unafraid.

CHAPTER III 

Rumors had been rife of stirring events over the border; how gatherings, recruitings, armings, provisionings, were being carried on in eight of Missouri's western counties. Inflammatory speeches aided in recruiting members for their semi-secret associations, the members of which were distinguished by a knot of ribbon, white or blue, in a buttonhole, the effect being the same as the wearing of the tricolor in France in the ever memorable days of '93. "Strange," commented Elias that night, "Strange how a bit of ribbon no larger than a rose can give birth to such big results. And the thing that floors me is that the real instigators, the men and women who do the head work and find the money to carry out the darned schemes, are themselves mighty decent people, just as convinced of the justice of their cause as we are of our own.. And, Lord, they, and not ourselves, have the backing and support of the 

United States Government."

A few days before the election, a long line of prospective voters wended their way across the Missouri River border line, and branched out to their several destinations, as per schedule carefully planned. Like careful generals, the leaders surveyed the field of action and estimated with approximate accuracy the number needed to achieve their objective, i.e. absolute control of polling booths to assure election of their candidates.

that if one invention could make slavery profitable, another might make it unprofitable, so I worked on a cotton picking machine that I hoped would not only do perfect work in picking cotton, but could be manufactured so cheaply that no cotton grower could afford to go to the expense of raising slaves from infancy, when the work could be done with a fraction of such expense. So, unless some other industry would absorb the surplus slave labor, the planters themselves would turn their slaves adrift to shift for themselves. Where I failed to perfect the machine, someone else with greater technical skill, will succeed, and slavery will automatically cease to be. For, look here, and keep it forever in mind, no men hold to any line of conduct, even if they contend it is their right to do so, after it ceases to be profitable and becomes a factor in their impoverishment. And now, Karl, a little of the antidote." 

Karl brought out his fiddle, and the puzzles that man has propounded for his own bewilderment gave place to melody pure and sweet.

When the three were comfortably esconced in their double-decked beds, Elias called down to the boys, "If I were running a hospital, which thank 

God, I'm not, I would keep a violinist on tap for the real cure of my patients-and a canary or twoand a few thrushes-and a bob-o-link. See?"

The matter of securing land warrants had been increasingly important to the two younger members of the Withers household, even before their visit to Asa Barlow, and since then, having conceived an idea of an earthly Paradise springing up under skilful handling, their determination to have and hold

their quota of Kansas black soil, and plant thereon a vine and fig tree of their own, had become imperative. So, when, at the breakfast table a fortnight later, Nat proposed that they consult Charles Pendleton as to location, and hurry the matter through, so some little progress could be made during the present season, it was all quite in order, and what any fair-minded person would naturally encourage. 

Karl, at any rate, eagerly seized the idea as appealing, so he said, to one's business sense, and when 

Elias was consulted, he acquiesced, saying rather dryly, he thought "no more plausible excuse for going to Lecompton could have been devised-if any excuse were needed."

Sunday morning found the would-be farmers on their way, armed with a rough map of the territory on which locations might be indicated. 

Their welcome was especially cordial, Mr. Pendleton explaining that the day was his birthday and he was celebrating the questionable happiness of being for half a century a dweller in this vale of tears.

It struck both his guests quite forcibly that Mr. 

Pendleton was a prey to private worries, and welcomed the diversion of their coming. 

Clio and Babs openly rejoiced that timely help had arrived to save Henry the Eighth and his wives from devouring certain nondescript foods on their cupboard shelves. The boys declared themselves ready and willing to take risks on everything, barring sorrel pie, Karl declaring that "sorrel taken in the form of pie was too stimulating for adults, though sorrel au natural devoured by the infants

of the species in no wise interfered with growth or capacity for deviltry.'

Nat drew forth the map and requested information as to desirable claims. All gathered round the table where Mr. Pendleton spread the map, beside one of his own on which were indicated claims already staked and also locations which he considered most desirable, and which he was hoping Buford's men would take when they arrived next season. "Though," he added regretfully, "my more recent correspondence with Buford rather makes me doubt many claims will ever be filed upon, for political matters in Kansas seem to have engrossed Buford's interest to the exclusion of settlement. This differs from his original idea which was bona-fide development of lands, and so acquiring legal citizenship. 

However, I can cite you any number of splendid locations. They're everywhere." "We'd like to get within easy distance from Lawrence, if we can," said Nat. "It is generally supposed that all the best claims within a radius of ten miles from Lawrence have already been captured, but, as is always the case, a few of the best have been overlooked. The Southerners have preferred Lecompton or Leavenworth, or other promising centers, partly to outdistance

Lawrence in the race for trade center, so here on my map, I have noted a number of locations, fine ones, and you can have your choice, as yet. Others besides myself will undoubtedly discover these places, so you'll do well to waste no time in staking your claims. Tell you what," he added, "I'll go with you on your way home, and show you the places

I think would suit you best, and you can put up your notices at once.' 99 They all grew quite excited about it, and fell to planning, and quoted Asa Barlow's methods as the best to copy.

Mr. Pendleton was especially interested, and suggested, as a result of his experience and observation, that the ideal Kansas farm bordered a stream and extended back to give the bulk of the acreage on the open prairie. "You'll then have the virgin bottom lands for your wood supply, both for building purposes and for fuel, and the river for water supply for domestic and stock use. The prairie can be broken up piecemeal for the crops, as you find time to attend to it, but lay out your home grounds at once, on some high location not subject to flood from the river or inundations from the depressions after heavy rains. 

Then, lads, go ahead and work out your problems as they come up."

They commenced to argue about the arrangement of the house lot, and Babs produced smooth, brown wrapping paper, which she spread on the table, and Mr. Pendleton, being the fortunate possessor of a carpenter's rule, a tentative chart of the house lot for the castle in Spain was drawn, each essential point being threshed out before committed to paper. "Our new friend, Mr. Barlow," said Nat, "says that five acres at the least should be allotted for the house and its appurtenances; that would include the dwelling house, graneries, poultry yards, cattle and horse barns, vegetable and small fruits, gardens, beside lawns, flower beds. In fact, all things

outside of crop lands, woodlands, pasture lands and orchards. He's working his place out along that line, and say, you should know what he's done in a year's time.'

So, on paper was finally sketched a dream farm. 

Nor did they stop there. As young men in their early twenties do not hesitate to complete the picture, Nat announced, "Of course, at first, we'll build a pole and thatch home, or hay-tent, but later, we'll build homes all of black walnut like Dr. Robinson's —it's as beautiful as anything this side of Jordon." "In mine," quoth Karl, "there'll be a music room covering the whole upper story." "In mine," said Nat, "there'll be a conservatory for rare and beautiful flowers."

The girls cried out in unison. "What about the kitchen and pantry, and an elegant parlor, and library and bedrooms facing the East, and dozens and dozens of clothes closets?" "All those things, and more," said Nat grandly, "are just a matter of course; the pantry shall be a thing of beauty with shelves and shelves and shelves for pies.' "Do you mean sorrel pies, Nat?" Karl inquired, which brought a general gale of laughter and a sudden flame of color to Barbara's cheeks.

It might be noted parenthetically, that young men and maidens are prone to complete such pictures in the secrecy of their own minds, and see these pleasant homes peopled with those to whom their dreams incline, and all I say to that is, that it is just as it should be, and God bless them.

Of course, they all had to visit the chicken yard

and view the family of Henry the Eighth, which had now grown to many times the original number, for Catherine of Aragon and Anne Boelyn and one fish wife, who had edged herself into the royal family, displayed flocks of cheeping fluffiness that everyone was proud of. In the family of the fish wife, Nat easily recognized Og, Gog and Magog.

In the stable, Clio pointed out a horse her Pa had given her on his last visit; a fine mare named 

Butterfly, gentle, beautiful and fleet. The girls had taken turns in riding her, and were loud in her praise. "Where did your Pa get her?" asked Karl. "From Missouri." "They surely raise some beauties there; they get some of the Kentucky breeds."

Dinner was served early so that the first step in the filing of claims could be taken before sunset. The pride and glory of that dinner was the birthday cake, which not only looked lovely in its white coat, but tasted delicious. When Nat hinted Elias hadn't tasted cake for a century or more, a large piece was carefully wrapped and placed in his capacious pocket "To be given intact to Mr. Withers, with no nibbling at edges."

There Mr. Pendleton led the way to the locations he had described as choice and still unclaimed. were four of these claims, two of which adjoined, and two at some distance from each other. They proved to be all and more than had been claimed for them, and were less than five miles from Lawrence, bordering the river and extending back on high ground over fine stretches of rolling prairie

land. The castles in Spain would have the most beautiful material base on which to rest. "We'll take the ones adjoining if Karl is agreeable," said Nat, "and build our palaces close to the line. I want to sit on my vine-covered veranda and listen to the fiddle evenings." "And I," said Karl, "will not need to go to the trouble of planting flowers, I'll pick my posies on 

Nat's side of the line."

Notices of claims filed were thereupon staked out and the first momentous step taken.

Only two things of special interest had claimed their attention on their route. The first was a claim shack about 12 x 14 feet, built of sod, with thatched roof; its only opening the front door space. Alighting for further inspection, it was discovered that the house, otherwise totally bare, contained a window sash complete with glass, propped against the outside wall. Mr. Pendleton laughed and explained. "The law requires that the claimant shall build a 'house with a window in it.' When this claimant secures his final papers, and is a bona-fide owner of this piece of ground, this window sash will be transferred to a friend's place, to comply with the law, and so on, one sash with glass doing duty for an indefinite number of claims."

The other incident was the finding of a toy house secured in place by heavy stakes driven into the ground at its four corners. Again Mr. Pendleton solved the riddle. "This claimant can testify that he has built a house on his claim 12 x 14 (being careful not to specify whether feet or inches) with a window in it. You see that window about two

inches square in the side? Well, that doll's house will be carried from one claim to another as need or chance arises; can be slung over one's shoulder and carried on horseback. Pretty nervy, eh?"

The long day was drawing to a close, as with thanks of the boys sounding genially in his ears. 

Charles Pendleton rode thoughtfully home. "I can't understand it," he mused, "it must be just the way they were raised, but how two such good, clean fair-minded young men can deny the right of a white man to own and sell slaves when such is the law of the land, is a mystery. It's just the blasted prejudice the North has bred." 

And Nat Winslow and Karl Wendel on their way home, discoursed along the same line. "How so good a man, who wouldn't hurt a fly and would break his neck to do a kindness, can believe it right (as he does) to breed and own and sell human beings, beats me." Thus spoke New England. To which Karl rejoined, "Let me tell you what Carl 

Schurtz and Franz Siegel said one night when a group were talking with Father at our place. Carl said that negro slavery was only one phase of all slavery; that the color of slaves was a mere detail, and the manner of slavery varied to be consistent with the time and locality. You see those German refugees as they all were, had studied and thought of the matter from more angles than you Northerners are apt to do. They are against all forms of slavery, so they'll side with the North if worst comes to worst." 

Elias was delighted with the day's accomplishment, and, boasting that he could and would cut

more poles in a day than the two of them could plant for their cabins, urged them to put up houses at once as a precaution against the playful habit of "jumping," or of the land being assigned by the "bogus" territorial government then in force, in payment for illegal votes, as was often done. "No summer ever came back, and no two summers were ever alike."

In later years, the three at the Withers cabin looked back to that summer and autumn as a truly happy time. In spite of the disturbed state of affairs, of highway robberies and burnings of settlers' homes, and repeated assaults, all committed with official acquiescence if not with avowed official connivance, our fortunates pursued the even tenor of their way. Their work at the office, at home, at their new claims, furnished occupation for all their waking hours, and added a never ceasing interest to their first summer in a virgin world.

This routine was broken by occasional visits to 

Mr. Pendleton's home, and chats with Asa Barlow.

As the road to their claims led past that of Asa's, the boys formed the habit of stopping to report progress, or exchange comments on vital happenings in their world of Kansas, the laws passed by the bogus legislature, raids and rumors of raids, on the border. "If I'm let alone," Asa said, "I'll be quiet as a lamb, but if all my work is brought to naught and destroyed, By George, I'll get satisfaction in kind,

in any way that offers. I'm not the man to turn. the other cheek."

It was agreed between Karl and Nat that Asa had been embittered by an unhappy love affair, and had a reckless streak in his disposition that would carry him to excess, but that he was brave, energetic, of quick intelligence, was always a kindly and loyal friend, and personally very likable.

And the summer wore to a close, and the wild sunflowers starred the Kansas prairies with their gorgeous bloom. 

And letters home, dispatched from time to time from faraway Kansas glimpsed lightly and casually the train of events contributing to the general unrest throughout the territory, assuring anxious relatives and friends of the safety of the writers. 

From Nathaniel Winslow to his parents: 

Kansas Territory, Sept. 4, 1855. 

Dear Father and Mother;- 

Until your last letter came to hand, I had not realized that so much time had elapsed since last writing. Will earnestly try to be more prompt, so you will have no occasion for anxiety on my behalf. 

You much desire to hear of the political events since my last letter, as the papers contain so little.

First, would say that the supplemental elections went off quietly, the Governor keeping his promise that the free-staters should have no

cause to complain of unfairness or illegality. The result was the election of eleven free-state delegates to the territorial legislature held at Pawnee 

July 1st. 

One of the delegates was Martin F. Conway, a fine young man, whom I am proud to call my friend. It was his personal opinion that the eleven delegates would never be permitted by the proslavery element to take their seats, but would be supplanted by the very ones who were chosen by the fraudulent votes on March 30th. He seemed to think it might be best if he resigned, and not to have any dealings with that bunch of rascals. And he was right in his judgment.

On July 1st, the first territorial legislature of 

Kansas met at Pawnee as planned; they only remained in session four days, just long enough to oust all the free-state delegates except Conway, who had resigned, and replace them with their former candidates. No, I am wrong there; one other free-stater decided to hold on, but this lone free-stater was later unseated, leaving a complete and harmonious proslavery membership. Some of the legislators are even citizens of Missouri and vote in their own state, as well as hold office and vote in Kansas. 

Pawnee had no accommodations, and many of the legislators cooked their own meals and slept in the open, but that was not the reason for removing to Shawnee Mission, but because of a cholera that seems to prevail from drinking impure water. But as Shawnee Mission is a Gov-

ernment Indian reservation, it is really Federal territory, and outside Kansas jurisdiction.

Governor Reeder refused certificates to the new usurping members and is himself dismissed as governor, by the President for this action, and 

Wilson Shannon is the incoming Governor of 

Kansas. God help Kansas when that man arrives!

Not to draw it out too long, the territorial legislature (termed "bogus" by us unregenerates) did the work of framing a constitution. I submit a few of the laws they made for you to reflect upon. "Anyone declining to take an oath supporting the Fugitive Slave Law will be disfranchised." "Death for aiding the escape of a slave." "Two years imprisonment for kidnapping and selling a FREE negro." "Questioning slaveholding, to be a felony." "The Governor of Kansas can pardon offenses against U. S. laws, but NOT against the 

Kansas laws passed by this territorial legislature." "No liberty of press-speech or assemblage.' 

Now, after enacting the above, the delegates were weary, and time was short, so they adopted the Missouri constitution, practically in toto, only stipulating that where the name "Missouri" was mentioned, the word "Kansas" should be substituted.

No antislavery person can sit on a jury where slavery questions are concerned. 

Would say that Gov. Reeder, since his dismis-

sal by the President has worked with the freestaters in the effort to have all administrative procedures fair and open. Before his dismissal, he was offered a Government mission to China but he refused to be gotten rid of that way. 

The Withers cabin family is kept pretty busy. 

The garden seeds you sent never reached us; were probably confiscated, but next year we'll try to raise both flowers and vegetables. All well at this writing. 

Your obedient son, Nathaniel Winslow. 

P. S. Mother, would you be so good as to send me a receipt for making mince pies? You make the best on earth. Karl does most of the cooking at our house, but has never made mince pies. 

Thank you, Mother, Nat. 

From Barbara Brooks to her Aunt Susan: 

Dear Auntie;- 

Kansas Territory, Oct. 22, 1855.

I'm wishing you could be here with me to see the prairie fires. It's the most gorgeous sight you ever could imagine. You see the wild grass here grows as high as a man on horseback would be, and when the fires come in the dry grass in the Autumn, the nights are, or seem to me brighter than the days. It's true, Auntie, the daylight seems a dull light beside the light of burning grass.

Clio and I like it here so much, Auntie, and we don't mind working at all. We thought we would, but we don't. Clio's Pa said we had low tastes if we didn't loathe disgusting kitchen drudgery, but it's real exciting. You see, when we put a dish in the oven to bake, we never know just what it's going to turn out to be, and we are most times surprised, but now, we can sort of reckon on how it's going to taste. 

Clio's Pa was here yesterday. He's going to

Topeka soon to be at the state convention which begins today. He didn't have to go, as he wasn't elected to anything, but he will sort of hover around in case, he says. He will protect himself he says and has taken 2 revolvers and a bowie knife.

It's a convention this time to write out a state constitution. Such a many conventions as they have out here, Aunt Susan, you wouldn't believe. 

I don't know what for, Auntie. 

Did you know that the word Topeka is Indian for potato? Clio's Pa says Topeka is nothing but a tacky village and the convention people will have to sleep in berths like they do in boats, or roll themselves in blankets out of doors. 

Yesterday, quite a dreadful thing happened.

A young man named Charles Dow was murdered -shot in the breast with a double-barrelled shot gun. He had been to a blacksmith shop and when he was walking home, where he lived with a Mr. 

Branson, he was followed by three men. He heard a gun go "click" and whirled around and was shot dead. He was left on the prairie but

the murderers sent word to Mr. Branson who came and brought him home.

Clio's Pa says it's a lesson to the other freestate men; maybe they will know enough to keep clear of Kansas. We wouldn't have known about this if Clio's Pa hadn't come, for Uncle Charlie don't talk about such things to Clio and me. 

Mr. Branson is a free-state man, so it is thought he might have trouble or be served the same way. I thought it wasn't right to kill the young man till he had done something. Uncle 

Charlie thought so too. Clio's Pa said while it served the damned Yankee right, it wasn't the best policy, it was always easy to find a plausible excuse if one went about it right. 

That word, Aunt Susan, just in front of Yankee, was the very one Clio's Pa used, so I had to write it.

Uncle Charlie says trouble will come of the murder, and he feels real worried, though he tries not to look so.

I have a receipt for mince pies that a friend gave me. I haven't tried to make any yet, but will when I get some apples. I'd like to make a nice pie for Thanksgiving dinner. A pie crimper made of whale's ivory came with the receipt.

Am trying to sew a little, but am very slow and find it horribly aggravating. 

Your affectionate niece, Barbara Brooks.

From Clio Carter to her Grandmother: 

Dearest Grandmother;- 

Kansas Territory, December 15, 1855.

This is the coldest place, Grandmother, I ever saw; the water freezes in the water pail at night, and if you will believe it, a loaf of bread froze and we had to toast it, slice by slice and cut each slice off the end of the loaf as we went along. 

But we keep so well, I'm wondering if it isn't just weather that makes people like to work, for both Babs and I enjoy it, at least part of the time. 

EXCEPT sewing-we just naturally hate sewing. 

Things are so funny out here, Grandmother. 

Killing doesn't matter unless the wrong man does the killing or gets killed. A Mr. Collins was shot by a man called Pat Laughlin, and not the slightest notice was taken of it by law officers, because Mr. Collins was a free-state man, and they said it was to be expected.

The trouble all comes when free-state friends of the dead man make a fuss about it, such as happened when Charles Dow was killed. The man he lived with was a Jacob Branson and he made a terrible fuss, and a meeting was held at Hickory Point to ask that the murderers be arrested. 

Pa said they had better quiet Branson, so they captured him to hang him, the Sheriff, Mr. Jones, leading the kidnappers. But what do you think, Grandmother? Mr. Branson's friends got out

early in the morning and chased after the sheriff and took Mr. Branson away from the men who were going to hang him. 

Pa says there'll be hundreds of Missourians who will come here and not only hang Branson, but every man (there were about 20 of them) that had a hand in his rescue. The murderer of 

Charles Dow fled to Governor Shannon and got the Governor to protect him.

This Sheriff, Mr. Jones, is clearing the country around Lecompton of free-staters he just naturally goes at night, turns the families out of their cabins and burns the haystacks and corncribs. Sometimes the cabins too unless one of his own men wants to jump the claim.

But, Grandmother, it seems to me something terrible and Uncle thinks so too. He says its the wrong way to go about it-men being beaten or shot or hanged or taken prisoners and kept in dreadful places, their horses stolen and their homes destroyed. He says too, that it's sure to bring payment in the same coin.

The Red-Legs are already doing the same terrible things, but so far, mostly in Missouri, but they'll be operating here in Kansas, if our side doesn't stop. 

Queer, isn't it Grandmother, I used to think our side was always right and lily white, and the people from the North born with hoofs and horns, and all foreign born people (Germans, for instance) were somehow all off color, but here in 

Kansas, folks come from everywhere and look and act just like us, so I must have been wrong.

Uncle just came in and when he found me writing to you, he wanted me to tell you that a treaty of peace has just been signed between Governor 

Shannon and the people of Lawrence, so things will probably be quiet and safe here in Kansas.

He thought you might feel easier about me and I could see he felt better himself, for things have been really quite stirred up.

The treaty came about something like this, although I reckon I don't know such a lot about it. 

Pa and Uncle don't agree so dreadfully, and its hard to know just what has happened, but as near as I can tell, this is what came about. 

The first Governor was discharged by the 

President because he didn't suit a lot of people in Missouri and South Carolina, and some in 

Kansas, so when Gov. Shannon came, he didn't want to make any mistake, so he wouldn't even attend the banquet and reception the Lawrence people got up for him when he first came. He was in Lawrence at the time, but wouldn't eat with them or anything, and rode through the town sort of scornful, though some said a little drunk.

Some of the young men in Lawrence, when all their hospitality was refused, did a very insulting thing. You know, Grandmother, that generally people give three cheers to speed the parting guest, but what did these young boys do but give three horrible groans as the Governor rode away, and of course he heard them, as they made sure he would, and he was powerful mad about it.

I know, Grandmother, this was terribly impo-

lite, and you always taught me to treat those in high places or in power over us with great respect, no matter how ornery they were. It is much the best way, and safer.

Well, everything seemed to go from bad to worse. Lots of abolitionists whipped and burned out, till finally the Governor called out his militia and hundreds came both from Missouri and 

Kansas to surround and punish Lawrence for disobedience and breaking laws. Then Lawrence folks asked the U. S. soldiers at Ft. Leavenworth for protection, which was refused because neither the Governor nor the President had ordered it. 

So the Lawrence young men commenced drilling and making a fort and everything to protect themselves.

But after hundreds of the Governor's militia were camped all round Lawrence, the Governor himself began to get worried. There had been so many killings, and the last one made the North madder than ever; it was a Mr. Barber, an unarmed free-state man, caught and killed by ten or twelve men, but the Governor says he can't help what his militia does, and he goes to Lawrence to look into things. Then he can't find any broken laws to point to so he makes a treaty and tries to disperse his militia. Some of the men dispersed, but some stayed to loot, and they were so mad at the Governor for making the treaty and stopping their chance of looting, that they threatened to lynch the Governor himself, and the Lawrence people had to protect him from his own mob.

So, now, Grandmother, I reckon you don't need to worry any more about me, or about our being burned out or anything, and won't say again 

I'd better come back to Alabama. Anyway Pa said there wouldn't have been any Lawrence left to trouble the world if Gov. Shannon hadn't had such a terrible thing happen to him. His liver turned white.

When Pa said that, Babs didn't at first take it in till Uncle Charlie laughed at her and told her not to worry about His Honor's liver; 'twas the same old liver he was born with. In a minute, of course, Babs took it all in and laughed at herself. 

Am so glad you can spare money to send me as 

Pa has so little for me. 

One thing more, Grandmother, I'm wondering if it would cost too much to send me your melodeon. We just long for music out here; then it wouldn't be a bit lonesome. Don't do it, Grandmother, if you can't spare the money, but I'd love to have a share in furnishing our cabin. 

Hope you will get this letter before Christmas and that you will have a Merry one. 

Your loving grandaughter,

Clio. 

To the people of Lawrence, the events so casually mentioned by Miss Clio Carter in her Christmas letter to her grandmother were of serious import. 

They realized to the full that the Kansas-Nebraska

Bill had, in its passage, logically insured Kansas as the field of action, defined the nature of the battle

to be fought, hand-picked the actors, and had set no limits to the methods or acts of its favorites to achieve the end designed by its sponsors; i.e. the establishment of the institution of slavery in Kansas. 

And Lawrence stood in the way. She had set her brazen face against the entrenched institution, and unblushingly demanded free labor in Kansas. It was an impertinence, if you like, for Lawrence to constitute herself the seat of learning and culture in this new territory, but that might be borne, but her leadership in passive resistence to the territorial legislature (which she dubbed "bogus") to the extent of non-recognition of any officials, acts, or authority of the same, including the tax-collector, was not to be endured. With Lawrence, passive resistance was true resistance and not mere passive acquiescence.

So Lawrence must not be allowed to spread pernicious doctrines and the cup of her iniquity being full, she must either be subjugated or destroyed. 

And this should not be difficult with a subservient governor and a sympathetic national administration. 

So it seemed quite in order that the governor should call out the militia to invest the small recalcitrant town that stubbornly refused to be betrayed into any act that could be construed as treasonable, but blithely pursued the even tenor of her way, flaunting the flag of freedom with conspicuous insolence.

Nor was she unmindful of the destiny that lay in wait for weakness at the mercy of strength, for mud fortifications had been erected, and Sharpe's rifles

were included in the possessions of many of her citizens.

When Lawrence found herself in December, 1855, surrounded by militia summoned and led by 

Governor Shannon, and numbering approximately eight hundred men, mostly from other states, she faced a perilous situation. Then the unexpected happened. Ideas began obtruding themselves into the bemused mind of the Governor. "Just why was he here? What did he intend to do? Above all, what pretext could be given to the outside world. for the looting and destruction of this beleaguered and unresisting town? For, in spite of all provocations, Lawrence had been guilty of no overt act that could be adduced as a pretext for her destruction, or for turning her over to a wild mob in the name of law and order. 

And the Governor could not take the risk. He declared himself to have been misled, and mistaken as to the situation, and the upshot was that altogether unprecedented thing, nothing less than a treaty of peace, which both sides hoped would in reality mean peace. 

But Governor Shannon's troubles were not ended. When his followers learned that peace had been declared, thus ending their hopes of plunder, their disappointment was bitter. While the majority wended their way homeward, a remnant, nursing their wrath, now directed against the Governor, still remained, not leaving until they discovered that the bird had flown, and the Governor had been secretly convoyed back to Lecompton, under protection of the very men of Lawrence whom he

had sallied forth to subjugate. And the 800 "law and order" men, baffled and balked of their prey, bade Lawrence not Good bye, but Au revoir.

However, they derived some satisfaction from their depredations in the rural districts, on their way to the border, which depredations invited and received retaliation by the notorious Red-Legs, and, as always, the innocent suffered with the guilty. 

Thus dire trouble fell upon the struggling homesteaders with entire indiscrimination.

CHAPTER V 

Less than a week after the strange treaty had been signed, Nat, working late, heard unusual sounds in the customarily quiet street outside the office door. Wondering what was going on, he slipped into his overcoat, for it was bitter cold, and was on the point of blowing out his kerosene oil lamp, when Martin Conway burst into the room. "By God, Nat, there's hell to pay," he gasped, "the Red-Legs are on the rampage-have located three to wreak their vengeance upon. I've tried to reason with them, but they're a wild, desperate bunch and there's no holding them and they howl for blood to pay for Shannon's last outrage. They boast that three more nigger-whippers will never see the sunrise." "Who're the three?" "Sam Linton and Tom Blodgett, who they say plan to import slaves and sell them at auction, and Asa Barlow, a damned renegade, hiding behind the 

Kansas-Nebraska Bill as a Douglas man."

Early May was bright and beautiful, with occasional dark clouds and sudden, heavy downpours that left the rippling oceans of grass starred with wild flowers all clean-bathed and smiling. But, in spite of all Nature's beauty and promise, the air was heavy with portent, and ever the tension grew, and the citizens of the menaced little town walked warily. 

Nat Winslow, with a groan, turned his eyes from the beautiful out-of-doors, gathered his writing materials and determinedly set himself to indite a letter home.

Before advancing on the town, from their station on Mt. Oread, the Marshal sent spies to report on possible measures of defense the citizens had taken.

Over on College Hill, west of the town, a group of women, among whom was Barbara Brooks, watched the burning of buildings, and the looting of homes. That night, the encampment was wild with songs and shouts of victory, nor did the victors dream that their day's work doomed their Governor and his minions to repudiation and exile, the President of their country to execration and political death. 

As tranquil, starry dusk succeeded the unquiet day, on College Hill the women ended their vigil, leaving in small groups to find their way to their ravaged homes. But Barbara Brooks, with arms clasped around knees, sat unheeding. A gentle hand on her shoulder roused her, and a kind voice said, "Won't you come with me? I don't want to leave you here alone. They're all gone, you see?" "Will I be safe here?" "Surely, yes, safe-the danger is over."

"Then," said Barbara, "I will stay-there is one who will come for me. I must stay-and think." "I really hate to leave you alone." "But I must be alone-to think." 

So the woman left her in the early twilight, sitting alone on the grassy slope-thinking.

EPILOGUE 

Time brought fulfilment of the hopes and dreams to the four young adventurers along the pioneer's thorny path, but not before the last shot had been fired in the long fratracidal war of which the struggle in Kansas to "take and keep" was the preface. 

Under the fostering care of Harriet Cushman, Barbara Brooks and Clio Carter fulfilled the promise of their girlhood, and grew to womanhood, brave, true and strong. 

Asa Barlow, man of conflicting passions, fierce in his hatreds, enduring and generous in his affections, guarded well the little cabin at Lecompton, caring for it as for his own, yielding it only to the bride on her wedding day, being ever, through good report and ill, true friend.

Elias Withers lived to ripe old age, loving, chid-

ing, teaching, cherishing the many children who called him Grandfather, only one of whom achieved fame, one Mistress Barbara Wendel, who, with violin under her chin, has the music-loving world (and the heart of her father) under her dainty feet. 

The Kansas-Nebraska Bill had raised the curtain on an appalling drama that ceased only when the last act was played. During all those troubled years following the sack of Lawrence, Karl Wendel and Nathaniel Winslow found scant time for ought but the firing line, first in "Bleeding Kansas" then in the beautiful ravaged South. Together they stood, through hazard or security, in foul prison and on war's bloody field, suffering together, enduring together, hoping together, binding together their minds and hearts in true affection, through all the changing years.
