PREFACE 

The National Federation of Jewish Men's Clubs from its very inception interested itself most earnestly in the problem of child guidance. The leaders of the Federation felt from the very beginning that while there are general problems which must be delved into in accordance with certain psychological rules, nevertheless, in order to be of help to the Jewish parent and the Jewish child, they must also be approached from the Jewish standpoint. 

It is now an accepted dogma that the growth of the child necessitates also a growing change in the attitude of the parents. It is necessary for the parent to realize that the child has certain traits and inclinations by which he must be guided and that the ignoring of this fact may prove ruinous to the child's future career. It is the duty of the parent to understand the child and live over with him the various problems that beset every young person entering the highway and byways of life. Such an attitude is bound to bring about a reciprocal love based on a due respect for each other.

In bringing about a closer harmony and a better understanding between parent and child, it is essential that the Synagogue, the Religious School and the home study the problem cooperatively. The preachings of the pulpit, the teachings of the School and the observances of the home must form a complete harmonious whole. If they exert divergent influences on the child, they will result in a split personality and in the creation of an adult that will experience a continuous inner conflict.

PART ONE  SECTION I MATURATION 

Few things in life give parents the thrill they experience when their child evidences some new accomplishment or achievement. Whether it be standing unsupported for the first time, using a new word intelligently, earning a high grade in school, or indicating by a look, word, or deed a sympathetic insight or understanding of another's needs or feelings any sign of growth or development fills the father's and mother's hearts with a keen delight, a satisfying joy. 

The pleasure is somewhat egotistic-in a good sense. 

There is a justifiable pride in the offspring's advance—a pride based on more than the fact that the child is of their flesh and blood. It is as though the parent were triumphant in the manifestation of the child's powers or abilities. To the parent, the progress made is a result of their own efforts. It is their duty as well as pleasure to teach the child, to train it, to assist in its learning. They consequently apply themselves to this task from the child's birth. Every step forward in its progress to adulthood, is, accordingly, credited in part to their own efforts. Hence, a feeling of personal success or self-satisfaction.

For the same reason backwardness in the child is a keen personal disappointment. There is a hurt to one's pride.

The injured feeling often expresses itself in resentment directed against the little one. At other times, parents be-

come over-solicitous. They hover over the child all the time, criticising, admonishing, and interfering effectively with the development of the child's individuality and selfconfidence. Often, too, the parents become impatient with the rate of their child's development. They expect accomplishments when the desired achievement is, as a rule, impossible. The results are harmful to parents as well as to children.

Most of the maladjustments of children and parents to each other and to the world about them arise in connection with the child's developmental and learning processes. While there is still an enormous amount of research necessary before we can understand these processes, enough is known to help parents in bringing up their children to avoid many of the mistakes and pitfalls which are responsible for so much unhappiness and misery. As an introduction to this. syllabus on Jewish Child Guidance, it was felt that it would be well first to treat briefly of some of the assured findings in the field of the learning processes. The presentation will center about three headings: Maturation, Experience, and 

Habit Formation. In life there is no separation of these processes. All learning and growth is compounded of these and other elements. It will help, however, to a clearer understanding of what is actually happening to follow through these threads separately as they intertwine to determine the child's life and personality. 

We shall first consider the phenomenon of maturation.

All parents recognize the principle. No one, for instance, expects that a week-old infant can be taught to sit up, or that a two-year-old runabout can be saddled with moral responsibility. It is largely in the details of the phenomenon that ignorance prevails. What are the order of events in the maturation of the child? What are the various areas of conduct in which natural growth operates? To what ex-

tent can physical growth or mental development by themselves be depended upon to result in desired achievement?

These are some of the questions on which a knowledge of the process of maturation can cast a helpful light. 

The average child at birth is immature in every phase of human activity. Physically, most of its bones are not fully ossified and many of its muscles are as yet unformed or too weak to serve it effectively. The development of the nervous structure is similarly incomplete, and effective connectedness of various nerve centers has not as yet taken place. 

The cerebellum is as yet undeveloped and the glandular system unripe. As a result, sensation has not attained the definiteness that it will have in a few months. Vision is indistinct, audition imperfect. It is doubtful whether the child feels pain. The child is, accordingly, incapable of any but the most elementary movements or adjustments. 

From a neuro-muscular standpoint, the following orders of development are interesting and instructive. The first step towards manipulation consists of the child's attending to and following with the eye the movements of any small object. After several months, the infant will move his hands on a table near by. At twenty weeks, he will attack the object, clumsily, with both hands. He may touch the object and yet be unable to grasp it. At six or seven months of age, he will be able to move the object by means of a scooping motion with his hands. Another month or two and he grasps a pellet, using thumb and fingers.

The steps leading to locomotion are somewhat as follows. 

At about three weeks of age, the child will lift his head when in a prone position. At nine weeks, he will lift also his chest, supporting his weight on his elbows and forearms. 

At twenty-five weeks, he will also draw up his knees. Rolling from back to a prone position takes another month. At

ten or eleven months of age, the baby is usually able to creep. 

While this manifestation of growth is taking place, the child is maturing in other directions. First comes the ability to sit on the adult's lap. Three months later, the child will stand while holding on to somebody. Another month or so, and he will rise from the floor with the help of a piece of furniture. Twelve or thirteen months is the age at which the average child can stand alone, and by the fifteenth or sixteenth month the child can walk.

It should be noted that in these and subsequent examples of the child's development, there is no intention to give the impression that the steps in any given direction derive their significance only from the end indicated here. The development of the bones, muscles, nerves, etc., that enable the child to achieve an erect position enters into combinations with other abilities to attain many other ends in addition to standing up and walking. The important thing to know is that until these developments take place no amount of training or teaching will enable the child to walk. 

The use of language comes also as a result of a process of development. At one month, the child emits different cries for discomfort, pain and hunger. Several months later, he will laugh and vocalize, playing with the various sounds he is able to make. In the next six months, the connections. with vocal sounds and things, acts, wishes and persons begin to be formed. He will say some words and make up his own vocabulary. According to some investigators, the average child's vocabulary at the age of one year is a few words. At two years, about two hundred and fifty words. 

At three, well over eight hundred. At six, over two thousand. The accuracy of pronunciation and enunciation similarly follows a line of development.

Emotional reactions follow a pattern of growth and differentiation. At first, only gross interference with the infant's freedom of movement and the satisfaction of vital needs, such as breathing, are certain to evoke some attack or defense reaction. After the first month, there begins a differentiation of the reactions corresponding to the feelings experienced by the child. As the glandular development proceeds, and the nerve and brain structure grows, the emotional reactions are further differentiated and refined so that they approach those of the adult.

The individual's acts, desires, etc., are subject to systems of balances. There is continued interaction and counteraction. When there is need for food, taste and smell are sharpened. It is possible to sense taste nuances when one is hungry, that are not evident otherwise. When the need for food is satisfied to superfluity, taste and other senses are dulled. There are glands whose secretions predispose to anger, and others that make for affection. Certain glands stimulate growth. Others inhibit it. The power to inhibit one's actions, impulses, desires and even reflexes is also something that is dependent on growth and maturity. The exact stages of development in this field are not fully known. 

It is important to realize that this phenomenon is to be reckoned with.

Modern research and investigation have disclosed a number of facts that are of value to parents. The following in the area of maturation are important. 

1. In the physical growth and control, the development is from the center of the body outward, and from the larger nerves and muscles to the smaller ones. It is, accordingly, best to observe the child's development and not to expect or train for actions or reactions that necessitate the use of the smaller muscles until they are ready.

2. Human growth is not simply a matter of the emerging and strengthening of organs, nerves and muscles. Patterns of balanced action between various parts of the body constitute the more important stages of development. Reflexes and so-called instinctive acts, such as walking, running, eating, etc., depend for their operation on the development of these patterns of action. When they are ripe, they can be depended upon to result with but little training in desired achievement. When the various abilities called into action by walking have developed, the specific neural arrangements making walking possible will usually also rise. The child will walk with but little help or guidance. Before the nerves, brain and body are ready, however, training will be of no avail. 

3. The rate of development and the strength and skill evidenced in the newly appearing actions, vary with the individual. Some will acquire the art of walking more quickly than others. They will pass through the various steps leading to walking, but will do so at a faster rate. For each specific type of performance, the rate of development is fairly constant. The child that creeps earlier will walk earlier. One who babbles precociously will talk precociously. 

There is, also, a slight positive relationship between different types of achievement. The physically stronger child will tend, also, to be more skillful in motor performances. The more intelligent child is usually above the average physically.

In the intellectual areas, too, maturation plays an important part. Just how memory and intelligence, for instance, are determined by the brain and nerve structure is still unknown. That they are dependent upon that structure is certain. As the outer brain, or cerebellum, grows and develops, the child's abilities in the mental fields increase. Training, exercises and practice cannot advance a

child beyond predetermined bounds. Several examples of development in these areas may prove helpful. 

The first manifestation of memory consists of the recognition of objects previously seen. Between six and twelve months, a child will indicate that it recognizes individuals and objects. The length of time which a child will remember increases with the age of the child. The number of things the child can memorize at one time, the span of memory, is also dependent on the age of the child. It should be noted that the degree of memory attained in one field of home interest is no indication of equal mnemonic ability in other fields. The child who is particularly proficient in memorizing prose or poetry may not prove so expert in remembering faces or mathematical processes.

In recent years, there have developed many forms of intelligence testing. To what extent these represent definite mental traits is, as yet, uncertain. Whatever traits or combinations of traits they do represent, however, present evidence indicates that maturation operates. Definite types of tests are suitable for two-year-olds; others, for three-yearolds, etc. By the age of seventeen, mental growth has almost ceased. Experience, training, and stimulation are helpful in making for better use of what intelligence the individual may possess. They cannot add to it. Here, too, it should be noted that there are various types of intelligence. Qualities of insight differ with the subject matter in which it operates. The intelligence that functions in mathematical relationships differs from that which is evident in social relationships. Aptness in sensing possibilities in spatial combinations does not always go hand-in-hand with artistic genius. Individual differences in amount and type of intelligence must be reckoned with. 

Some words must be said with respect to the subject of aptitudes. Some children develop abilities early in life that

enable them to attain to a degree of excellence in some one or more pursuits far superior to those of the average individual. There are geniuses in different fields of human interest and activity. We have great intellects and physical giants, musical virtuosi and artistic geniuses, mathematical marvels and great inventors. It is evident that we are dealing here with extraordinary manifestations of physical, neural and cortical development. Training is important, but the controlling factor is the original endowment. 

There is a tendency to attend only to the genius or highly talented. The fact is that abilities and aptitudes do not attain uniform growth or development. Everyone does something better than he does other things, and all aptitudes and abilities are deserving of attention and encouragement. 

Furthermore, interests vary with capacities. The child, as well as the adult, will be more interested in those things which make use of his aptitudes. It is, accordingly, necessary that parents should be alive to the development of these capacities, and provide opportunities for their exercise. 

Maturation can be applied also to another phenomenon.

Many of the skills and capacities which the adult exhibits may appear to be simple in nature. In fact, they are constituted of numerous other skills that have been learned slowly and painstakingly and then combined to operate in the adult world. Similarly, grown-ups will deal with such concepts as Nationhood, Religion, People, etc., as though they were simple perceptual elements. They will expect young children to assimilate the meaning of these words before they have developed the power to grasp even part of their connotations. It should be realized that the child cannot possibly understand such terms in the same sense that adults do. It often happens, moreover, that parents themselves may be under erroneous and immature conceptions of what these terms denote. There are certainly many

differences amongst them as to the realities underlying their meaning and what their actions and attitudes should be with respect to these realities. Yet much of the behavior expected of children is urged on the ground that Religion, God or National Loyalty demands it.

The following procedure is better. Let the parents consider carefully the elements of which these various concepts consist. Then let them provide opportunities for the child to develop behavior patterns in accordance with these elements. When definite progress has been made, the more generic terms can be learned and utilized. One or two instances will help clarify this point. 

What shall God mean to the child? Is He to be some personage continuously commanding, exacting obedience and punishing transgressions and rewarding compliance? Or is the child to understand by God some mysterious, inscrutable force, strong, awful and often vindictive? Shall not the child rather sense His presence in the world through the love, friendship, blessings and security he finds in it? Parents can build up any one of these concepts by emphasizing one element or another in their attitude to the children and the world, and by the way they associate the elements they stress with His name.

Again, how is the child to learn Jewish history so that he may actually identify himself with the past, as well as repeat from memory events in chronological or causal connection? Efforts to have the child obtain a perspective of past events before he can comprehend the nature of time must prove futile. It is best, instead, to try to bring to him past events in story and drama as they are associated with his present interests in the course of his every-day, Sabbath and festival activities. As the capacity for placing events in their proper place in the past develops, a study of history as such becomes possible.

SECTION II EXPERIENCE 

The child learns also through the process of experience. 

It appears that the words, deeds and reactions of the child leave some impress upon him, which he is in a position to use in dealing with new situations. It is not yet fully understood just how experience helps, nor why it should help. Many studies have been made in this field, however, which point to facts that are of value to the parent and teacher.

Under the heading, Experience, there are generally subsumed a number of operations that may or may not have some organic relationship with one another. 

First of all, there is the generally recognized principle that activities which are pleasant will tend to be repeated. 

Situations giving rise to them will be sought after. Similarly, unpleasant experiences will be avoided. For the most part, the pleasantness or unpleasantness of the experience is referred to the sensations felt in connection with it. The child learns to avoid the fire because he may have burnt his fingers. He craves candy because he found it to be sweet.

Deeper consideration necessitates broadening the principle. Painful or disagreeable sensations are not always deterrent, nor are agreeable sensations always sought after. 

It is not simply that sharp and unpleasant tastes may be cultivated as when we learn to smoke or drink. It is possible, also, to crave hardship and pain for various other purposes. Some children will induce vomiting in themselves, beat their hands and heads against hard objects, or

court punishment to awaken sympathy or to attract attention.

A more correct way of designating this principle is to speak of satisfying and unsatisfying experiences. This brings the subject out of the narrow realm of sensation and enables us to consider it from the standpoint of the varied forms of vital urges. Activities engaged in for the furtherance of certain purposes or causes are satisfying irrespective of the sensations that accompany them. Other activities conceived as tending to undesirable results are unsatisfying. 

This does not mean that the quality of the sensation is to be ignored. It will often determine conduct by itself. 

Furthermore, the cause and purpose will not always win out against painful sensations. However, the vitality of the purpose and the strength of the attachment the individual has developed for the cause are the determining elements in the situation. When the animal's hunger is strong enough, it will brave an electric shock to get its food. 

The more intense the hunger, the stronger the shock it will find itself able to endure.

Since the act often derives its satisfying qualities from the aim it serves, it is important to bear in mind the nature of some of these satisfying purposes. First of all are those which serve vital functions, such as obtaining food, water, shelter, and a sense of security. Later in life, the sexual urge will begin to play its dominating part. There are also social urges.

Whether the desire to be with groups and the development of social loyalties are innate or acquired does not matter. For many, they constitute the most satisfying of purposes. Expression of one's abilities, capacities and aptitudes is also vital. Conversely, the acts which tend to interfere with the satisfaction of the above mentioned urges are considered as unsatisfying.

25 Religious observances and customs will best be learned. when they are made part of the pleasant experiences of the child's life. Three conditions should be satisfied. There should be an adequate stimulus for the observance. The best is that of parents or other adults performing the ceremony. In the second place, the observance should have meaning for the child. It is not enough to give such an explanation as "God has asked us to do it." Better results. will follow when, in the case of benedictions, for instance, the child is asked "Don't you feel glad when you have food. to eat and water to drink?" "When we feel this way and are grateful to God for having ordered the world so that we may find in it what we need, we give expression to our thoughts in this way." The benediction follows. Similarly,. in explanation of the prohibition against the eating of leavened bread on Passover: "When our great, great, greatgrandparents long, long ago, left Egypt, they were so anxious to get out of bondage to freedom that they had no time to bake bread such as we eat every day. They made "matzoh" instead. Every year we all act as they did so that we can feel as our great, great, great-grandparents did in those days." Finally, the observance must bring in its train a feeling of pleasure and satisfaction, the best form of which is social approval. 

Many of the observances can make use of the child's capacities or talents. Every effort should be made to find elements in the ceremony or festival celebration to which the child can contribute through the use of his powers. The need for expressing one's self is one of the strongest stimuli. 

An adequate expression of an ability in a religious cause can do more than anything else to strengthen loyalty to that cause.

There is another form of learning which may be included under the subject, Experience. At every moment

26 

we undergo a variety of experiences. Our senses are attacked by stimuli from all sides. Events of which we are aware are happening all about us. The tendency is to organize into units all experience of which we are aware at one time. This tendency is evident not only with respect to experiences taking place simultaneously, but also with respect to experiences following one another in time. For example, if something unpleasant happens there is a tendency to regard every circumstance occurring immediately before or during the experience as sharing in the responsibility for it, or as tinged with its painful feeling tone. 

Consequently, when we suffer pain or discomfort or unhappiness, the occasion, the place we were in, the various things that happened at the time-all are considered contributing factors of our misfortune. This is not due to any conscious effort. It is the natural reaction to the environment.

Scientists have performed a number of interesting experiments which exemplify this phenomena. The following is well known. Just before, or at the time food is presented to the child or animal, a bell is rung. After a number of repetitions of this experience, the ringing of the bell alone will cause the saliva and other digestive fluids to flow. We are all familiar with the fact that the house in which a near and dear one passes away becomes pregnant with morbid associations. Books or manuscripts containing valuable or holy writings assume a value or sacredness in themselves. Personal belongings of dear ones take on an enhanced value. The more valued the friend, the more valued will be his gifts. The bite of one dog may cause a shrinking and a fear of all dogs.

Psychologists term this principle "simultaneous stimulations" or "associative shifting". Many of the pleasant or unpleasant feelings we have for persons and things are not

27 due to anything inherent in these objects, but are a result of the operation of the principle of associative shifting. 

As can well be imagined, this principle, without any corrective process, might very well make life impossible. The indiscriminate attributing of causal connections to every circumstance, relevant or irrelevant, surrounding anything that happens must result in such an abundance of errors as to lead to utterly irrational impulses and inhibitions. How does the individual learn to pick out the relevant element, the factor that is responsible for what is happening?

We come now to another factor in Experience. If a number of instances occur in which the natural stimulus is unaccompanied by a particular element, and if, in addition, instances occur in which the element is present without the natural stimulus, that element will lose its power to evoke the original response. For example: The child is presented with food at noon when the clock strikes twelve. The food stimulates the flow of the digestive fluids in the mouth and other organs of digestion. In time, the striking of the clock at noon will also come to evoke the discharge of the saliva, gastric juice, etc. As life goes on, however, the child experiences occasions when the striking of the clock is not followed by food. It also happens that food may come unaccompanied by the bell of the clock. This results in the child's learning that the stroke of the clock is not a relevant element in the food situation. 

It is a pity that so little planned use is made of this principle in the up-bringing of children. It goes without saying that much of the successful learning of the observances and practices is a result of the operation of associative shifting. The glow of happiness accompanying the approval or love of the parents when attending school or services is often, unfortunately, the only reason for that attendance. 

The mother scolding or the father punishing may be the

only element that makes a forbidden act unpleasant. However, this psychological law can be given a broader and wider application. Several instances will serve to make this clear. 

The learning of ceremonies should be associated with the satisfaction of definite needs. Children will learn benedictions best at the time when these utterances express a present feeling. Benedictions for food should be taught when refreshments are about to be eaten. The "kiddush" should be taught in connection with definite arrangements for its recital by the child at a forthcoming function in the home. 

If the Sabbath is to be a day of "delight" to the child, there must be social recreational activities arranged for that day that will give it pleasant associations to the child. This will necessitate cooperative thinking and planning on the part of the teacher and parent, but it is the only way in which the spirit of the Sabbath can be preserved for future 

Judaism. 

Suppose the individual is confronted with a new situation, something he has never encountered before. How shall he react to it? Elements in the situation may have constituted part of past situations, the reactions to which have been established. The tendency will be to discern these elements and to react to them in the new situation. What the individual should do is to wait, to refrain from acting, so as to get the time to examine all the elements in the situation.

But here there may be a difficulty. A child passes a room from which a voice is heard, "Hello, Billy. Come in, won't you?" He looks in and there is a room full of people. amongst whom there is but one familiar face, his aunt's. 

What will Billy do? On the one hand, the strange adults are ominous, especially as they seem to want him for reasons that may be not at all friendly. Strangers are not always

29 friends-that he knows. On the other hand, there is his aunt, inviting him to come in. When only past experience is the determining factor, there may be conflict. Will the child's response be decided by the algebraic sum of the psychic forces?

Sometimes Billy may be possessed, also, of a generally friendly disposition, or he may be hungry at the moment and he may note some refreshments in the room. In such events, there would be a favorable response. The act of the child, then, would not be determined by a past experience but by a dominating element in the present situation. 

In any difficult situation the child's reaction will tend to be adequate if he does the following things. 1. He inhibits action and attends in turn to the various elements in the situation. 2. He realizes that certain of the elements are more significant and important. In the first act, experience is given an opportunity to function; the second is often a result of insight or intelligence. 

Training in deliberation before action is necessary. First of all, it will make the subsequent response more adequate and will prevent hasty and unwise decisions. In the second place, the power of inhibition is necessary in the development of the "good life". Moral, ethical considerations do not present themselves, as a rule, with the immediacy and urgency of sensual and selfish desire. A certain amount of inhibition of action is accordingly essential to permit the spiritual to operate in our lives. When occasion offers opportunities, accordingly, parents should say, "Now, let us wait and consider. The proposed act will be pleasant, or involves a sacrifice, but how will it affect our friends, our poor neighbors? What about our fellow-citizens, our fellow-Jews?" The desire and ability to discern more ultimate consequences is an important element in righteous living. Care should be taken, though, to utilize for this

purpose only natural situations that actually demand this treatment. It is also necessary that, when parents are confronted with situations making possible decisions on highly ethical principles, they should have regard for the higher demands in life.

Can insight be explained as the sudden calling to mind of a relevant past experience? Many psychologists think 

So. There are a number of facts, however, that seem to point in another direction. There is the sense that, inherently, insight is a sudden grasp of relationships. The same elements in a situation may have been gone over again and again. It is when they take their place in some form of functional and meaningful relationship, one to the other and all to the individual concerned that the understanding takes place.

This capacity to sense relationship varies, firstly, with the individual irrespective of the multiplicity of his past experiences, and, secondly, with the subject matter in which the insight may operate. Intelligence is innate, and intelligence tests have predictive value, at least as far as the particular type of intelligence tested is concerned. But there are various types of intelligence. There are people who have an uncanny faculty for perceiving spatial relationships. 

Mechanical inventors belong to that tribe. Others have mathematical insight; still others have what is termed social intelligence.

Insight probably does not represent just one type of mental operation. There is the insight based upon past experience: discerning in a puzzling situation elements experienced in the past which give a clue to the best response to it. There is also an insight that is due to the ability to sense possible relationships between the various elements in the situation, one to the other and all to the welfare of the individual concerned. The latter may be called creative in-

For our purpose, social insight is of paramount importance. Parents should try to recognize the manifestations of this form of intelligence in children. Do they sense another's feelings? Do they realize the consequences of their behavior upon the attitudes of others to themselves? 

Can they appreciate the social relationships that exist between the individual and groups in their circle? Individuals vary in the extent to which they possess the capacity to sense these things. When the child, manifests this ability, it should be encouraged and helped.

SECTION III HABIT FORMATION 

The child desires to tie its shoelaces. This necessitates many complicated and intricate movements on the part of a large number of muscles in the body. The child must bend the body and flex its legs; the arms, wrists and hands are engaged. Then the smaller and larger muscles of the fingers and wrists are put into play. The attention of the child must all the while be centered on the lesser objectives which these movements are to serve the formation of the loops, the insertion of the ends of the laces, etc. 

After a number of repetitions, all the child needs is to feel that the laces ought to be tied. The series of movements are then gone through without any further conscious effort or attention on his part. Sometimes the mere discomfort of seeing the laces dangling is enough to set off the movements. He does not even have to think, "I ought to tie the laces". The mind and consciousness of the child is free to solve other problems. The habit of tying shoelaces has been formed.

Similar processes take place when the child learns to dress itself, to button its clothing, to use various utensils, or to do any number of other tasks necessary for living in this world.

One way of describing the formation of a habit would be something like this. When it is desired to execute a series of movements by one or more muscles in order to achieve a given objective, much attention must be paid to the goals of each movement and to the principal aim in view. After

an adequate number of repetitions of these movements have taken place, attention is no longer necessary to their execution. The starting of the first movement in the series or the presence of the necessary stimulus will set off the whole series, while the attention of the individual may stray or be given to other matters.

There is another field in which habit operates. The formation of words, phrases and sentences also involves movements of a large number of muscles in the thorax, neck, mouth and face. The child at an early age finds these muscles strong enough for the production of various sounds -vowels and consonants. The connection between certain combinations of sounds-words-and what they represent is learned to some extent through the process described in the chapter on Experience. Some combinations of sounds the child makes in his vocal play are the same as meaningful words. The approval of parents and others at these utterances help to fix the words amongst the child's activities.

The greater number of words, phrases, sentences, etc., however, are learned through the habit formation processes.

Children note adults using these combinations of sounds in connection with objects and acts. There is a desire to do the same, and the effort is made to repeat these words and phrases. In time, the child learns to give utterance to these without attending to the sound elements of which they are composed. There is a stimulus, a mental set, and the proper words are produced. Memorizing words, phrases and sentences, learning a new language-all imply the use of a type of memory which in reality is habit formation. There is a practice in the sequences of movements necessary to the pronunciation and enunciation of the needed vocal material. In time, the need for the production of the sounds

37 or the inscribing of the written symbols starts the muscular process necessary to their emergence.

There are other types of activities or occupations involving habit formation. Typewriting will serve as an example.

The objective is the recording of certain words, figures, sentences and passages by means of the typewriter. At the beginning, the attention is directed to every element in the pattern of the necessary movements. With practice, however, attention to the letters or words, the minor ends, is obviated. All that is kept in mind is the principal goal, the passage to be typed. The connections between the stimulus and the results require a minimum of conscious attention. 

The eyes see the stenographic symbols, the fingers and other muscles go through the movements necessary for recording them. The above are instances of pure habit formation, where the acts are the result of their necessary movements, made semi-autonomous.

How that is done, what changes take place in the brain and nerve matter so that acts and performances may become habitual is not, as yet, known. There are differing schools of opinion into which there is no need of entering here. However, there are a number of principles operating in habit formation, which have been experimentally verified, and the knowledge of which may be of help to parents. 

1. There must be a desired goal or objective. The individual must want to lace his shoes, learn the words, or learn to send a telegraphic message. Mere repetition of the correct sequence of movements without the direction of the attention to a desired end will not result in the formation of a habit, no matter how many times repetitions are made. 

The source of the desire may be either the satisfaction of a felt need arising from the organism directly, or from the desire to comply with the wishes or commands of others. In early childhood, especially, the wishes of par-

ents and adults are very often regarded as bound up with one's own welfare. They are consequently adequate motivations for the attainment of the goals requiring habits. 

When, for any reason, the parental wish or command loses its inward compelling force and no other motive appears, the habit will not be formed. 

2. When the movements in a particular habit are many, the tendency is to split them up in groups and to make each group automatic by itself. When one learns to typewrite, the first practice sessions result in a gradual increase of speed. Then, for a time, continued practice does not seem to make for an appreciable improvement. But, as practice still continues there comes a new and greater rise in achievement. A careful study of this phenomenon, which is quite general in habit formation processes, has shown that what is happening is the following. After the preliminary practice, which results in obviating unnecessary and interfering movements and making automatic some of the necessary moves, the words and passages become connected, as it were, in units and these units become automatized. While the organization of these units is taking place, we experience the temporary halt in the rate of improvement. When the organization of units begins to be completed, there is another spurt of progress. 

There are other regularized or regularly recurring activities which are commonly called habits, but which are dependent upon other than the above described processes for their being learned.

When certain organic processes take place at definite times, we speak of them as habits. Retiring at certain hours and awakening at definite times, the evacuation of the bowels and bladder at certain intervals, eating at stated hours all are termed habits. These, however, are quite distinct in nature from the habits treated above. They re-

Within limitations, this rhythm is subject to conscious control. When, as a result of the regularization of other activities, time for these processes becomes definite, they take on the appearance of habits. Their automatic nature, however, is due to the internal structure and interconnections of brain tissue, nerve and muscle, rather than to any definite learning. 

Habitual preferment or aversion of certain foods may be due to a variety of causes. The child may be so constituted as to dislike certain foods, which in reality may be bad for him. Associative shifting may have taken place by which certain foods have acquired for the child an unpleasant connotation. It is also possible that the child has found the rejection of certain foods, or capriciousness in taste and distaste of foods, a means of drawing attention to himself, or expressing the sense of his importance. In the first case, a doctor's diagnosis is necessary; in the others, a psychologist's or a psychiatrist's, followed by careful reeducation, very often of parents as well as the child. 

The establishing of set times for study, play, work, etc., is dependent upon social and environmental conditions rather than upon organic rhythms. These habits of regularity are very specific in nature. One learns regularity, steadiness, or orderliness with respect to the specific situations in which such demands have been made. There is no transfer to other things or activities. Nor is orderliness with respect to physical things an indication of system and order in mental process or in storing of knowledge. 

Parents are afraid that their children may form bad habits, such as smoking, drinking and masturbation. Here, too,

the word "habit" does not adequately describe just what is happening when one is addicted to a vice. Smoking and drinking are usually indulged in because of social influence. Friends are smoking. Pals think it smart to drink.

It is sportsmanship to do what the gang or the set is doing. 

At times, it may also serve to symbolize the revolt against parental and adult authority. What makes it habitual, however, is not the practice in the muscular movements that are entailed in smoking or drinking. It is rather the fact that as a result of such indulgences, changes take place in the organism, which create a sense of discomfort bordering on suffering unless relief is procured by further indulgence.

Another element may enter the picture. When, for any reason, there is no provision for stimuli or opportunities for healthy expression of one's energies, capacities or aptitudes, when one feels narrowed or distorted by life, escape is often sought in practices that soothe and dull the senses and activating impulses. These practices and indulgences are doubly dangerous when they also help create imaginary situations in which one can obtain what he wishes most. When smoking, drinking or the use of narcotics begins to serve in these capacities, cure becomes practically impossible without changing the social and environmental situation so as to permit fuller and richer living. As the world is now constituted, this is often impossible. 1 Gambling derives its strength from the fact that it offers a possible opportunity for obtaining a much sought for end, the possession of money, through the exercise of such powers as one may readily possess. Many desire their children to observe various religious practices, such as the utterance of prayers and benedictions at stated times and the adherence to various ceremonies and observances. The acquiring of the skills necessary to the observance of religious rites, such as the memorizing of prayers and benedictions, is subject to the general laws of habit formation. The principal motivating goal should be more than the satisfaction of parental wishes.

The maintaining of the practice, however, depends on other factors. In times past, when these observances formed part of the warp and woof of community life, the social influence sufficed at least for lip service. Nowadays, this situation does not obtain. The best thing to do is to invest these ceremonies and rituals with living feelings and sentiments. When the Sabbath and Festivals are observed mechanically, when prayers and benedictions are not meaningful, and when the Shema at bedtime has no association with present loves and aspirations, religious ceremonies will not be maintained as a rule after the early adolescent period. The observances must be impregnated with living sentiments and emotions in order that they may live.

The problem of teaching children to be honest, brave, truthful, etc., is still more serious. Here, there is no set motor pattern to train for. The act required under one circumstance may be entirely different from the one necessary in another situation. Furthermore, the attitudes and idealogies underlying various acts generally termed honest vary widely. Refraining from cheating one's brother is an entirely different sort of thing from refraining from cheating one's next door neighbor or friend. Stealing from your grocer is different from stealing from the car company. The same difficulties are present with respect to other so-called character traits. Can unifying elements be utilized in the building of these traits?

When one belongs to a group or set that has a tacitly accepted principle of conduct, the members of the group while under its influence will as a rule act in accordance with this principle. Families, school classes, gangs and chums act according to the same standards in cases where the members of these groups feel strongly their relationship to the group.

This is one unifying principle. Another is the sense of kinship which may be created for ever widening circles of human beings. Our attitudes toward those whom we feel are our own will determine our conduct in situations in which they are participants.

Habits function in conduct as the machine does in production. They help to obviate the necessity for the expenditure of energy in continued conscious effort. If every time one had to dress or eat it would be necessary to attend to every movement involved in the accomplishing of these purposes, life would be fatiguing and enervating. Habit, as such, enters into every phase of living. 

The satisfaction of physical, cultural and intellectual needs utilizes varying groups of habits which ease burdens and

43 facilitate progress. 

The formation of adequate habits in learning music, for instance, frees the attention for the exercise of the interpretive faculties that are the possession of the true artist and genius. As the home develops in the house built of bricks, so the larger and the higher accomplishments in life require inner strata of skills built of habits.

It is possible, however, for machines so to clutter up one's residence or factory as to interfere vitally with the work that needs to be done. Furthermore, it is best not to be tied too much to certain machines which may become outdated and less useful in view of the new needs that may arise. In other words, machines are tools made to serve, not masters that enslave.

So it is with habits. In various areas of human life one encounters habits formed originally for good and valid purposes and maintained after these purposes need no longer be served. At times their existence prevents new and important ends from being envisaged and gained. People learn to swim through the use of certain strokes, and then became disinclined to learn new and better ones. They memorize poetic and prose selections from certain writers, and keep mind and eye closed to new messages of truth and inspiration. Ancient saws are repeated endlessly, and present needs are neglected. Some even pray to remove bygone evils, and are not conscious of present-day needs for aspiration and communion.

Mechanics are needed in life, but all of life must not be mechanized. The best guard against the danger of mechanization is the cultivation of the use of intelligence. 

With the training of the right habits, the object the habits are to serve must also be kept perfectly clear. It is not enough for the child to do as he is told. As soon as he is sufficiently mature to understand it, the form and ways

of living to which he has been trained should be treated not as something sacrosanct, but as reasonable or requisite in the social environment we are in. Under all circumstances, intelligence must be permitted to operate.

DISCUSSION 

1. When we are hurt and irritated, we tend to seek a scapegoat-to find someone or something that we can invest with responsibility for our unhappy state. Friends and relatives may become the objects of suspicion and anger. If, however, the individual has no possible connection with the source of our pain, or if there is absolute understanding and confidence in his love and friendship, no blame will be attached to him. His companionship, on the contrary, will help sustain hope and courage.

Evidently that love and understanding does not exist between Mr. B. and Fred. There is loyalty, but it is not deep enough to prevent their attributing to each other a share in their unhappiness. Contrast Fred's attitude to his mother with that to his father. What may account for the difference? Is it due to the fact that his mother understands him better, is able to enter more deeply into his feelings and needs, and has more confidence in his abilities, more appreciation of his ideals, and strengthens and shares his hope for opportunities to find and make his way in the world?

2. Fred is not suffering from lack of purely physical necessities. There is fear that he may experience deprivation. For the present, though, he has all he needs. What hurts him is the fact that he lacks the opportunity to establish himself, to build up and maintain a place for himself in the world. He expects these opportunities as a right, and his disappointment is resulting in a breaking down of loyalties and the reorganization of his attitudes. It seems manifest to him that his greatest handicap is the fact that he is a Jew. This results in the weakening of his attachment to his faith and people. Since his name betrays his origin, he would change it, even if this involves pain to his parents. 

Wherein does Fred err? Has he a right to a place in the world, to a legitimate and useful outlet for powers and capacities, and for resultant appreciation and social reward? 

Is he correctly assuming that the reason given for denial of these opportunities is justified that discrimination against the Jew is made necessary by something in the Jewish nature and character?

3. Suppose he does not make the assumption. Suppose he regards the prejudices as unreasonable. Would it still be right for him to leave his people in view of his very real and vital needs? Why?

Suppose he does not make the assumption. Suppose he regards the prejudices as unjustified and due only to a resentment at the general unhappiness in the world, venting itself against a scapegoat, the Jew. What should he do then? Would leaving the Jewish fold constitute a solution? (a) What effect would he experience from cutting himself off from his cultural heritage? From his social ties?

(b) Would his renouncing his faith and people result in the securing of social and economic advantages? What light on this question is cast by the attitude of the Nazis in Germany to the assimilationist Jew, and to the converted Jew? 

What light on the subject is cast by the persecutions of the 

Maranos in Spain?

4. Would allying himself with the forces making for a happier, a regenerated world, help? What is the principal objective of Judaism in the world? Can he derive strength and inspiration from a closer affiliation with his faith and people?

What satisfactions can Judaism offer to counteract his disabilities? Can it open a vista of a new world order based on both understanding and cooperation?

If Fred had been given an education through which he were made conscious of the beauty and worth-whileness of Judaism, of the saintly personalities of Jewish sages and martyrs; if Fred had actually lived Jewishly, would he have been as ready to sever his ties with his people?

5. It is evident that his parents have failed in integrating him with Jewish life and interests. But how about the community and the congregation? Is it all a matter of the home? If the school had provided him with the joys

and satisfactions of companionship and friendship, arising from cooperation for things Jewish; if, as he entered adolescence, there was a club, a junior congregation, through which he could spend his energy in strengthening Jewish life, would he have felt as he did about Judaism and the 

Jewish people?

In the middle ages, when many avenues of occupations and professions were closed to the Jew, he specialized in those fields that he was permitted to enter. To counteract the enmity and cruelty in the world about him, he created institutions and norms of conduct tending to ameliorate the conditions brought about by the inimical environment, such as the Hachnosas Orchim and Pidyon Sh'vuim.

Has the time come for congregational and national leaders to study the present situation, and offer guidance and help in the choosing of vocations that are more open to the Jew, and in finding ways for helping the new Jewish generation to establish itself? Is there justice in Fred's demand that rich men of his congregation give him work?
