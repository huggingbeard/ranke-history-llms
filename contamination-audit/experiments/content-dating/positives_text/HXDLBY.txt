Poor Hands 

Y father and mother were fond of Whist, and

M often I had to play with them. Once they had some guests, only eleven altogether, and my father called out to me "Come here and make the fourth at this table."

I did not want to go a bit, for I was greatly interested in the instalment of "Daniel Deronda" in Harper's

Magazine. After a few hands I threw down my cards. "I won't play any more," I said, "I can't do anything with these poor hands." "Dammit, boy," my father cried out, "Come back here. Take up these cards. Don't you know that any fool can play a good hand and that it requires a wise man to play a poor hand well."

I took up the cards. I have forgotten the result, but father's words however stuck in my mind. Sometimes it seems to me that all my life I have been playing poor hands. I do not know whether I have played them wisely, but I do know that I have taken the greatest interest in playing them.

I have forgotten all about "Daniel Deronda" but my father's rebuff through a long life has done me more good than many sermons. I take up what is dealt to me, and try to do something with it. Some one else might do a great deal better, but I have acquired some degree of wisdom in the effort.

A Dutch Treat 

'HE most glorious walk I had that first summer in

TH 

Switzerland was from Meiringen to Grindelwald over the Grosser Scheidegg. I was lifted up beyond measure and got entirely outside of myself. I wanted someone with me, not so much to talk to as to share my enthusiasm and thereby double it. I had to do the walking alone, for the Americans whom I knew in Lucerne weren't given to walking and the Swiss, great walkers, did their walking on Sundays when I had to be at the church.

The next morning in the breakfast room of the hotel at Interlaken I noted a rather intelligent looking German with a heavy black beard, sopping up his coffee with a croissant.

Getting out of the little train at Lauterbrunnen I saw him ahead of me making for the path to Wengen.

The funicular railway was not running then and I overtook him before he got far and found out that he too was walking to Grindelwald. Here in the midst of grand scenery I was to have the companion I had longed for.

We had lunch together at a hotel well situated to get a view of the Jungfrau and her attendants in bridal attire. He knew no English and I knew very little German. He knew less French than I knew. I had a little of

my college Latin and Greek still sticking to me and he had more at his command. We got to know each other very well in this polyglot sort of way, talking on all sorts of subjects, not about the scenery, except when the avalanches thundered. We differed somewhat but had much in common.

He was a professor in some German university. The grandeur of the mountains was a background to our thoughts. My mind had an uplift as well as my eyes. So the day went on happily. 

At Grindelwald we had a half hour to wait for the train back to Interlaken. There was a beer garden near by where we sat down. "Ein stein?" he asked. "Ja," I replied gladly, thinking the beer would cement forever the friendship of the day in a sacramental sort of way. I was really elated by the thought rather than by the beer.

The beer cost two pfenings, not much, but it heightened the sacramental idea. 

I had drained my stein, when he tapped me on the shoulder, meaning it was my turn to treat. 

I beckoned to the waiter and held up two fingers. 

The beer came-I drank half of mine. 

The illusion was gone. 

The Dutch treat killed it.

LESSINGS heighten, so it is said, when they take their flight. Often, however, after such a flight blessings follow of still greater height. 

That first summer at Lucerne was a glorious summer for me.

The getting there from Liverpool was full of interest, and the being there seemed near to heaven, not on the mountain tops alone, but on the walks around the lake. Then the people there not only the Swiss, but the friends that happened there from Massachusetts, Rhode Island and Connecticut made it homelike.

Towards the end of August there was no prospect of other friends. The old bridge got to be a familiar story and Thorvaldsen's famous Lion lost its novel charm. I was getting homesick. The Old Catholic priest asked me to climb with him and a Swiss artist the Uri- Rothstock, the one mountain about the Lake I had not climbed. I went with them until the ascent began and tried to understand their Schweizer-Deutsch. I said I was sick and must go back.—I was homesick.

It was Thursday. I went to bed, remained in bed all that day and nearly all Friday. Saturday with Sunday duty ahead I got up and feeling better tried to write a

sermon. I couldn't do it. I picked out one of the half dozen old sermons I had brought with me and tried to doctor it to suit the occasion. A sermon thus treated is like a stew made out of a roast of the day before. 

Sometimes, if onions and spices are added, it is palatable and sometimes it is stale. 

Late in the afternoon I walked back and forth on the chestnut lined quais, entranced by the glorious view before me of lake and mountains in the changing lights of the setting sun, but among the crowd of the crowd of persons I met as I walked back and forth I did not see a familiar face.

After dinner I remembered that in the lobby of the Schweizerhof there was a black board on which it was then the custom to chalk the names of the new arrivals and I said to myself, "Perhaps I will find the name of a friend there."

As I scanned the board hopefully I could not find an 

American name, only German names were there. I forgot the rosy tints I had seen on the lake and on the mountains, and my heart began to sink when suddenly I felt it rise again as I recognized before me a large man I knew, Dr. McVickar of Philadelphia, afterwards 

Bishop of Rhode Island. I felt that my own Bishop, Phillips Brooks of Massachusetts must be near for the two often travelled together. True enough. He presently appeared and warmly greeted me. 

My fears were over. I need not use the patched up sermon for my Bishop would surely preach for me. 

After friendly talk I said,

"Bishop, I am glad you are here, just in time to preach for me, tomorrow." 

"I will do anything you want me to do, when I get back home, but I won't preach for you tomorrow." "The people knowing you are here will expect you to preach." 

More decidedly he said, "I won't preach for you bere." "I am sorry, very sorry," I said and then tried to talk cheerfully of other things.

On leaving Dr. McVickar walked with me toward the door, and recognizing the position I was in, said quietly, "Come around in the morning, he may change his mind."

Clinging to that hope, I went to bed and slept. 

The next morning at the early service I told the people there that Phillips Brooks was in town and that there was a probability of his preaching at the next service, and it would be well for them to speak of it to others wherever they were staying. 

I found the two friends at their breakfast.

The Bishop was adamant. He would not preach. 

I bowed to fate or to opportunity and preached before the largest congregation of the season and tried to think that the greatest preacher of the day was only one of the many persons there.

I did not see Dr. McVickar again. He left that night or the next morning. The Bishop stayed on for three days waiting for a brother who did not come.

These three days I had the great Phillips Brooks to myself, were beautiful and inspiring beyond the gorgeous views. It was the glory of a presence. 

One question somewhat personal had a memorable answer. "Bishop, you come over here often; do you go to different places every time?" "No," he said decidedly, but with a quaver. "I try to go to places where I have been before with dear friends and I live over again the associations I have had with them there and I feel their presence and their influence."

There was no more homesickness, not even after his departure. He left an uplifting influence from which 

I had no wish to get away.

Shortly after my return to Massachusetts the Bishop's private secretary, a friend of mine, called to me saying he had something to show me. It was a letter of 

Phillips Brooks written on the Sunday he spent in

Lucerne in which he said, "I had a strange experience today. I attended service in the Old Catholic church and heard from one of my own clergy an excellent sermon."
