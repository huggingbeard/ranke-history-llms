REPORT OF FINDINGS AND CONCLUSIONS OF THE UNITED STATES TARIFF COMMISSION IN INVESTIGATION NO. 58 UNDER SECTION 7 OF THE TRADE AGREEMENTS EXTENSION ACT OF 1951, RELATING TO BICYCLES Introduction United States Tariff Commission 

August 16, 1957

This report of the findings and conclusions of the Tariff Commission in connection with Investigation No. 58 under section 7 of the 

Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended, is made and published pursuant to section 7 (d) of that Act. The purpose of the investigation was to determine whether bicycles provided for in paragraph 371 of the Tariff Act of 1930 are, as a result in whole or in part of the duty or other customs treatment reflecting the concessions granted thereon in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, being imported into the United States in such increased quantities, either actual or relative, as to cause or threaten serious injury to the domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products. 

After investigation and report to the President of findings and recommendations of the Tariff Commission in accordance with section 7 (Investigation No. 37), the President issued a proclamation modifying the tariff concessions on bicycles originally granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (Proclamation 3108, dated August 18, 1955; 20 F.R. 6113). On January 11, 1957, the Bicycle Manufacturers

Association of America filed an application for a new investigation under section 7 with respect to bicycles, contending that the tariff increases imposed by Proclamation 3108 were not adequate to remedy the serious injury which the domestic bicycle industry has sustained.

The Commission instituted the instant investigation on January 28, 1957, and public notice thereof was given by posting a copy of the notice at the office of the Commission in Washington, D. C. and at its 

New York Office, by publication of the notice in the Federal Register (22 F.R. 683) and in the January 31, 1957 issue of Treasury Decisions.

A public hearing in connection with the investigation was subsequently ordered by the Commission and public notice thereof was given in the same manner (22 F.R. 872; February 14, 1957 issue of Treasury Decisions).

The hearing began on April 9, 1957 and was concluded on April 11, 1957, and all interested parties were given opportunity to be present, to produce evidence, and to be heard.

In addition to the information obtained at the hearing, the Commission in this investigation secured pertinent information from its files, through questionnaires submitted to producers and importers, and by field work of Commission experts assigned to the investigation. Finding On the basis of its investigation, including the hearing, the Tariff Commission finds that bicycles provided for in paragraph 371 of the Tariff Act of 1930 are not, as a result in whole or in part of the customs treatment reflecting the concession granted thereon under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, as modified by Presidential 

Proclamation No. 3108 of August 18, 1955, being imported into the

United States in such increased quantities, either actual or relative, as to cause or threaten serious injury to the domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products.

Considerations Bearing on the Foregoing Finding 

Description of products 

Generally bicycles are classified as lightweights, middleweights, or balloon-tire types. The width (cross-sectional diameter) of the tires, the shape of the frame, the style of saddle, and the diameter of the frame tubing are the characteristics that mainly determine the type. The weight of equipped lightweight bicycles having wheels, 26 inches in diameter is generally between 35 and 40 pounds, but some weigh more. 1/ Balloon-tire bicycles of comparable size usually weigh 

45 pounds or more, although there are substantial variations in the weight of individual models and of accessory equipment. Some middleweight models are somewhat lighter in weight than similar balloon-tire models, but some are heavier. However, the difference in weight between most similar middleweight and balloon-tire models is small. Although the balloon-tire models are usually the heaviest and lightweight models the lightest, there is a broad overlapping of weights among the three types. The three classification categories set forth above consequently reflect principally bicycles of different design rather than of distinctly different weights. 

Lightweight bicycles have narrow tires, usually not exceeding 1-5/8 inches in cross-sectional diameter, and narrow mud guards. The 

These weights refer to equipped lightweights. Without accessories, most of the imports of lightweight 26-inch bicycles enter at the lower duty for bicycles weighing less than 36 pounds.

lightweights designed for men have triangular frames of straight tubing. 

Most lightweights are equipped with variable-speed gears in the rear hubs rather than with single-speed drives, and have hand-operated caliper brakes rather than coaster brakes. In 1956 about 80 percent of domestically produced lightweights and 95 percent of those imported were so equipped. 

Balloon-tire bicycles have broader tires, usually 2-1/8 inches in cross-sectional diameter, some frame members of curved tubing, and broader and different-shaped saddles than are used on lightweights. Some balloon-tire bicycles have double tubes in the frame between the saddle post and the head post, and simulated gasoline tanks. Practically all balloon-tire bicycles are equipped with coaster brakes.

Modern middleweight bicycles were first produced in 1954, and by United States producers only--a product of American ingenuity. A major difference between the middleweight and the balloon-tire bicycle is in the diameter of the tires, the former having tires 1-3/4 inches in cross-sectional diameter, compared with about 2-1/8 inches for balloontire types. Also, the saddles on most middleweights more nearly resemble the saddles used on lightweights. Many middleweight models have what is termed a cantilever frame; the top tube, or tubes, of this type of frame extend in a continuous curved line from the head post to the rear axle. Most middleweights are also equipped with coaster brakes, but some are equipped with a variable-speed gear hub and some with a combination of variable-speed gear and coaster-brake hub. In 1955 about 23 percent of the domestic middleweights were equipped with variable-speed gear hubs, and in 1956 about 15 percent were so equipped.

There is no separate classification in official import statistics for middleweight bicycles, but on the basis of reports submitted direct to the Tariff Commission by importers who in the agregate accounted for 96 percent of total 1955 imports reported in official statistics and for 92 percent of the 1956 imports, there were no imports of middleweights in 1954, and only about 2,700 in 1955, but more than 113,000 in 1956. About 6 percent of the middleweight bicycles imported in 1956 were equipped with variable-speed gear hubs. Most of the middleweight bicycles imported in 1955 and 1956 were from Germany. 

Certain vehicles, commonly referred to as sidewalk bikes, pedal cycles, or park cycles, are dutiable as bicycles under paragraph 371 of the Tariff Act of 1930. Imports of such vehicles, if any, are negligible.

United States consumption of bicycles

The number of bicycles purchased in the United States is affected chiefly by two factors: First, the number of children of bicycle-riding age, and second, consumer purchasing power. After World War II there was a substantial pent-up demand for bicycles, and in 1947 consumption-which had been around 1.3 million annually in the late 1930's--rose to 

2.8 million. In 1948, consumption was close to the level of the preceding year, but it dropped to 1.5 million in 1949, when family purchasing power was curtailed by a recession. Since then consumption has shown a general upward trend, rising to a postwar peak of about 3.0 million bicycles in 1955 and declining to 2.9 million in 1956 (see table 3).

United States industry

There are at least 11 concerns that produce bicycles in the United States. The 9 producing members of the Bicycle Manufacturers Association accounted for slightly more than 90 percent of total domestic production in 1955 and 1956. 1/ Two of the association members are located in Illinois, two in Ohio, and one each in Arkansas, Indiana, Massachusetts, Michigan, and New York. Two producers, which are not association members, are located in New York.

All bicycle manufacturers except 4 engage in the manufacture of products other than bicycles, although some do so to a minor degree. 

Such products include children's wheel goods (other than bicycles, but including sidewalk bikes), power lawn mowers, electric fans, freight-car loading devices, service-station equipment, exercisers, and school furniture. For individual concerns that are members of the Bicycle Manufacturers Association the ratio of the value of bicycles and parts to the total value of shipments of all products in 1956 ranged from less than 10 percent to 100 percent; the 2 relatively small concerns that are not members of the Association produce only bicycles. 

1/ The 9 companies referred to include 1 company which was not a member of the association at the time the application was filed, but which rejoined the association after a 2-year lapse in membership.

The volume of work in most bicycle plants on products other than bicycles has increased in recent years as domestic producers have taken on new products to supplement the seasonal output of bicycles, to achieve greater product diversification, and to offset decreased bicycle production. 

Increased output of products other than bicycles also reflects the entry into the bicycle industry in 1954 of a concern that has a very much larger output of products other than bicycles in the plant producing bicycles. For the bicycle-producing plants owned by members of the Bicycle Manufacturers Association, bicycles and parts comprised 68 percent of the total value of all shipments in 1951, 54 percent in 1954, and 53 percent in 1956.

Most domestic bicycle producers manufacture the frames (but not the tubing from which the frames are made) and purchase some of the component parts used in their assembly of complete bicycles. Such parts include tires, variablespeed gear hubs, generator hubs, chain, saddles, spokes, and pedals. Several of the larger bicycle manufacturers produce such items as tubing for frames, as well as rims, handlebars, and sprockets; the smaller manufacturers generally purchase these components and parts. Most of the domestic concerns supplying bicycle parts are not themselves producers of bicycles. In recent years an increasing share of bicycle manufacturers' needs for certain parts have been supplied by imports 1/; imports of parts have more than doubled since 1952. 

Bicycle parts are classifiable for duty purposes under tariff paragraph 371. Imports of bicycle frames are dutiable at $1.25 each, but not less than 

15 percent nor more than 30 percent ad valorem. Other bicycle parts (not including tires) are dutiable at 30 percent ad valorem. These rates were bound against increase in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, effective Jan. 1, 1948.

Number of importing concerns and coverage.--There was an appreciable increase in 1955 and 1956 in the number of companies importing bicycles. 

Of the 38 importers filing questionnaires in connection with this investigation, 15 started importing bicycles after 1954, and of the latter, 8 started importing only in 1956. Beginning with the third quarter of 1956 and continuing with each succeeding quarter through March 1957, imports were smaller than in the comparable quarter of the preceding year. However, imports in the third quarter of 1956 were larger than in the corresponding quarter of 1954.

Reference to charts 1 and 2 and table 5 in the appendix indicates that a decline in imports in comparison with the corresponding month of the preceding year became evident in May 1956. 

Imports in each month from November 1956 through May 1957 were substantially smaller than in the corresponding months of the preceding year. Probably the major reason for the decline in imports in 1957 was that two large distributors discontinued, at least temporarily, their imports of balloon-tire and middleweight bicycles, and several other importers, influenced by this shift, substantially reduced their purchases from abroad. 1/

Another reason for the decrease in imports was a decline in the popularity of the lightweight bicycle, due in part to the difficulty and cost of upkeep. Furthermore, the shift in consumer preference and domestic production from the balloon-tire to the middleweight bicycle has meant that foreign suppliers of lightweight and balloon-tire bicycles have had to redesign their models to meet the current trend in the United States market. 

Although the changeover requires little retooling, it does necessitate adjustment of some component parts to the use of a middleweight tire. It is not clear at present, however, to what extent foreign producers will manufacture middleweight bicycles and seek to sell them in the United States, although it is known that some German and British manufacturers now produce such models and sell them in this market.

The changes in the proportions of sales to various outlets noted above are attributable to a number of factors. With the rising popularity of the light weight bicycles in the United States--most of which were imported--chain stores, department stores, and mail-order houses obtained an increasing proportion of their bicycles from abroad. Also, beginning in 1954, certain large retail distributors made arrangements to obtain substantial quantities of imported balloon-tire bicycles manufactured to their specifications, and such imports continued through 1956, With increasing proportion of the needs of large

retail distributors being supplied by imports, domestic producers relied to an increasing extent upon jobbers to distribute their products. 

On the basis of information brought out at the hearings and generally available in the trade, it is believed that substantial changes are now taking place with regard to the distribution pattern. For example, with some recent decline in the popularity of lightweights, the number of such bicycles imported and sold directly by large retail distributors has decreased. Furthermore, the decision of certain large retail distributors to shift their purchases of bicycles other than lightweights from foreign to domestic sources in 1957 will probably result in domestic producers selling a larger proportion of their bicycles to such distributors and a smaller proportion to jobbers. 

Pricing practices and terms Both domestic producers and importers issue standard price lists covering the bicycles and parts they sell, but there are so many departures from these price lists that the lists cannot be used as a basis for making direct comparisons of comparable domestic and imported bicycles. The principal concessions from the printed price lists are (1) negotiated contract prices to volume purchasers of "private brand" bicycles, (2) quantity discounts, (3) cut prices for "distress" or odd lots of bicycles, (4) reduction for shipments delivered f.o.b. dockside, (5) discounts for cash payment, (6) advertising allowances, and (7) future dating of invoices.

Even if the net delivered prices of a domestic and an imported bicycle with comparable specifications could be determined, there are other factors that the buyer ordinarily considers in addition to the price differential, such as (1) quality of workmanship, (2) style, design and finish, (3) dependability of supply, (4) availability of parts, and (5) the producer's name or country of origin, either as a positive or a negative factor. It is for some of the foregoing reasons that purchasers will pay more for one bicycle than another, and sometimes considerably more, though the specifications for the two bicycles may appear to be the same. Thus, although price is a factor, it is not necessarily the determining factor in the purchase of a bicycle.

From about one-third to possibly as much as one-half of all bicycles sold in the United States go to "private brand" accounts. Prices for private-brand bicycles are not on any price lists. They are sold on contracts negotiated in competition with other manufacturers, generally on an annual basis. Prices are determined in accordance with the specifications of the particular purchaser and his anticipated requirements. Purchasers of large quantities of private-brand bicycles make their specifications and anticipated requirements known to two or more manufacturers, domestic or foreign, in the effort to get the lowest price possible by competitive bidding.

United States purchasers of imported private-brand bicycles usually negotiate directly with foreign manufacturers, and also act as direct importers; occasionally, however, they purchase private-brand bicycles through United 

States importers.

Competitive conditions

Since 1954 two concomitant developments have affected the competition between domestic and imported bicycles. These are the declining popularity in the United States of the lightweight bicycle and a rapid rise in consumer preference for the middleweight bicycle. 

Although the lightweight bicycle represented more than 30 percent of domestic consumption in 1954, by 1956 its share had declined to less than 20 percent. The trade attributes the lightweight's loss in popularity partly to the relatively high cost of upkeep, a factor that did not become apparent for some time. In most foreign countries the lightweight bicycle is used predominantly by adults, and is usually adjusted and repaired by the owner; in the United States, where most bicycle riders are between 5 and 14 years old, adjustment and repair is usually done by bicycle shops, and the costs are relatively high. 

Another factor contributing to the decline in popularity of lightweights has been the importation (particularly from the European Continent) of lightweight bicycles that were lower in quality than those previously imported.

The middleweight bicycle contains many features, some new and some borrowed from both lightweight and balloon-tire types, that have 

Middleweights have tires that are smaller in. appealed to consumers. cross-sectional diameter than conventional balloon tires, but are larger in cross-sectional diameter than tires used on lightweights.

Thus middleweights have more comfortable riding qualities than lightweights, yet are easier to pedal than the balloon-tire types because the tires have less rolling resistance. Middleweights are available with coaster brakes, with multispeed gears and hand brakes, or with combination multispeed gears and coaster brakes. Thus when middleweights are equipped with the multispeed gear, they have the gadget appeal of the similarly equipped lightweights. The new shape and appearance of the so-called cantilever frame of some middleweights is also an important factor in consumer appeal. Although only 4 percent of the bicycles produced domestically in 1954 were middleweights, by 1956 the proportion had risen to 55 percent (see table 7), and in 1957 it apparently will be substantially higher. 

At present the domestic industry is engaged in large-scale production of middleweight bicycles for the United States market. 

Foreign production of such bicycles is also primarily for this market. 

Hence, in the production of middleweights, foreign producers do not have the advantage they enjoy in the manufacture of lightweights, namely, large-scale production of a kind of bicycle that is in general use throughout the world.

As noted previously in this report, the imports of small-size bicycles (20and 24-inch) have shown a large expansion. Although imports of all bicycles declined moderately from 1955 to 1956, most of the decline occurred in imports of lightweight bicycles with 26-inch wheels. On the other hand, sales of imported bicycles with 20-inch wheels increased by 18 percent, and sales of those with 24-inch wheels increased by 45 percent. Thus, while importers supplied about 36 percent of the entire United States market for bicycles in 1956, they supplied 43 percent of all 20-inch bicycles sold. Despite the large increase in sales of imported 24-inch bicycles in 1956, the imports in that year supplied but 28 percent of the domestic market for bicycles with this size of wheel.

The relocation of 3 domestic companies will undoubtedly affect competition within the domestic industry as well as competition with imports. Thus the domestic industry is undergoing significant structural changes that will undoubtedly affect its competitive position.

Also the competitive position of the domestic industry has been improved by the increased duties that became effective August 19, 1955. The increases in the duties were largest for the kinds of bicycles produced in greatest volume in the United States--balloontire bicycles and middleweights--and smallest for the lightweight bicycles, which have never been produced in this country in large quantities. The full effect of the higher duties has not been immediately apparent for a number of reasons. The chief reason was that

a substantial part of the imports continued to be delivered under relatively long-term contracts for the accounts of large distributors (mail-order, chain, and department stores). However, several of the larger domestic mail-order and chain-store purchasers were either terminating their contracts with foreign producers or curtailing their deliveries of imported bicycles and they had placed firm orders with domestic manufacturers. It must be presumed that the increased cost of the imported bicycles resulting from the increased duties had some influence on this decision for a change in purchasing policy, although it apparently was not the only or perhaps the main reason.

Apart from contributing to the increase in landed cost of imported bicycles, the increased duties may also have contributed indirectly to other developments that aided the competitive position of domestic producers. Foreign manufacturers doubtless sought means to reduce costs in the hope of being able to lower their f.o.b. factory price and thus to absorb at least part of the increased duty. It cannot be determined whether this accounted for the deterioration in quality of imports--particularly of balloon-tire bicycles--reported by the large domestic distributors. To an increasing extent, however, it is clear that the quality of imports has not measured up to the standards of the largest domestic distributors, which have now turned chiefly to the domestic industry for middleweight and balloon-tire bicycles.

Conclusions 

The Commission concludes that there is no sufficient reason at this time for a recommendation to the President under the provisions of section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 for the imposition of increased import restrictions on bicycles. This conclusion is based upon an extremely fluid competitive situation which has been in evidence since the modification of the original concessions, and from which positive trends are not clearly perceptible. If future developments should indicate a definite change in the competitive situation in the direction of substantially increased import competition, the Commission would not consider the decision in this case as prejudicial to a new application for an investigation. 

The Commission's ultimate finding in this case is not to be taken as a determination that the current tariff treatment is no longer necessary to remedy the serious injury which the Commission found to exist in its 1955 report to the President. Concurrently with the issuance of this report, the Commission is reporting to the President under paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401 (3 CFR, 1952 Supp., p. 105) and is advising the President, in effect, that there is at this time no reason to believe that a restoration, in whole or in part, of the original concession on bicycles is warranted. 4.
