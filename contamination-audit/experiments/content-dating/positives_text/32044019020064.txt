MUSKETEERS OF THE MOUNTAINS BY ONE OF THE M 

HEY were three, they met every summer, year after year, on the ****** same mountainous island, they were all enthusiastic climbers, and they fell into the habit of climbing together. 

No wonder, then, that they became devotedly attached, and no wonder that they dubbed themselves "The Three 

Musketeers." Indeed, one of them, Athos, {7}

had long been accustomed to call himself "The Old Man of the Mountain," although he was, in fact, the youngest of the three. He, in the winter time, was a lawyer, while Porthos and Aramis were teachers. Not in sanguinary temper did they call themselves "Musketeers," but only in identity of interest and in close companionship. They were no more addicted to firearms than Taffy, the Laird, and Little Billee. No gunners they, no golfers, and no tennis-players. They agreed in their contempt for people who come to scenes of transcendant beauty, only to indulge in the same old sports they cultivate in their prosaic homes. The sight of a squad of golfers in the midst of sea-girt mountains was a violent irritant for all three. The expression "sea-girt" {8}

raises the question whether the Musketeers were as susceptible to the charms of ocean as to the fascination of high lands. 

No. Although Porthos and Aramis had both been in the past devoted sailors, and Aramis still indulged in rowboat and canoe, they all three now acknowledged fealty only to the mountain. And none of them cared for tramping along a flat road. 

The hills had to come to them, led by an automobile, although none of them had any love for the automobile in itself. In earlier days, before they met the opulent Athos, Porthos and Aramis had been in the habit of achieving on foot the prelude and the sequel to their ascents and descents, with considerable fatigue and on Porthos's part -horror of toil. Curiously enough, Athos was very much given 19}

to invective against luxury and fashion; 

Porthos and Aramis, less exposed to such blight, had less fault to find with it.

It may be conjectured that Porthos and Aramis had been companions before they were joined by Athos, and such was the case. They had been very old and very close friends, by land and sea. Aramis, lively and restless, showed as yet no indication of the episcopal temper; he was always ready for any adventure by oar, bicycle, or foot often to the consternation of the big and powerful Porthos. The latter yielded to none in his devotion to climbing in the abstract, and he always looked back on his climbs with delight, when they were over. All the winter long, he would contemplate rapturously a summer of mountaineering either in the past 

10

or in the future. But when it came to setting out on any particular ascent, especially a steep one, he was very likely to think of some reason why the expedition should be postponed to some other day.

It was too hot, or it looked like rain or fog. 

In truth, although he never would admit it, he was pretty heavy for strenuous climbing; and for that very reason he deserves all credit for such vigorous climbing as he did. Unjust, then, was Aramis's nickname for him: "Porthos the Alibite."

Was it his fault that the weatherwise whom he consulted invariably foretold rain? Or that the barometer invariably clung at "wet"? For he always consulted the same experts and the same barometer.

Aramis, always impatient to be on the road, was quite ill-natured about it. De- 

II

spite many disappointments, however, many were the expeditions accomplished together. A happy idea, first suggested by 

Porthos, was a change of foot-wear: a pair of sneakers on the trail, with, in the knapsack, a pair of shoes for the road. How gay were the moments of rest when footgear was changed, faces and hands washed in the brook, and cravats resumed! How happily, weary though they might be, they would stroll into the metropolis, seat themselves in the familiar café for refreshment with ice cream and ginger ale, to be followed by a substantial meal at a cheap restaurant! Then, back to the west end of the island by steamer or by stage. One day, waiting for George's stage on the wharf, the two climbers had lighted two long and superfine cigars, when they saw {12}

two ladies mounting the vehicle ahead of them. Without hesitation our heroes, before entering, sacrificed their half-smoked weeds, dropping them into the sea through a crack in the wharf. What, then, was their horror, as soon as the stage started, to see the two ladies coolly pull out packages of cigarettes and begin to smoke!

Other discomfitures fell to their lot. After a peculiarly hard climb, on one occasion, they had reached the summit, congratulating themselves on their prowess, when they were taken aback by the spectacle of two other successful climbers approaching them. With redoubled mortification they recognized their strange competitors as women; and, to cap the climax, the older of the two was on crutches. The younger was her niece, escorted by her elderly aunt 13

to high places to view the landscape. 

Fortunately for our musketeers' selfesteem, they learned that the gentler athletes had ascended by a less difficult trail than the one our proud Tartarins had taken. But the crippled lady was manifestly a good deal older than either of the male adventurers. So the latter avoided mention of the incident when they reached home. The same mountain-top witnessed another painful moment. Porthos and 

Aramis, having reached the summit hot and thirsty, and knowing that no spring was to be found there, sought eagerly for a pool of rain-water. For rain-water, if fresh and fairly cool, is good enough to suit anyone. After an extended and agonized quest, they spied, not far off, in the hollow of a rock, just such a supply as {14}

they desired; but, as they drew near, what should they see, to their horror, but another hot climber (a male one, this time) bathing his feet in it! A very cheery and affable man he appeared, but by the two disappointed water-seekers he was deemed fit for no congratulatory greeting. On the road, of course, the two pedestrians gladly accepted a lift. Once they sprang on the tail end of a truck filled with great empty cans. In the bumping and rattling, they could not seat themselves without danger of falling; so there they stood, for several miles, clinging to the tottering cans, while the vehicle carried them straight through the fashionable town, even down to the wharf, where the vehicle came to a sudden halt, enabling them to jump from their uneasy perch. {15}

Athos the two companions, appropriately enough, first met on a mountain, and, after that, the twain were always three. Or, rather, four; for Athos had a beautiful black dog, named Sambo, who was just as eager to climb as his master, and a better Alpinist then any of them. 

An intelligent animal, too, very affectionate and very picturesque, fond of posing on the edge of a cliff, where he could be viewed to advantage from below. He appeared to be cut out for a companion, and suggested the name and personality of d'Artagnan. He would have been added to the fellowship, under that appellation, had not a formidable competitor come into the field. This was a comparatively young mountaineer, an abler pedestrian than Athos, Porthos, or Aramis, and as 16}

enthusiastic as any of them; endlessly obliging, too, ready for any enterprise, and an excellent automobilist. Many were the debates over the question whether he or Sambo should be elected into the fraternity as d'Artagnan. Finally, Sambo lost his chance by straying away on a mountain, causing for a while much trouble and anxiety, and the newcomer was recognized as the fourth of the nominally Three Musketeers. But Sambo always kept his place as a companion. He knew the honk of d'Artagnan's machine, and his joyous bark could be heard away down the road, as d'Artagnan was conveying Porthos and Aramis to the house of Athos on a high hill, in preparation for an expedition. As the car approached, Sambo would leap out of doors and, in his ( 17 )

delight, jump all over the companions as they alighted. To have left him behind would have broken their hearts as well as his. Four men and a dog the Three

Musketeers soon became. He never begrudged the new companion his usurpation of the title of d'Artagnan. One and inseparable was the union of the merry five. "We are the Musketeers, the Musketeers, the Musketeers!" became their club song. Sambo did not join in the words, because, although he could understand almost everything that was said to him or about him, he could not speak. 

When, on a mountain-top, the others were eating luncheon and he was longing to partake, he never raised his voice nor tried to snatch; he would simply lay his paw gently on some one's knee and gaze ap- 18

pealingly. D'Artagnan, in addition to his other accomplishments, had the advantage of being an admirable photographer; thus were the scenes of many achievements deathlessly recorded; would that this pose of Sambo had been in his collection, as it is pictured in four memories! 

And all four were sure that their speechless mate joined in the spirit of their song. 

That he shared in the spirit of their labor, no one who saw him could doubt for a moment. 

Just what did climbing mean for 

Sambo? That he shared, intensely, in the enjoyment was obvious; but his enjoyment could not have been identical with that of the human Alpinists. His perceptions were altogether different. He could hear about as well as they; his sight, ( 19 )

though inferior to theirs, was adequate; but his principal organ was the sense of smell. He learned to know very well all the trails. When he recognized a trail, however, he must have done so mainly with his nose. When he strayed from the path, he returned to it, not by visual, but by odorific, recognition. While he was wandering, his excited bark, from time to time, would show that he was undergoing emotions to which his master was a stranger. His cry would indicate the scent of squirrel or rabbit or deer. Even deer: for he actually ran down a number of them, without ever catching any. The only wild animal he ever really caught was a skunk, and that experience was surely not an exhilarating one. A wild life his nose led him, on those delicious {20}

Alpine excursions. He must have enjoyed the exercise, the sheer delight of muscular power, as the Musketeers did; but his greatest enjoyment, I am sure, was the continual suggestion of game and pursuit. Little appealed to him the glory of sky and sea, the romance of rugged rock, the deep peacefulness of the forest.

Brook or lake were not so much pictorial satisfactions as water supplies; for, though not so thirsty an animal as most beasts, he felt the need of an occasional drink, and he was sometimes glad of a cool bath.

While the Musketeers, in comparison with the dog, surely took their recreation in the same way, there were differences of reaction even among them. D'Artagnan especially rejoiced in the pictures that could be visualized as copied by the {21}

camera; Athos reveled in the perfection of trails; Porthos loved the rest at the top of a climb, extended on a rock, with a pipe in his mouth; Aramis craved the continuous sense of motion. Not one of them shared the philosophy of Sambo, which counseled a nap at every pause, even the briefest. The luncheon in mid-career appealed to all. For nearly all their expeditions were all-day journeys: up, up, up, all the morning, through forest and over rocks; rest, food, and a smoke on the glorious free summit; down, down, down, over rocks and through woods, to the waiting car. Much happiness lingered even in the homeward drive, while the muscles still felt the pleasurable ache of successful exertion and the memory was still full of the beauties witnessed. Often a halt ( 22 )

would be made at the house of Athos, which, on its moderate summit, commanded a view hard to surpass on the higher peaks. 

What outlooks had gladdened their many ascents! There was one bare summit where they sat on the brink of a precipice some six hundred feet high, from which they looked out on blue sky and blue sea, and, between them, an incredibly indented coast-line, all points and inlets, while behind them towered, rugged and serene, the highest mountain on the isle.

There was a narrow gorge on the further side of this lofty mount, where they could climb beside a brook, between two steep walls, over a trail that was little more than a long series of high steps. Following the gorge over the neck between two peaks, 23

they could descend gently, beside another brook, over broken rock, into the forest, to emerge eventually into the road. There was a long ridge, over which, once the woods left behind, they could mount easily, in the bracing air, over several miles of bare highland, with views of sea and rock constantly changing and always beautiful, to the summit which at the start had seemed impossibly distant. On another part of the island they could follow a lovely woodland path to the bottom of a long trail, which led them through primeval forest to open land, revealing outlooks to the west and north, invisible from other heights, then plunging into woods again, to pursue its course through a dark, fantastic, scrubby vale, and up again to the main peak, crowned with a {24}

rustic summer-house, just the spot for eating and smoking and conversing. Yes, conversing: for discourse never failed, on nature, on philosophy, on mankind. Most judicial in his judgments was Athos; but 

Porthos, too, was full of sage opinions; and Aramis, less eloquent, listened and profited. D'Artagnan did not fail to contribute his share, especially when discussion turned on music or art. For they were men of many interests, although they seldom disagreed, and every one was concerned with the utterances of his comrades. Sambo, meanwhile, showed his philosophy by rest and sleep. There was one sharp, narrow ridge by which they loved to descend; for, aside from its dramatically and picturesquely steep decline, it afforded a look upon the great wooded { 25 }

theatre that lay between two peaks. 

While admiring the ever-changing landscape, the Musketeers gave close attention to the preservation of guiding cairns and sign-boards and wooden arrows. The trails they followed had been made, in large measure, by themselves, and were naturally objects of affectionate care. 

There was one path, planned by Athos and by him affectionately named for Aramis, which took its start from a lesser peak, and, after a steep dip into a high valley, surmounted a narrow ridge to go down again into another vale traversed by a brook; then, after following the woodland brook up almost to its source, turned to the right, and climbed steeply but easily up, over soft forest footing, to the bare summit of the second mountain of 26)

the isle -a summit so quickly reached that the climber could scarcely believe he was really at the top. All were proud of this trail, especially Aramis, whose name it bore. After all, it was perhaps he that had first discovered this route, finding his way through the wilderness, though by a much rougher ascent and descent than those now made available by Athos. Far away from this spot was the top of the familiar precipitous cliff, overhanging the lake and reached by a roundabout route through the woods. How attractive these woods were, before they had been disfigured in the lower part, a few years ago, by a huge cutting, and even now beautiful and wild! At the summit, one was astonished to see no stones except tiny pebbles. This was because, the lake lying {27}

apparently at the very foot of the rock, visitors had always been tempted to throw a stone into the water a hope never fulfilled but ever renewed, for it was impossible to believe that so broad a rim of forest intervened at the base of the cliff. 

Over in the north of the island, seldom visited and accessible by no trail, were two peaks repeatedly scaled by Parthos and Aramis. One could reach them, if one did not lose the way, by a tangle of old logging-roads. The more exciting method, however, was to push up the more northerly of these moderate but wild heights through the woods on the north, and, after enjoying the view from its top, bared by an old fire, to push on through thick and scratchy spruce, steering by the sun, through a bushy valley and then on 28

to the more southerly one, near the summit of which a projecting ledge afforded a wide-open view to the west and the north and, to the southward, the whole main range, seen from the opposite side from the one usually beheld, with the sea strangely appearing through the notches. 

Athos, disliking the scrubby scramble through the bush, had never cared to attempt this trip, always preferring the familiar trails over the loftier summits.

D'Artagnan, too, was a stranger, to these inferior twin peaks. Often, however, he, with the others, had scaled a peculiarly picturesque central mountain, on the upper part of which the rock had built two great natural theatres, while, at the east, a projecting narrow ridge formed a curious little causeway which they called {29}

the "cock's comb," visible from afar and distinguishing this peak from all others.

This mountain still bore its Indian name, which was originally the native designation of the whole island, renamed by 

French explorers in the early 17th century.

Exhilaratingly beautiful all these scenes were, but most beautiful, after all, was the companionship of the Musketeers, all fired by one desire, all loving the same wild landscapes, all enjoying the same delight of achievement, the same relaxation at the end of an arduous climb, the same recollections carried through the year. 

For the little group never fell apart, and, however separated by distance and diverse occupations, always remained, in the thought of every member, the Three 130

Musketeers, and so would remain, even after age should bring to a close their ac tive Musketeering.
