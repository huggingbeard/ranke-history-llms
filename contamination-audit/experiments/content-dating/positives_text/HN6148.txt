BEFORE YOU READ ANY FURTHER 

HERE IS an invitation to you to do your own free, independent, creative thinking in religion, to work out your own philosophy of life. 

This booklet is not intended for those who are satisfied and happy in their religious faith. 

It has no intention whatever of turning anybody from one church loyalty to another. It does not even try to win its readers to a certain doctrine or "discovery" or theory.

Neither is it intended for those who are satisfied and happy without any interest in religion.

It is addressed only to those who are unsatisfied religiously, whether they are inside or outside the churches; to those who may be indifferent, even antagonistic, to that which commonly passes for "religion," but who feel the lack of the stability and the unity and the dynamic that conventional religion gives to the lives of others. It assumes on the part of its readers an acceptance of the fundamental concepts of modern knowledge.

The materials have been gathered from many sources; in this sense no one writer is the author of the booklet. It is an attempt to focus

some of the best thinking of the time on your spiritual problems. To what is offered here we append a list of books that will help you to explore much further into this thinking. 

Your explorations may be arduous; we are not offering a personally conducted tour through the short cut of any "ism," cult or "school of thought." But it will be an adventure worth the making, for whatever philosophic or religious treasure you win will be yours to serve you in your daily adventure of creative living.

FINDING A FAITH TO LIVE BY ARE YOU READY TO GO? 

ARE YOU unsatisfied religiously? 

Are you unhappy in your present church because you have ceased to believe in some of its doctrines? 

Or, are you out of touch with the churches entirely because you feel they have little or nothing to meet your needs? As a student in the midst of new knowledge or as a man in the midst of life's problems, are you "off" organized religion because it attempts to straddle old and new ideas, because it does not answer your honest questions about life, because it does not seem to measure up to the problems of today? 

Do you feel that, since you can no longer accept many historic doctrines of Christianity, there is nothing left of religion for you? Does much of modern knowledge seem to rob men of faith and hope and idealism? 

Or have you gone further? Considering the misery and degradation in which millions are living, the throwback to barbarism in the [7]

-- World War, the emphasis on the animal origin and nature of man, the immensity of newly discovered universes, considering all these things, have you wondered whether man was only a biological accident and whether his idealism was only a delusion cherished by those who had shut their eyes to the facts of life? Perhaps you, too, may look upon religion as an "opiate for the people."

Perhaps, however, the church has, almost imperceptibly, been crowded out by your work, by your family responsibilities, by your social, cultural, political and community interests. You may have no particular indictment against the church, but you simply do not seem to have any particular incentive to make a definite place for it in your life.

It may be, indeed, that in your present environment you should stay away from the churches for the sake of your own integrity.

At any rate, your situation is increasingly that of thousands of intelligent, high-minded men and women everywhere.

We are assuming that you are, however, unsatisfied in this negative situation and are seeking something better. Your work and your family and your friends, your social and cultural interests these alone do not satisfy. 

Something is needed to gather all these up into a unified, purposeful, dynamic outlook on life. [8]

For many men and women today, the timehonored religious certainties do not give them this outlook. They have abandoned old ideas without getting new ones to take their place. It is easier to drift out, or even to think oneself out, of an old philosophy than to think oneself into a new one.

A great number of people have tried short cuts.

Some, finding that they did not have the absolute knowledge of certain great mysteries they believed they had, say, "I don't know," and label themselves "agnostic." This is an honest and honorable course, provided one does not refuse to explore further and does not ignore those spiritual truths that are known. Or, seeing no first-hand evidence of an almighty, allwise and loving Providence, others assert, "There is no God," and settle down into dogmatic atheism. Still others escape into various cults, which, with their ready-made mysticisms, their pat formulas for health, success and happiness, are in spirit just so many more dogmatic creeds-in spite of their show of "modern" or "liberal" thought. They are pernicious, moreover, in attempting to avoid patient, thorough exploration into the facts of life, and patient, thorough planning for the conduct of life, both individual and social. [9]

You are probably not one of the thousands who have been deceived by these short-cuts. Not even our radicals, the intelligent, constructive radicals, are found in such company. 

We assume that you are seeking, more or less consciously, for religious satisfaction, for a working philosophy of life. 

We are using the terms "philosophy of life" and "religion" interchangeably. As we see it, your philosophy gives reasoned purpose and direction to that devotion and dynamic which is religion, and your religion gives vitality and working power to your philosophy. Both of these are the goals of our discussion. 

We assume, too, that you are willing to do your own thinking to achieve your life philosophy and your religion, and are willing to make a real effort to put them to work. We assume, finally, that you accept the fundamental findings of modern science. 

Now, however much or little you may have departed from orthodox ideas, we believe that it is unwise to begin your search by trying to determine what you believe and what you don't believe about God, Jesus, the Bible, salvation, immortality and other doctrinal concerns. 

That is a long and bewildering process, even though it is the route by which most people have found their way to an enlightened faith. [ 10 ]

It is better to begin at the beginning. Forget all about theologies and the Bible and the church, the cults and the "isms." Lay aside as much as possible your inherited ideas, preconceived notions and unverified assumptions. 

Consult only your first-hand experience and observation and the undisputed, universally known facts of human history. Begin with a few fundamental facts about the nature of human life and the universe, and then "go on with the story." 

You may, or may not, in your explorations, re-arrive at some of the ideas you now hold, but, if you do, they will be your ideas in a sense they never were before. [11]

BEGINNING WITH YOURSELF 

E ARE going to suggest two of these fundamental facts and briefly outline a way of going on from them. If our presentation does not satisfy you, but only stimulates you to gather your own facts and to reason from them, we will consider the time well spent. 

Why not begin with yourself, "where you live," as it were?

Your life, as it is actually lived from day to day, is more or less concerned with the satisfaction of certain basic desires. 

You want security, not only economic security to insure for you and your dependents the necessaries, comforts and refinements of life, but security also from danger, disease, untimely death and any downward turn of your fortunes. 

You want companionship, friendship, love; the experience of home and other group life; you crave response from friends, loved ones and associates. 

However modest you are, you desire some recognition, for what you are, for what you achieve; you expect a certain respect for your personality from everybody; you value the good opinion of your world. [ 12 ]

You want interesting and pleasant experience, usually new experience and the experience of achievement or adventure; you want to find out things, to explore, to carry out projects, to have power and to exercise it. 

All this is obvious and may seem to have little to do with religion. Indeed, religion too often has ignored these fundamental needs or at least has seemed remote from them; whereas religion should be concerned with all the needs of man. Also, the higher or ideal life, with which religion primarily deals, is intimately related to these basic desires. 

For most of us, it is hard to achieve a wholesome religious faith if these desires are not reasonably satisfied, or if some of them, or some phases of them, are not so abundantly satisfied as to compensate for those that are not. 

Perhaps your unrest may be due to this as well as to your lack of a religious philosophy. It may be that you should seek to achieve a more balanced life in this respect by endeavoring to meet those needs that go more or less unsatisfied, or to compensate yourself in some way for particular desires that must go unsatisfied. If circumstances, for example, prevent people from making a home of their own, they sometimes make homes for those who are homeless or otherwise underprivileged. If recognition is lacking for a worthy idea or act or project, [ 13 ]

compensation must come in satisfaction over the merit itself. Many have had to stand alone in this way. Cultivating a hobby often compensates for lack of creative achievement in everyday work.

It is well, too, to consider these basic needs, in order to see how large a place in your life their satisfaction takes, to see, also, how such satisfactions-the joys of friendship and of home and of achievement in the day's work, for example, serve on the higher levels of life. 

FOR, of course, life is infinitely more than all these things. The people whom you love and admire are not those who are solely concerned with the satisfaction of these wants. Certainly the men and women who have brightened the pages of history with their achievements in nobility and righteousness and beauty have either seen far beyond their own satisfactions, or have realized deep satisfactions through a high level of desire and attainment. At the same time, there are intricate relationships between the highest idealisms of man and his basic needs and struggles,-relationships so intricate that we can do no more than merely suggest them. 

For example:

The right sort of men desire security for others as well as for themselves. With some people, this is enlightened self-interest, an ac- [ 14 ]

knowledgment that we are closely bound together in our fortunes. Others, however, animated by the friendliness and good will related to the second fundamental desire, seem naturally and spontaneously to desire the good of others. They are generous, fair, helpful, selfsacrificing. Out of these traits and out of social wisdom grow the passion for thoroughgoing justice in all relationships of life. This may show itself in a lifetime of endeavors to defend the rights of the underprivileged or a lifetime of patient research to discover ways and means by which greater justice may be made to prevail among men.

If we desire friends, we must be friendly; if we wish people to like us, we must be likable; if we desire love, there must be something about us to love. So there are the everyday qualities of friendliness, good fellowship, of love between man and woman and parents and children. These expand into widening and higher circles of good will, revealing themselves in acts of mercy and peace and helpfulness, and embracing the reasoned passion for justice just described, until at the summit we have the supreme souls who were dominated utterly by a love of their fellowmen, and who, to serve humanity, suffered privations, hardships, misunderstandings and death itself.

Then, too, if we desire recognition of our personality, we must respect the personality of [ 15 ]

others. If we wish appreciation for our qualities and achievements, we must appreciate the virtues and attainments of others. To do this, we must understand others, their ideas and ideals, their handicaps, their situations. So there come about mutual respect and understanding and courtesy among better-minded and better-tempered persons, who may not be bound by ties of friendship or love. Such respect and understanding and courtesy are closely akin to the good will just mentioned. In higher kinship to them is a recognition of the potential value and dignity of human nature. 

This understanding and this recognition are necessary elements in that passion for justice, which, as we have seen, is related to the first two basic desires. Also, the person of insight and wisdom will give due recognition to the superior man or woman, to the intellectual and social and spiritual leaders of mankind, to the supreme personalities of history. That is, he will yield homage to these men and women in so far as they were, or are, dominated by certain high qualities and ideals: he will not blindly worship them nor blindly imitate them nor blindly accept their ideas. He will reverence the highest and the best he knows wherever and in whomever he finds it. Here we have suggestions of the loyalty, the worship and the adoration that have distinguished the higher life of mankind through the ages. [ 16 ]

We desire interesting experience, creative adventure, achievement. Above and beyond the achievement in routine labors, beyond our simple pleasure-seeking, a good golf score or a stiff game of tennis, or a sparkling comedy, beyond the work-a-day researches into data and methods, lies a vast realm of experience and adventure and achievement and creativity. The desire to do things, to explore, to investigate, to create, joined with one or more of the higher qualities just described and functioning on the higher levels, has given us most of the things of chief value to mankind. It has given us the discoveries of science and the marvelous labor-saving inventions; it has blossomed into great art, great literature, great music; it has wrought political and social and religious reforms; it has given us prophets and saints and sages; it has banished plagues from the face of the earth; it is liberating men's minds for self-determination and self-discipline; it is working to create a new and a better world. we

THUS We have, all intricately knit into the pattern of life, the basic desires and satisfactions, and the higher values and experiences. 

Among the latter, some of the most important and universal values are the search for truth, the passion for justice, the love of beauty, the urges to self-sovereignty, to high adventure [17]

and creativity; the qualities of generosity, helpfulness, sincerity, good will, love, reverence, loyalty and heroism.

We are not attempting at this point to account for the existence of these values. We are not saying now whether they have simply developed through race experience from basic desires and satisfactions, or whether, primarily, they are derived from some vast divine life in which mankind is embraced. That question comes up a bit later. Just now we are emphasizing that any worth-while life philosophy must take account of all these levels of experience. 

Hence any religion worth the holding insists that men and women shall have a living and shall gain rightful satisfaction of their basic wants. But it also insists that people shall respect each other's right to the same good things of life, and, in striving to achieve them, shall deal justly and generously with each other. 

When this has not been done, when this striving has been predominantly competitive rather than cooperative, there have been greed and hate and injustice, oppression and exploitation and slavery and war. 

Thus, the disinterested virtues—justice, generosity, good will-temper and transcend the struggle for goods and power and achievement. They are the best guarantee that earth's multitudes will learn to attain their desires [ 18 1

through cooperative quest. That is one reason why religion concentrates its attention on these virtues.

But religion stresses these, and other higher values, also because they represent the highest quality of experience men and women can attain. They make for the deepest satisfactions and the highest joys. They enlighten, liberate and elevate humanity; they engender happy and harmonious relations among the peoples of the earth. They describe the highest and best that mankind has achieved; they point to the highest and best of which you are capable; and they foreshadow the highest and best that mankind may attain in the ages to come.

Hereafter, then, our discussion of religion will be based on these values and the persistent urge among men to achieve them. They constitute the first fact with which you can begin your quest for a philosophy of life. 

They are realities of human experience. 

They are in visible operation in those people right around you who are friendly, honorable, and just; in men who are passionate for the wellbeing of their fellows; in people who are appreciative of the beautiful in nature and in art; in those who are keen to lead in a worth while undertaking and to join in a good cause.

They are revealed in the lives of the great and the good and the wise, whose achievements in [ 19 ]

one or more of these values are undisputed facts of human history. They come to light when you are living at your best, when at times you surpass yourself in some act of generosity or self-sacrifice or some worth while creative achievement. [ 20 ]

THE FIELD OF FACT 

THE URGE among men to achieve these values of truth, goodness and beauty, this striving toward what is felt to be the highest and best, has persisted through centuries of bloody struggle for survival and goods and power. 

Concepts of these values and ways of attaining them have changed, but for ages there has persisted this desire for the highest and best within the compass of man's knowledge and man's dreams.

Whether literally all persons have this desire may be debated, but it does not greatly concern us here. To be sure, there are multitudes of lives that are predominantly gross and sordid. 

How much this is due to ignorance of something better is a question that goes back at least to the days of Socrates. But even among such people there are probably few who do not cherish an ideal in some department of their experience, however feebly they may strive toward it. 

Hence, the urge, the desire, the striving, in various degrees of intensity, can be said to be fairly universal. 

No one, of course, attains the best in all departments of his experience. Indeed, sometimes rightly, sometimes wrongly, one value is pursued at the expense of other values. Darwin, spending his life in seeking the truth about [ 21 ]

evolution, lost much of his appreciation of beauty as expressed in music. Even with the utmost allowances, some artistic geniuses have been morally corrupt. Sometimes, too, the exercise of a value covers only certain phases of experience. A man may be a just and loving father, but a ruthless and crooked politician. Or he may be a champion of the rights of mankind at large and treat his employees unfairly.

We say that this striving has been toward what is felt to be the highest and best, because sometimes the object of honest but mistaken striving has not been the "best," even for that time and that place. But this urge and this striving through age after age has eventuated in certain values that are universally recognized as the highest and best in human experience. 

Therefore, let us state our first fundamental undisputed fact as follows:

There persists more or less universally among men and women a desire for and an urge toward what they consider to be the highest and the best in various departments of their experience.

Now, every religion not only asks questions about the nature of man but also about his place in the universe. However life came and however man came to be, it is obvious, in the light [ 22 ]

of modern knowledge, that he is a natural product of the creative process as manifested on this planet. He is not an alien or a stranger here. 

He is, in a sense, a child of the earth on which he lives. Whatever he is, and all that he is, is part and parcel of the natural order of the universe.

This statement does not necessarily leave God out of the creative process. But it does deny that man is a special creation of a supernatural God, that is, a God who is above nature in that he may suspend the working of a natural law to achieve his will, whether that will be for creation or damnation. On the contrary, a widespread view among people who accept the findings of modern science is that natural laws and the natural order, including man, are expressions of the omnipresent, intelligent creative power which they call God.

We are not here, however, concerned with the ultimate character of the creative process. 

Hence, we thus state our second fundamental undisputed fact:

Man is a natural product of the creative process of the universe. [ 23 ]

How significant these two facts are for your quest may be sensed by combining them into one:

Human life, with its persistent desire for, and urge toward, what it considers to be the highest and the best, with all the values it has achieved, is a natural product of the creative process of the universe. 

It follows from this that the universe contains the materials, conditions and forces necessary to the cultivation of values. With all its inhospitable circumstances, our corner in the cosmos sustains our endeavors to live the good life. If this were not so, the lives of good and wise men could not have been. Man, in his efforts to achieve joy and satisfaction, has indeed met opposition from many elements of his environment, earthquakes and storms and disease. He has had to struggle with his animal inheritance. He has had to battle his way to the place he now holds. But he is a product of the natural order, and this order has on the whole sustained him and his endeavors.

THESE TWO FACTS-man's idealism, his strivings toward the highest and the best, and his sonship in the universe, are the visible and certain beginning for your quest of a philosophy of life. They are undisputed facts, outside the realm of controversy. They may be stated in [ 24 ]

different phrasings, from differing viewpoints; they may be explained in various ways, but they cannot be explained away. They do not answer all your questions or solve all your problems; but they provide, in our opinion, the best available beginning for the best available answers. 

Neither do they ignore another fact, that man with all the evil he has wrought is likewise a "natural product of the creative process." 

They do not ignore the evils in nature and in man,-disease and untimely death and physical disaster; hate, greed, bloodshed, oppression and injustice; lying, dishonesty, hypocrisy, treachery. These are also realities of human experience. 

The "problem of evil," however, can best be treated later. Right here, though, we may observe that we cannot altogether rightly restate our first fact in terms of evil. There does not persist more or less universally among men and women a desire for and an urge toward what they consider to be the lowest and the worst in various departments of their experience. In other words, men do not fashion ideals of error, unrighteousness and ugliness, but they do cherish ideals of truth, goodness and beauty. 

And when they do cherish these ideals and battle without ceasing against all the evils that thwart the attainment of ideals, they are religious men and heroes in the highest sense of the word. And the nature of things seems to be [ 25 ]

such that the people who have thus matched their heroism against the evils within and around man have found the deepest joy in living and have done the most to create, or perhaps to reveal, the values that enrich the life of mankind. Hence, all religions, except those based on the authority of supernatural revelation, regard this urge toward the highest and the best as the starting point in man's spiritual pilgrimage. 

THIS FIRST FACT may lead you to explore into questions that lie beyond experience. From whence came this urge? What is the fundamental nature of these ideals and values? Are they grounded solely in experience or also somehow in the constitution of the universe? 

But this first fact of mankind's idealism with the resulting values, is more than a starting place for your explorations. It describes the quality of life which religion around the world endeavors to achieve. It has been, and is, a great common denominator of religious experience in men and women everywhere who hold the widest variety of theological convictions,- 

Christian and Jew, Catholic and Protestant, oriental and occidental, liberal and conservative, atheist and theist, "believer" and "unbeliever," rationalist and mystic. 

You may feel that you do not have to make up your mind about certain questions of the nature of the universe and your relation to it, [ 26 ]

but you do have to "make up your life." These values point you to the kind of life that you at your best wish to live, and to the kind of world in which we would like to live and which we, at our best, are trying to make. They are the touchstones for the testing of all conducts, habits, customs, measures, traditions and institutions. If, for instance, an institution fosters any one or more of these values for the people it serves and the people associated with it, it stands justified. In so far as it fails thus to serve humanity, it stands condemned. The endeavor to achieve these values in human life gives rise to the manifold problems of individual and social morality, to questions of sex and the family and citizenship; it has to do with education, the arts, the provision of decent living conditions for all people, needful economic and social reconstructions, and the attainment of international good will.

In seeking solutions of these problems and in ordering your own life, you will need to exercise a sense of comparative values, even sometimes among the higher values. For example, justice may occasionally have to be obtained with resulting loss of love or good will on the part of certain people against whose selfish interest a decision may have been reached. You will frequently have to choose not only between what you want to do and what you ought to do, but also between two or more things you want [ 27 ]

to do and two or more things you ought to do.

But the high moral obligation to choose the best in each situation is none the less. Indeed, what we call evil is often simply the choosing of the lesser good. This harmonizing of desires and ideals within yourself, and in relation to others and to your environment, always with reference to the highest and best, is the whole story of the art of living.

THE SECOND FACT, your sonship in the universe, invites you to explore further into the nature of the universe, into your relation to it and to the power or powers that permeate it. 

This, in our opinion, is the highway by which you can best seek for a conception of ultimate reality, for your ideas of God and immortality. 

Perhaps you have an intuitive faith in God.

Perhaps, for you, God is the fundamental, undisputed fact, the beginning of all religion. 

Even so, your faith in God will be all the stronger if you also approach it by way of the observation, experience and inference we are here suggesting.

At the very least, the realization, for all that it is worth, of the fact that you are a product of the natural universe should make you feel at home in it. Men may seem insignificant in the starry spaces but this insignificant man has measured these spaces and is reckoning as [ 28 ]

to the duration of earth's probable hospitality to human enterprises. You are a living, working, integral part of the mysterious creative process that has produced all these planets and suns and galaxies, all the world's beauty and love and nobility, all its science and wisdom and art and literature. 

Whatever modern knowledge may have done to certain doctrines you once held, you have here two undisputed facts that alone give you some sense of the spiritual value and significance of human life,—regardless of how little or how far you go beyond these facts. 1 29 1

ADVENTURES OF FAITH THE DIFFERENCES in religious beliefs among thinking people may be stated almost wholly in terms of how far they make the adventure of faith beyond this field of fact. All religionists, whether they are rationalist or mystic, make this adventure. All of us exercise faith in the everyday relationships of life. But the faith of intelligent people, whether exercised in religion or in everyday concerns, is grounded on as sufficient evidence as can be gathered. It is, however, part of life's adventure that we must so often make supreme decisions on insufficient evidence.

Therefore, when many open-minded, intelligent and conscientious people, of varying temperaments and varying habits of thought, confront life's mysteries,-all having access to the same set of facts,-they will inevitably come to different conclusions.

We propose to indicate very briefly some of the conclusions that men and women have come to when they have considered the two fundamental facts of man's idealism and his sonship in the universe. We are stating these impartially, for we are not trying to give you a philosophy but to suggest a way by which you can work out one for yourself. [ 80 1

To begin with, we may roughly divide religious people into two general groups: First, those whose adventure of faith starts out chiefly from our first fact, man's idealism; and second, those whose faith derives chiefly from our second fact, man's sonship in the universe. 

This classification is very general, for the two groups have a great deal in common, and the faith of any individual may easily be compounded of elements which are characteristic of both groups. It is highly important to remember this in reading the following pages. 

But, by describing these two groups and their various typical conclusions, we believe we can most clearly map out the field of religious thought. We shall not try to describe every possible attitude or raise all the possible problems or point out all the solutions which men have suggested for them, but rather indicate typical attitudes and conclusions in such manner that you can explore this field for yourself with some understanding of where you are going. 

OUR FIRST group, viewing man's urge toward the highest and best, stress his capacity to create values by his own efforts, and judge the worth of these values solely by their effect in human life. [ 31 ]

They believe that these values of love, truth, justice, beauty, courage, creative adventure, and self-sovereignty are primarily grounded in human experience; the second group hold that they are rooted in the very constitution of this universe which has produced man. The first group believe that man can better his condition and work toward his ideals only through his own efforts, exerted in cooperation with the forces, materials and conditions of a universe, which, although it sustains life, seems to be indifferent to man's desires and aspirations. The second people hold that the universe not only sustains the life of man but is friendly to his deepest desires and highest hopes; they are confident that there is a power or life permeating the universe that backs us in our endeavors to achieve the good life. This power or this friendly life they call God.

Our first group throw doubt upon the existence of such a friendly cosmic purpose by pointing to the fact of unmerited evil and suffering in the world, to the millions of thwarted and degraded lives, to the long, wasteful, bloody history of evolving life. Surely, they say, no pre-existent, intelligent, purposeful, beneficent God would have deliberately taken the method of evolution to achieve human personality. It is, they maintain, a plain fact of history that all man's physical, mental, moral and spiritual progress has been achieved through his own. [ 32 1

efforts, and that there seems to be no evidence of his having been purposively helped by a power not himself.

They admit that all this does not give a final explanation of the fundamental nature of man's idealism, of the ultimate character of the universe and of the nature of the power or powers that permeate it. On these matters they are agnostic. They say that we really know little or nothing about these matters and hence are not justified in making any assumptions about them the foundation of our religious faith. 

But they do, of course, believe that we should find out all we can about the laws and processes of the universe so that we may learn to adjust ourselves to it, and, where possible, to reshape our environment "nearer to the heart's desire." We can and should seek to discover and cooperate with those features of our environing universe that help us in attaining the good life. Those features that hinder us we should seek to control or avoid. The universe, while probably not friendly "as one man to another," can thus be made, in effect, more favorable to man's endeavors. But they see no justification for interpreting this little-known universe in terms of our desires, for attributing to it purposes like our own, which are purposes that have resulted from the particular interactions between a certain form of life and a particular bit of this universe. [ 38 ]

If existence, as a whole, is without purpose and plan, they see hope, not despair. For, out of the infinite possibilities that surround man, he can assemble and create his own purposes. This adventure, they feel, gives greater dignity and significance to human life than if it were simply the instrument of some cosmic purpose which is working toward a pre-determined end. Man is really a maker of destiny.

They do say, however, that, as knowledge "grows from more to more," we may possibly come to learn something about the fundamental nature of the universe and the ultimate nature of our relation to it, but until then our attitude should be that of open-minded, inquiring agnosticism. 

Therefore, this group find their dynamic life philosophy chiefly through their faith in life itself, through their belief in man's capacity to enrich and ennoble his life, and through their efforts to achieve this result both individually and socially. They endeavor to be just, honorable, sincere, truthful, and generous; they cooperate with intelligent efforts toward the upbuilding of human life, toward achieving justice, righteousness, peace and good will among persons and nations; they seek the highest and the best they know. They stress the importance of a scientific study of human experience, a scientific approach to problems of human behavior, and scientific social planning for human [ 34 ]

welfare. Religion's chief business, they say, is to furnish the incentive, the enthusiasm, the dynamic for this undertaking, to keep passionately alive that urge toward the highest and the best.

THESE PEOPLE, as well as those in the second group, differ somewhat among themselves as to how far this urge has carried man and how far it will probably carry him; in other words, as to how much progress man has made and is likely to make. With respect to this, there are four general attitudes.

First, there are those who believe that, with all man's recent advances in knowledge and organization, he has not, during the past two or three thousand years, made noteworthy advance in achieving higher values. They even question whether he is happier than the cave man. They protest against what they call "the dogma of progress." They point out a danger which is recognized by all religionists, namely, that man's wonderful material and mechanical achievements may utterly destroy him if he does not soon obtain moral and spiritual control of them. But, as contrasted with present-day "futilitarians," they believe that man has possibilities of real progress on the higher levels. 

In short, they stress future possibilities rather than past achievement. [85]

Second, others hold that the greatest possible achievement in certain higher values has already been made by a few supreme men and women. There will, they say, never be a greater sculptor than Phidias or a greater spiritual leader than Jesus. They differ as to what values have thus seen their highest flowering, but generally they believe that future progress will chiefly consist of raising the general level of achievement and appreciation. If we can never produce a greater philosopher than Plato or a greater spiritual genius than Jesus, we can see that a vastly greater proportion of our population are intelligent, cultured and spiritual-minded than was the case in ancient Athens and Palestine. 

Third, there are those who take a more optimistic view of human progress. Viewing the past few thousand years, they see advances in spiritual as well as material knowledge; increasing ability to control natural forces to human ends; a growing tendency, in spite of the late war, toward international understanding and peace; a keener sense and more thoroughgoing doctrine of justice; an increasingly humane and intelligent treatment of the sick and the maimed, the underprivileged and the criminal; a new recognition of the rights of women and children; a marvellous development of the arts; a growth of democracy. 

They hold that these things and many more have [86]

meant a real increase in value, a definite advance of mankind toward the highest and the best.

They see the possibility of the rise of greater geniuses than Plato and Jesus and of raising mass intelligence and achievement to a much higher level. Even if we concede that we are not as happy as the cave man, that means, they say, that we simply have not yet learned to achieve happiness in this complex, reflective and sophisticated age. With all the retrogressive and stationary phases in human evolution, they believe mankind, as a whole, has gone forward to higher levels of experience. Moreover, with all the varying ethical and cultural standards among different peoples today, there is a growing clearness and agreement as to the nature of righteousness, justice, love, and beauty. 

Fourth, some look upon the progress of man as more or less inevitable. They believe that this inevitable upward climb is in the very nature of life itself, that man's idealism is unconquerable, that there is a "law of progress.' 

In so far as they do not particularly concern themselves with accounting for this idealism or this law, they belong to this first general group. 

If, however, they believe that progress is inevitable because there is a cosmic purpose behind it, they belong in our second group. Here is one instance of how our classifications merge into each other. [ 37 ]

The religion of this first general group is man-centered; that of the second group is more or less less God-centered. Philosophical-minded persons in the first group, however, do speculate about the nature of man's cosmic background, about God and immortality, about "ultimate reality," but they do not generally attach great importance to these speculations or make their results matters of working belief. Some see a pluralistic universe, with a number of forces at work. Others, taking a monistic view, see one pervading force or creative energy or immanent life. They differ as to names and descriptions of this energy or life, but a typical view is that this creative force worked somewhat blindly at first but gradually flowered out in man into selfconsciousness and purpose and intelligence and strivings toward ideals. This intelligence and this idealism were thus new products, things that had never existed before, at least in this part of the universe. At most, they were potential in the creative, evolutionary process, just as the color and shape and odor of the sweet pea are not present but are potential in seed, soil, air and moisture.

Some call this pervading creative force 

God; others consider God to be simply the sum total of man's ideals, or the sum total of those factors in the universe that, with man's cooperation, make for increasing good. Still others regard God as the integrating process in the uni- [ 38 ]

verse that works for increased interdependence and cooperation toward realizing man's ideal possibilities. Others generalize and speak of 

God simply as that something in the universe which makes for increasing good.

Many, who hold these and other views, do not use the word God, because they feel their use of it would be so grossly misunderstood. But whether the word is used or not, when people begin to attach special importance to these Godideas, particularly the last two, they are moving over into our second general group.

THE SECOND general group, as we have said, hold that man's intelligence, idealism and values are grounded in a friendly universe. 

They believe that the evolution and progress of man is ultimately directed, or at least stimulated, by a power not himself. They contend that the urge that pushes life on to higher and higher levels "is incomprehensible except as a response to external stimulation." Some believe that it is "a result of the pressure exerted upon the life of our planet by a vaster life in which it is environed and included." 

Here, they say, is a process, which seems to be material and mechanical in its lower ranges, but which has evolved into "a world of conscious, aspiring beings, into faith, hope, and love, into philosophy, science and art, into a vast society of social beings, with imperfections [ 39 ]

no doubt, yet with dreams of perfection and earnest efforts to find out and fulfil the law of their being." Hence it is fair to assert that this process "is to be judged by its outcome rather than by its earlier phases, and that the noblest fruits of its long development are the best expressions of its real nature."

Furthermore, if man, with his increasing achievements in values, is a product of the creative process, these values indicate not only the tendency and the basic character of the process but also the character of the power permeating the process. This power can not be less in the scale of being than its highest product. Man's intelligence, therefore, must be an expression of a cosmic intelligence, akin to but far superior to his own. The marvelous order and interrelation of parts in the universe is also the expression of intelligence. Personality is the outgrowh of personality or something greater. This power, this intelligence, this personality, or super-personality, this will to good, working in the universe, and in human life, but not identical with humanity, these people call God.

Man's persistent urge to the ideal life, therefore, must be the expression of cosmic intention, and of a cosmic friendliness to man's endeavors to achieve such a life. In the light of such purpose and such friendliness, in the light of God's long purposeful process to achieve human personality, is it reasonable to believe [ 40 ]

that he will squander personalities, that he will doom them to extinction with all their unrealized possibilities? In such a way has come man's belief in personal immortality, in a God who "guards and guarantees the rationality and moral significance of the universe," who conserves the highest products of his creative will. 

Those who cherish this belief look upon man's higher values, his achievements of goodness, beauty, wisdom and heroism, as testimonies to the unimaginable excellence of God, as faint reflections of the divine nature, as outgrowths from the vaster divine life that enfolds the life of man. And most of them say that

Jesus, although human in all respects as they are, was the highest revelation the world has ever beheld of this divine life. This vaster life contains all the unrealized possibilities of mankind. Man and his values, they say, can not have objective worth or objective standards of excellence, neither can we be assured of their permanence, unless they are grounded in this 

Supreme and Eternal Worth.

There are many varieties of man's concepts and intuitions of God. Some, who can not altogether agree with the reasoning outlined above, see a finite God, struggling along with man for the achievement of right and truth and beauty.

Other ideas we reviewed in describing the first group. [ 41 ]

Many people who hold enlightened conceptions of God do not use the word at all, because of its long association with so much of clannishness and gross superstition in human history. 

Others believe that, because the word God has for ages stood for the highest and best of which man has conceived, it should be retained. 

You will work out your own concepts of reality, or perhaps you will hold the question in abeyance, and, like the first group, concentrate on the attainment of certain high qualities and experiences of life as the sole concern of your religion,-in other words, concentrate your efforts on the common ground of agreement between the two groups. 

We believe, however, you will agree that any one of these ideas of God is a nobler and more reasonable concept than that of a God who condemned unborn millions to sorrow and suffering because of the trifling disobedience of one man and who contrived 4,000 years later to "redeem" mankind through the "atoning sacrifice" of his son, Jesus. And we believe that any of the ideas about man we have suggested do him more credit than a religion that bids him take advantage of such an arrangement. [ 42 ]

THE "PROBLEM OF EVIL" 

AFTER you have made the natural and reasonable approach to religion that we have just outlined, you will perhaps understand why we advised you back on page 11 against beginning your quest for a philosophy of life by trying to determine what you believed about such questions as God, "salvation" and immortality.

Your attack on these and other problems of belief should now have become simplified. You will outgrow some of these problems, find rational attitudes on others, and perhaps hold the solution of others in abeyance. 

The widespread agitation today over such problems as "salvation," the "divinity of Jesus," heaven and hell, Biblical infallibility, the teaching of evolution, and the conflict of science and religion, is due largely to the persistence of outworn ideas of life and reality, which are a serious encumbrance for one trying to "find himself" religiously.

These "hangovers" from ancient conceptions also account for a great deal of the conflict of opinion over moral standards, the relations of the sexes, the status of women, birth control, militarism and pacifism, individualism and socialism. The solution of these problems, as complex as they are, is much simplified when we face them in terms of tested knowledge and in the light of modern needs. [ 43 ]

Of such is the "problem of evil." Complex as it is, it becomes somewhat simplified when it is not burdened with ancient theological complexities. Most religious people today, differing widely on the matters we have outlined, agree in squarely facing the fact of evil, in frankly acknowledging its terrible reality in human experience, whatever may be their differing metaphysical explanations of its presence in the world.

Because a philosophy is largely judged by how it grapples with evil, we wish to touch briefly on both its practical and theological aspects. 

WHAT is evil? 

Evil is anything that thwarts mankind in the pursuit of fullness and beauty of life. 

Evil arises from three sources: (1) from nature outside of man, (2) from the individual man, and (3) from human society. A particular evil, however, may often be traced to two or more of these sources. The second and third are so closely intermixed that we could group them under "human nature."

From nature outside of man come disease germs that cause illness and untimely deaths, with all their attendant suffering and sorrow; also tornados, earthquakes, storms and drouth, with all their train of human misery, suffering and death. But, as an instance of a threefold [ 44 ]

source of evil, famine may arise from unfavorable weather plus lack of individual industry and of efficient interhuman service.

Within the individual we see ignorance, indifference, laziness, hatred, fear, cowardice, envy, selfishness, hypocrisy; bodily and mental defects not traceable to outside nature; and inner conflicts that give rise to a multitude of mental and nervous ailments. But there are few of these evils that do not in some measure have their source in human society or that do not have their effect in society. The poverty of an individual, for example, may be the result chiefly of laziness, ignorance or bodily weakness, or it may be mostly traced to an unjust economic system.

Evils that arise chiefly in human society are war, class and race conflicts, crime, poverty (at least of large groups), injustice, exploitation, tyranny, human servitude. Here, again, the individual and social elements are interknit; discussion of these leads to much controversy, as, for instance, when we try to ferret out the causes of poverty and crime. 

But whatever the evil, there are four ways of dealing with it: (1) We may exterminate it by removing causes; (2) we may control its manifestations; (3) we may mitigate its unfortunate consequences; and (4) we may bravely endure it, rise above it, transcend it. [ 45 ]

Take diseases for instance. Certain plagues, once thought to be punishment from on high for sin, have been practically eliminated by attacking the germs that caused them. Control of the manifestations of disease is achieved through quarantines and other health regulations. 

Science has devised ways of mitigating the painful effects of many maladies. And where not even this can be done, heroic spirits have made the best of their condition and triumphed over bodily ills. In the case of a social evil, such as crime, we are just beginning to study the causes, having spent most of our energies so far in controlling its manifestations through laws and penalties. With war, we have done little else than to mitigate its evil consequences and to endure it heroically; we have made a beginning at control through an outlawry agreement; but we have hardly begun on the discovery and treatment of causes. An earthquake, however, is an instance of where we can probably never remove the cause and can do little more to control its manifestations in human life than to build better buildings or possibly learn how to discover and avoid probable quake areas. 

Now, as individuals, men and women meet evil in various ways. Some are utterly crushed; they grow embittered; their lives become cheap and inconsequential. Others, defeated, refuse to face the facts; they maintain a "front," they "whistle to keep up their courage," or they be- [ 46 ]

come cynical. Still others face up bravely to misfortune, and, whether defeated or victorious, keep their heroic temper and their sweetness of spirit, ready for the next encounter.

This is all we need say here about the old argument as to whether suffering makes character. Perhaps it reveals rather than makes character. At any rate, the universe seems to be peculiarly fitted for heroes. The adventurous soul rises to meet the challenge of evil. It is not alone the desire for the highest and the best that fashions zestful and heroic lives; it is the constant battling against odds to achieve the best that chiefly does this. Evil thus, in a sense, becomes an instrument for good.

Our reasonable hope is that we will practically eliminate or bring under control those evils that notoriously thwart and degrade human life. But we do not hope to, and we would not wish to, remove all those natural and inevitable obstacles to satisfaction and joy and achievement that, for normal people, give life its zest and thrills and adventure. Life would be unutterably dull if there were no hills to climb or foul weather to face.

MOST OF those in our first general group of religious people do not go further than this in their consideration of evil. They simply regard evil as a fact, as an unconquered realm of [ 47 ]

human experience. Their religion, as contrasted with that of the second group, does not necessarily involve faith in the moral purpose of the universe outside of man. Hence they assert that they do not feel the need of reconciling the fact of evil with any beliefs about the nature or government of the universe. They declare that suffering, especially that which is unmerited and which tears down rather than builds up character, makes it impossible to believe in an allwise, all-powerful, all-loving God; the presence of irrational evil denies either his goodness or his omnipotence, and does no credit to his wisdom.

The second group, however, point out that their conception of God is not that of an all-wise, all-powerful, all-loving God who wills every single event, whether it be good or bad. Furthermore, most of them point out that much needless difficulty has come out of the idea that "good" and "evil" are two absolute "principles" or qualities or even entities, which God "put" in the world for some mysterious purpose of his own.

In fact, people in both groups generally agree that "good" and "evil" are not qualities of things as such but qualities of relations. Good or evil is not a quality of an object or a desire or a thought or an act as such, but a quality of our relation to that object or desire or thought or act. [ 48 ]

In nature outside of man, a tornado is an evil only when man and his works are in its path. Tubercle bacilli are harmful only in relation to the human body. A drouth would not cause famine if men did not sow or reap. 

Within the individual, ignorance is an evil only when it makes one unable to face his problems or properly enjoy life. The desire for security becomes an evil when it all but overwhelms the desire for experience and achievement; when one would rather be safe than brave.

The desire for recognition is an evil when it expands into bragging, self-conceit, or misrepresentation of one's abilities. Even strivings toward a higher value may be evil. The desire to write truth in literature may descend to nasty particularities or to profuseness of detail that violate the canons of beauty and proportion.

In social life are abundant examples of the relative nature of good and evil. Sex satisfaction may be an act of criminal bestiality or the expression of the purest love. Efforts to achieve economic security are good up to the point where they infringe upon the rights of others to the same satisfactions, where they violate the values of justice, good will, and peace. Evil results when an employer, seeking goods, power and achievement, underpays his workmen; when a nation increases its economic power through despoiling a weaker people; when men assert their personality by belittling others or by [ 49 ]

"kicking a man when he is down"; when a dictator exercises power over his people at the sacrifice of their right of self-sovereignty, even if they thrive under his rule. Evil results, even when we choose between the lesser of two higher values. For example, there are situations when speaking the literal truth needlessly breeds ill will; there are other situations when the truth must be spoken at the sacrifice of good will. In many situations, we are confronted with a scale of values. Our duty is to choose the best for that time and place; the less-than-the-best is then and there evil. What is wrong in one situation may be right in another.

Thus we see that a particular object or force or desire or thought is not good or evil as such. It is good in any particular situation when it results in the highest and the best for the greatest number of people concerned; it is evil when it results for less than the highest and the best. The "number of people concerned" may be one or two or a million or the entire human race; the "particular situation" may cover a split second for one man or a period of years for a family of nations.

Good may also be defined as that which brings people together, that makes for harmony, organization, interdependence and integration, for larger and larger units-families, tribes, nations and societies of nations. Evil is that which tears people apart, that makes for chaos and [ 50 ]

discord, that disorganizes and disintegrates. 

Obviously, hate, envy, jealousy and selfishness repel, keep people apart; love, good will and generosity bring them together. War, race and class conflict, crime and injustice are disorganizing forces. A society in which lying and murder were recognized as honorable conduct would quickly go to pieces. Also, within the individual, unresolved inner conflicts break up personality. Ill health, disease and fear also break up personality; they are disintegrating forces. But, not only do we seek to unify, to integrate, the individual personality and human society, but we strive also to achieve harmony between man and his environment, to effect as close an organization or integration of man and nature as we can. Naturally, earthquakes, tornadoes, drouth and disease germs, when they cross human interests, tend to break up this organization.

This concept of good and evil does not do away with their relative nature, for a particular object or force or desire or thought or act is not an integrating or a disintegrating influence as such, but only as it is related to human desires and aspirations and plans.

REALIZING the relative nature of evil may make moral decisions and judgments more difficult but it simplifies the theological "problem of evil" for our second general group. We have [51]

outgrown that phase of the problem which made us conceive of personal origins of two opposing "principles" or entities in the universe, God and 

Satan, for example. The other horn of the dilemma, a God who is the author of both good and evil, is, at least, not as acute as it once was. 

For, as we pointed out before, the people of our second group, who believe in moral purpose in the universe, nevertheless do not generally conceive of God as an all-wise, all-powerful and all-loving being who directly determines the character of each event, good or bad. Such an idea is inconsistent with the fact of natural law and with the concept of a God revealed in such law.

But the problem of a God-idea that involves conditions making for evil is not thereby altogether done away with. The people who see purpose in the universe outside man, however, point out that, as we have just said, the challenge of evil fashions heroic, zestful souls, who, in overcoming or transcending evil, make it into an instrument for good. Now, they ask:

May not this be a necessary element in God's plan for the evolution of human life? May not even the suffering of animals be part and parcel of the intent of this Universal Life to achieve strength and vitality in its creatures? Is not evil a necessary element in the design of this living, purposeful universe to develop the best on each level of life, both animal and human? [ 52 ]

Thus, they say, evil achieves a disciplining and character-building purpose in God's plan of life. We have seen that mankind, at times and imperfectly, makes evil a means for good. 

Some suggest that all evil is thus transmuted in the experience of God. Through the discipline of evil, they say, God, the Will to Good, fashions worthful and imperishable personalities. These personalities survive this life, and continue eternally and progressively to be perfected through this Will. Also, in the world to come, starved and thwarted lives will have their chance to grow and thrive. God's purposes are not for time but for eternity; they are infinite, and in the fire of his holy infinite purposes all the evils of finite existence are held to be utterly consumed. 

People who hold this faith do not, however, assert that they have answered all questions about evil. They admit that it is a mystery. But it is far from being the only mystery of life. 

They maintain it is more logical to believe that evil serves some purpose in the universe than to deny that there is any purpose. They contend that the persistent urge of man toward good in a world without God would be much more of a mystery than is the existence of evil in a world with God. [ 53 ]

IN ALL EVENTS, this struggle of man with what he calls evil is the militant, heroic side of his strivings toward the highest and the best,which strivings constitute the first fundamental fact we noted at the start of our quest. 

And it is in this passionate realization of the great ideals of life and in earnest endeavors toward their fulfilment that religious people of varying beliefs find their great common ground of worship, self-discipline and service. 154 1

THOUGHTS ON THINKING 

WE HAVE suggested some of the directions your religious explorations may take you. 

Our approach to your problem of working out a life philosophy is not unique nor is it original with us. With all the differences of language, of treatment and of classification, these are the routes by which most religious thinkers today make the adventure of faith beyond the field of fact. These adventures are fully described in the books that we list at the end of this booklet. 

But, however much or little you may adventure beyond this field, whatever you include or leave out of your religion, be sure that it is your religion. Be sure it is the result of your own thinking, your own reading, your own living. Only a philosophy so achieved will serve you in your business of more abundant living.

This does not mean that it must necessarily be "different" from the religion of any other man. It means that, to the best of your ability and opportunity, and in the light of the world's best thought, the tested findings of science and your experience and that of the race, you will determine for yourself what is true. Your conclusions will likely be similar to those of many other seekers. [55]

If you do accept any authority, it should be authority on the particular matter you are investigating, the authority of men who have given open-minded, conscientious study to this matter, preferably several men who fairly well agree on the conclusions and inferences they reach after a study of the same set of facts. 

Within limits, you will accept the authority of men who have explored a certain problem by the same method you would employ if you had the time, ability and equipment. But you will be wary of accepting the conclusions of these same men regarding any other questions on which they express themselves. You will use caution in examining a physicist's ideas on immortality or a theologian's views on heredity. 

And you will realize that the authority of the

Bible and of Jesus' teachings is no greater than the truth they contain. We value certain truths in the Bible, not because they are in the Bible, but because they are true.

Authorities have differing functions. The high service of the scientists to the layman is to place the facts at his disposal. The high service of the technicians-physicians, engineers, lawyers, statesmen-to the layman is to put this knowledge to practical uses. The high service of the philosopher and the theologian to the layman is to help him interpret this knowledge in terms of the ideal life. The best type of clergyman combines the functions of the technician and the theologian. [56]

What has been said here does not mean that you will accept nothing that cannot be "proved."

You will exercise "faith," plenty of it, but faith on the basis of the known or the probable. You cannot cross the street or put through a business deal or get married or carry on a crusade, without faith. The epoch-making flights of

Lindbergh and Byrd were adventures of faith, but a faith grounded in their intimate knowledge of aviation. Faith is involved in every relationship and achievement, from the lowest to the highest. There has usually been a suprarational element in the supreme adventurings of humanity, in the thrusting ahead into quests and encounters where the "prudent" dared not go. 

How much you will venture on faith will be largely a matter of your individual temperament. In all events, let your faith be grounded in fact and highly reasonable assumptions. 

This, you see, is not the sort of "faith" that both Catholic and Protestant orthodoxies require you to exercise so that you will bring yourself to believe their dogmas.

The matter of prayer and worship also will depend on your temperament as well as on the faith you hold. For you may be of the rationalistic or of the mystic temper. But, the rationalist of insight will not belittle others' mystic experience, nor will the understanding mystic adjudge people as lacking in religion if they do [57]

not have such experience. All people have some degree of reverence. But worship for some may be simply a passionate realization of the great ideals of life and a passionate dedication to their fulfilment; or for others it may be communion with God, with the Divine Life, the source of these ideals.

To what extent our attitudes and actions are determined by temperament, by heredity, habits, training, surroundings, or deliberate thinking and conscious convictions, is a matter for the biologist and the psychologist to investigate. There is a great deal of discussion as to the relative influence upon us of heredity and environment. It all brings up the age-old argument between "free will" and determinism. Do we exercise "free will" or is everything we do determined by a chain of cause and effect that goes back to causes outside our control? Whatever answer may sometime be found to that enigma, we largely order our own lives on the assumption of "free will." That assumption gets results. At the same time, we are determinists in that we surround our children with every possible influence for shaping their behavior, and in that we strive to discover and to remove the causes of social evils. A sensible working attitude is that we, as individuals, will not blame our failures on circumstance but will endeavor to reshape circumstance to make more possible the good life for ourselves and for society. [ 58 ]

At the same time, it is a curious fact that certain beliefs and attitudes have vastly different effects on different personalities. Some go from their worship with renewed zest for mak-` ing life more abundant for themselves and their fellows, but others seem to have lost the vision when they come down from the heights. Some who believe in God are inclined to let God work out his great purposes alone; others labor zestfully for human betterment to fulfil the will of God with the help of God. Some who do not believe in God see no great purposes in life, view the human scene with pessimism and live and act accordingly; while others, seeing no help from outside, are impressed with the need of man helping himself, and likewise labor zestfully for human betterment. Some who believe in personal immortality are indifferent to the problems of this world because everything will be all right in the next world. Others are spurred on to great endeavors because their future life will begin where this one leaves off. 

The disbeliever in immortality may say, "let us eat, drink and be merry, for tomorrow we die"; or, since the present life is his one and only chance, he will strive to make the most of it. 

The effect of a belief on attitudes and deeds is largely determined by what kind of a person holds the belief. By and large, your fundamental beliefs are important, but it is still more important what you do with them. [59]

MAY we give you some other counsels for your quest, which have grown out of the experience of other people in your situation? 

Do not believe something just because you want to believe it, because it is comforting, because it is strengthening, because it advances your fortunes, because it makes you stand well with your fellows. It is so fatally easy to rationalize such beliefs, that is, to find many good reasons for holding them-after you have formed them. This habit of rationalization is one of the most common and most insidious enemies of straight thinking. It undermines integrity, degrades the intellect, blinds one to new and higher truths, and often endangers the very comfort and strength and prosperity and good opinion which one seems to achieve by it. 

Do not hold an idea just because it is old, because it has been widely held; on the other hand do not believe it just because it is new. 

Not, is it comforting or strengthening, or is it old or new, or popular or unpopular? but is it true? That is the only question you should ask.

If you have the stuff of real manhood or real womanhood within you, the things that you sincerely believe to be true will in the long run be the most comforting, the most strengthening, the most serviceable. [ 60 ]

In this business of searching for truth, the first requisite is the free mind, free from fear of new facts, free from arbitrarily imposed authority, free from the tyranny of circumstance, free from the dictates of sects, crowds and majorities. If you have been brought up in the bonds of an orthodox creed, it is a wonderful day when you realize that you are free to think for yourself in religion. It is exhilarating to realize that no fields of inquiry are closed to you. It is a comfort not to feel that you have to fit new discoveries into the rigid pattern of old beliefs.

The mind must not only be free, but it must be continually open to new truth; it must be of the inquiring temper, alert, eager to know and to learn. And a mind of this sort must be, and is likely to be, honest, as well as courageous enough to hold minority opinions and when necessary express them. 

RIGHT HERE, however, you may ask: How am I to have a stabilizing and directive philosophy of life, if I am always to be seeking for truth? If I build my religion on the foundation of certain beliefs, and new knowledge upsets these beliefs, what becomes of my religion? 

How am I to live a life whose philosophy is constantly subject to change? 

Our answer to that is, first, that your philosophy should be such as to make you say [61]

confidently: "I am ready for change." The readiness and ability to change your religion or any phase of it should be a fundamental element in your conceptions of life and of truth. It is this attitude that has animated great leaders of thought and enabled them to change the mind of the world. Your conceptions and your codes and your outlooks should be always subject to revision in the light of new facts, just as are the methods that you employ in your trade or business or profession. 

Second, real changes take place very slowly.

You are not likely to wake up and find something in the morning paper that alters the whole course of your moral and spiritual life. Ordinarily a new discovery is heralded a long time before it is tested by the best minds. The layman has plenty of time in which to appraise it, and finally, if it passes these tests and the test of his own intelligence, to adjust himself accordingly. The physician continues to heal the sick, even while adjusting his methods to new discoveries in medicine. Even so, you may practice the art of living, availing yourself of the new knowledge and the deeper insights that the years bring with them.

Third, certain broad, fundamental values remain, through all changing conceptions of them and through all the changing techniques for putting them to work. We shall always desire to have justice, although our notions of it [ 62 ]

may be modified from time to time, our standards of it may be raised, and our machinery for attaining it may be scrapped and refashioned dozens of times. The sense of the beautiful is immeasurably older than any theories about it, and it is likely to last a long time yet, but it has been constantly refined, and new arts have arisen to give it expression. The love of truth, the desire to know the facts about ourselves and our world, is no new thing, but this very desire has resulted in new and upsetting discoveries and in revolutionary changes in ideas as to the nature of knowledge itself and as to methods of obtaining it. Through all the changing ideas as to the springs of conduct, as to moral responsibility, as to the control of human behavior, it will always be well for men to love, to be truthful, to do justly, and to be courageous for worthful ends. 

To realize the tentative nature of most of your knowledge, to maintain your stability while adjusting yourself to change, to be alert to new truth and yet steadfast in living the truth already won, to be free and yet achieve self-discipline,-these are the double conditions of growth, both for yourself and for society. [ 63 ]

THIS IS THE LIFE! 

ALL THAT has been said so far points to the three central problems of living that we all must face: How shall I "live my own life" abundantly? How shall I live as a member of human society? How shall I live as a part of this vast, mysterious reality that surrounds me?

As to the first, we can live a life unswayed by the fads and fancies of the hour. We can be "captains of our souls." We can "budget" our lives, our time and energies and affections, with reference to the things we most wish and most ought to do, with reference not only to basic desires but also to the higher loyalties. If we can find a single consuming worthy ambition or ideal, so much the better; we will live broadly but to the point.

With respect to the second, we can cultivate a sympathetic insight into the hopes and the fears, the troubles and the problems of our neighbors, whether they be next door or across the seven seas. If we are truly religious, we will not only strive to do justly by our family and friends and associates, but exert our influence, great or small, for the perfection of a just social and economic order, whatever it may cost us in power and position and privilege. We will do our share toward making our community, state and nation better places in which to live, toward achieving good will among the peoples of the [ 64 ]

earth. Not only will we want to live as wholesomely, as richly, as beautifully and as heroically as our abilities and our environment permit, but we will want to help make it possible for others to do likewise. Our religion will make us men and women whom right-minded people are glad to meet, to deal with, to live and work with. 

But it may also create opposition for us from those who allow self-interest to obstruct efforts toward the attainment of social ideals. 

As to the third question, we can at least seek to learn more and more about the mysterious universe which has produced us and which sustains us; and we can bring ourselves into as intimate and worshipful and inspiriting relation to this reality as our faith and our experience enable us.

In other words, we will strive to achieve the highest and the best for ourselves and for others, and we will be uplifted, inspired and gladdened by the highest and the best which we may find in the reality that encompasses us. 

At the same time, because of the very nature of life and the world, we all fall far short of living out our ideals. At times they seem very remote things in a world of hard facts. Of course, we have to reckon with actualities, but we need not be a slave to them. The conflicts between actualities and ideals are not of our choosing; the problems that come to us are not always of our making; all these must be resolved as best we may. [65]

We must not lose sight of our ideals, but we will not look so steadily on them that we fail to see the next practical step ahead toward their fulfilment. In our dealings we will not compromise our standards of right and wrong, even though we must at times make sensible and honorable adjustments to people and things as they are. We must think discriminatingly, but not let fine distinctions keep us from taking the best possible present course. We can espouse the better cause if the best cannot at the time be marshalled. We may keep our sense of beauty and fitness, but not cultivate it to the point of supersensitiveness.

A WELL-ROUNDED working philosophy makes for social-mindedness as well as individuality, for creative faith as well as intellectual integrity, for robustness and vitality as well as refinement. It honors the claims of both intellect and emotions, of both individual and society, in each case not unduly exalting one above the other. It joins idealism with common sense, and to a solemn realization of life's purposes, it adds the saving grace of humor,-which we are tempted to include as one of the great spiritual values of mankind.

A well-rounded life in a unified, integrated life. It is a life in which all desires and aspirations and endeavors are harmonized and are marshalled toward the achievement of the highest and the best of which one is capable. [66]

This may mean a balanced life, in which one may maintain a high level of appreciation and achievement in several values. Or it may mean that all desires and aspirations and endeavors are marshalled toward achievement in some one or two values. In these two types, and beyond and between them, lies an infinite variety of life patterns. The second type of life has given us the great scientists, single-minded in their pursuit of new truth; the great statesmen, devoting themselves to a reform of an interhuman evil; the great artists, spending their lives in achieving works of beauty and truthpictures, sculpture, buildings, literature; the great saints and preachers, giving the world their new visions of reality and righteousness. 

But even several of these realized a high level of appreciation and achievement in things outside their specialty; and today we may see exhibitions of paintings by physicians and business men, hear music composed by clergymen and political leaders, and behold poets and novelists active in a crusade for social righteousness. 

However far we may miss living supreme lives, the great life patterns of past and present times should incite us, not to imitate them, but to fashion the best possible life-pattern for ourselves, and, in all events, to live as truthfully and serviceably and heroically and beautifully as within us lies. [67]

TO SUM UP 

PERHAPS you will have a clearer notion of the ground we have covered if we make the following summary of all that has been said:

People who are unsettled religiously and who wish to achieve a philosophy of life should begin with a few fundamental facts about human life and the universe (pp. 7-11). 

Certain basic desires and certain higher aspirations and values are intricately knit together in human life. Religion should take account of all of these, but it is chiefly and rightfully concerned with the higher values and man's urge to attain them. (Pp. 12-20.)

Therefore, the first fundamental undisputed fact with which we may begin to work out a religion is man's urge toward the highest and the best (pp. 22, 23).

The second fundamental fact is man's sonship in the universe (pp. 23, 24).

These two facts are the beginning of our quest; they point to the quality of life we wish to achieve; they lead us into the questions of inter-human relations; invite us to inquire into man's place in the universe; and alone give us some sense of the spiritual value and significance of human life (pp. 24-29). [68]

We may divide religious people into two general groups as to how they proceed from these facts into the adventure of faith (pp. 

30, 31).

The first group stress man's ability to achieve the highest and the best through his own efforts, exerted upon human nature and upon the raw materials of a universe that seems indifferent to man's desires and aspirations. They believe that human values are primarily grounded in human experience; that, in the present state of knowledge, we should not make any ideas as to the ultimate nature of the universe or of the existence and nature of God the foundation of our faith. These people as well as the people in the second group differ somewhat among themselves as to how much progress mankind as a whole has made as a result of his urge to the highest and best. (Pp. 31-39.)

The second general group believe that man's values are grounded in a friendly universe, and that therefore his strivings toward the highest and best must be the expression of a pervading purposeful intelligence or power, which they call God. Since God's purpose in the long evolutionary process has been to achieve aspiring, worthful personalities, it is likely that he will conserve these personalities so that they may continue in an immortal life to realize their possibilities. (Pp. 32, 39-42.) [ 69 ]

You will work out your own concepts of reality and God, or you may hold the question in abeyance and concentrate on the ideals, and the high qualities and experiences of life as the chief content of your religion (p. 42).

If you make this natural and reasonable approach to religion, you will outgrow many problems and find the solution of others somewhat simplified (p. 43).

The "problem of evil" is simplified when it is not burdened with theological complexities and when evil is faced as a fact (p. 44).

Evil is anything that thwarts mankind in the pursuit of fullness and beauty of life. It arises from three sources, from nature outside of man, from the individual man, and from human society. (Pp. 44, 45.)

There are four methods of dealing with evil: removal of causes, control of manifestations, mitigation of consequences, and heroic endurance (pp. 45, 46). 

Individuals meet evil in various ways, but the universe seems particularly fitted to heroes who rise to the challenge of difficulties, which, even with real evils eliminated, will always and should always be with us (pp. 46, 47).

The first group of religious people do not go beyond this in their treatment of evil; they do not try to reconcile the fact of evil with beliefs about the nature of the universe or the existence of God (pp. 47, 48). [70]

The theological "problem of evil," is simplified when we realize that no all-powerful, allloving God orders every single event, that good and evil are not qualities of things as such but of our relations to them, and that good is an integrating and evil a disintegrating influence (pp. 48, 52).

Some conceive of God as simply the integrating force in the universe, but generally the people of the second group believe that in God's plan for the evolution of human life, evil serves a character-building purpose which continues eternally and progressively in each individual life (pp. 52, 53).

Man's conflict with evil is the militant side of his strivings toward the highest and the best and these strivings form the great common ground for people of varying beliefs (pp. 53, 54).

These are the routes by which most religious thinkers today make the adventure of faith beyond the field of fact (p. 55). 

Only a religion which you work out for yourself will serve you in your business of living. 

You will exercise extreme care in relying on any authority. (Pp. 55, 56.) 

Even the most adventurous faith is grounded in the known or the probable. Your ventures in faith will depend largely on your temperament, as will also, in a measure, your mode of prayer and worship. (Pp. 57, 58.) [71]

How much our acts are determined by our beliefs, how much we exercise "free will," is debatable, but certain beliefs have vastly different effects on different personalities (pp. 58, 59). 

You should hold an idea only because you believe it to be true; no other reason should determine your beliefs (p. 60).

In seeking truth, the mind should be free, open to new truth, honest, courageous (p. 61).

It is possible to have a philosophy of life and yet be open to new and upsetting truth, by making readiness for change a part of your philosophy, by remembering that real changes take place slowly, and by realizing that certain fundamental human values remain, through changing conceptions of them and changing techniques for putting them to work (pp. 6163).

All that has been said points to the way by which you can "live your own life," live as a member of society, and live as a part of the reality that surrounds you (pp. 64, 65).

In striving toward our ideals we must reckon with actualities, but without being enslaved by them, and we must take the best possible present courses under existing conditions (pp. 

65, 66).

An ideal philosophy gives balance and perspective, and shows us how to marshall all elements of our life toward the achievement of the highest and the best (pp. 66, 67). [72]

TO HELP YOU 

OUT OF a genuine desire to help you for your own sake, we have tried to show how you can achieve your own philosophy of life. On the following pages we are listing a few books that give the results of some of the best thinking of the time on your religious problems. If you wish any other suggestions and helps, write to the Unitarian Laymen's League, Boston, Mass., the organization that has issued this booklet. 

We have done this also because we wish to do our part toward developing a race of hardthinking social-minded idealists. Only in this way can our civilization ever become committed to winning for earth's multitudes the joy of the good life, and the opportunity to achieve the highest and the best of which they are capable. 

Because religion is devotion to the highest and the best; because it involves the intellect, the emotions and the will, art, science and philosophy, worship and conduct and service: because it is of all these things, religion is the most pervading and commanding and beautiful fact in the world. [ 78 ]

FOR FURTHER READING 

WE BELIEVE that a careful reading of some of the following books will help you to find a faith to live by. 

With so many good books on religion published in recent years, it is difficult to choose even among the best. This is simply a list of a few works that we feel to be especially helpful to the layman who wishes to find himself religiously and who wishes to have a general picture of the field of religious thought, past and present.

Only modern books are listed, not because we ignore the wisdom of the ancients, but because the best religious thought today takes into account the recent findings of modern knowledge, as well as the tested wisdom of the ancients.

Authors representing the viewpoints of both our two general groups (described under "Adventures of Faith") are included. The better understanding you have of differing interpretations, the better equipped you will be for working out a well reasoned faith of your own. 

Any of these books may be borrowed from the lending library of the Unitarian Laymen's League in Boston,

Mass., and the League will also be glad to suggest books on special topics, such as Jesus, the Bible, immortality, the problem of knowledge, the "social gospel," evolution, science and religion, and other subjects. 

The Enduring Quest, by HARRY A. OVERSTREET, Pro fessor and head of the Department of Philosophy and 

Psychology, College of the City of New York. Published by W. W. Norton & Co., New York. 277 pp.

Subtitled "a search for a philosophy of life," this book is a clear, helpful, and remarkably comprehensive introduction to that search. One of the best presentations for an age of confusion in thought, especially valuable for one who has done no previous reading on philosophy. [75]

A Preface to Morals, by WALTER LIPPMAN, formerly editor of the New York World. The Macmillan Com pany, New York. 337 pp. Gives the reasons for the breakaway from old beliefs and the confusion in men's minds today, and endeavors to reconstruct a creditable basis for belief and conduct today. Readable and stimulating.

Religion, by EDWARD SCRIBNER AMES, Professor of 

Philosophy in the University of Chicago. 320 pp. Henry

Holt & Co., New York. A good general text book, which considers the origins and values of religion, its relation to science, philosophy, art, and morality, and includes several illuminating chapters on the meaning of God. 

Requires and repays careful reading.

The Quest of the Ages, by A. EUSTACE HAYDON, 

Professor of Comparative Religion in the University of 

Chicago. Harper and Brothers, New York. 

234 pp.

The dramatic story of how through the ages man has sought to attain the good life, of how the religions of the world have solved the various great problems of existence; coupled with a vision of how the good life for mankind is to be achieved in the light of growing knowledge and in the actualities of man's environment. 

Also requires-and repays-careful reading.

Pathways to Certainty, by WILLIAM ADAMS BROWN, Professor of Systematic Theology at Union Theological 

Seminary. Charles Scribner's Sons, New York. 284 pp.

A clear and systematic exposition of how one may arrive at reasoned convictions regarding God and other basic concerns. One of the best and most explicit modern presentations of the viewpoint of our second general group.

Theism and the Modern Mood, by WALTER MARSHALL HORTON, of Oberlin College. Harper and Brothers, 

New York. 179 pp. Professor Horton has the knack of making theology interesting and meaningful to the average reader. From a sympathetic criticism of the [76]

viewpoint of our first group he goes on to point out ways by which, in the light of modern knowledge, one may build up a conception of God.

Religion in a Changing World, by RABBI ABBA HILLEL SILVER of Cleveland. Richard R. Smith, Inc., 

New York. 204 pp. An excellent presentation by a brilliant young Jewish liberal. Emphasizes the necessity of keeping firm hold on the great unchanging religious realities; stresses the ultimate spiritual character of the universe; and admirably discusses the function of the church with reference to social justice and world peace, and the religious attitude toward social service, family life and education.

Religion Without Revelation, by J. S. HUXLEY. 

Harper and Brothers, New York. 381 pp. A carefully considered endeavor to show how the values and the experiences of religion may be conserved without faith in a personal God and immortality. The author is the grandson of Thomas Huxley. Written from the view point of our first group, it considers many phases of religion, covers a large field, and requires close reading. 

A summary of his conclusions is found from page 357 to the end of the book.

Religion Coming of Age, by ROY WOOD SELLARS, 

Professor of Philosophy at the University of Michigan. 

The Macmillan Co., New York. 289 pp. One of the "Philosophy for the Layman Series," this book is particularly suitable for the lay reader who wishes to know the "why and wherefore," the philosophic foundation, for the views of our first general group; who desire to see how they deal explicitly with the questions of God, Jesus, a friendly universe, creation and evolution, immortality, morality, values and the church. May be profitably read in comparison with "Pathways to Certainty."

Religious Perplexities, by L. P. JACKS, Principal of Manchester College, Oxford. George H. Doran Co., 

[77]

New York. 92 pp. A brief religious classic; a heartening challenge to make a pragmatic test of the friendliness of the universe by trusting it as a friend in all our endeavors to "achieve a better-than-what-is"; a vision of religion that reveals "a world fit for heroes to live in," and arouses the heroic spirit to cope with the evils and difficulties of life.

Problems of Religion, by DURANT DRAKE, Professor of Philosophy in Vassar College. Houghton, Mifflin Co.,

Boston. 415 pp. A serviceable book for one who wishes a comprehensive view of the field of religion and a critical, but not destructive, examination of the grounds of belief, and a sympathetic insight into the values of traditional doctrines. Contains several chapters particularly valuable for fortifying the layman against loose reasoning in religious questions. 

The Mind in the Making, by JAMES HARVEY ROBIN- 

SON. Harper and Brothers, New York. 235 pp. A valuable aid to straight thinking. Warns against many slipshod methods of forming opinion, touched upon in our section entitled "Thoughts on Thinking." Although the book primarily deals with "the relation of intelligence to social reform," it is of great service to the individual who is endeavoring to think out a philosophy of life and attain an enlightened religious faith. And the chapters on social reconstruction are closely related to our second question under "This is the Life!"-“How shall 

I live as a member of human society?"

Methods of Private Religious Living, by HENRY N. 

WIEMAN, Professor of the Philosophy of Religion in the 

Divinity School of the University of Chicago. The Macmillan Co., New York. 219 pp. From dealing with the common things of life to the reconstruction of society, this book shows how religion can be made to produce definite results in the improvement of human living on all its levels. It is thoroughly modern in its viewpoint, sane, scientific, and inspiring, without being sentimental or "Pollyanish." It is of distinct value to [ 78 ]

people of both general groups. Professor Wieman states that he and others have tried out these methods and have found them helpful, "sometimes to a startling degree." A book to be used.

The New Morality, by DURANT DRAKE, Professor of Philosophy at Vassar College. The Macmillan Co., New 

York. 359 pp. An excellent book for giving one a comprehensive and intelligent understanding of the concrete major problems of individual and social morality today. His discussions of these issues are illuminated with facts from unquestioned sources and with comments by trained observers. Even if some readers disagree with certain of Professor Drake's solutions, they are given an insight into these problems that helps them to work out their own solutions. [79]
