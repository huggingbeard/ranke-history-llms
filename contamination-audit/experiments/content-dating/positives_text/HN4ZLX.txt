PREFACE 

"Modern Chess" is an attempt to outline briefly the development of modern tournament play from the earliest recorded games of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Such an outline must necessarily appear inadequate to those who follow the games of the different masters in the numerous international tournaments that are taking place today.

Each of the masters mentioned merits a complete work to indicate properly his status as a chess artist. Many of the figures whose games are recorded in the following pages have indeed been the subjects of full sized volumes. 

From the thousands of games at our disposal, the task of selection has been difficult. The chess expert will find a large number of the famous games of these artists missing from these pages. They have been omitted advisedly, because they are so frequently a part of every collection that the student meets them at every turn. We have incorporated numerous brilliant games, but have placed greater emphasis upon the more routine, though less brilliant, games of the grand master. It is these work-a-day games and victories of which the bulk of winning totals is made, and these should be studied for the principles of strategy exemplified therein. 

When confronted by the task of selection from the multitude of brilliant and worthwhile games, we realize the amazing pace at which chess literature is growing today.

The lessons in sound play are based chiefly upon Tarrasch's "Three Hundred Games" which was translated by the author in 1915 and incorporated at that time by Mr. David A. Mitchell in his "Guide to Chess."

Acknowledgment is made of the numerous games which were obtained from the "American Chess Bulletin," and of the material which Mr. Herman Helms has placed at our disposal. 

In a single volume only a few of the many high-lights have been touched upon, and it is our hope to fill in this rough outline from time to time by particular studies of individual masters and the periods in which they played.

It is with deep regret that we have been unable to emphasize end game play, because of its increasing importance in modern strategy. 

We have been compelled to treat end game play only as part of the 3

We have dwelt little on general advice and so-called maxims, as we doubt the practical value of most generalities in the art of chess play. It is our belief that the surest method of acquiring chess skill is by playing over the best games of the modern masters.

Diagram 2 illustrates the moves of the Pawns and their method of capture.

The student will observe from the position of the Pawns that only one of these can capture with White moving first. The White Pawn marked (1) may capture the Black Pawn (2). No other Pawn may be taken by White. Should White move either his King's Pawn (3) or his Queen's Bishop's Pawn (4) two squares on the first move, Black may capture en passant with whichever Pawn is passed. (See Capturing En Passant.)

The Pawns, eight on each side, are placed on the second row directly on the squares in front of the major pieces. 

CAPTURING EN PASSANT 

The Pawn has the privilege on the first move of going two squares, but in case it is played beyond an adversary's Pawn which could

have captured it had it been moved only one square, the latter has the privilege of capturing it en passant (in passing). This applies only to the Pawns, as a piece cannot capture en passant either a Pawn or another piece.

PAWN PROMOTION 

A Pawn upon reaching the eighth square-what is commonly known as the King Row in checkers-may be promoted to any piece with the exception of a King. Thus a player may have three or four 

Queens, Rooks, etc., if he succeeds in pushing his Pawns to the eighth squares. The player must, however, claim a piece of higher denomination. A Pawn on the eighth square cannot remain a Pawn. 

In addition to the Pawns there are eight pieces on each side, a King, Queen, two Rooks, two Bishops, and two Knights. They are arranged on the board as shown in Diagram 1. Here they are in type:

THE BISHOP 

The Bishop moves any distance diagonally, provided the course is not obstructed. It has the power of capturing wherever it has a right to move. The Bishops always remain on the color squares on which they start. Thus a Bishop starting on a white diagonal will always move along the white diagonals. The White King's Bishop always plays on white squares, the White Queen's Bishop on black squares; the Black King's Bishop on black squares and the Black 

Queen's Bishop on white squares.

THE ROOK 

The Rook moves any distance at right angles; forward, backward, or sideways, but never diagonally. It also captures wherever it has a right to move.

THE QUEEN 

The Queen is by far the most powerful piece on the board since it combines the moves of the Bishop and Rook. It moves any distance, forward, backward, or diagonally, and captures wherever it has a right to move.

THE KING 

The King moves only one square at a time in any direction, forward, backward, or sideways. The King also captures wherever it has a right to move. The King has not, however, the power of capturing a piece which is protected by another piece, for by so doing he

THE KNIGHT 

The movement of the Knight is the most difficult of the chess pieces to explain. The Knight moves two squares in one direction and one square in the other; or one square in one direction and two in the other. In thus moving, the Knight has the power of jumping over either the pieces of his own color or those of the opposition. The 

Knight is the only piece which has the power to jump. It may be likened to the cavalry in the mimic battle. The diagram below shows the moves of the Knight:

Hence the Black Bishop (1) may be taken by the Knight. 

VALUE OF THE PIECES 

The Queen is worth, say, 10 Pawns; the Rook, 5; the Bishop, 31; the Knight, 31.

It is often good play to give up either a Knight or a Bishop for 3 Pawns; but it depends entirely upon the condition of your game. 

The Queen is often exchanged for three pieces; and more frequently for two Rooks.

The exchange of a Knight for a Bishop is considered equal. 

The rank of the pieces is: first, the Queen, the most valuable and powerful piece on the board; second, the Rook; third, the Bishop and Knight. Since the King cannot be captured it is hardly necessary to discuss its value.

TERMS USED IN CHESS 

The terms used in chess are as follows:

Pieces. The eight men on each side, excluding the Pawns, are called pieces; although in problems, etc., nowadays it is customary to call all the forces by the above term. 

Doubled Pawns.-Two Pawns on one file are called Doubled Pawns.

En Prise.-A piece is said to be en prise when it may be captured by your adversary.

The Exchange.-Winning the exchange means that one player has succeeded in exchanging a piece of less value for one of greater strength. 

Such as a Bishop for a Rook. 

False Move.-A move made contrary to the rules of the game. 

Forced Move.-A move you are compelled to make.

To Interpose.-To play a piece or Pawn between the attacking piece and your King.

Isolated Pawn.-A Pawn left on a file without other Pawns on the files next on either side. 

Minor Pieces.-The Bishops and Knights are called minor pieces, as they are not as strong as the Queen and Rooks, the major pieces. 

The Opposition.-A player is said to have the opposition when his 

King is opposite to the hostile King with only one square between them, and his opponent being compelled to move. The opposition in chess is the same as gaining the "move" in checkers.

Passed Pawn.-A Pawn which has advanced beyond the scope of your opponent's Pawns is called a Passed Pawn.

Discovered Check.-A check uncovered by the removal of a Pawn or a piece.

Smothered Mate.-Checkmate given to the King when he is completely blocked or smothered by his own pieces. It is given by the Knight.

CHECK 

Whenever the King is threatened by the opposition it is customary to call "check," which is the same as saying "I attack your King.'

When the King is attacked or placed in check, it must either be moved, the checking piece captured, or another piece interposed.

MODERN CHESS CHECKMATE 

When your King is so situated that you cannot move out of check, capture the checking piece, or interpose one of your own force between your King and the attacking piece, your King is checkmated and the game lost.

J'ADOUBE 

J'adoube (I adjust) is the term you are required to use to inform your opponent that you desire to touch a piece for the purpose of adjusting it, and not to make a legal move. 

TOUCH AND MOVE 

Once having touched a piece, unless you say J'adoube or words to that effect, you must move it if you can legally do so. 

If it is your turn to play and you make an illegal move, you must retract this move and the play may be treated under Rule 5 of the Revised American 

Chess Code.

"King Rook's Square" means the square in the lower right-hand corner originally occupied by the King's Rook; that is, the Rook on the King's side of the board. COUNTING 50 MOVES 

If your opponent persists in repeatedly giving check, or in making the same line of play, you can demand that the game shall be limited to 50 moves on each side, and if within that limit neither side wins, the game is drawn. When you have a lone King remaining on the board, you may insist on counting from that time, and after 50 moves claim a draw. This also applies when you have a King and Queen; a King and Rook; a King and Bishop, or a King and Knight, against a superior or an equal force.

CHAPTER III  

As soon as the student has mastered the simple elements of the game, he must acquire a good general knowledge of the openings. 

For this purpose, several simple variations from each of the openings are included. These have been brought up to date, and in several cases we have included an old, a modern, and an ultra-modern variation in the more popular openings. 

A few illustrative games are given also with annotations from the work of Dr. Sigmund Tarrasch, the German master. We have tried to avoid duplication of variations; hence many lines contained in subsequent games are omitted. The variations given by Dr. Tarrasch and his critical comments should be studied most carefully. 

THE GIUOCO PIANO 

This has been called the most simple of the openings, but it is simple only in the sense that the reasons underlying the moves are most obvious. It is, indeed, the most direct of the openings, but the positions resulting are among the most difficult in chess. The games of the parties are so evenly balanced, and the advantage of the opening move is so slight, that a single weak move is sufficient to turn the balance of power.

In its slower forms the Giuoco is essentially a battle of Knights. 

Placed at K B 5, a Knight almost invariably will enable a successful King's side attack. The K B also is especially valuable; the advance of the King's Bishop's Pawn is most effective. 

After the few opening moves White or Black by Q B-K 3 may offer the exchange of Bishops. By capturing the Bishops the K B file is opened for the attack upon the King. On the other hand, the adversary's center Pawns are weakened. (See note to Game I.) If, instead of capturing the Bishop, White retreats the Bishop to Kt 3, Black may take the Bishop, doubling White's Pawns, but also opening the Rook's file. The latter may be of service in the mid-game, but most probably the Rook will have to leave the file for the King's side attack. Nevertheless the open file may determine the end-game in 

White's favor.

MODERN CHESS THE RUY LOPEZ 

The Ruy Lopez, formerly the most popular of all the openings, is by no means easy of explanation. The different variations arising from Black's fifth move, B-K 2, Kt × K P ....., lead to games totally dissimilar.

White, by his third move, B-Kt 5, initiates an attack upon Black's K P. The attack is, however, only apparent, for Black can regain the Pawn without loss in position. But White, by his subsequent moves, all of which aid his development, threatens to convert the apparent attack into a real attack, and to capture the Pawn with impunity. Black is thus forced to make a series of cramping moves that leave him with anything but a free game.

At the same time White has developed his pieces and they are admirably placed for the attack upon Black's King position that follows. From B-Kt 5 or R 4 to Q B 2 is but a step for the Queen's 

Bishop, while the Q Kt has been brought over to the King's side for the same purpose.

This theory of the Ruy as an attack upon the King-position, masked by an apparent attack in the center, will aid in explaining many of the moves, but it should be repeated, the evolution of the opening has extended so far that the resulting positions must be considered specially by the student.

THE QUEEN'S PAWN GAME 

This is the most difficult of all the openings. By the advance of the Queen's Pawn a "close" game is produced; exchanges in the early part of the game are rare, and all the forces of both sides, both pieces and Pawns, are actively engaged.

Three lines of play are open, and each or all of these have been emphasized by the masters: (1) There is an attack upon the Castled 

King, which is facilitated by the effective position of the King's Bishop at Q 3 or at Q Kt, and the position of the Queen's Bishop at 

K Kt 5 or Q Kt 2. This was the idea of the first players of the modern school, notably Pillsbury, who utilized the Pin on the King's Knight with the Bishop most effectively. (2) By playing the Rook to Q B, opportunity is offered for an attack upon the Queen's Pawns. This aspect of the game has generally been stressed by Black, but only because his chances for a King's side attack are slight, and because of his frequent preponderance of Pawns on this side of the board.

(3) By Kt-K 5 a cramping attack in the center is obtained, which harmonizes with either the attack on the King or on the Queen's Pawns.

Both players must, therefore, be prepared to meet any or all of these attacks; hence, it can readily be seen that the Queen's Pawn advance leads to the most subtle and difficult of games.

CHAPTER IV THE DEVELOPMENT OF MODERN CHESS 

THE student has learned the essentials of the game, has analyzed several opening variations, and has played over the games given as examples of sound tactics. He is now prepared to grasp the principles which underlie the play of the modern masters. For this purpose, in the following chapters we shall give a short summary of the leading characteristics of the play of the masters since the days of Paul Morphy and Howard Staunton. However, the student can understand the methods of the modern experts better if we make clear the manner in which Tournaments are held and the work of the modern masters. 

The master is usually a man of keen intelligence who played chess at an early age, and early manifested striking chess talent. Fascinated by the game, he has devoted to it all his time and effort, and participates in the various Tournaments, supplementing this with giving exhibitions and lessons. He is familiar with all the important books on the game, knows practically all the Tournament and match games that have been played by the masters for nearly a hundred years, keeps in touch with any new lines of play that are worked out, is always studying the game and at these Tournaments tests his own ability against the best players in the world. His methods are comparable to those of the lawyer who is kept informed of the latest decisions of the courts, and attends the meetings of his Bar Association.

They are also comparable to those of the scientist or medical specialist, who is working in his own laboratory and at the same time keeps abreast of the latest discoveries of his co-workers through the various scientific journals.

The chess master is essentially a scientist, and the Tournament is the battle ground of new ideas. He knows practically all the old lines of play and unless he has a new line, he prefers to remain on solid ground. He wishes at all times to know exactly what he is doing and tries to avoid ventures into the unknown. Of course, many of the masters have their idiosyncrasies, but they have a great respect for each other, and play cautiously. Hence the great number of draws.

Further, at the early stage in the history of chess, the difference of ability between the players was greater than it is today. Brilliant games with colorful climaxes, which only take place after a great superiority has been established by one side, were very frequent. 

Today, with chess knowledge more widely disseminated and with 61

MODERN CHESS 

frequent contests, the differences between the masters are so slight that victories must be built upon the slightest advantages. Thus it has come about that the more spectacular play of the past is often lacking.

As a result of his talents, supplemented by study and master play, the chess expert reaches a high degree of skill, and the slightest misstep on the part of his adversary spells disaster. Knowing this, his own mental processes are different from the attitude of the average amateur or the college player. He knows that chess is full of the most unexpected surprises, and that defeat may lurk in the most harmless combination. Therefore, his opening play is an endeavor to remain within recognized lines and to avoid the thousand pitfalls that he knows exist at every step. Further, he is always wary of specially prepared lines or new ideas that may have been worked out by his opponent. Translated into the language of the law, his reaction to any move is a search for authority. His first impulse is to recall what previous masters have played in the same situation. His original thinking begins after he has mentally compared the different steps taken by the different masters in the past. 

The student should not, however, infer that the master player seeks safety at all times. There are a number of players whose slogan seems to be "Simplicity at all costs." This has been the notable characteristic of Capablanca. Emanuel Lasker, who held the championship of the world for twenty-six years, was also a great exponent of the simple style, never desiring to enter into complexities the outcome of which might be doubtful. But of Lasker it was said that he played P-K 4 with a view to the end game, and as confidence in his own powers is another characteristic of the chess master, Lasker always desired a full and complete battle. He wished to enter the mid-game with his pieces intact and so mobilized as to give scope to operations on a grand scale. And of this style and simplicity a contemporary master wrote: "It is pure limpid water-with a trace of poison in it." 

The younger generation of chess masters disdains simplicity. Filled with confidence in its own skill, it invites rather than avoids complications of the most extraordinary depth. Such a player is Alekhine. 

His contests against the older masters, intent on simplicity, form a great study. A notable game played by him against Capablanca in the Masters' Tournament at New York in 1927 is a good example of the younger master inviting complications in an endeavor to defeat the simpler style of the then champion. In that game simplicity repulsed the attack and triumphed, but Alekhine has scored many a splendid game since by just such tactics. The most recent opponent of simplicity is Nimzowitch, whose style is more hyper-modern than the hyper-moderns and can only be characterized as bizarre. The 

Danish master not only invites complications, but courts danger, and

THE DEVELOPMENT OF MODERN CHESS 

63 submits to weaknesses and pressure to obtain positions outside of the beaten paths and to avoid drawing variations, in the belief that he can negotiate all perils by his superior combination and position judgment. Such games are lost or won by the slightest margins, and a victorious attack is carried through by the master whose own position is perilously suspended on the brink of disaster. These games. are, of course, the most difficult in modern chess.

As he plays the opening, the expert has a general outline of the middle game positions that will result, and is also aware of the general type of ending he desires to obtain. The knowledge of the openings has continuously gone deeper and deeper, and the handling of midgame positions has been studied more intensively from year to year. 

The various methods by which masters in the past have turned certain small advantages in the mid-game into winning endings have been studied and constitute what is known as the technique of the game. 

With this wider knowledge the play has become more profound, and the advantages sought have become more subtle from decade to decade. Thirty years ago, one of the masters was thought tempermental because he retained his two Bishops at almost any cost, believing his two Bishops to be an advantage over the Knight and Bishop of his adversary. A decade later the exchange of one's Knight for his opponent's Bishop was considered a distinct advantage and was referred by one noted authority as "winning the minor exchange."

The major exchange, of course, is to win your opponent's Rook for your own Bishop or Knight. Today the possession of two Bishops against an opponent's Bishop and Knight is considered practically sufficient to force a win in the combined mid-game and end game play. 

The greater general knowledge of chess openings on the part of the public and its increasing familiarity with mid-game tactics, have led the master to emphasize end game play, which is most difficult of all. 

The recent work of Henri Rinck, of Professor Berger, and other noted end game composers has brought to chess masters generally a realization of the possibilities of this branch of the game, and today it is end game play which constitutes the difference between a grand master and an expert. The present British champion, Mir Sultan Khan, has brought to the attention of Europeans the greater emphasis upon end game play which exists in India. It is my belief that the end game constitutes the great unexplored territory of the game, and that the work of the masters of the next several decades will be to systematize and classify the different types of endings, and to evolve winning methods in various end game positions which are usually drawn today.

With this general outline, let us go back to days of Lopez and Philidor and examine the contribution of the great masters of the past to the development of modern chess, playing over several of their games and noting the peculiar quality of their work.

CHAPTER V DAMIANO, RUY LOPEZ, AND SALVIO 

IN 1813 a special volume was published in England embodying a translation of Damiano, Ruy Lopez, and Salvio. The volume is of great interest as Damiano was among the first to write a treatise on the study of chess, his work having been printed in Rome in Italian in 1524. The first edition of Ruy Lopez was printed in Spanish in 

1561. Its influence on the game can be gathered from the numerous translations which have been made. A French translation of 1655 is extant and apparently had a wide vogue. The work of Salvio was published in 1604.

The value of this volume is further increased by the comments of the editor, indicating the status of chess in 1813. 

To the modern expert, the openings and variations given have a strange and outworn appearance. These opening moves, however, are no indication of the playing strength of the ancients. It is in opening play that the greatest advances have been made from decade to decade, and once out of the openings, these authors would give modern experts a hard fight. These old games remind us that chess today has the accumulated experience and research of four centuries of chess players behind it, not taking into consideration an additional thousand years of play before such variations as are given by Damiano or Lopez were evolved. 

The actual playing strength of these men can be gathered by their mid-game play and by the end games and problems which have come down to us. Some of these contain ideas of extraordinary depth which have not been surpassed even today. In a sense, we must look upon these players and analysts as pioneers and discoverers, who in the sixteenth century were venturing out upon the then uncharted seas of chess.

I caution the student against playing too many of these old variations. It is my belief that playing strength is a matter of habit and that the imitative instinct is strong in chess as in other games like golf or tennis. The style of these old variations is archaic and they have an historical interest only. Intensive work should be done with the most modern games, and it is for this reason that these constitute the bulk of this volume. 64

CHAPTER VI MODERN TOURNAMENT STRATEGY 

THE modern chess Tournament remains one of the few encounters outside of physical athletics where men may battle upon equal ground, unaided by chance or adventitious help. The public generally believes these contests to be purely mental, but for the student who may some time take part in a chess Tournament, certain suggestions are given. 

In the first place, chess is as much a physical encounter as mental. 

A Tournament or match is a succession of daily battles, and unless the contestant is in the best physical condition, the strain on mind and body will be too great. A sound body as well as a sound mind is the pre-requisite of the chess master. Alekhine, Euwe, Lasker, Capablanca, etc. not only are splendid specimens of physical manhood, but have that co-ordination of mind and body which is typical of a 

Tyrus Cobb or a Robert T. Jones. All of these players keep in physical training for their contests. 

The task of playing for four or five hours is, however, a difficult one, and even the athletically trained find it difficult after hours of play, to secure the even balance of mental and physical action which the human body requires. For this purpose some kind of athletic activity coincident with the playing of the chess games is followed by many masters.

Chess is further a matter of temperament and judgment. The player who permits small incidents to get on his nerves, or allows the loss of a game to dishearten him or permits a bad break, such as frequently occurs in actual play, to affect his morale, will fair badly even though chessically he may have great ability.

In this as in other contests some players play better under pressure -"fighting heart" the athletes call it—and, of course, these qualities are essential for victory. The able chess player who allows the nervous excitement of the game to affect his play will be looked upon as a talented expert, but will rarely figure at the top in Tournament play.

In an interview many years ago, Emanuel Lasker stated that the masters were so evenly matched that in a great Tournament, victory might go to any of them, and the player who permits victory to go to his head will soon find himself at the bottom of the list. In chess as in other contests, victory is intoxicating, and many of the masters have confessed that after a succession of victories, they began to believe they were unbeatable, only to be brought sharply to earth 69

MODERN CHESS 

70 again by defeat properly administered. Tarrasch, whose observations on the psychological and human sides of Tournament play are most stimulating, has expressed the thought in a different manner. 

He says, "The chess master must not only be a strong player, but he must play strongly." The master who feels the game won with his first few moves will be brought to a greater respect for his opponent's abilities. Several of the masters, notably Zukertort and Janowski, filled with the arrogance that frequently comes to genius, made it a practice at times to play with practically no deliberation whatsoever, conveying to their opponent the thought that they can win with little effort. Such tactics disconcert an inexperienced player, and even win against a master occasionally, but in the long run lose more games than they win. Too little time spent on the opening moves very frequently means serious reflection over difficult positions later on in the game.

Chess today is played with a clock and the usual time of eighteen or twenty moves per hour, and even the slower time of fifteen moves per hour, afford little chance for profound thought at all stages of the game. Actually, most of the work of a chess master is done before the game begins. In this way, time is conserved from the opening moves to be used at some critical point in the game. The chess master sees very deeply instinctively, and yet in a critical position even half an hour for a single move is a short time. The numerous games which Rubinstein has lost by overstepping the time limit in complex positions indicates that such situations are difficult, even for the talented player.

Chess is an easy game to learn, but as played between the masters becomes an extraordinarily difficult contest. Dr. Lasker once remarked of a Masters' Tournament in Berlin, how surprisingly few games among the masters are free from some obvious error on both sides. The ability to play a single game of chess without some obvious error of judgment is granted to a surprisingly few chess masters in the entire world. In going over some of the games of recent Tournaments held in England, I was amazed at the number of games which were lost by simple mistakes, and this is true even of the greatest experts. This aspect of the game is covered more fully in a later chapter.

It is this the grand master has in mind when he states that he sees one or two moves ahead, for he knows the possibility of obvious oversights at any time. However, generally speaking, master play is profound and accurate and soundness is essential; for the slightest miscalculation in any combination will be discovered by the adversary. 

It should also be remembered that a good game of chess is a work of art, which can be played over for generations and enjoyed all over. the world at any time without losing any of its original freshness. The sole requirement is chess appreciation on the part of the audience.

MODERN TOURNAMENT STRATEGY 

71 As an artist, the real chess master deserves a high place in public esteem. His work deserves high rank among all the arts because it embodies not merely the creative genius of the artist, but the transcendent ability to conquer all opposition. A man may study chess all his life and be an excellent player, yet his games may never be regarded as part of chess literature, because in the contests with other masters of his day, he never has an opportunity to show his ability owing to a slight inferiority in playing strength. The artistic productions of many talented experts are therefore limited to games against weak players, and as such do not rate high in chess literature. 

It should further be noted that two parties are necessary to create a work of art in chess. The winner must play well, but the loser must offer the maximum of opposition at every point. Should he fail to do so, the game breaks down and footnotes of brilliant variations that might have taken place are a poor substitute for a real game. 

For this reason, the chess player should have a high regard for a worthy opponent and should be charitable toward the mistakes of other masters. This applies not only to those who play chess, but also to those who at important Tournaments are merely spectators. The latter also should have a healthy respect for the skill of the contestants. Often at important matches, one hears comments of the most curious nature. Moves over which the masters have studied deeply are criticized harshly by third-rate players and suggestions are offered which in most cases have little merit. That chess masters accept all these comments as good-naturedly as they do, is a tribute to their high intelligence, culture, and patience.

CHAPTER VII PHILIDOR (FRANÇOIS ANDRE DANICAN) 

(1726-1795) ONE of the most important treatises on the game was the work of Philidor which was published in 1749. Philidor was a talented musician and did much to increase the general popularity of the game. 

He played at the French court and spent considerable time in London, creating great interest in chess in England.

Philidor enunciated the principle that the "Pawns constitute the soul of chess." This will be apparent from the few variations of his work, and the single game illustrating his skill at blindfold play. 

These also indicate the progress which has been made in chess tactics since the days of Lopez.

The great power of the Pawns which Philidor sensed is not a feature of modern master play. Against weak players, the Pawns can be used after the manner of Philidor. In a later chapter, it will be indicated that the maneuver, P-K B 4 was a favorite of Morphy. 

However, these tactics are so well-known that they can rarely be played to advantage. Without a definite reward, the modern master is adverse to early advances of the Pawns as they commit this game irrevocably, and such early commitments are not favored today: further each Pawn advance creates weaknesses. 

The development of chess skill will be of interest to the expert, as the master goes through the same stages of development, unless he is schooled only in modern tactics. The average player at an early stage learns the value of the Pawns in his attacks, and only ceases such maneuvers at a further stage in his chess development. CHAPTER IX PAUL MORPHY 

(1837-1884) IN 1858 Paul Morphy, a native of New Orleans who had taken first prize at the American Chess Congress in New York the previous year, visited England and the Continent and defeated the most notable European experts by decisive scores. His supreme genius is recognized by the modern experts here and abroad. His games speak for themselves and indicate a chess talent far ahead of his time and generation. His mind instinctively recognized deep principles of chess play which the modern master has learned only through diligent study and research. His superiority over his contemporaries was undoubtedly greater than that enjoyed by any master since that time. 

The striking characteristic of his career is its brevity, showing that his talent was not a matter of slow growth, but was fully developed in all his games even in his first encounters with the leading American players at New York.

Modern critics have speculated a great deal as to how Morphy would have fared when subjected to the rigorous tests of present-day Tournament competition. In the strain of successive Tournament play, other qualities besides chess talent are important. Morphy showed a distaste for certain aspects of match and Tournament play, and a sensitiveness to trivialities that militate against continued victories in the stress of modern competition. It is also noteworthy that a champion becomes increasingly vulnerable after his games are carefully studied by his opponents, and particularly so once he begins to publish the story of his success and indicates to the astute reader some of the underlying principles of his play. Genius in chess, as in other fields, brings new ideas, but these are quickly absorbed by the other masters. This has happened to Capablanca. Steinitz and Lasker each reigned for a quarter of a century in spite of the fact that as champions they had to defeat not only the world but themselves. The characteristic style of a supreme master is, of course, easily imitated; even characteristic moves and idiosyncrasies become the stock in trade of every expert. Lasker's trick of placing a Rook on the third rank for maximum mobility; Capablanca's simple Q-K 3 are examples of this.

In addition, Morphy was an amateur and lacked the stimulus to continued competition which spurs on the professional from Tournament to 

Tournament. With the increasing number of Tournaments, a single success loses its overwhelming importance, and today every grand master has his collection of first prizes. Certainly no master today could offer odds of any kind to his contemporaries after the manner of Morphy. 

Morphy's career will always be an inspiration to chess players.

His games are classic and should be known by every student. 90

CHAPTER X GENERAL PRINCIPLES VERSUS SPECIFIC INFOR- MATION 

To acquire an elementary knowledge of the game of chess sufficient to appreciate the work of the masters is comparatively simple. The average high school graduate can do this in one year with proper instruction and guidance.

To become an expert is an entirely different matter. In chess, as in other games, to play at an early age is a distinct advantage; and it is very difficult for anyone to attain a mastery of the game unless it is taken up before the age of fifteen or sixteen. The masters have all acquired the essentials of the game as boys and have demonstrated an aptitude therefor. This has been supplemented by incessant study and practice, resulting in play without effort and without error, and an instinctive choice of proper strategic lines, and an easy grasp of the complexities of a position. 

Every expert knows that under time pressure, combinations cannot be "worked" out. The pieces simply move before the eyes of the master in their different rôles, and when any effort is required for this purpose, the master feels he is beyond his depth. As one expert expressed it, "I do not move the pieces; the pieces move themselves."

The player sits as the presiding genius over actors that enact their own rôles. This grasp of the chess board only comes with years of practice.

Occasionally, simple "systems" are advocated, designed to enable the student to win over all opposition. Any such system is simply a delusion and a snare. There is no easy road to a mastery of the game of chess. The same can be stated in respect to general advice, much of which is offered to the student. Advice to play carefully, to develop the pieces, to castle early, has the value of general maxims in most of the affairs of life. The general belief that one can become a champion in this way has been fostered by the attitude of some of the great experts. Capablanca has written of his immediate mastery of the game at a tender age, and in his book disclaims an early knowledge of chess literature. He has also affected a disdain for book knowledge. 

All of this, with due respect to his genius, is simply a pose. Other

American experts disclaim serious study, but actually play chess every day of their lives. Alekhine makes no such pretense. His natural 99

genius is surpassed by none: at sixteen he was champion of Russia, but to his natural ability is added continuous intensive study. 

The grim fact is that the process of becoming a great chess player is similar to the process of becoming a great pianist, a great billiard player, a great writer, or a great physician, with the additional fact that the field is open to the world, and that on the checkered board neither place nor ancestory, nor wealth plays a part. ability plus infinite capacity for work are both essential. 

Great natural

General principles are valuable and necessary and should be learned by the student to enable him to play a game that is comparable to that of the average amateur. In fact, a knowledge of these principles is more essential today than ever before, because these principles are so well known that ignorance of them is not tolerated in any chess circles.

However, a knowledge of general principles is not sufficient to win games. This must be supplemented by a thorough study of every branch of the game so that in a specific situation, the player will know not only the moves that are dictated by general principles, but that particular move which meets the requirements of the particular situation. This requires study of thousands of games and a specific knowledge whose place cannot be taken by a few maxims. 

Among the systems which have been enunciated, one of the most interesting is that put out by Franklin K. Young in a series of books which seek to express chess strategy in terms of military science. 

To explain his system Mr. Young has devised an elaborate and difficult nomenclature. The system begins with an explanation termed "Minor Tactics," in which White is advised to place his pieces in certain ideal positions, and Black is furnished with certain ideal defenses. The positions are interesting and worth knowing, but give the student an erroneous impression of the game. The idea that any particular formation of pieces can meet all eventualities, reduces chess to a simplicity that, alas, is not a characteristic of the game. Chess masters for generations have been evolving the disposition of their forces to meet attacks and defenses, and this constitutes the real study of the openings. To reduce these thousands of different positions to a number of ideal offensive and defensive positions it to find simplicity where simplicity does not exist.

A great many of the pages of this system are taken up with an exposition of facts so simple as to need no explanation. Whole pages and diagrams are devoted to illustrate the simple facts that a Bishop can capture a Rook, that a Knight commands several squares, and that a Pawn can attack two minor pieces simultaneously. The most elementary ideas are given hundreds of pages and most of the system is merely a pseudo-scientific repetition of the obvious. 

There is also a high sounding and formidable comparison between the science of chess and the science of war, which may be quite flattering

101 to chess players generally, but actually is not a fact. Chess is no more war than it is business, or law, or medicine, or stock market speculation. Certain general rules of human conduct which one may call good common sense permeate every line of activity. It is good chess as well as good business to accept one's losses quickly, to liquidate a losing position, to complicate when losing and simplify when winning. 

It is good tactics in the court room as well as on the chess board to launch an aggressive attack at an opponent's weakest point. It is higher strategy in both fields to restrain the attack against such weakness and simply use such slight advantage as a means of creating further weakness in the opponent's position, and to gradually convert such slight weakness into winning advantages. But such general ideas merely indicate that common sense can be utilized on the chess board as in every human situation. It does not mean that the great lawyer or general is thereby a great chess player, any more than he is thereby a great pianist. Such greatness may disclose artistic talent, but chess has its own special technique.

The use of military or of other special terms will confuse the student, and is ill-advised. Insofar as it indicates the author's resolution of chess strategy to some simple formula, it tends to hamper the development of real chess talent. In fact, any preconceived ideas which cause the player to pre-judge a position, restrain to that extent the full exercise of his power.

The following games with our notes will make clear that inflexible theories militate against good chess. Today you cannot move on general high-sounding principles.

CHAPTER XI STEINITZ, LASKER, AND TARRASCH 

AFTER the retirement of Morphy, William Steinitz, a native of Bohemia, became champion of the world by defeating Anderssen in 1866, and exerted a profound influence upon chess. His real contribution to the science of chess is well treated by Emanuel Lasker, in his most recent publication. During this period, Tournament play increased, and many new and brilliant names were added to the ranks of chess masters. Zukertort, one of the greatest (and fastest) 

Tournament players of all time, Blackburne, Winawer, Tchigorin, Kolisch, Mason, Schallopp, Gunsberg, are only a few of the names which figure prominently at this period. In the early 1880's, Dr. 

Sigmund Tarrasch, a young physician of Nuremberg, participated in a series of international Tournaments with remarkable success. His chess career has extended down to date, and he remains one of the most interesting figures in chess history.

A practicing physician, he has never allowed chess to interfere with his medical career, and at the same time has scored a series of successes which places him at the very forefront of the masters of history. Unfortunately, his own brilliance was a bit dimmed by the transcendent genius of Lasker, with whom he unsuccessfully contested two matches for the world's championship. Tarrasch, however, is a tremendous personality from every angle. He is the kind of master of whom chess can be proud, indeed. His book "Three Hundred

CHAPTER XII SPACE AND TIME 

THE student who has played over the games included in the first part of this volume will find the difference between these and the modern games somewhat mystifying. The sharp differences between the opposing positions seem to disappear in modern play, and a general equality, often a symmetry or balance of position exists for a long time until one of the opposing forces attains a definite advantage. 

The student will understand these tactics more clearly if he will keep in mind that the great emphasis of the older schools was upon the element of time. For this reason Pawns and even pieces were surrendered with a generosity that is repugnant to the modern master. 

However, the most hypermodern of present-day experts understand fully the importance of time, and given the slightest advantage in this element, can translate it into victory in the most scientific manner. 

Pillsbury, Charousek and Capablanca have been the foremost exponents of the importance of the time element in the modern game. 

With the increased knowledge and greater technique of the past two decades, the art of successful defense has been strengthened and a new emphasis, stressing the idea of space, becomes apparent in the play of the great masters.

Thus it has come about that the modern chess experts, particularly in the opening and mid-game, seek to command as much of the board as possible, feeling confident that in the ensuing complications the side that governs most of the squares with its pieces or Pawns will emerge victorious.

The proper co-ordination of space and time would seem to be the ideal strategy, but there is frequently a conflict between them and in positions where the choice must be made between a permanent advantage in space and the advantage of time, the master today, generally speaking, prefers space, whereas two decades ago time would be the paramount consideration. The thought is that a disadvantage of time can be made up and restored in the future play; that with proper defense a temporary disability only is incurred; but advantages in space are often permanent and if they can be secured, will endure throughout the game. This is particularly true of advantages which arise from the Pawn skeleton, and the struggle for these advantages forms the opening battle of most modern games. 116

SPACE AND TIME 

117 With the emphasis on space, the values of the different squares have been studied, and it has been found that the central squares are of the greatest importance. That the occupation of these squares by one's pawns or pieces has long-range future benefits that cannot be definitely pointed out, is the creed and faith of modern chess science. 

I doubt if this is as new as it seems when stated in new terms by such chess experts, notably Nimzowitch and Reti. Expert chess players have always sensed the importance of centralization, but have never expressed this technique in the exact terms of its most recent exponents. In chess as in other fields the restatement of well-known ideas in a new terminology is often put forth as new doctrine. The real contribution of such work is a more pure and accurate definition and understanding of the old ideas-and for this the hypermodern school deserves real credit.

CHAPTER XIII PILLSBURY, CHAROUSEK, BREYER, RETI, AND TORRE 

IN 1895 great interest was aroused in the chess world by the victory of Harry Nelson Pillsbury, of Boston, in the International 

Tournament at Hastings, in which were entered the leading players of the world. The field included Lasker, Tarrasch, Steinitz, Tchigorin, and practically every grand master of the day. Not since the days of Morphy was there such excitement chessically on this side of the 

Atlantic. Pillsbury's subsequent career fulfilled the promise of this splendid achievement. He emerged in a tie with Tarrasch at Vienna and figured at or near the head of the list in every Tournament in which he took part in his brief career. In simultaneous play, he excelled, and his blindfold play set a new record for his time which has been surpassed only by Alekhine and Reti. Within a short time, however, Pillsbury was stricken with a fatal disease. He has left behind him a record that is second only to Morphy's. Like Morphy, a brief span of years covers his entire chess career, and yet his brilliant games fill several large volumes. 

To a lesser degree, the career of Rudolph Charousek, brilliant son of Hungary, parallels that of Pillsbury. He, too, was noted for his attacking style, and in his brief career he played many notable games of which we have record. 

Among the moderns, Richard Reti and Julius Breyer were the leaders in developing what are known as hypermodern ideas in chess. 

Both were tremendously strong players of striking originality, and both have left their mark on the game.

In the last few years, Carlos Torré, a young Mexican, came to 

New York and impressed the experts of Manhattan as a real chess artist. He was forthwith sent to the Tournaments at Marienbad,

Baden-Baden, and Moscow, and at the age of twenty-two indicated that he was possessed of chess talent in a superlative degree. At

CHAPTER XIV , RUBIN- STEIN, VIDMAR, SPIELMANN, TARTAKOWER 

ARON NIMZOWITCH, long a leading competitor in international chess, has developed remarkably in the last few years, and today is just a shade below Alekhine and Capablanca in strength. Like 

Alekhine, he is an enthusiastic devotee of the game, and in his writings, the contest on the board becomes a living battle. For each of his pieces, he has real affection and sees moral and ethical values in the conflict of the forces.

Akiba Rubinstein a decade ago was looked upon as the successor of Lasker. He is still a formidable opponent, but has not quite kept the pace of the younger masters. 

E. Bogoljubow scored a notable victory at Moscow in 1925, and his performance against Alekhine in their match showed him a dangerous competitor.

Dr. Milan Vidmar is considered by many the strongest amateur in the world. He is an engineer by profession, and has written technical books in his field and has made his mark as professor of engineering science at the University of Jugo-Slavia. His game is characterized by its inherent soundness. 

Géza Maróczy, a native of Hungary, is also an engineer of high standing. His chess career dates back thirty to forty years, and includes many a notable triumph against the best. After his victory at Monte Carlo in 1903, he was hailed by many as the coming champion. His style is classic and worthy of close study. Rudolph Spielmann, of Austria, recently added a first prize in a major tournament to his many victories, which include two sensational games against Capablanca. S. Tartakower is gifted with one of the most brilliant chess minds, but inclines toward the bizarre in style. CHAPTER XV BRILLIANCY 

OUR purpose in noting the numerous instances where the masters have gone astray is not intended to reflect upon their ability, but simply to emphasize that to play correctly in a national or international Tournament is an accomplishment of extraordinary difficulty. 

In fact, under the time limit of fifteen to twenty moves per hour, the expert at the same time sees very deeply into the position, and in another sense merely scratches the surface of the numerous combinations that exist. I believe all experts who re-examine their games after actual play frequently find important variations that escaped their attention. In many positions, proper lines easily suggest themselves and an expert should not be censured for adopting a winning line whether it wins as quickly or as artistically as another road to victory. In complex situations, however, with no obvious continuations, the expert in actual play is guided by a sixth sense which serves him better than complete analysis outside of playing hours. 

The other side of the picture is more important. At every Tournament, games of wonderful depth and precision are brought forth, and a list of games which have been awarded brilliancy prizes would form many interesting volumes. Brilliancy per se means little, especially where the opposition is weak. It is brilliancy in master play against strong resistance that is important. In the following game played in

The recent publications of Dr. Emanuel Lasker are full of thoughtprovoking comments on the art of chess by the former world champion. 

Lasker is a philosopher, and finds in chess the triumph of those philosophic ideas of struggle about which he has written for many years. 

Certainly no mistake is made in reading in the game of chess those strategic principles which underlie every vocation and every aspect of life. Such is the principle of proportion beautifully expressed by 

Lasker. Briefly, it means that the immediate objective should be proportioned to one's position, equality justifying an effort to turn the position slightly to our advantage, a slight advantage justifying an effort to obtain a larger advantage, and only decisive superiority permitting the bold bid for a win that generally culminates the game. 

Viewed rightly this principle reveals some of the mental processes underlying master chess in contrast to the game of the amateur. 11

CHAPTER XVIII ALEXANDER ALEKHINE 

IN 1919 the author was playing over some games with E. S. 

Jackson, Jr., a strong amateur, who stated that he had discovered a player whose style he believed to be the most profound of living experts. The reference was to Alexander Alekhine, one of whose games we were analyzing. The statement proved to be prophetic, for in less than a decade, Alekhine became champion of the world by defeating Capablanca at Buenos Aires in a match of thirty-four games. 

His performance at that time speaks for itself, particularly if we recall that at New York in 1927, Capablanca had emerged victorious after an exhibition of chess that indicated that he was at the very height of his ability and in the judgment of many critics, unbeatable. 

Also noteworthy that for fifteen years in match and Tournament play, Capablanca had not tasted defeat except at rare intervals, notably at the hands of Rubinstein at San Sebastian in 1911, by Lasker and Tarrasch at St. Petersburg in 1914, and by Reti at New 

York in 1924. In fact, a decade had elapsed between the two defeats at St. Petersburg and New York. The term "Capablanca fright" had been coined by European masters to indicate the mental condition of his opponents when seated opposite an apparent chess machine. 

At Havana, Lasker had been unable to capture a single game, and the fate of Kostics, who suffered five successive defeats at Havana, was enough to awe a less courageous master than Alekhine. That match is now history, and we have included the thirty-second game showing the obvious superiority of Alekhine in this contest. 

These two masters will, however, soon meet again for the world's championship, and a comparison between them is of interest. At 

Buenos Aires, Capablanca's unexpected defeat in the first game no doubt affected his morale, and only in a few of the games does the real Capablanca appear. CHAPTER XIX THE BALANCE OF POSITION 

ONE of the most difficult problems the amateur chess player faces is this. He plays a game with a master, and after twenty or thirty moves, he finds himself in a grave position, that soon goes to pieces. 

Re-examination of his moves fails to reveal any obvious mistake. Yet the fact remains the position obtained was bad. 

This situation arises in two ways. In the more open attacking games he failed to make the exactly proper defense which best chess theory declares adequate to meet the particular attack. Other moves are not "bad"; they are inferior and the experience of the masters has been that under the gradual pressure of the attacking forces, the defense position slowly disintegrates. The attack gets too much space-there are just too many subtle threats to defend-a number of not obvious tactical considerations lead to the final breakdown.

In the more modern type of close game, the debacle is brought about in a more subtle manner. Certain strong lines are opened for the attacking pieces--certain pivotal squares are commandeeredand unless the effect of each of these maneuvers is overcome and counteracted step by step, a losing position results. If one's opponent gains an open file, that file must be disputed, or some tactical advantage must be gained offsetting this. Otherwise, the small gain brings greater gains in its wake, and the result is a decided superiority of position. 

The most disconcerting dilemna is to find oneself suddenly the victim of a strong attack that has come out of a clear sky. The master senses these attacks long before they are dangerous. They result from the strategic grouping of the opposing forces; the pieces are gradually massed against one or more points on the board. Hence, as soon as a single piece is brought to bear against the King or a weak point, the master moves to equalize by bringing his own pieces to the same point. Long experience indicates that a number of attacking pieces can do little damage when opposed by an equal number of active co-operating fighting units.

THE BALANCE OF POSITION 

175 This process of maintaining the balance of position is the distinguishing mark of modern chess, and explains the large number of drawn games among the masters. To draw in the international chess arena is a feat requiring the most finished chess technique. Draws do not occur by chance or by exhaustion of opposing forces or because one side is left with the advantage of a Knight or Bishop. They result from the scientific opposing of force to force-unit to unit-and the equalization of every opposing step by another equal in degree if not in kind.

The process goes a bit further-Nimzowitch calls it "overprotection" of weak points that are likely to be attacked or to be the center of future battle. It is precaution; a sinking fund for emergencies: it indicates time at one's disposal for these things; it is a subtle weapon, and of course, can be overdone, resulting in defensive rather than offensive play.

The sense of balance cannot be acquired in a day or a year. It comes from a keen appreciation of the "weight" of the pieces individually and collectively. It differentiates the master from the studious book player. The following maxims result from the foregoing:

An attack against the hostile position not predicated on some advantage we have or some disadvantage in the defensive forces must fail. 

First acquire some superiority in position-then attack.

Generally, attack should be directed on the side of the board where our advantage lies—e. g., where we have a majority of Pawns.

Such an attack should be countered by counter-attack on the side of our own preponderance of force.

But where the adversary has already begun his attack, counterattack on the other flank will probably be too late. Hence, a flank attack is better met by attack in the center if possible. The flank attack may have no effect on the first attack, but the central pressure will soon cause itself to be felt.

CHAPTER XX  

AFTER the student has acquired a certain proficiency in the game and matches his skill against opponents of real strength, the depth of possible maneuvers on the board will become clear. Between masters of high rank, a struggle of a peculiar character takes place before one or the other acquires a material advantage. This can only be defined as a contest to obtain the maximum co-ordination of the forces, and because this is one of the most difficult parts of the game, it has received little treatment at the hands of annotators and is pervaded with great mystery, even for the expert.

The talented amateur and master have developed a fine sense of position, and have a predilection for certain openings and certain groupings of their forces. But the underlying reasons why with equal forces and with no particular advantages of time or space, one side finds it difficult to make progress or to effect a definite plan, while the other side brings its forces easily into fruitful action, have thus far escaped real analysis.

The fundamentals, of course, are well known. The pieces should not impede each other, open files for the Rooks, important diagonals for the Bishops, central squares for the Knights, pieces grouped-not scattered, no open lines of entrance for the opposing forces. Every tyro learns these things quickly.

To go a bit further, open files for the Rooks may mean one of several things. Against a hostile King or a loose piece, or some palpable weakness, the possession of the open file is an end in itself. It confers an appreciable advantage that may directly result in victory. 

This is the less frequent result of the intial acquisition of such a file. 

In many cases the open file is only a means to an end; it is an open road, a means of communication, and its value depends upon its length and quality. If the end of the file is a stone wall, it has little value. The same is true of the diagonal of the Bishop. Against the 

King's fianchetto, Bishop at Q 3 has little value, the target is too strongly defended. In such case, the Bishop is better placed at K 2 or K Kt 2, the first particularly if there is a possibility of the advance of the King's Rook Pawn.

For the same reason, the Queen is a poor piece as the advance guard of the attack. Such work is best done by the Pawns, the 176

Knights, and the Bishops, in the order named. Pawns, however, are slow of movement, and Pawn attacks are only successful if combined with pieces and with other threats that hold the opponent as they lumber slowly up to the scene. 

Because of their tremendous scope in combining with other pieces, the Knights are the most deadly weapons of offense against the castled King. The Rooks suffer from the same disadvantage as the Queen, and in addition, their moves are too simple and direct, and hence too easily parried. The use of the Rooks as a battering ram against the 

King is too cumbersome, and except in cases of definite weakness, a Rook to one's third in front of the Pawns for attack is poor strategy. 

The open files are principally a means to an end, for the Rooks a path to the sixth or seventh rank, where an attack against the Pawns or the hostile King can be directed. The same is true of the Bishops and Knights which from the central squares attain the maximum power.

The problem of obtaining the maximum co-ordination between these pieces will become clearer once certain principles are emphasized. 

This problem, incidentally, is very much at the fore today since the first game of the match between Capablanca and Alekhine at Buenos Aires. In their forthcoming battle, the task of these super-experts will be to attain positions where their own forces co-operate, and the co-operation of their adversaries is limited.

The ability to anticipate where the decisive battle will occur and the exact nature of that fight requires not only experience and study, but in addition, imagination and genius. To maneuver so that the fight will occur on prepared ground is also strategy of the highest order. 

3. The third principle becomes evident. It must be clear that there is no such thing as an ideal or good disposition of our forces, unrelated to our opponent. Our forces are well placed or otherwise depending upon how they bear upon the hostile forces. In short, it is the interrelation of our pieces with the enemy that is the essence of every chess position.

In a celebrated game, Capablanca vs. Scott, Scott had an ideal position that would gladden the heart of Mr. Young. But in relation to the forces of Capablanca, his position had no meaning at all. Scott was marshalling his divisions on the parade ground, not in actual combat. Even an expert may wonder why with pieces so well disposed, Scott lost. The answer is that with special reference to his opponent's plan, Black's pieces were non-existent. They were well arranged for an imaginary attack but were of no value in meeting the actual attack of Capablanca. They were mobilized for offense-when defense was the rôle of Black.

CHAPTER XXI  

IN most athletic contests, the danger of pressing is recognized. 

In golf, a graceful, easy, rhythmic swing is not only desirable but essential; and the golfer who tries to get unusual length from his clubs or strains to make up a lost hole will fare badly and score worse, not better than usual. Grace and rhythm in billiards, tennis, are readily understood. Because chess is principally a mental contest, it seems strange to speak of grace and ease and rhythm in connection therewith. Yet these are the real essentials of master chess play.

These qualities indicate chess genius, because they bespeak selfconfidence, a thorough grasp of the position and a mastery of the complications inherent in the game. They contrast strongly with the nervous tension of the expert who, not having studied thoroughly the variation being contested, is overwhelmed by the manifold complexities, and lacks the sureness of the other. Such a player attempts to win too quickly, and his moves are hurried and strained. To the master, a chess game is a full-length drama in three acts: the opening, the mid-game, and the end-game; and the master knows that he will have ample opportunity to display his superiority. 

The opening is a battle ground of chess knowledge. Here an ounce of accurate information is worth a pound of finesse. Fortunately, books and previous study of the openings or of a particular variation do not tell the entire story, as Mir Sultan Khan has demonstrated, and a resourceful player can generally steer clear of the many pitfalls of the openings. But a real knowledge of the openings is a distinct asset and particularly helpful-if only for one's nerves. However, chess has its compensations and the player who is a book student of traps and opening stratagems and hopes to overwhelm his opponent with some deadly variation, loses in real chess strength, and the mental attitude resulting will prevent him becoming a first-class master. 

Such a player sets out to win in the first fifteen moves, and betrays the utmost chagrin on finding that victory can be achieved only by real work throughout a long game.

Where both experts understand fully the variation they are discussing, neither may obtain any advantage, and the mid-game will be entered on equal terms. Should one of them obtain a slight opening advantage, it will be sufficient to bring with it some additional advantage in the mid-game, and slight as this may be, carried into the end-game, it will bring victory. This is the story told by the finest games of modern chess.

Capablanca figured in many games where the gradual pressure of his position has resulted in the collapse of his opponent. In a sense, these games are lost by his opponent. Such a description, however, only tells half the story. It is easy to find the proper continuation against an opponent who exposes himself; against simple precise chess, the task of playing becomes tremendous, and it is for this reason so many of these errors occur in games against Capablanca. They also indicate that if we play correctly, the gods will present us with many a game without further effort. Nor should we be captious in our judgment of the master who accepts these gifts of the gods. Playing chess is hard work, and the master is not to be blamed if he takes these little gifts with a smile and enjoys the respite thus gained. Such games, of course, have little value in chess literature, but if every victory had to be hewed out classically with new and original strokes, even genius would tire. The inspiration that makes possible real works of art is not a running stream that can be tapped daily.

During the last twenty years, Capablanca has contested in successive Tournaments and his games form a series of classics, noted chiefly for their grace and simplicity. This simplicity is, of course, the result of that art which conceals art, and the games show such a grasp of the essence of the openings played, that each of them seems the embodiment of a single theme. An examination of these games discloses that simplicity and pure logic are the essentials of Capablanca's style. The real value of his games as works of art lies in their frequent contribution of a single new idea to our chess technique. Merely winning a game is not sufficient to create an artistic product. Certain errors on the chess board have been made and atoned for innumerable times in the past, and a game that merely covers similar ground holds little interest for the sophisticated chess public. It is noteworthy that while his games have frequently consisted of his obtaining a good position and thereafter a blunder by his opponent, the number of games that exemplify a new and striking theme (often one of extraordinary simplicity) is probably the largest contributed by any master in the history of the game. In the Nimzowitch partie, it is the use of the Q B file for his

CHAPTER XXII  

ONE of the most difficult parts of chess is to convert a slight winning advantage into victory. The reasons for this are twofold. In the first place, the effort to obtain an advantage has usually been great and with the objective gained, a sense of relaxation follows. An indifferent move at this point will frequently throw away an advantage gained after hours of hard work. In the second place, the opponent who is at a disadvantage fights desperately and having nothing to lose, invites complications that throw the result of the game in the balance. From every angle, the attainment of a small advantage must mean increased rather than lessened efforts if it is to be translated into victory. On the chess board, as in other things, eternal vigilance is the price of success.

Very few of the experts, even among the masters, play with precision in a difficult end game. In their treatment of difficult endings, all the masters flounder about a great deal, and correctness and accuracy are at a premium here.

CHAPTER XXIV DEFENSIVE STRATEGY 

CONFIDENCE is the prime requisite of successful defense. This arises from the belief that one has played correctly, has maintained the balance of the position, and has created no weakness in his game. 

Under such circumstances, some of the masters, notably Capablanca and Nimzowitch, welcome the attack of their opponents, certain that it will be repulsed, and that in the subsequent reaction, their own pieces will come strongly into play.

In his match with Marshall, it was Capablanca's defense in difficult positions which stamped him as a master of the highest rank. 

The limitations of a single volume compel us to treat the art of defense most briefly. Defending a position against a hostile attack remains one of the hardest tasks of the chess player. To withstand even a simple attack requires play of high order. Against the complex attack of a master, genius alone can find the proper lines.
