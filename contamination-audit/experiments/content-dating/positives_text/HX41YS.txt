PREFACE 

The Education Department of Hadassah takes pleasure in issuing Hebrew: the Story of an Old-New Language. This engrossing study describes the Hebrew language as "the key to the cultural history of our people." It traces the earliest beginnings of Hebrew as the tongue of our ancestral desert-wanderers, then through its great flowering in the Bible and its dramatic struggle for survival against the languages of dispersion, until it burgeons forth anew in the Zionist renaissance. 

The author, Mrs. Murray Sarlin, Hadassah's national chairman of Hebrew studies, is a graduate of the Hebrew University in Jerusalem and brings to her work a rich store of knowledge and conscientious research in original sources. Hebrew: the Story of an Old-New Language is fascinating study material in itself. 

It is also an excellent stimulus to serious study of the Hebrew language, and should therefore be "required reading" for every member enrolled in a Hebrew study group. In addition, we recommend its use for feature presentations at general meetings, as a basis for discussion at board meetings, and as a serial column for chapter bulletins. Finally, its intrinsic interest for the general reader should be emphasized.

"For four reasons was Israel redeemed from Egypt: among them, that they did not change their tongue, and they did not change their names- Reuben and Simon went down into Egypt and Reuben and Simon came up from Egypt." - from the Midrash

Just as the Jewish people was never wholly destroyed, so also its language never died. Like a life-giving underground stream, Hebrew nourished the roots of Judaism during the years of travail. With the return of the exiles to their homeland, the Hebrew language issued above ground, vigorous and undiminished. 

Jewry had always drawn its strength for the struggle against extinction from its cultural and linguistic heritage. Pages of Jewish history record communities which forgot the use of Hebrew. Thus sundered from their fellow Jews they disappeared from sight-their deep attachment to their faith notwithstanding. Modern Zionism was the logical conclusion of those who understood the importance of land and culture as instruments for the preservation of the Jewish people. 

Today, a common cultural inheritance forges the strong bond of sympathy between American and Israeli Jewry. "Culture" has been called a "communicable intelligence"; and the primary agency of communication is language. Without language, communication is impossible. Language is, therefore, the key to the cultural history of our people. It is unlikely that utilitarian Hebrew alone will provide this key. For Hebrew is not only a language, it is a historical complex-the result of a cultural and religious history of thousands of years. It is this woof of the Hebrew language running through the warp of Jewish history that we must discern in order to enter into our Judaic heritage. 1

The Origin of the Alphabet 

When the ancient Egyptian wished to write, he drew a picture of his idea, called a hieroglyph. Since each idea or object required a separate symbol, the written language acquired thousands of charac- পপ ters, and deciphering it was a complex and arduous process. In Mesopotamia (see 

Frontispiece) the ancient 

Babylonians and Assyrians

Egyptian Hieroglyph and Assyrian Cuneiform used wedge-shaped or cuneiof the Word "Head" form writing. Somewhere between Egypt and Mesopotamia there lay buried in the earth, among the debris of ruined forgotten cities, those broken shards, tablets and pottery whose mysterious scratch marks would reveal the first alphabet in the world. 

And indeed, in the Sinai peninsula, inscriptions were found dating back to 1500 B.C.E. and closely resembling hieroglyphics—but with an important difference. Instead of the multiplicity of symbols usually found in hieroglyphic writing, only twenty-two symbols were used. 

At the beginning, because of the limited number of these symbols, it was thought that here at last was the first true alphabet, in which each symbol represented not an object but a sound. It was the combination of sound-symbols that represented words. In trying to discover what sounds the symbols in the Sinaitic inscription represented, the archaeologists gave every character the sound of the Hebrew letter that had a corresponding meaning. Thus the hieroglyph of a human head, as it appeared in the Sinai inscription, was given the sound r because in Hebrew a head is rosh, to which is attributed the origin of the letter resh.

The Alphabet, maintains that the alphabet originated not in the desert of Sinai, but in 

Palestine, an area admirably suited to its creation. Palestine, itself possessed of a highly developed culture, served as a bridge between the civilizations of Egypt and Mesopotamia, and was the meeting place of various cultures through their emissaries, the traders and merchants. The Hebrew Alphabet 

The early Hebrew alphabet had twenty-two consonants, and was written from right to left. Like other Semitic languages it was based on roots composed only of consonants. In speech, various vowel sounds were interchangeably inserted to indicate such details as tense, part of speech, and mood.

Today's printed Hebrew alphabet is unlike early Hebrew. Those older forms were employed not only by the Jews, but by other 

Semitic peoples of that time-Moabites, Phoenicians, Canaanites,

Edomites and Aramaeans. It is known to us as Ktav Ivri, Hebrew script. This writing is believed to have existed in Palestine at least 

1800 years before the destruction of the Temple.

Numbers in Hebrew 

The Jews originally used special signs to signify numbers. It is not known when they began to apply the letters of the alphabet for counting and arithmetic. The dates on the Maccabean coins are given in letters, and it is believed that this custom had already prevailed for many hundreds of years. After the Maccabean period, the letters of the alphabet were used by Jews to represent numbers in mathematics and engineering till past the Middle Ages.

Hebrew in Biblical History 

According to Biblical tradition one of the sons of Noah was Shem, from whom were descended a group of peoples consequently called 

Semites. Assyrian, Babylonian, Aramaean, Syrian, Phoenician, Carthaginian, Arab and Hebrew were all cousin Semites. Their languages, similarly related, are the Semitic languages, and Hebrew is one of them. ما

Hebrew was spoken not only by the Hebrews, but also by a number of peoples in and around Palestine, among them the Canaanites and the Moabites. These other groups having vanished from the earth, only the descendants of the Hebrews remain as speakers of Hebrew, and Hebrew is thus thought of as their specific language. 

The original inhabitants of Palestine were called Canaanites, and the land Canaan. It is thought that about the 19th century B.C.E. 

Canaan was infiltrated by a tribe led by Abraham coming from the general direction of Mesopotamia, of Aram Naharayim - Aram of the Two Rivers (see Frontispiece). In the Bible, Abraham is called 

Ivri, because, as our scholars explained, he came from across the river, m'ever hanahar. The Jews were therefore called the people of ever, "from across," or Ivrim - Hebrews, and their language, sfat ever, the language of ever or Ivrit - Hebrew. The word Ivrim, therefore, has a geographic connotation only.

It is not clear whether the Ivrim brought the Hebrew language with them, or whether they acquired it from the Canaanites who already spoke it. The history of Hebrew before the conquest of 

Canaan is still obscured by archaeological debate. With the conquest of Canaan about the 13th century B.C.E., however, its development can be studied more objectively, although there is no unanimity of opinion on any phase of the Hebrew language. 

Through the period of the Judges and Kings (12th-10th centuries B.C.E.) Hebrew was the common tongue of all the Israelites, although differences of pronunciation existed between the northern and southern parts of the country. In the 8th century B.C.E., however, Assyria conquered the northern kingdom of Israel, exiled most of the population, and in its place settled a people whose tongue was Aramaic. 

Influence of the Aramaic Language 

These Aramaic-speaking peoples came from a region called Aram --known to us today by its Greek name-Syria. They had composed a loose confederation of states that had been politically conquered by Assyria. So potent, however, was their civilization that culturally and economically they remained ascendant. By 700 B.C.E. all Syria and a great part of Mesopotamia spoke Aramaic. A thousand years after the political decay of the Aramaeans, their language was still powerful. It is still spoken today. Only Arabic, supported by the weight of its religion, Islam, could destroy it. 

The Aramaic influence on Jewish life became very great. Over a long period of time it dominated Hebrew to such a degree that it supplanted it as the language of daily living and was second to Hebrew only in religious and literary life. It seems quite miraculous that 

Aramaic did not completely obliterate Hebrew. The father of modern 

Hebrew, Eliezer Ben-Yehuda, attributes the salvation of Hebrew to the fact that Aramaic itself had to compete for existence with Greek. 

We today still feel the authority of Aramaic. It is the language of the Kaddish, of the ketubah (marriage contract), of the get (divorce). 

The greatest part of the Talmud is in Aramaic, and it remains in the passages from the Passover Hagadah, "This is the bread of affliction" -Ho lachmah anyah, and in the Chad Gadyah, the famous song about the goat that father bought for two zuzim. It is in our daily Hebrew speech in such words as medeenah—state, and sar—a cabinet minister.

The bar in bar mitzvah is also Aramaic (the Hebrew equivalent would be ben), as is the word mar which has long been used for "Mr.", though it is now being replaced by the Hebrew word adon. 

With the kingdom of Israel destroyed politically, the greater part of its population removed, and the land inundated by a strange people and language, it was natural that those Jews remaining in the north of Palestine should begin to speak Aramaic. In the still autonomous kingdom of Judah in the south, however, the people continued to speak Hebrew. The story is told that when Sennacherib "came down like the wolf on the fold" and besieged Jerusalem in Judah, his envoys demanded the surrender of the city by addressing themselves in Hebrew to the men guarding the walls. The Judean officers pleaded with them to speak Aramaic, so that the people might not understand the request.

After the destruction of the First Temple by Babylonia in the 6th century B.C.E., the people remaining in Judea continued to speak 

Hebrew, though the threat of Aramaic had become serious. Those who were exiled to Babylonia slipped easily into Aramaic. Although in Babylonia the official tongue of the government was Assyrian, Aramaic was the language of the masses, of commerce and diplomacy. 

This, together with its similarity to Hebrew, made it difficult to resist. 

Eliezer Ben Yehuda maintained that in Babylon it was the richer Jew, tending to assimilate, who would surely acquire Aramaic, whereas the poorer Jew was less likely to leave his old ways and therefore held more firmly to the Hebrew tongue. As a result, when the Persian Cyrus defeated the Babylonians and allowed these exiled Jews to return to Judea (about 530 B.C.E.) it would be less likely the wealthy, assimilated Aramaic-speaking Jews who returned, but rather the more nationalistic Hebrew-speaking Jews, poorer materially but richer spiritually.

Therefore, with the return from Babylonian exile and the start of the period of the Second Temple, Hebrew was again spoken in Judea and we hear the voices of the later prophets like Haggai, whose Hebrew is indistinguishable from that of the period of the First 

Temple. 

The Babylonian captivity left its mark upon the Hebrew language. 

The names of the Jewish months and of the angels are derived from the ancient languages of the Assyrians. The name of the first modern all-Jewish city, Tel Aviv (Hill of Spring) originated in Babylonia.

Biblical history was now drawing to a close. The books of that period (about 250 B.C.E.), written in the literary Hebrew of the day, no longer resemble Biblical writing, but are regarded as the beginning of modern Hebrew. The books of Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, Ecclesiastes and Ben Sirah close the period of the Bible. 

By the end of the Biblical period Aramaic had captured the daily speech of the Jews in Palestine and was the language of the Jews in Babylonia. The knowledge of Hebrew, however, was kept alive through translations of the Bible, the study of the law, the teachings of the prophets, and through the creation of houses of prayer, later known as synagogues. 

Hebrew and Greek

Great political upheavals took place in the world. In 335 B.C.E. 

Alexander defeated the Persians, and with his own death Palestine came under the rule of the Egyptian Ptolemies. With the Greek soldiery came the Greek language, Hellenizing the occupied lands and their peoples. Greek superseded Aramaic as the language of commerce.

In the course of hundreds of years, a Jewish community had become established in Egypt. There were exiles from Palestine, merchants and descendants of Jewish mercenaries in the armies of 

Egypt. 

A great Jewish community had grown up in Alexandria, the very center of Greek influence. Aramaic had already begun to replace 

Hebrew as the tongue of Alexandrian Jews. With the Greek conquest around the 4th century B.C.E. the Jews in Alexandria turned to 

Greek, completely forgetting their Hebrew except perhaps for names of holidays. They became the first to write books for Jews in a foreign tongue.

The Jews of Alexandria were religious Jews, loyal to the faith of their fathers, and Alexandria was a seat of Jewish learning. They feared that since the Hebrew language was being forgotten, Jewish learning too was in danger of extinction. To forestall this possibility, they translated the Bible into Greek. In succeeding generations, they studied the Bible only in its Greek translation (the Septuagint-3rd

century B.C.E.), and it is said that no one remembered Hebrew, not even the heads of the community nor even the great Jewish philosopher Philo.

In spite of such devoted efforts to perpetuate their Judaism, this thriving center of Jewish learning disappeared not long afterward. 

Alexandria is the classic example of a community which withered away because its vital element, Hebrew-its means of communication with the rest of Jewry-was wanting.

From the 2nd century B.C.E. Hellenism had strong partisans in Palestine. 

Greek was well known to the Jewish masses. In the synagogue (itself a Greek word) preachers delivered their sermons in Greek, which then in the Aramaic towns were translated into Aramaic. In 

Caesarea on the coast, there were Jews who read the Shema in Greek. As a result of this close connection with Greek many Greek words were absorbed into the Hebrew language, found their way into rabbinic literature, and are still being used today. For instance, Sanhedrin ("sitting together")-the Great Council of Rabbis, beemah- stage or platform, mistorin - mystery, are all Greek loanwords in the Hebrew language. The custom of double names, one Hebrew and one Greek, was common at that time, like that of Alexander Jannai (Jonathan), a descendant of the Hasmonean family, and his brother Judah, who preferred the 

Greek name of Aristobulus. 

In 198 B.C.E. the Syrian heirs of 

Alexander's kingdom wrested Judea away from the Ptolemies, and

Aramaic became more dominant than it had ever been before. Ties between Judea and the Jewish exiles in Babylonia were reinforced, and many exiles returned to settle in Galilee, bringing with them their Aramaic culture. They spread southward, adding strength to the 

Aramaic speakers in Judea. 10

With the appearance of the Maccabees in the 2nd century B.C.E. and the rise of a new political state, attended as it was by rampant nationalism, Hebrew took a new lease on the daily lives of the Jews. 

Maccabean coins have Hebrew writing and the original Book of Maccabees was written in Hebrew. 

By the first century B.C.E. Jews were already dispersed throughout the known world. They were now an Aramaic-speaking people,* but the continuity of Hebrew was guarded in the synagogues and in the scholarly academies. Hebrew was used also in the compilation of the 

Mishnah — a simple concise Hebrew that, although perhaps not spoken, was used in correspondence for centuries, and after the Bible most influenced the Hebrew of today.

Introduction of Vowels 

As long as Hebrew was spoken in daily life, pronunciation of the written word was handed on from generation to generation. However, as Hebrew became less and less the common tongue, difficulties began to appear in reading the Holy Books. Since the written language consisted of consonants only, there was no indication of how the words were to be pronounced.

We can imagine ourselves faced with a word written only pr. The possibilities are many. Can it be poor, pure, pore, par, per, pear, pare, pair or peer? To the Jew who no longer heard Hebrew spoken, the letters (bkr) might signify boker, morning; bakar, cattle; or baker, criticize.

Confronted with this dilemma, scholars began to devise signs that would guide the reader. In the course of many hundreds of years, well into the 5th and 6th centuries of the Common Era, various vowel systems were evolved. The one we use today is called the Tiberias system. These vowel signs, called nikud, were therefore a tremendous help to the masses in their liturgical readings. Today in Israel, the fact that Hebrew is again a spoken language makes the vowel signs less essential except in poetry, where they are often needed because of poetic license. For this reason, all verse is printed with the nikud. 

Nikud is used also in textbooks, prayerbooks and in the Hebrew lessons for new immigrants that appear in the daily press; but most ordinary printed matter appears with the consonants alone. *In the Middle East, Aramaic was supplanted by Arabic when the conquering Islamic faith swept over the area in the 7th century. But so tenacious was Aramaic, that to this day islands of Aramaic speakers yet remain. Among immigrants to Israel from Northern Iraq in 1951 were small groups still speaking 

Aramaic targum lashon-"the language of the [Bible] translation." 11

Creation of Dialects 

As the Jews spread all over the known world, the language of their daily lives began to vary in accordance with the language of the people among whom they lived. Yet there was an ingrained reluctance, an inability to extirpate Hebrew completely, and a remarkable thing happened. Hebrew words were liberally insinuated into the language of the country of exile. Thus a "jargon,” a dialect, was created everywhere the Jew went. It was a sort of compromise between linguistic assimilation and linguistic exclusiveness. Almost always, the Hebrew letters were retained in the written dialect. 

In Spain, the Jews adopted Spanish, introduced Hebrew words into it, wrote it in Hebrew characters right to left-and this dialect, Ladino, is used to this day by Sephardim, Jews of Spanish descent. 

This is how the Spanish word mucho (much) looks in Ladino: AND 

Jews, perhaps from Galilee, had found their way to France, and the first dialect they created was an amalgam of French with Hebrew. 

The Jews of Alsace, too, mixed Hebrew, French, Aramaic, Slavic and German into an Alsatian-Yiddish of their own. And in Italy, the basis for the dialect was the language spoken in Rome during the Middle 

Ages, to which Hebrew was added.

From France Jews wandered to Germany and there created Yiddish, a combination of German and Hebrew. They carried it with them to the Slavic countries, overwhelmed the already existing Jewish dialects, and added Slavic words to Yiddish.

For hundreds of years this Yiddish dialect existed only as a spoken language. The language of writing continued to be Hebrew. The few books written in Yiddish at that time were only for women. This fact is attributed by Ben Yehuda to the low cultural level of the Germanic and Slavic languages during that period of their development. In the highly cultured Arabic countries, on the other hand, the Jews not only spoke Arabic but wrote it as well. 

Thus was Hebrew perpetuated during the Middle Ages: by every observant Jew who prayed and studied in the synagogue; by amchah, the Jewish masses, through their dialects, in which no sentence but contained in its pith a juicy though possibly distorted word of Hebrew; by the literate through use of Hebrew in correspondence and in business accounts.

But this was not the whole picture. For here and there occurred 12

periods of pure Hebrew creativity, the fruits of which have become classic works of world literature. 

The Golden Age in Spain (9th-15th centuries) 

During the period when Ladino was the vernacular of the Jews of Spain, a great flowering of Sephardic culture occurred. Although many 

Jewish works of major importance, such as Maimonides' Guide to the Perplexed, were written in Arabic, there were also many books written in clear and concise Hebrew. Hebrew literary effort burst forth in the poetry of Ibn Gabirol, Ibn Ezra and Judah Halevi, who had a remarkable mastery of an unspoken language. These highly cultivated men understood clearly the crucial position of Hebrew in preserving Judaism. When the child of the physician Judah Abravanel was kidnapped and forcibly baptized, the father cried out to him in a Hebrew poem, "Remain continually mindful of Judaism, cherish the Hebrew language and literature, and keep ever before thee the grief of thy father, the pain of thy mother." Note the sequence: mindfulness of

Hebrew and Judaism takes precedence over father and mother. 

Many of the famous books written in Arabic by these same authors might have been lost in obscurity were it not for their translation into Hebrew. These Hebrew translations made them available to every 

Jewish scholar in the world. At the same time these men rendered an invaluable service to Western culture by preserving in this manner classical works of antiquity which might otherwise have been lost during the Dark Ages.

Hebrew writing was not confined to Spain, but spread to other countries as well. Rashi, who lived in Champagne in the 11th century, wrote his famous commentaries on the Bible and Talmud in Hebrew. 

Frequently when at a loss for a proper Hebrew word, he made use of a suitable French one. For example, in the commentary on Genesis 

15:2, he thoroughly explains in Hebrew the meaning of "childless," but seems impelled to add—“in the language of this people "T [desenfants]." 

In Italy there had been a Hebrew literary tradition dating back to the 4th century and we know that poets wrote in Hebrew in 17th century Venice. It is said that until the 19th century ninety per cent of the males in Italian Jewish congregations understood Hebrew. 

Christians too took up the study of Hebrew - as a possible aid in the conversion of Jews. By the 14th century, Hebrew had become part 13

-- of the curriculum in some European universities. With the coming of the Renaissance it was studied by the cultured. Its cultivation was given added impetus during the Reformation through emphasis on the original text of the Bible. Two of the Mayflower Pilgrims 

Governor Bradford and Elder Brewster-are thought to be among the first Hebraists in America, and in colonial days the study of Hebrew was compulsory at Harvard and Yale. The Yale University seal still carries the Hebrew words "Urim vetumim." One or two of our founding fathers, at the height of their enmity against Great Britain, even proposed Hebrew as the American national language. 

The Renascence of Hebrew 

During the first half of the 19th century Jewish education in Eastern Europe, in the Levant and in North Africa consisted of the study of the Bible and Talmud and their commentaries. The children attending cheder, the adults in the synagogue, listening to the Hebrew sermons of the maggid or engaged in group study-all found themselves daily in an atmosphere saturated with Hebrew, a condition that lasted well into the 20th century. 

With the rise of the Haskalah (Enlightenment) Movement of Russian Jewry in the middle of the 19th century, a new Hebrew literature was created, rooted in Hebrew thought and language. It was Russian Haskalah which led ultimately to Zionism and its concomitant, living Hebrew. At that period, however, a particularly stilted kind of Hebrew writing was in vogue, abounding in esoteric 

Biblical allusions.

After the pogroms of 1881 Hebrew cultural activity intensified. 

Hebrew periodicals, newspapers and literary clubs saw the light of day, as did the writings of Ahad Ha-Am, Chaim Nachman Bialik, Joseph Klausner and Joseph Chaim Brenner.

But during all this time Hebrew was still the language of the book and the pen. It was the common written language of people whose spoken dialects differed. Here and there, to be sure, there were individuals who were determined to speak Hebrew at home, at least on Holy Days, and Hebrew enthusiasts banded together to form 

Hebrew-speaking clubs.

As Jews grasped at the promises of Emancipation and began to leave their ghettos, they tended to leave their dialects behind as well, and to speak the language of the people among whom they dwelt. 14

Frequently they also left behind their intimate connection with Judaism and the Hebrew of the synagogue and of study. This threat to the existence of Hebrew was met in the Diaspora by the Zionist movement through its educational institutions and its stress upon Hebrew as the language of Zion, and in Palestine through the creation of a Hebrew-speaking community. 

Through the years, Jews had gone to Palestine to die and be buried in its holy soil. They came from the Spanish countries, bringing with them Ladino; from the Ashkenazic countries, bringing Yiddish; from 

Iraq and Persia with the Goorzhi dialect.

They all came together in Palestine but lived in their separate communities. Their specific dialects separated one from the other. 

When communication was necessary between communities, their common tongue was Hebrew. Thus an oral Hebrew language came into use, composed of words from the Bible, Talmud and post-talmudic literature. 

This was the Hebrew language in Palestine that greeted the first Zionist immigrants in the 1880's. It was of necessity archaic, inadequate for every-day speech, and its accents were motley. The bearded 

Jew from Poland, with his Ashkenazic synagogue Hebrew, might be speaking an entirely different language than the Jew from Yemen. 

Hebrew faced external difficulties as well. The populace was apathetic, content to speak its own familiar dialects. There was violent opposition to the use of Hebrew as a secular language, and it was considered a sacrilege to use the Holy Tongue for the affairs of daily life. Even today, in the Mea-Shearim quarter, one can witness young 

Jerusalem-born boys with their payot flying, kicking a football around and shouting signals in Yiddish. And there were otherwise intelligent people who seriously believed that a child, taught Hebrew from birth, might not have his mind develop properly. 

Eliezer Ben Yehuda

The language and the time demanded a prophet. A man was required who possessed a fanatical attachment to the Hebrew language, the ability to restore to it the pliancy of a modern tongue. and the iron will to force its sovereignty. Such a man was Eliezer 

Ben Yehuda.

In an autobiography shining with sincerity and earnestness, he wrote that in the 1880's when nationalist movements were involving 15

many nations, suddenly "a mighty inner voice spoke in my ear the revival of Israel on the soil of the fathers." He prepared to support himself in Israel by studying medicine in Paris. In his spare time he immersed himself in political science, and came to the conclusion that two conditions were necessary if the Jews were to become a living people-a return to the land of the fathers, and a return to the language of the fathers.

He said that the moment in which he came to this conclusion was the decisive moment of his life. Whereas the return to Israel at that time was dependent on factors over which Jews had no control, the return to the ancestral language was within their power to achieve. 

However, he then was confronted with a crucial question: Could a language, not spoken for hundreds of years, adapt itself to daily usage? There was no precedent in history for such a phenomenon. 

He reasoned that if one individual could adapt Hebrew to his needs, a whole nation also could do so; and resolved that he himself would be that individual. And one day, for the very first time, in a Paris cafe, he conversed in Hebrew with a friend. "And the strange tones of this dead, this ancient oriental language mingled with the merry tones of the living, beautiful, rich French language." He viewed it as a great political event.

As he continued to speak Hebrew, Ben Yehuda felt himself stifling within the narrow confines of the Hebrew words at his command. He resolved to search among all the Hebrew writings and find words to enlarge the Hebrew vocabulary. Thus was born the idea of a dictionary of Hebrew. And thus too was born the word considered by many as the first modern Hebrew word to be created! For in thinking of a dictionary, he of necessity thought of its title, and from milah-word, he formed milon-a dictionary. 

He undertook the compilation of a dictionary, he confesses, with the irresponsible enthusiasm of youth and without proper background. 

Now followed years and years of philological study in the libraries of 

Germany, France, England and the United States.

Before his death, Ben Yehuda completed five volumes of the lexicon. Volumes 6-14 were published after his death, and two more are yet to appear. He used as sources the literature of ancient and modern times; and when he could find no source root upon which to base a word, he borrowed from sister languages like Turkish and Arabic. Examples of this are words like bool-stamp; rismee-official; 16

ratzeenee—serious. When he needed a word for tray, he referred to maygeesh-offers, and created magash-tray.

Modern Hebrew 

The work of modernizing Hebrew began in earnest. A Hebrew linguistic committee, Vaad Halashon Ha'Ivrit b'Eretz Yisrael, was formed in 1890 with Ben Yehuda as its president, and with the participation of the greatest scholars in Palestine: Chaim Nachman 

Bialik, David Yellin, H. N. Tur-Sinai, Joseph Klausner.

To the Vaad Halashon is officially entrusted the creation of a flexible modern language, and its work goes on unceasingly. Each new situation in Israel requires a proper nomenclature. For example, along with the institution of the immigrant transit camp went the creation of its Hebrew name: maabarah, from maavar-transition. 

The Vaad Halashon publishes lexicons for the sciences, trades and crafts. A tremendous new field was explored in the creation of nautical, aviation and military terms.

How Are New Words Formed? 

One method is by translation through association. A loudspeaker became ram kol (ram—loud; kol-voice); a telegram, whose international symbol is the lightning flash, became a mivrak (barak—lightning); the wireless is alchoot (al-no; choot-wire); a passport became a darkeeyah or darkon (derech-road). About two years ago the newspapers publicized the new word for taxi-monit, based undoubtedly on the root meaning "count," although taxis function in Israel on flat rates and a meter on a taxi is yet to be seen. These translations proved successful and were adopted by the Israelis. 

Some new words, however, fail to capture the public ear and are quickly discarded. A telephone remains a telefon in spite of the fact that sach rachok was proposed (sach-speak; rachok-distance), a literal translation of the word telephone. An Israeli going to the movies uses the words cinema and kol-noah (kol-voice; noahmove) interchangeably.

A second method of increasing the Hebrew vocabulary is to Hebraize an internationally current term. Television, as of this writing, is televizia. A more classic example is the word irgun-meaning organization and derived from this very same word. Energy, as a 17

scientific term, is energeeyah, and a carp, Israel's very own fish, is called carpyon.

Of course, in the revitalization of Hebrew after this fashion there are certain dangers which those who love the language recognize. 

Complaints are voiced of sloppiness and of foreignisms entering the language. At a meeting of the Academy of Hebrew Language in 1949, Professor Joseph Klausner deplored the barbarisms penetrating Hebrew as a result of direct translations of Yiddish idioms. He gave several horrifying examples: Er macht meer zum toit, which literally means "He does me to death," has become in Israel, Hu oseh lee et hamavet. Es ist mir gewehen finster in die oigen, which literally is "It became dark in my eyes," was translated as Hayah lee choshech b'aynayim.

Another widespread frailty is the addition of a Yiddish diminutive like leh to a Hebrew word: Abba-papa, becomes abbaleh or in its English equivalent, papaleh. Eema-mama, becomes eemaleh-mamaleh. 

The Russian influence is still strong. A bachoor-boy, becomes a bachoorchik. One who lives in a kibbutz is then a kibbutznik, and a member of the Labor party is of course a Mapainik. No self-respecting mother will call her little sweetie matok when everyone else says motik.

It is interesting that many years ago, in his youth and in the youth of the Hebrew Revival, Dr. Klausner had believed that since all languages influence each other, they must also influence Hebrew. 

He had indeed been one of the first to make the transition from the classical, heavy style of Ahad Ha-Am to a simpler and more supple language. The foregoing examples, however, appear to be just too much to bear.

There is a rumor, vigorously denied by some, that even the poet 

Bialik fell into the error of direct translation from Yiddish idiom. translating the Yiddish phrase hert a rayach which means "heard a smell" literally into Hebrew: shamah rayach.

Dr. Tur-Sinai (Torczyner), a great Hebraist and a member of the 

Vaad Halashon, illustrates the danger of transferring words willynilly into Hebrew, particularly for a people of strong nationalistic tendencies. 

He describes the hypothetical case of an Israeli child, brought up to use the following words from infancy: telefon, rahdio, frigidehr, combinatzia, protectzia. 18

In the child's innocence this is good Hebrew. Then, when he is old enough, he goes to an American movie, and lo, someone says radio, telephone, frigidaire. What astonishment, what exhilaration! - look, even Americans talk Hebrew!

Another method used to increase the Hebrew vocabulary is to give a good old Biblical word a new connotation. Sar, originally an officer or highly important personage, is now used to designate a cabinet minister. Sar chutz is a foreign minister. Adon, originally a lord or person of importance, is now used for "Mr." - as in Adon Cohen. 

A fourth method, used frequently by Ben Yehuda, is to build a new word-form on a classical Hebrew word. In this way the word for fashion or mode is offnah, based on ofen, meaning a manner or way. 

Setting Standards for Pronunciation 

It has been mentioned that differences existed in pronunciation in the north of Biblical Palestine and the South. The people of the Galil, non-conformists in many ways, perhaps because they were hill people, were not as careful about proper pronunciation as the people of Judea. 

Today there are still marked differences in accent. A multiplicity of Hebrew accents exists not only in the Diaspora, but within Israel itself: Sephardic, Ashkenazic, Yemenite and Persian among others.

In Ashkenazic Hebrew, distinctions in pronunciation between some letters have been almost obliterated. The aleph and ayin are pronounced alike, whereas in the Sephardic the ayin is glottal. The vet and vav, the chet and chaf, tet and tav, kaf and koof sound alike in Ashkenazic. On the other hand, Yemenites differentiate between vet and vav, and the Sephardim pronounce the chet something like the 

German ch, almost half an aspirate.

The elders of the Revival decided to accept the Sephardic accent as the standard Hebrew accent, to the despair to this day of Americans of the old Hebrew school. Actually, this Sephardic accent is not pure either, but a combination of accents. 

There are people who believe that nonconformity to the accepted standard accent should be no cause for dismay. They point out that the differences that exist in various Hebrew accents are no greater than those that exist in the English or American tongues that the person who says rathuh instead of rather is dropping a whole consonant. The difference between the Bostonian's soft (sahft) and a 

19

New Yorker's soft (sawft), is just the difference between the Sephardi's baruch Atah and the Ashkenazi's bawruch Ataw. 

Another problem, while not acute, is still interesting. It is the evolution of a distinctive cadence to the language. Every language seems to have a specific melody, easily recognizable, even when the individual words are not understood by the listener. This specific language intonation or cadence is the reason why comedians can be so funny when they sing songs ostensibly in French, in spite of the fact that the actual words may be a combination of English and Yiddish.

This distinctive rhythmic quality is still lacking in Hebrew. People speak Hebrew with the intonation of their native language. An 

American speaking Hebrew, even with the Sephardic accent, sounds like an American; the German like a German. One of the obstacles is that no one seems to have any idea of the melody of classical Hebrew. With succeeding generations, as the influences of foreign tongues are cast off, a Hebrew cadence is sure to evolve. 

Another unique feature of modern Hebrew is its close similarity to classical Hebrew. In the European languages, obsolescence is rapid. 

Many of the French words that appear in Rashi's commentaries are out of use in France today. His Hebrew, on the other hand, is strikingly fresh and up to date. It is generally true that the more cultivated the Hebrew speaker, the greater will be his reliance upon and use of words and phrases whose source is biblical and rabbinic Hebrew. 

This then is Hebrew today. To the native-born Israeli it may be a casually accepted means of communicating with his fellows, a satisfactory way to express his needs and desires. In America, on the other hand, Hebrew is not an essential factor of integration into the social order. The place of Hebrew in the life of the American Jew is neither so simple nor so obvious.

Yet to both the Israeli and the American Jew, Hebrew is immeasurably more than a simple vehicle of expression. It is a language steeped for thousands of years in the great creations of the Jewish mind and spirit. It is a language that has made these ideas an integral, indivisible part of itself. It is the thread that links the 

Jewries of the world to each other and to their common heritage. 

In the words of Henrietta Szold: "There is a vast storehouse filled with treasures. The key is the Hebrew language and is in our guardianship. Have we a right to throw the key into the ocean of oblivion?" 20
