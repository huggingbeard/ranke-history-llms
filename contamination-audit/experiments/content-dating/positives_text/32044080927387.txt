These Are My Children 

This is a saga of a suburban family. It is the story of Lisa, who forty years ago came to America, a young widow with a baby daughter and a little money, and married Nicki Hueffler. Our con cern is with the second and third generations-Kathi, the daughter, and Kathi's five children, our own contemporaries. Anitra, the eldest, mother of a little girl, is divorced. 

Sylvia, in love with a medical student, emerges from her dreams only when Alex is home on vacation. Henry, fiery young Communist; Jennifer, talented and ambitious, will some day be an actress.

And Edward, the baby, is everyone's pet. Kathi, mother of this diverse group, at forty is in love for the first time. Her husband, Eugene, to whom she has been submissive for many years, no longer matters; soon, even for the children's sake, there can be no pretense. 

Miss Spitzer does not attempt to psychoanalyze. Yet in her portrayal of life within a family group, in which the individuals scarcely know each other's characters or thoughts or private life, she has a penetrating and lively pen. She has an eye for detail also, and little intimate touches are smoothly included. Plot and style are combined to make this an interesting and unusually readable book.

PART ONE LISA AND NICKI

THE house loomed high on an unpaved street in a small town on the outskirts of Manhattan. One had to notice it, for it possessed a rather sinister appearance, even to the most casual and unimaginative passerby. 

It was three stories high-of a sickly tan color-yet there was something majestic about it; like an ancient courtesan who even with the toll of time still retains a semblance of her early seductive beauty. Year in, year out, the windows remained spotted, while the faded cretonne curtains which decorated them, hung sloppily from their twisted rods. The garden surrounding the house was cluttered with dead weeds; in the wild thickness of the tall grass one could discern rusty tin cans and seamy milk bottles. No one tended the exterior of the house; it was as though no one lived within. Yet inside was life-joyous, pulsating, happy-sad life. In the early mornings pattering footsteps of a small child could be heard skipping from room to room. The smell of freshly cooked coffee always seemed to emanate from the region of the kitchen. Eager, laughing voices of Youth rang frivolously through the rooms. 

Grandma Lisa, returning from her morning walk, stood dejectedly before the house. Her eyes, usually 9

so sharp and keen, filled with tears as she absorbed its sordid appearance for the millionth time. The house had not always been that way. Her mind reverted back forty years and she remembered it, when she, Nicki and their brood of children, had first come to live there. Then it had been a lovely house, filled with sunlight and laughing children. She had worked hard in those days, for she was young and strong. 

Now, gazing at it through her seventy year old eyes, it looked even more horrible than ever before. 

She sighed, hopelessly. She could do nothing about it any more. She was too tired. Nor could Nicki assist, for he had gone on the long journey which awaits all mortals. He had died in that house three years agodied quickly—as he had wished to go. She missed him so much. Forty years of association filled as it was with romance, squabbles, struggles and dreams, had left a deep pain of loneliness in Lisa's heart. As she stood ruminating thus, the sound of voices came from within the house; familiar voices, yet she felt so lonely, so alienated. These were her grandchildren-among them, too, was a great grandchild; but they were strangers. She brushed away her tears and went inside.

She walked slowly through the dark hall leading to the living-room of the house. An unusual quietness pervaded the interior, a delightful sense of longed-for peace filled her heart. She opened the door and passed quickly through the living-room into the kitchen-the 10

large, messy kitchen where everyone liked to congregate.

Her daughter, Kathi, mother of Anitra, Sylvia, Henry, Jennifer and little Edwin, stood ironing near the window. "What a beautiful woman she is!" Lisa thought.

She was beautiful, too, not only in the eyes of her old mother and her children, but to all who knew her. 

Tall and statuesque, with a skin that was the envy and admiration of all who gazed upon it, her beauty was crowned by woman's old-fashioned glory, a wealth of silken gold hair. Not even the simplicity of a cheap house dress, which fitted none too well, could detract from Kathi's charm. She scarcely looked like the mother of five children, one of whom was already a divorcée. Even though her figure was full and bulgy in places, there was a vitality about her, a healthfulness which denoted youth. Most certainly she did not look her forty-two years of age. 

Kathi smiled warmly at her mother. "Where have you been?" she demanded. She did not raise her eyes from the iron which flitted determinedly across the ruffles of her grandchild's dress. "I've been down the road, getting some sun." 

Kathi smiled. "Getting some sun," she said, “and picking up a little gossip?" "Gossip doesn't interest me much any more, Kathi.

I've had a lifetime of it. But I still enjoy the air and the sun and the sky. These things are real and good, II

when one gets old." There was no sadness in Lisa's voice, no rebellion at old age and its ultimate toll. 

She sounded merely tired. 

Kathi did not answer. Her intercourse with her mother was limited these days. Something had come between them. Something they both knew but avoided putting into words. Words had a way of hurting--. 

After awhile Lisa asked: "Hasn't Jennifer returned yet?" 

Jennifer! Merely saying the name of this, her favorite grandchild, warmed her heart. My darling, courageous grandchild, she thought. "The child is stage struck," Kathi answered. “I can't do anything with her! She just left for the city. 

You must have missed her, for she dashed out the back entrance. She is determined to be an actress and that's all there is to it."

Kathi tried to sound unhappy about Jennifer's wilfulness, but she could not fool her old mother. Lisa knew that the day had come when Kathi was not vitally interested in what happened to her children. 

Something else had come into her life. 

Lisa banished this thought from her mind. It sickened her-made her yearn for death. 

A door slammed. Someone came whistling down the hall. It was little Edwin, Kathi's youngest child, but not her youngest charge. She also took care of 

Anitra's offspring, Alicia, who even now was sleep- 12

ing peacefully in her shabby carriage in the back yard. "Hello mom." He flung his books down and embraced Kathi. Then he noticed his grandmother. “Hello,” he said, his eyes brightening with anticipation. Grandma Lisa was liberal with her pennies, and the ice-cream wagon was waiting on the corner. 

One of the legs of his pants hung down to the boy's ankle. He yanked at it constantly. "Kathi," Lisa said, "why don't you fix Edwin's pants? He oughtn't to go to school that way. It isn't right." "Aw shucks!" Edwin grumbled. "It always tears. 

It won't hold."

Kathi sighed, and went on with her ironing. "Take a glass of milk, Edwin, and some bread and butter," she said. "Don't want no milk." "You see, mama, I can't do a thing with them. 

They've grown beyond me." 

Lisa smiled. She remembered when Kathi was a mother to whom her children listened with fear and respect. Anitra, Sylvia, Jennifer and Henry, all of them filled with love and admiration for their parents. All of them lovely, happy, obedient children. 

Now that they had grown up, they lived their own lives. Kathi's mother-heart was being crushed by an outsider. Her love for her children had been sublimated. The children were not aware of this. Lisa 13

knew it and suffered. It was not right, it was not good. Someone would suffer for the whole unhealthy situation.

Even before Lisa reached for her change purse, Edwin was at her skirts, his little hand upturned. 

Grandma Lisa never turned a fellow down. She knew what little boys liked.

After Edwin's departure, mother and daughter were silent for a time-silent save for the soft humming notes that came from Kathi's pretty mouth as she ironed one garment after another. Kathi seemed so happy, but Lisa knew she did not have the right to that kind of happiness. It was not decent happiness-not in her sense nor understanding of the word. "Kathi, have you heard from Eugen?" Lisa pretended to read a newspaper.

Kathi frowned, and a cloud seemed to spread across her jubilant face. "He telephoned last evening," she said, stiffly. "Anitra spoke to him.” "How is he? When is he coming home?" "I guess he is all right." Kathi sounded sullen. "He isn't a child. He can take care of himself." "But he is your husband, Kathi. Don't you forget that." 

Kathi glared at her mother. "Listen mama, I've lived with Eugen for almost twenty years. I've worked for him and for his children, I've sacrificed for them, I've let life go by for them. I think I've done my duty." 14

"You have been a good wife, Kathi. There is no denying that. What I don't understand, however, is why you've suddenly stopped. Why is Eugen living in Albany, away from you and the children, and why don't you care?" "Listen mama, you're not a young woman any longer and there are some things in life you've never yearned for. You'll never understand any of this. I don't love Eugen. I've not loved him for years-way before Henry was born. I've a right to life, too. And, oddly enough, I'm going to take it." 

Kathi's eyes filled with tears. The word "love" brought a vision of William to her heart. William, her salvation; William, her lover; William, who was giving her Life. Let them criticize. Let them talk. 

She was living. "There are some things in life you've never yearned for." These words of Kathi's ran through Lisa's brain. 

The words jumped around like little insects, biting, nipping, tormenting her. "There are some things in life you've never yearned for."

Lisa laughed aloud. Kathi looked at her as though she had suddenly gone mad. But Lisa had not lost her mind. She felt alive, strong, almost young again. She was filled with memories-with yearnings-with a whole lifetime of accomplishment and sacrifice behind her.

She continued to laugh as she mounted the rickety steps leading to her quarters upstairs. She laughed for 15

a long while, and then, when the pain of that long life behind her became too much for human endurance, she wept. She cried all through the night, like one sick and ailing, for whom medical science could do naught.

She was sad with the strangeness of modern life. 16

SEVENTY years seem a long time and they are, when each year has been filled with loves and deaths and births. Yet to Lisa, reclining on her bed in her attic room, it seemed such a sudden swift transition. 

How ironic, that seventy years of intense living could be drawn from out of one's inner consciousness in two or three short hours. She thought: "Why is this? 

Why did it take me seventy years to live through all these things and now I can think of them in hours? 

Is it because in recollection only the highlights come to mind, and all the petty details which transpired to bring them about are forgotten? The pain, the struggle, the effort in back of everything counts for nothing in retrospection."

She sighed and thought of Nicki. It was three years since he had died and left her, but here in the room they had shared together for forty years, his spirit still remained. Indomitable spirit! It would never die. No matter what happened to this room, this house, Nicki's spirit would go on forever from generation to generation, for his was a personality that even the great reaper, Death, could not completely annihilate. 

She smiled tenderly as she thought of Nicki. She 17

didn't feel quite as lonely as she had of late. Her heart, which in the past few months had seemed to flutter so weakly, now grew strong. So it seemed to her, lying in her bed and remembering. 

Why was it that her real life seemed to have begun with Nicki? Yet before Nicki there had been much in her life-the early days in Vienna, her first marriage to John, the birth of her daughter Kathi. Yet all of that was hazy in retrospect. She seemed only to have begun living the day she met Nicki. Nicki! Even now the thought of him made her thin blood run warmer.

And now for the first time in many years she thought of her father, that stern, massive male of whom everyone in Vienna had been afraid, except she. He had been an admirable character. All who knew him respected him, and as for the young men he taught at the university, they both feared and loved him.

Unlike many middle-class men of Vienna in those days, her father believed that girls should receive as thorough an academic education as boys. Therefore, while all of her girl friends drifted from an elementary education into marriage and child-bearing, Lisa continued for many years to pore over her books and to study under the tutelage of her brilliant father. 

Happy days those, almost unreal in their serenity. 

Though her three younger sisters married and gossipers whispered among themselves: "Poor Lisa! It 18

looks as if she will be an old maid," she remained unconcerned. She was happy with her father and her books. Young men interested her socially, but not individually. Her mother nagged, her sisters regarded her with sympathetic tolerance, but her father, like herself, remained indifferent to the situation. 

One day he brought a young man to their home.

He was a student at the university. His name was 

John Houseman. He was tall and pleasant looking, and although only twenty-seven years old he gave the appearance of being more advanced in years. Like her father, he wore a big mustache and side-whiskers.

He was well-spoken and would undoubtedly achieve success at law, his chosen profession. 

Lisa looked wisely at her father when he presented the young man, and, although she never could be sure, she was almost certain that her father's eyes had twinkled mischievously as young Mr. Houseman bowed low and kissed her hand.

Three months later Lisa and John were married, and within a year her first child, Kathi, was born. 19

SHE thought she had loved John but it was only after meeting Nicki in America that she realized the true meaning of this vital word. Admiration for 

John's character and his intelligence, respect for his position in Vienna, the fluttering fear and maidenly submission of a virginal young woman to her first male; these she had felt and no more. Yet these emotions had seemed like love to Lisa, and she had been content.

Of her mature life in Vienna several things were remembered through the years of living with Nicki, but even these were like faded reproductions which clutter up a dusty family album. Her wedding and her first knowledge of the intimate life of man and woman, the birth of Kathi, the pain, the crudeness of the delivery; the joy at feeling her first born sucking at her breast; evenings when her father came and remained talking until the early hours of the morning; 

Kathi's first word, the day word was brought to her that her father had dropped dead while lecturing at the university, and the sudden, unexpected death of John just as he was at the threshold of success as a counsellor in Vienna,-these episodes were indelibly stamped on her mind. 20

Without her father and John, life in Vienna seemed empty. Her husband had left her comfortably fixed, for he had been thrifty, and so the day came when she decided to broaden her vision by travel. Lisa at this time was twenty-eight years of age, still young, and with an enviable vitality and zest for living. The sum of almost eight thousand dollars to her credit freed her of immediate financial concern. From the moment she set sail for America, she felt that she was going toward a greater fulfillment of her life-toward adventure and love.

The boat docked in Brooklyn. When she had started her journey she had not the slightest conception of where she was going to settle, but she reached a decision by the time she landed in America. She had been told by a travelling acquaintance that New York City was no place for a lone woman with two children. (New York City in the Nineties, eventually known as the Gilded Age, with the figures of Moody and Sankey, Lillian Russell, Diamond Jim Brady, crooks, chorus girls, title hunters, and matinee idols was most certainly not the best atmosphere for a retiring, cultured young matron from the Old World.) 

Her kind informant told her of a small fishing village on the outskirts of New York, that would be the ideal place for her to make her home. 

Directly from the boat she had gone to the village recommended by her ship-board acquaintance. 

Almost immediately she found a suitable boarding 21

house where she and Kathi were comfortably ensconced. From the beginning she enjoyed contentment there.

After the first few weeks of adjusting herself to her new surroundings she bought a bicycle in order to get around. Since she was "boarding" and did not have an abundance of domestic duties, she had plenty of leisure time to go about and see the strange countryside. Each afternoon, while Kathi slept, Lisa rode her bicycle.

One afternoon, just as she was leaving the village, the handle bars on her vehicle went to pieces. On her numerous jaunts into the countryside she had noticed a plumber's shop along the outskirts of the town. 

She wheeled the bicycle toward the shop. A man was seated in front of the place, his chair tipped back comfortably in the shade. He was reading. The sight of German script, and poetry at that, struck a nostalgic chord in Lisa's heart. "Do you repair bicycles?" Lisa asked timidly. The man did not answer, nor did he remove his eyes from the book. 

Lisa tried again. "I say, do you repair bicycles?" She raised her voice this time. The man looked up. The blood mounted to Lisa's cheeks. She felt odd, yet happy. This man, who was he, that he should affect her so without even having spoken a word? She turned her face away, that he might not notice her flushed appearance. 22

"Excuse me, madam, for being so rude. But I didn't see nor hear you. I was absorbed in one of Schiller's poems. A great man, Schiller!" He had risen now, and she noticed that he was tall and slender, as well as handsome. "Something wrong with the bicycle? Don't let it worry you, madam. There isn't anything I can't fix.” 

His voice was deep and mellow.

He smiled. His whole face lit up. Lisa did not trust herself to return his smile. She wanted to get away. 

She felt she could not remain there another moment without the man sensing that her heart was pounding with emotion for him. Why deny it? Something about him something inexplicable had reached out and plucked poignantly at her heart strings. • · He discovered at once that a bolt in the handle bars had become loosened and commenced to fix it. 

She stood by, powerless to move or to think clearly. 

She glanced toward his shop as though to escape the man, but there, printed in bold, black type was his name: NICKI HUEFFLER. TINSMITH, PLUMBER, CARPENTER. Nicki, Nicki. She re peated the name. It made such a lovely, lovely sound. "Your tires need air," he said. "Can you spare another few moments while I pump them?" 

He did not wait for her answer but disappeared into the shop. Lisa was able to breathe more freely now. She told herself she was behaving like a fool. 23

Had the American air gone to her otherwise passive head?

No sooner did he return than the pounding of her heart commenced again. "When I leave," she told herself, "this tumult will stop. I'll never see him again." She knew she was pretending. She was certain she would return to Nicki Hueffler's shop even if she had to wreck her bicycle in order to create an excuse. "There now, that job is finished," he said. He looked up at her and for the first time she noticed his eyes. They were large, of a deep brown coloring, and oh so soft and dreamy. His skin, bronzed from constant exposure to the sun, glowed with healthful vitality. His massive head was covered with wavy black hair, and his long curling mustaches were luxurious. A handsome man, she concluded. Why did he have to be a mechanic? The next moment she was ashamed of her snobbery. It was as though she had slapped the man for something he did not do. "How much do I owe you?" she asked. "That little job will cost you nothing." His eyes had strayed from her face now and he was gazing off into the distance. “But I wouldn't think of it," she replied. "You've repaired my bicycle and I wish to pay you for it." 

He smiled. "I never charge customers for these little jobs," he said. "I wait for the big ones." 

"I am afraid you are not a very good business man." 24

"I manage to get along."

She opened her purse and handed him a dollar bill. “Oh, but that is too much, madam." He took the bill and returned three quarters to her. The touch of his hand as he gave her the change sent a quiver through her body. She must escape. He must not suspect what a complete conquest he had made. "Are you a newcomer in these parts?" he asked.

His eyes, as he surveyed her, were quite impersonal. 

She was sorry now that she had not worn her new bicycling outfit. The crimson hue was so becoming. "Yes," she answered. Her voice was trembling. "I've been here only two months. I am from Vienna.”

His eyes widened and once again filled with dreams. "Vienna!" he repeated. How beautifully he said the word. "I was there once as a young boy. I was born in Budapest." "That, too, is a lovely city," she replied. "I used to go there frequently with my father to attend lectures and concerts."

The conversation ended. There seemed nothing more to say. Yet she was loath to leave the spot. 

There was an embarrassed silence. They both smiled. 

Then he said hastily: "Those handle bars won't give you any more trouble. If they do, come back." He walked away. 

She mounted her bicycle and pedalled away. When she turned around she saw him seated once again on 25

his tilted chair in front of the shop, his head buried deeply in his volume of poetry. Nicki, Nicki. She repeated the name all the way home, until the whirring of the bicycle seemed to sing his name, too. 26

THE woman who ran the boarding-house at which Lisa lodged was a pure American type. She had been born and brought up on a farm in Kansas, with the result that her hands and arms seemed to have absorbed the soil she had tilled in her younger days.

She had run away from the farm at a tender age on the arm of a travelling man who had found lodgings at her father's home. The old story of the girl who believed too well was enacted again. Filled with dreams and happiness, she had come to New York with her husband to live the life of grandeur, which he had promised beneath the Kansas moon. But alack and alas, the Lothario lost his job. Seeing that his spouse was a fine, strong girl, he had urged her to go out as a domestic. The first few years, Lisa gathered from Mrs. Wilson's confidences, had been a labor of love, but soon after she realized her husband was a loafer. "Yes sir," she would declaim, given any kind of an audience, "I wouldn't see hide nor hair of him for week upon week, but when pay-day came around he was always on hand. The first few years I turned over my salary to him, fool that I was, believing that he was putting it away for our future. Future, my eye! He skipped town with a saloon-keeper's daugh- 27

ter, and that was the end of my married life and my money.' "But don't you ever hear from him?" Someone was sure to inquire. "Hear from that . . . !" Without another word

Mrs. Wilson would rise in all her majesty, walk slowly but deliberately to the kitchen, and return with a butcher's knife. With almost too much realism she would wield the knife through the air and say: "This is waiting for that . . . !" 

One evening, a few nights after Lisa's meeting with Nicki, Mrs. Wilson joined her boarder on the verandah. It was a lovely spring evening, and from the porch Lisa enviously watched the young men and women bicycling along the avenue. How gay they looked, how young! Her own heart was heavy.

She was lonely. She dared not admit, even to herself, for whom she was lonely. It was three days since she had discovered Nicki and for three days and nights she had tried every known method to put him from her thoughts. She had called herself a fool. She had conjured up visions of her father and his high principles. She had delved deep into her sub-conscious for reminders of her life with John. Finally she had thought of Nicki, since she must think of him, as a plumber. What place could a plumber have in her life, she asked herself over and over again, but each time a persistent voice had answered: "A most important place." 28

Plumber or poet, what did it matter, when he possessed some unknown spark, which set her heart afire?

Mrs. Wilson's voice began its nightly drone of gossip and philosophic observations. "Another day gone by" she said wearily, "and we're all well and happy. 

Ah, what a grand feeling at the end of the day, to know that good deeds have been done. A wasted life 

I call it, when a day passes without my helping someone along the hard path of life." She sighed dramatically. "Now take those society folks, the Four Hundred. All they think of is parties and balls. Sinful life, I call it. The Bradley-Martin Ball at the Waldorf- 

Astoria Hotel last week. Disgraceful! Lighting cigars with hundred dollar bills, finding real pearls in their oysters. Why, I even heard that naked girls popped up from out of pies." Mrs. Wilson blushed at this orgiastic picture. 

Lisa pretended to listen to Mrs. Wilson but her thoughts were far away. There was an emptiness in her heart, a dull ache, a feeling she had never experienced before. "Perhaps a sight of Kathi sleeping upstairs will help me," she thought, as she excused herself.

She tiptoed into the darkened room and by the light of the moon glanced lovingly at Kathi, but even that did not allay the emptiness in Lisa's heart. 

When she returned to the porch she discovered that Mrs. Wilson had a visitor. She was just about to go 29

upstairs again, where at least she would be assured of solitude, when the voice of the visitor halted her. 

That voice belonged to but one person-that deep, mellow, warm voice. Nicki! Again the blood rushed up to her face, but she did not care. She walked out onto the verandah. 

Mrs. Wilson was berating Nicki. "I've sent my hired girl, Tilly, to your shop six times this week, Mr. Hueffler, and each time you promised to come and fix the washtubs, and each time you failed to appear. I cannot do business with a man of your type." "There isn't another plumber in town with my ability," Nicki smiled confidently as he said this, "and you know it! A beautiful night," he added, “a night for romance." He turned his head as he said these words and saw Lisa standing in the doorway. He nodded. "Good evening, madam. How is the bicycle?" "Working fine," she replied. She noticed that he was well-dressed and that his hair, which had been so unruly upon their first meeting, was now neatly combed. "You don't mean to tell me, Mrs. Houseman, that you trusted the repair of your bicycle to this careless man?" Mrs. Wilson demanded. "Don't mind Mrs. Wilson," Nicki interrupted. "All my life I've taken her abuse and her jobs. I enjoy taking either from such an attractive woman.” 30

Nicki winked mischievously to Lisa. Mrs. Wilson blushed and fussed with her hair. She rose self-consciously. "Come along, Nicki Hueffler, and get down to work. I warn you, though, this is your last chance."

Nicki's eyes twinkled as he followed her to the cellar. 

Lisa remained on the porch. She knew she would not budge from this spot until Nicki returned even if the job took several hours. A tumult, such as she had never known, raged within her. She felt as though the old, sedate Lisa had died, and a new, bold woman had been born in her place. 

Mrs. Wilson returned, shaking her head. "That 

Nicki Hueffler" she said, "the laziest man in town, but the best plumber. Always readin' books, as if that will get him a decent livin'. How his children thrive is beyond me, yet they certainly look healthy enough." 

Children? Nicki Hueffler's children? Lisa's romantic world collapsed. Of course, he would be married.

Most attractive men are, she thought. But somehow, he didn't look married. There was a light irresponsible air about him. Well, that finished that. Now she could forget Nicki Hueffler-in fact she would have to forget him.

Mrs. Wilson said goodnight and shuffled off to bed. 

Lisa was glad to be alone.

She did not know if hours or moments passed. 

Finally Nicki stood before her. 31

"You're up late," he said. "It is already past ten o'clock." "I didn't realize the hour." She must control the tremble in her voice. "I'm of the opinion," he said, seating himself nonchalantly on a step of the porch, "that people spend too much time in sleeping. Why more than half our lives are passed in bed. The world might come to an end and we might miss that spectacle, that is, if most of the world sleeps as soundly as I do."

Lisa felt tongue-tied. She had nothing to say, and yet so much to say, if she but dared. "Full moon, tonight." Nicki ventured, ignoring her silence. "Yes, full moon." Lisa reiterated. "I always feel kind of funny on a full moon night." There, perhaps if Nicki noticed anything awry with her, he could attribute it to the position of the capricious moon. "What do you mean, funny?" "I don't know. My thoughts run wild." "How interesting!" Nicki's eyes grew soft. "Rather temperamental, I should say. But of course you're temperamental. I can see it in your eyes.' 

She lowered her eyes modestly, and wondered what else he had been able to read there.

The sound of a piano, not in very good tune, filled the air. "Where in the world is music coming from at this hour of night?" she asked. 32

"That is from Maloney's Dance Emporium down the road. By Jove, tonight is amateur night. There'll be big doings there. Come to think of it, I could enjoy a glass of his fine beer this minute. Could youwould you care to go with me?" 

His voice was eager. "But your wife, Mr. Hueffler? 

I couldn't do that.' 

Nicki's face grew sad. "My wife?" he said, "my wife died two years ago. I am a widower." He lowered his eyes and seemed to be carried back into the past.

There was an awkward silence as both Nicki and Lisa thought of their beloved ones who had once constituted their lives.. Their thoughts were similarsadness intermingled with hopelessness. Each felt dreadfully alone in these few moments. "I'm sorry," Lisa whispered, tenderly. “I know how cruel Death can be. I lost my husband."

Nicki pulled himself back from the dim past. "We must not dwell in the past" he said. "Life is for the living."

The sound of the piano from Maloney's Dance Emporium continued its inharmonious onslaught. "Shall we go?" Nicki inquired. "I'd love to." Lisa answered. She was beaming with happiness and romance. "I'll just get my bonnet."

It was the most beautiful evening Lisa had ever spent. It was also the beginning of Nicki's ardent courtship. 33

One Sunday afternoon during a period when they were still calling each other "Mrs. Houseman" and "Mr. Hueffler," although each thought of the other in more personal and intimate terms, Lisa and Nicki went picnicking for the day. The weather was glorious, and although Mrs. Wilson grumbled constantly about Lisa's friendship with Nicki Hueffler, she kindly offered to look after Kathi for the day. 

They bicycled far out into the country, their basket of food and drink attached to the rear of Nicki's bicycle.
