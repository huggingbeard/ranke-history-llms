CHAPTER I THE BIRTH OF A BOOK 

"Life is a sheet of paper white 

Whereon each writes his word or two, And then comes night." 

The word "Genius" has been defined as 99% perspiration and 1% inspiration. Hitherto, I have been too busy recording my hum-drum existence as a house-wife, to lay any claim to that title; although 

I have had a secret longing for over twenty years to write a book about Maine people and their mode of life, with which, as a Down-East Yankee, I am very familiar. When I had the forced leisure of later years, I decided that my Book of Life was too near its close to begin another. But now, buoyed up by the glaring headlines of how these modern oldsters try to outdo each other by climbing out of their rocking chairs, throwing away their crutches and cutting up all sorts of capers never before indulged in, I have been toying with the idea of getting into this hectic race, myself. So at long last some hidden force has given me the necessary impetus to make this belated attempt to write that book of my dreams. Possibly the chief reason is because I have become too wearied by the insistence of a few cherished friends that, with my strong sense of humor, I could write a very humorous book if I tried; so I have finally told them that I would write this book to prove that I couldn't. 

Now I have decided that if Grandma Moses, in 3

house-dress and apron, could drop down into a chair at her kitchen table, with her hair twisted into a tight knot on top of her head, and paint a few strokes while waiting for the kettle to boil, which has practically rocked the universe-perhaps I could blunder into something that would put me in a blaze of glory also.

For her line of decorations on practically everything but garden shovels has set the whole world agog. 

I do not care to compete with the 85-year-old grandma who wings her way across the country in an airplane, every month or so, because I get dizzy at the top of a four-foot stepladder; neither shall I try to out-do the old man who pedals across the country to California, now and then; nor those who hobble over long stretches of country roads with the aid of a knotted cane, getting blisters on their feet, for consolation; nor shall I try flagpole sitting at my three score and ten years of age. So it would seem that my best chance of riding to fame on the wings of glory, with the other boys and girls of my age, is to attempt a serious, sensible book about the Maniacs; hoping that I may pick up, somewhere along the line, the necessary 1% inspiration for making me emerge a full-fledged genius. However lofty my aim, I begin to fear, as my friend hopes, that this may turn out a funny book after all. It might even exceed his desire, if I followed the example of another author who omitted all marks of punctuation in his book and put a complete list of them on the back. page, telling the readers to put them in the copy to suit themselves. 4

So like Grandma Moses, Grandma Clough, with a modern hair-do, sits at her white kitchen table; and waiting for her coffee to "perk”, feels the same urge on this glorious December morning, to jot down a few rambling thoughts to introduce whatever book may follow.

Many well-bred books start out with apologies of some sort; so I feel justified in making a necessary one to Dwight Eisenhower. Now that he has once more given Maine its rightful place in the nation as being inhabited by real live and determined people, rather than its implied descent during the last twenty years to simply a small, pink enclosure on a printed map, it seems most uncharitable of me to foist this book on the public. It might cast another blot on the fair state which he has just redeemed, with my help at the polls and that of the driver whom I swung over to our side, on the way, because after all, he admitted that he did like Ike. 

As for Ike, if he could emerge from the worst smear campaign of the nation's history as a nobler man and greater hero, I decided that he could certainly withstand the repercussions of this book. For over against this is a long list of his purported misdemeanors, written in satire, by one of our most brilliant writers. He says that "according to the other side," Ike beats Mamie daily, hates cats, abhors dogs, and sticks up filling stations for fun. It was his iceberg which sank the Titanic, and his sister chopped up a family with an axe. He invented the yo-yo, and was accused of practically every disaster since the 5

Chicago fire, except kidnapping Lindberg's baby.

The writer would not doubt the story that he ate 

Red Riding Hood's grandmother, because he's got a mean cruel face and always looks hungry. He adds that he almost forgot to tell how Ike ate Jonah, while masquerading as a whale. The writer goes on to fill a whole column in a Portland paper with such facts, supposed to be found in Harry's evaluation of Ike. 

Yes, we have some very smart, witty people in Maine although we have faded from the public eye during our twenty years of forced exile from the nation's notice because we were on the wrong side of the fence. We have gradually degenerated to the level of being merely the state which furnishes the best potatoes, lobsters and clams for our aristocratic neighbors to eat. Unfortunately they judge our intellects to be on the same level as those of the clams. 

Though of decidedly higher intellectual status, we do have one point in common which has fastened the word "dumb" upon us; for often it takes as much digging to find the true treasure in our hidden souls as to upheave the clams. We shut up equally as tight when anyone strikes us the wrong way; hence the common expression "he shut up like a clam." Personally, I cannot figure out why the aristocrats of 

Massachusetts call us dumb, in comparison to themselves; for what could be more dumb than for them to call the Up-Northerners, the Down-Easters?

Encouraged by the ease of writing thus far, and spurred on by the hope that it may continue to the end of a book (of a sort), Grandma idly turns the

pages of a magazine while waiting for a new idea and second cup of coffee. Soon she comes up with this.

While many modern readers know the dictionary meaning of the word Yankee, they have little conception of the primitive Yankee of 1840; so I shall try to elucidate under the title of—

THE VANISHING DOWN-EAST YANKEE 

With the continual migration of farmers to the city, and with city folks slaving away to fulfill their cherished dreams of buying some old farmhouse, which their elders scorned as much as living in tents, there are only a few left in the country who could rightfully be called "hicks" or "hayseeders." The prevailing tendency to seek new environment is well expressed by a retired merchant who said that he worked like a horse on the farm so that he could live in the city, then worked like a horse so that he could live in the country again. And a neighbor added, "Then he worked like a dog to get enough money to buy an auto, with which to get away from home." 

Here I will try to give an accurate picture of this rare and supposedly speechless type, which caused so much ridicule from veneered city visitors. In passing, I will say that the fun which city slickers had in relaying the actions of these dumb hicks to their friends was infinitesimal to the laughter and guffaws which these shrewd Yankees caused while sitting on cracker barrels in country stores and relating, midst much tobacco spitting, the ridiculous yarns which they made those dumb city smarties believe.

For example, a coach driver, conveying two typical females to a big resort in Jackson, N. H., saw some sheep with the usual contraption of cross sticks sticking up above their heads. These kept them from jumping over fences, made of tree trunks. At each silly question that the most offensive woman asked, the driver merely shifted his cud to the other cheek and hollered "Giddap" to the pair of horses. When she asked how the sheep kept these sticks on, this was too good an opportunity to let pass; so he spit out his cud (deeming the loss of it well worthwhile) and drawled, "Well, you see Ma'am, it is just like this. We drive the sticks right down through their necks." Horror overcame her, and the indecision of taking the matter of this cruel custom to the legislature kept her silent for the rest of the journey. Can't you just see the sly twinkle of amusement in the eyes of this plain old country man? See what Barnum meant when he said that there was a sucker born every minute? And believe you me, as both a country and city dweller, you can make a bigger fool and in shorter time of the city person than vice-versa. 

The above joke furnished a whole week's topic for laughter from the cracker barrel devotees.

A true-to-type Yankee does not have overfondness for the tourists who tear up and down the roads in summer, throwing dust all over him and crowding him into ditches, where heretofore he has had free use of his own streets. So if one of these drivers should almost run over a workman at some crossroads, pull up shortly and yell, "Hey, you! Where

does this road go?" our Yankee is almost sure to answer "Dunno. I have lived here nigh onto forty years and never seen it go nowhere," with the usual dumb look ascribed to him. Or if another kind of pest annoys him, by condescendingly running on for half an hour about the advantages of New York over those of poor, down-trodden Maine, and then runs on fifteen minutes more about what a great guy he is, our real old-timer will give him a brief withering look, shift his cud of tobacco, spit a long stream of tobacco juice in the general direction of "the great guy" and remark, "You don't say." Then he will pick up his shovel and accidentally, of course, throw a huge shovelful of dirt where it will do the best job of covering the shiny Cadillac with the same kind of dirt that it has thrown over the Maine hicks all summer.

But not so with the females, who always run true to form. They do not take insults sitting down but are sure to hit right back. I had occasion to do just that years ago, and still chuckle over the consternation which followed. 

During an early morning ride to Portland on the trolley car, I was aroused from a reverie about my duties in the city when I observed one of the ladies in front of me waving her arms about and expressing, in voice and manner, great scorn of some person or thing. Then I roused up to hear, "Why don't Portland put a stop to this? Do you suppose that Massar-chusetts would allow this? You bet they wouldn't.

Why don't the people of Maine wake up and get on

to themselves? They are so dumb and provincial." 

Well, one of them woke up, pronto; and with the same viciousness as I would swat an annoying fly, I leaned over and said, "Madam, we people of this backwoods, country village (as you call it) like each other and the place where we live; and we hate Boston as much as you hate Portland. So why do you continue to stay where the dislike is so mutual? Why don't you take the next train for Mas-sar-chusetts, so that all of us can be happy?"

They would not have looked more shocked if a comet had burst at their feet. After the first gasp, and still trying to catch her breath, the speaker merely turned an understanding look on her companion, which plainly said, "See what I mean about them?" 

Thanks to the publicity of Phillips Lord, the lovable Seth Parker of radio fame, and to Parker Fenley, who held his radio audiences spellbound for years with his Snow Village accounts of New Hampshire, Maine began to gradually achieve world-wide recognition. When Seth Parker was put on the screen in 

Portland for a week or more, the theater was packed, with lines forming way down the street; so there must have been some charm about these simple, country folks. The most appealing part was when 

Ma played on the sweet old country organ, with the group singing the old hymns; and the last one followed by Seth's deep-throated "Amen," stirred the audience to unusual reverence, and brought tears to many eyes.

The steady influx of hordes of people from all parts 10

of the country has demonstrated the beauty and grandeur of Maine as a national asset until it bids fair to rival the long-heralded attractions of the Sunny 

South or Golden Gateway of the West. The high regard in which these people hold us is shown by the increasing number of letters which they send each year for publication in our papers and magazines. I hope that the Mas-sar-chusetts woman has taken due notice; and if she is sufficiently repentant, I would welcome her back to our illustrious "village" of Portland, in person.

A Broadway columnist recently described a visit to Bangor, Maine, in a national magazine, and declared that the Maine way of living is a good way. 

She states that Broadway does not envy Bangor, which is all right with Bangor which has small reason to envy Broadway. She tells how Maine has preserved its charm without missing a single step in the march of progress. Though Queen Anne chairs may be found in the sitting rooms, the latest style dishwashers may be working in the kitchens; though claw-footed bath tubs still can be found in the old white houses, so can television sets. As for the peo- ple-they are not the silent, dumb people that Titus Moody depicts and which radio skits have led our students of cartoons and comic sheets to believe. Life is so satisfactory to them by day that no rite of escape is needed when darkness falls, hence no cocktail bars. Sunset for them does not signify "closing the hatch on a day to be quickly forgotten." She closes by saying that it strikes a visitor that the rest 11

of the world would be better off, if it had a lot more of whatever it is that people have in Bangor. 

This long portrayal of old-time country folks seemed necessary to me because all of the incidents in this book are based on their mode of life and manner of speech. I hope that it has not proven too tiresome for the readers. 12

CHAPTER II THE BIRTH OF A STAR 

"Silently, one by one, In the infinite meadows of heaven 

Blossomed the lovely stars, The forget-me-nots of the angels." 

On a beautiful clear starlit night in December of 

1880, a new star was born, instead of being discovered in a galaxy of stars while astronomers peered through their telescopes at the heavens. This birth was announced by the lusty wailing of a baby girl in a lonely farmhouse, perched on the summit of a high hill. 

She was soon named Stella, meaning a star, because to the enraptured parents something of the radiance of the stars on which they had drawn so heavily, during their long ordeal of waiting, seemed reflected in the peaceful face of their sleeping child. This birth was not heralded by any blare of trumpets nor attended with any significant events such as characterized the lowly birth of another child, born in a manger, whose birthday we celebrate on December 

25. A star also played an important part in that birth -The Star of Bethlehem, standing over where the young child lay, and guiding the Wise Men "Who rejoiced with exceeding great joy, and presented treasures unto Him." But to James and Ella Cole something of the same reverence and awe welled up in their hearts, and great joy that God, in His goodness, had sent them this little girl, for whom they 13

had so long hoped and prayed because their other child was a sturdy boy of two years. They named her Stella Blanche, but always called her Blanche, their former choice of names.

Since the Milky Way shone with such brilliance on that night, to one who believes in horoscopes (that our destinies are guided by stars at our birth) it might seem that this played a potent part in Stella's life, too. For from her first sweet, contented smile of joy and happiness, this same natural exuberance of spirit prevailed throughout her life, except in times of sorrow and affliction. Then, according to the law of averages, she was more completely submerged than one whose life ran on a more level keel. 

As the loftiest trees have greatest cause to dread the thunder, Blanche soon learned that her unbridled capacity for joy was always offset by equal fits of depression in the valley. But as no human system could stand being so weighted down for long, she always hunted for some ray of light; and no matter how dim, she kept her gaze fixed on it so steadily that she soon saw this sorrow sent as a test or challenge to the natural resources of strength and courage with which God had so abundantly endowed her. 

She was a born optimist; and later in life, when, as her pastor so charitably expressed it, she from necessity joined "The advanced class of the High School of Life," her favorite verse was "Two people look through the self-same bars; 

One sees the mud, the other, the stars."

As she was always desirous of seeing others as happy 14

as herself, she always kept the following verse in reserve, in case that the above terse, direct implication missed its mark- "The inside of every cloud 

Is ever bright and shining; 

I therefore turn my clouds about 

And wear them inside out, To show their silver linings.' 

When the stork came in for a landing that December night, in the town of Limington, it caused more than the usual stir because jovial James Cole was known and loved as the old-time fiddler and dancing master all over Maine and New Hampshire. He loved everybody; but his big heart loved children, especially. He would pick them up everywhere and hug and kiss them, no matter how many layers of dirt covered their faces. Since he was so well loved, everyone in the neighborhood looked ahead to the new baby's arrival with the same joyful expectancy as Jimmy and his young wife did.

All of us are familiar with the picture of the enormous bird zooming through the air, holding in its beak a knotted cloth with a baby reposing in it. This stork idea of bringing babies has always been on a par with the Santa Claus idea for explaining mysteries too deep for inquiring childish minds; and though no one has ever seen the stork on such an errand, we have to tell them that there must be one, or how could any one have gotten its picture? However, as good logic sometimes fails to convince even adults, the same old question oft continues to rend the air, 15

while a tired mother sits calmly knitting, "Mamma, where do babies come from?" As former Junior

Supt. of Woodfords Sunday School I shall never forget the wide-eyed young teacher who rushed up to my desk from a classroom, saying, "Mrs. Clough, while teaching the lesson about baby Jesus, a little girl just asked me where babies came from. What in the world shall I tell her?"

As the child's mother was a friend of mine, I told Charlotte to explain to the child that we had barely time for the lesson, and to go home and ask her mother about it. I heard plenty from the mother, but as I told her, it was her child, not mine, who almost caused the teacher to quit, and that we could not have that question rearing its inquisitive head at every lesson. This idea of the stork bringing babies from heaven is well borne out by this verse from an old poem- "Where did you come from, baby dear? 

Out of the nowhere into here. 

Where did you get your eyes so blue? 

Out of the skies as I came thro.' 99 

None of the neighbors heard the stork go over that night, as it travels very slowly while carrying such a precious burden; but they were awakened by the noisy flapping of its wings, on the return flight.

So every ear in the family at the foot of the hill was strained to hear the first jingle of the string of bells, fastened around the doctor's horse, which would soon follow.

Their vigilance was rewarded in about half an 16

hour by the sound of the familiar chiming bells and the clip-clop of the horse's feet as they moved swiftly over the icy roads, then slowed down at the bridge for the long climb to the top of the hill. The poor old doctor in the sleigh, after travelling all day within a radius of fifteen miles, had probably just bedded his tired old horse down for the night and was himself in bed, hoping for the unusual treat of a good night's sleep. 

And what a bed! Surely not as we know it, with a box spring, foam rubber or innerspring mattress, an electric or ten dollar wool blanket, and with radio alarm clock and telephone at arm's length. No, his mattress was made from dried corn husks stuffed into a large cotton bag, having an opening at either side so that a person could put his hand inside to stir up the husks each day, after they had become bedded down during the night. On top of this husk bed was a fat feather bed (sometimes two) which also had to be stirred up each day; and for covers, every one surely knows about the heavy patchwork quilts which are having such a run of popularity now. Their blankets were made from their own sheep's wool, the yarn being spun on the old spinning wheel by grandma, whom I have watched doing the same for many long hours. I still have the same old spinning wheel and flax wheel on which they spun flax into linen thread.

The doctor probably found his bed already occupied by strange bedfellows. Since the rooms were as cold as barns, the hot soapstones or bricks were in 17

there, each wrapped up in a heavy cloth to keep the heat within. I recall the night when my aunt was so glad of a visit from my brother Gus that she put in a third soapstone to show the extra warmth in her heart for him. As each stone was about 8 by 10 inches, he said that he thought he was in a graveyard when he woke up.

The reader need waste no pity on these poor people of by-gone days; for if he sleeps with his window open as health demands, and wakes up some cold morning to find his upper shoulder almost frozen, he has reason to envy the old-timer, sunk deep in his feather bed. My own discomfort at 3 A.M. this morning prompted the same spirit of envy; for after vainly trying to keep a folded sweater tucked in behind said shoulder, I remembered the good old days when the feather bed surface was level with my shoulder.

Besides this advantage of warmth, the necessary stirring up of husk and feather beds had an added advantage for a wife whose husband had offended by letting too much hard cider turn him into a big bully. Instead of dropping poison into his coffee, she simply spread up his bed, without stirring, and left it looking like a newly-plowed field so that hubby had to sleep as straight as if in a cast, rather than attempt to jack-knife himself over these ridges. A furtive glance across the table next morning at his unusually demure, innocent-looking little woman made him resolve to square things with her before night and get caught up on his lost sleep. 18

No, Grandma was never dumb when it came to putting the ball and chain on arrogant husbands when they began to strain at their leashes; far less so than our modern perfumed, made-up women of today who sneer at them. Divorces were seldom heard of then, because each party of the marriage agreement realized the great dependence that each had on the other; so they took marriage gravely, as a life time pact. 

While our modern butterfly wife gets a divorce because her husband snores or does not get his hair cut to suit her, the smart old grandma used her brains to out-smart them as God intended, else he would have never endowed us helpless females with a preponderence of them. All men take notice, please. Today it is not unusual to see Mama pushing a lawnmower, while Junior reads a comic book. While we think that grandma had it tough without all our laborsaving devices, we forget that she demanded and got help from all the family, else they ate plain crackers and milk for supper and slept on ridgey beds until the battle was over. Nope, Grandma was no dope! 

As expected, the good old doctor's hope of a good night's sleep proved in vain for his summons to Hilltop Farm came on scheduled time. Then he had to grab his shiny black bag, equipped for any emerand hasten to the cold barn of zero temperagency, ture. There he had to back the tired old horse out of his stall, take off the heavy horse blanket, put the metal bits in the mouth of the protesting horse (after blowing his warm breath on them to prevent their freezing to the horse's warm tongue), throw the 19

bridle over Dobbin's head, put on the saddle and rest of the harness, fasten the long string of bells around his body and hook up the traces to the sleigh. As the three mile drive to the farmhouse took only one half hour's time, he did not bother with a soapstone, but pulled heavy overshoes up over his high boots and heavy homeknit stockings. If modesty permits, I might add that he was covered from neck to ankles with heavy woolen underwear. Then he pulled a big fur cap down over his ears, tied a knit scarf around his neck, put on a big fur coat, and got into the sleigh, where he wrapped himself up in a large fur robe. Next he opened the barn door, climbed into his sleigh and with a flourish of the whip, shouted "Giddap" and off they went, the jingling bells proclaiming to all the neighbors along the way that favorite Jimmy's little girl would soon be here. 

For Doc Smith, no closed limousine that would have covered the distance in a few minutes. Our doctors do not know how to appreciate such luxuries nor will they make any night calls unless one is almost at death's door. Those good old doctors drove, day and night, within a radius of 15 miles, for the fabulous sum of $1.00 a call, which had to be hoarded much more carefully than the increased charge of $5.00 today.

When the old horse finally dragged himself and the old doctor up the long, slippery hill, they saw the farmhouse ablaze with lights downstairs, as it was a Cape Cod house, one and one-half stories high. I can still hear Ella apologizing to strangers for such 20

a poor, cheap looking old house, and hoping to build a better one, some day. So we folks of yesteryear have to laugh every time that we hear a proud city owner of such a house boast that this is the latest type of house-as if one can call something 150 years old, a new discovery.

Eager hands led the doctor's horse to the barn as he quickly jumped from the sleigh and went in to meet a very anxious group consisting of father, mother and grandparents. Hot water and blankets were ready; and soon the little girl protested her entrance into this new world with a loud wail. As the saying goes, "She was just what the doctor ordered"-with tiny, beautiful features and eyes so blue that they indeed looked, as the poet had said, as if she got them from the skies as she came through.

There was no trained nurse to whisk the baby off to a white sanitary room filled with modern appliances, with every one wearing masks over their mouths. No! She was simply carried out to the warm kitchen where grandma, who was doctor's helper at all births, took over. All hands stood around in the closed room, watching proceedings and luckily oblivious of the fact that they might possibly be filling the baby's lungs with germs enough to cause practically every disease known to mankind. When the last safety pin was in place, the sleeping child was carried off to the parent's bedroom which was kept warm by a crackling fire, in the little airtight stove.

A hot sensible meal had been prepared for Doc 

Smith but he merely ate three doughnuts with his 21

first cup of coffee. Then passing his mustache cup for more, he said, "Bring on that good mince pie, Liza, that you are hiding from me." He had ordered her not to eat these two kinds of food, so while the pie was warming in the oven she shot this barb at him: "I thought you said that doughnuts were harmful," to which he replied, with a wink at the others, "Just so, Liza, just so. Doughnuts are not fit for anyone to eat, except doctors." It is too bad that Liza was born too soon to pass him this priceless poem on diets, which I passed to my own doctor, after we had waged a war on diet, of nearly the intensity of the Korean one- "I do not quarrel with the plan 

Or scheme of life for any man. 

I merely say concerning diet 

I hope my doctor lives to try it; 

That when he comes to fifty-three 

He'll starve himself as well as me. 

I do not quarrel with his cure 'Tis meant to do me good, I'm sure, But still I notice, passing by, He's not at all afraid of pie, And puts what he denies to me 

Three lumps of sugar in his tea. 

I wish my doctor well and pray 

He'll live until his hair is gray, Reach fifty and receive a sheet 

Of food he likes, but mustn't eat. 22

My next hope, as he reads this diet, Is I'll be here to watch him try it."

There were no phones in those days, and as the doctor went back home he could see heads in the windows of the five houses along the road to Cornish, and before he could get over to the post-office in the morning the news was already there ahead of him about the birth of Jimmy's little girl. When he started his rounds of calls down through Limington, nine miles away, he was stopped, as usual, at three different houses to tell the news of the night before. 

At sound of his bells, Liza was standing at the kitchen window. He waved merrily at her, chuckling over the thought that she was struggling against the desire to see him burn in oil (or other wicked thought) at seeing him so hale and hearty after his repast of doughnuts and pie, forbidden to her who cooked them.

Everyone was astir at the farmhouse early the next morning, and after the farm chores were done Ella and Liza made out a long list of things to be bought at the village store. Jimmy set forth in the same kind of attire that the doctor had worn the night before; but as so many friends stopped him along the way and in the village, it was a long time before he could get the old sorrel mare hitched in front of Small 

Bros. store. They declare that when he read off the item "clothespins" from his list of things to buy and they asked him how many, he said that he guessed that half a dozen would do. Whereon they told him 23

that with a new baby perhaps he had better take a whole dozen to be sure of enough. True or not, this story greeted him wherever he went to play for dances, even as far as Bartlett, N. H.; and no one enjoyed the fun over it more than himself. It was from him that Blanche inherited the wonderful trait of wanting to see every one laughing and happy, even if the joke was on herself. 

Jimmy had to literally tear himself away from the warm handclasps and greetings of his enthusiastic friends as he was impatient to get home and find the baby awake; and never had the merry sleighbells made such sweet music to his ears. He was disappointed to find the baby asleep but he was still very happy and proud of the picture that greeted him as he tiptoed into the sitting room. Blanche had been bathed and powdered, and was now asleep in the picturesque old cradle which Grandma sat jogging with her foot to leave Susan free to get dinner for the family and hired men. She was softly singing the sweet old lullaby which has lulled many of the most important men of the nation to sleep, 1 "Baby is sleeping, so cozy and fair,

While mother sits near in her old oaken chair, Her foot on the rocker, the cradle she swings, And though baby slumbers he hears what she sings.

Chorus 

Rock-a-bye, rock-a-bye, mother is near, 24

Rock-a-bye, rock-a-bye, nothing to fear, For angels of slumber are hovering near

So rock-a-bye baby, mother is here. 2 Grandma sits knitting beside the fireplace 

With snowy white hair, and a smile on her face 

The years have passed by, though it does not seem long,

Since she rocked baby's papa to sleep with that song.

Chorus 

Rock-a-bye baby, on the treetop 

When the wind blows, the cradle will rock;

When the bough breaks, the cradle will fall 

And down will come baby, cradle and all."

With the same feeling of pride that he had bought such a liberal supply of clothespins, Jimmy passed them over to Ella, telling her to use as many as she needed; and if not enough, he would buy plenty more. Yes, sir! No sacrifice would ever be too great for this daughter! 

So Blanche slept on during her babyhood days, snuggled down in her little feather bed as snug as a bug in a rug, while Grandma continued to idly rock the cradle with her foot while knitting, or Grandpa would do the same while shelling by hand the winter's supply of colored beans. Again both were happily oblivious of the scathing remarks which would 25

be heaped upon their heads within twenty-five years, by myself and all other new mothers. 

I was almost as riled last week by the condescending remarks of a prospective nineteen-year-old mother as I was years ago by the Mas-sar-chusetts pest. Looking down her nose at me, she carefully explained all about the new ways with babies, which were exactly the same as in 1880 except for cradle rocking. And I glow with pride that I have mentioned the cradle before some other chit of a girl will tell me all about the newest and best discovery of all times-the cradle. They already use them now; only with the obstinacy of youth, they refer to those swinging contraptions suspended from the roof of the automobile and having the same motions and success of the old cradles, as baby hammocks. In the neighboring branches of Blanche's family tree were her brother Gus, her father and mother, and grandparents Gilman and Eliza. As her mother was an only child, her only other relatives were an uncle and three aunts, with numerous cousins on her father's side. This tree had firmly thrust its roots down into the solid earth of Hilltop Farm which had quite an historical background. 

The house was built before there was any means of transportation to Portland (for commercial purposes) except by ox-teams. There were usually two yokes of oxen drawing a long sled, heavily laden with shooks, lumber, hand-woven cloth, and butter and eggs to be exchanged for rum, molasses, fish and machinery for their farm use. In my day the 33-mile trip required two days of travel, the first over-night stop being at a Gorham tavern. Jimmy was born here. His father, Christopher Cole, died when Jimmy was six years old, leaving his widow and five children with no means of support except from money earned by running this tavern and from a small farm. This was the first stop for ox-teams after leaving Freedom,

N. H., fifteen miles to the north. Many an evening 29

You can imagine the excitement outside as these oxen and drivers pulled into the door-yard; and the extra shouting as the oxen were led into the barn, fed, watered and bedded down with straw for the night. Inside, the old cider and liquor flowed freely as these teamsters were rough men and these trips provided the only means of escape from the eagle, watchful eyes of their respective wives. They reveled in these occasional experiences of riotous living. A Sunday School teacher once asked a child what one had to do when he had sinned; and was amazed at the reply that he had to sin, first. Spanking, though considered barbarous by modern mothers, was much more effective than to tell Johnny how naughty he had been, get a promise that he would never do it again; and then, for fear that his little feelings were hurt beyond repair, send him to the store for candy or ice cream. Sure of what punishment would follow, it became to him merely a question of deciding whether the fun was of sufficient value to offset the suffering, and following discomfort of having to stand up or lie down for several hours. 

As the same routine was followed on the farm every day, the animals became creatures of habit, also. A favorite trick of Jim's was to tie a small string around a kernel of yellow corn, watch the hen swallow the corn, and then lead her around watching with glee her efforts to swallow the long string. Not conscience, but fear of a spanking would lead him to cut the string near the hen's beak, whenever he heard approaching footsteps. If the person wondered at the hen's sudden gulp of satisfaction, he was no wiser as to the cause of the hen's strained effort to swallow the string instead of the usual kernel of corn.

In line with her favorite verse, later in life, about every cloud having a silver lining, it was lucky that her constitution became hardened in the process because her brother did not share in the family happiness at her birth. To him, she was just a nuisance, and the more they petted her and praised her to the neighbors, the more out of joint became his more mature nose of two years. When she began to toddle around and grab his things away from him, they heard him ask the doctor one day if he didn't have a little boy in his valise for which she could be swapped. When the doctor said that they were out of boy babies, just then, and wouldn't have any for a long time, Gus wondered why they hadn't drowned her before she got her eyes open, as he knew that they had done with some kittens. Just a few minutes more would have finished the job which he thought should have been done when they found her in the doctor's valise instead of a worthwhile boy.

They did not do perfect teamwork at all times because she had a very sunny disposition and was too tender-hearted to enjoy mischief which would harm anyone or make others unhappy. Her one grievous fault was in being overinquisitive about everybody and everything and, sad to relate for those who were in close contact with her daily life, this fault increased every year. If anyone told her that anything was so, that was not the end of the matter, but only the beginning. 

A striking example of this came to light when she was about four years old. Doctor Smith, having dropped in for a cup of coffee one morning after bringing Eliza home from a night's vigil with a new mother, was in the midst of a detailed discussion of the night's events when they heard the tiniest squeak of a door. Liza pointed to the door and said, "Little pitchers have big ears," and so they changed the subject to commonplace remarks. In the meantime, Blanche quietly moved a chair up to the dish cupboard and began moving the dishes around, finally putting a row of pitchers out front. Finally Liza said, "What on earth are you doing that for, Blanche?" To which she replied, her voice dripping 48

with disgust, "They don't either have big ears. All these pitchers have little ears." She couldn't see why this was told, over and over, to the neighbors and why they laughed so much about it. To her it was just a naughty story for which she would have been punished for telling, according to her way of deduction.

This was her first contact with the unfairness and injustice of the cold world. As she had resented something which the doctor had done, who laughed now until his false teeth dropped down (another thing which her sharp eyes noted to ask about, later) she thought that this would be a good time to let him know of the smouldering resentment which she had held back for a long time rather than to hurt his feelings. So she said, with a defiant toss of her head, "Papa said that Melissa was born with a silver spoon in her mouth. Now why didn't you put one in my mouth, when you took me out of your valise?" This was too much for the old doctor who, keeping his finger against his teeth this time (as he had seen the sharp eyes of Blanche checking this matter for future inquiry), laughed harder than ever. Then slapping his knees he said, "Blanche, if you don't stop asking questions we shall either put you in the iron kettle and boil you with the rooster, or I shall swap you for the brother that Gus wants. And no knowing who would ever want such a bundle of mischief as you are." That brought the expected protest and tearful promise never to be naughty again. The doctor, forthwith, opened his shiny bag which she 49

watched with dreaded gaze, expecting to see the new brother pulled out; but her eyes danced with joy at sight of a long stick of red and white candy which he had bought for his favorite baby girl. After he had collected his wage of a big hug and kiss and promised to bring that silver spoon the next time he went by, Blanche decided that big folks were hard to understand. Sometimes one got scolded for asking too many questions and trying to find things out all by herself; then again one got kisses and candy for doing the same thing. She soon learned that a kiss was an open sesame to many other wishes from her uncle and the men of the neighborhood, not learning until later this common weakness of mankind.

And what did Blanche do for amusement all day?

Besides poking her nose and fingers into everything 51

She had no one to play with in these pre-school years except her jealous brother; and playing with him always ended in having her favorite things destroyed or in getting a slap, as soon as the game could be maneuvered into getting her beyond earshot of her parents. Ernest Cole, a cousin, laughed a few weeks ago about her continual wail, "Jim, Gus hurt me.' 

Every family had a huge box filled with buttons which had been cut from worn-out garments, and 

Blanche used to string these on long pieces of thread just to pass away the time. But one day, she noticed that they seemed to jump around like children when she pulled the string up with a few buttons on it; so she visualized them as other playmates. A two inch string would be made to stand in the corner, representing naughty Gus; while a longer string would be the father walking over to punish him. She could create any number of playmates and improvise all sorts of games with them, which brings to light another saying, "Necessity is the mother of invention.'

While these children would have enjoyed television and movies as much as the more favored ones of today, they would never have developed the self-reliance and resourcefulness which they were forced to have for every occasion. 

At six A.M. Blanche used to crawl out from between her soapstones and run through the back room to a hot wood fire, where some one helped her to pull on her long-legged underdrawers, over which 52

were the usual black knit stockings. Then she put on a white flannel petticoat, and over it a red one having deep crocheted yarn lace at the bottom. The people of today, rejoicing over the new fad of half-slips, add new sources of hilarity for the old people who realize that they are merely wearing the same old style petticoats that we did. Same in style, only, as one of ours would have equalled about ten of the new ones in weight and cost about one third as much. Everyone wore high boots. The women's were buttoned on the side with a button hook which was usually misplaced, in which case some bright female thought of using a wire hairpin instead.

Blanche had a great desire for pretty things then as she does now. When three pairs of boots had been brought home to be tried on, her eyes sparkled at sight of a wonderful creation from the top of which dangled a long black silk tassel. But she knew what a struggle she might have in getting her folks to keep them, for usually the only considerations for buying was-is it warm? and will it wear well? Blanche held her breath during the usual pinching of toes and the usual debate as to wearing qualities. Between her squeals of delight she would say "O, they are so pretty! Can't I keep them?" But still the discussion went on. Now Ella never made up her mind to buy anything until she took it home to think over, and nine times out of ten, she took it back to swap. Thus when she finally said that they would keep the tasseled boots, Blanche said, "O, Mamma, have they got to be swapped first?" 53

Blanche ate every kind of food on the table, not from soup to nuts, but from fried pork and gravy to hot mince pie. Then every bristling mustache, still moist from being cleaned after eating by being drawn through the owner's mouth, and every bewhiskered mouth of the farm hands with fresh particles of pie still nestling in the whiskers, brushed across her face as the strange assortment of farmhands kissed her goodbye. As this was kept up practically three times a day during her childhood one wonders how she ever dared to face another day, but she accepted it stoically as a necessary affliction such as a nosebleed or having a tooth pulled. 

After breakfast, Blanche derived amusement according to the season of the year. At the first patch of bare ground, in the spring, mud pies held great fascination. At the price of another slimy kiss, a farmhand could be found to make wonderful cupboards by piling two or three boards on separate bricks, also a stove could be contrived. Here she would spend the whole forenoon skillfully turning out a long row of beautifully baked pies and cakes.

Then she would set her old black Dinah doll up to the table for a wonderful feed. 

Dinner was exactly at twelve when every one pumped water at the kitchen sink, if he had one, and washed up for dinner; otherwise, 'the water was drawn from the well. Then the usual process was gone through of pouring hot tea into a saucer, and blowing on it to cool it off, for those who were not manly enough to grow a heavy mustache. But the 54

more favored specimens of vigorous manhood used mustache cups which had a little earthen shelf on one side to hold the mustache above the liquid which came through a small center opening. I could fill pages on this particular pride and joy of man, not only from observation, but from bitter personal experience. This was undergone by me not only at home but later on at the only form of recreation in the neighborhood-a kissing party once a month. As there was only one other girl of my age, and she openly rebelled by carrying a pin to stick in every male specimen who tried to kiss her, one can see what kind of a licking Blanche took, being too kind-hearted to offend any one who really liked her. Had she died an old maid at twenty (the hopeless age for marriageable maidens then) she would have been way ahead of the score allotted for the usual span of life. 

The mark of highest triumph for a man who had a rival for a woman's affection was to look scornfully at the other man's smooth upper lip; then with a far-away look, slowly twirl the ends of his mustache and draw each one into a long pointed waxed end, about two inches long. As every one was at work during the day, meal time was story time; and the following one was told to impress a budding youth with the importance of this worthy adjunct for getting some girl's affection. 

In a certain home was a very bashful quiet girl of about seventeen who, presumably had never given nor received any attention from the opposite sex. 

When an owner of a bristling mustache said that a 55

This quick reply from such an unexpected source caused some one to spill the hot tea which he was blowing in his saucer, into his lap, and in the ensuing commotion the girl, realizing what her admission implied, beat a hasty retreat, in great confusion. Once again the truth of the old saying that "Still waters run deep" was verified. I was disbarred from this one-sided contest because, like the girl, I had never eaten peas without salt, either.

After haying came harvesting when Blanche tried to be everywhere at once, and as near to every one as her inquisitive mind could lead her feet, and always as near Jimmy as she could get. Every little while, when his good nature got stretched too far, one would hear "For heaven's sake, Blanche, get out from under foot." No one seemed to realize how hard she worked nor how tired she got at the end of the day!

One of the most interesting harvest experiences was the potato digging process. Blanche was amazed to see so many potatoes roll out from a hill where she had dropped only one seed potato in the spring. 

Another amusing incident happened here. Every motion which Jim made was as quick as lightning (a trait which Blanche inherited from him); and the harder he worked, the more he whistled and sang. 58

Blanche was bound to help him pick up potatoes, and though he knew what a nuisance she would be, he took her along. Soon he had dug up a long row and, coming back to her, began to gather them up at his usual speed, two handfuls at a time. She could not keep up with him where each potato was as large as her small hand. Soon she leaned back and began to cry as hard as she had when she had been sitting on the wasp's nest. When he asked her if she was sick or what the matter was, she began to wail, “You are taking them all away from me." It struck him as very funny since there were bushels on the ground and he could hardly see the end of the row. 

Then there were apples and pears to pick, and the most exciting of all times was cider-making time, watching the big horse hitched to a long pole, going round and round in the same circle all day long. This turned a wheel in the cider press which squeezed out the juice which ran off in a trough to a tub while the apple pulp dropped to the floor. Men hauled their apples to the cider-mill and paid so much a gallon for use of the mill. Nearly every cellar was kept full of barrels of this necessary tonic for the winter's work of logging and cutting enough wood to keep three fires going. My mother, who would never allow more than two barrels in our cellar, said that she never could understand why men had to keep full of cider in the winter to keep warm, and in the haying season to keep cool. To an uninterested observer this made good logic. There was never a shortage of help to a farmer whose cellar was full of cider. 59

If, perchance, any cider was left in the spring, women dosed every one with cider bitters, and a novice at this spring tonic learns for the first time the true meaning of the word bitter. Like the terrible tasting stuff that doctors put into their medicine, it seemed as if the women had an uncanny sense of what herbs or tree-barks would make the bitterest kind of liquid. And every one got dosed, even if his nose had to be held to facilitate the process. And lives there a person of yesteryear who will ever forget that scourge of humanity?

Though crude and homemade, it seemed as if children enjoyed them far more than the costly and complicated ones of today. As you may recall, in the preceding chapter I referred to the Chase and Sanborn slogan which was, "What Mr. Chase didn't know about coffee, Mr. 

Sanborn did." I added that the same teamwork existed between Gus and Blanche, which it did, but only in perfection of operation. If we juggle the words around in the quotation to fit these imps it reads, "What Gus didn't know about mischief, Blanche did." While the result of the Chase and

Sanborn teamwork was for the good of the public, this unholy alliance resulted in benefit to the two culprits only because it was a constant menace to the general public. 68

There is an age-old controversy as to whether heredity or environment is the most potent factor in governing a person's behavior. In this case there is no doubt that environment was the prime factor in aiding and abetting the various schemes of these children, who gave their parents the same feeling of peace and security as living on the edge of a volcano would have.

In trying to think which of these angles of environment to mention first, since there were so many on this large farm, the words of this Spiritual song came to mind instantly. "I got-a shoes; you got-a shoes; all God's chilluns got-a shoes. When I git to heb’n, goin' to put on my shoes, Goin' to put on my shoes, Goin' to walk all ovah God's heb'n."

I presume that it was the similarity of purpose between God's chillun and these two mischief makers to cover as much ground as possible on their chosen mission, and the repeated word chillun which made memory reproduce this song so quickly. The difference was that while God's chillun were so elated at having shoes to expedite the process of getting over a big territory, quickly, Jim's children were just 'as elated at having discovered how much more quickly and stealthily they could cover large patches of ground without shoes. Whittier's poem describes the same ecstacy which he may have felt, as a barefoot boy, "Blessings on thee, little man, Barefoot boy, with cheeks of tan." 69

He closed with these words, which struck an answering chord in the children's hearts- 'All too soon, these feet must hide In the prison cells of pride; 

Lose the freedom of the sod, Like a colt's, for work be shod. 

Happy if their tracks be found 

Never on forbidden ground." 

Blanche and Gus would have taken issue with the poet over the last two lines because they were happier when their tracks did lead them all over forbidden ground.

If one looks at the buildings in the picture he will see how well the house was planned to allow many avenues of escape for children who felt it necessary to be absent from the public eye. At the northern end was a roll-away door opening from the cellar; and on the front was a door into the main house, a door on the piazza, a stable door, and a barn door. At the end of the barn, was another door which opened into the cowyard. On the back of the house were two doors, and a culprit in hiding could sneak up the front stairs, past the parlor, then go the whole length of the house through two attics and drop down to a hay mow. If the trail was getting too hot for him he could crawl down to the barn floor, then out through the cowyard, up across a field, over a stone wall, and then on to New Hampshire, if he so chose. As stated before, there were always stone walls running in every direction and beckoning to fugitives from justice. Thus one can see why these chilluns did not 70

want shoes for these occasional meanderings.

Heredity also played a prominent part in the lives of these two children, so for clarity's sake we need to scrutinize the adults more closely than we have done. 

When Ella Norton was in her teens Eliza Norton married Gilman Crane of West Baldwin where they lived until they came to live with Jim and Ella after 

Gus was born. 

Eliza was what we call "spry-tempered" and a great talker, an affliction which was passed down to Blanche of the third generation with increased intensity. When Ella's father Frank Norton died, Ella was a child and so Eliza had to work very hard to support her. Probably she married a second time for the security of a home and the good living which Gilman's farm could provide, for in those days the only way that a woman could have a home was to work out in some one else's, or to marry one. While Eliza was an awfully busy talker and did a great deal of scolding, she was a wonderful neighbor and ready to go wherever there was sickness, day or night. At heart, she was very kind and forgiving, never holding grudges nor "Letting the sun go down on her wrath."

Gilman, as one would expect from life's mismating pranks, was the exact opposite in disposition and habits. He was just the kind of a meek, submissive and self-effacing man that a strong-minded, hustling woman marries. I have never been able to figure out this combination of mates, as to whether the man got caught under the mistletoe by the scheming 71

woman or whether he lacked the spirit and effort to withstand her verbal attacks and the wearing-down process, common to a determined woman when she starts out to snare a mate. As I remember him, he was always content to sit in the same chair, at the same side of the fireplace, smoke his pipe and make a real science of keeping the same fire going from October to March by raking ashes over the embers at night and stirring them open in the morning. He seldom spoke; just listened and smiled, and never showed any spirit until some one dared to poke his fire or put a stick of wood on it. Then the effort to express his wrath was so unusual that by the time he had coughed, taken the pipe out of his mouth, and spit vigorously before uttering the torrent of words for which he had prepared, the words never got any farther than his throat where they seemed to stick. 

Only a few convulsive movements of his prominent Adam's Apple gave any sign of the inner storm which subsided as quickly as it had started.

He was always very patient and kind to Eliza and they were in perfect agreement about work, she being willing to milk three cows, churn and make the butter, besides doing nearly all the work in the man's realm around the barn. He was just as willing to let her do these chores; in fact, she never expected anything else. In their twenty or more years at the farm he was very chivalrous in carrying one milk pail to the barn while she carried two, and in hunting up the stool on which she would sit to milk three cows. He never learned to milk because he said that his fingers 72

were too long, which seemed a very good excuse; after all, learning to milk did not seem of enough importance to have his fingers amputated at the first joints. As in the story of the "Three Bears," whenever work confronted him something about his constitution made it all wrong for him; but for Eliza, work and utensils were always just the right size. 

After milking, he would watch her strain the milk into large tin pans after they had carried the milk down cellar. Then he would sit down again in his corner after putting on the fire a new forestick, with as much precision as one would use in rigging out a battleship.

Ella was different from her mother in practically every way. While she did not have the mean, cruel look which Ike was purported to have, by Harry, she did have a very mean, jealous disposition and was a chronic nagger in her family. It was her boast that she never forgot an injury nor slighting remark, a real Indian trait of which she seemed very proud.

Evidently she never approved of her mother's second marriage; or she never approved of Gilman, for she never noticed his presence in any way, by word or look, if she could help it. How true is the saying that no kitchen is big enough for two women to work in, at the same time. There was always discord in this one which usually ended in real verbal battles over who should wait for the other to pump water, use the sink, or what have you.

As Blanche was terribly frightened she asked many questions in the morning about its hurting, etc., but not a word was heard from Gus. I have given little space to the twin subject-that of Spanks-because that was a universal form of punishment-handed down from the Puritans, an accepted form of correction for wayward feet which had strayed too far from the paths of righteousness. "Spare the rod and spoil the child" was the accepted slogan in all families, and the majority of well-disciplined men in public life today proudly acknowledge that the frequent visits to the woodshed with father, after wrongdoing which justified a good thrashing, was a prime factor in making them successful men. 

In contrast is the modern idea of indulging Johnny in his desire to run the family car while the family has to sit at home, of allowing him to boss the whole household, indulge in drinking and all sorts of wrongdoing while they tearfully and fearfully wish that he would be as well-behaved as Steve's boy. Steve's boy will be prepared when he faces the hard realities of future life and not have to learn the hard way that Johnny will that life is no bed of roses for those unprepared to meet its hardships. 

We laugh today at the old-fashioned phrase "Mother knows best." But Grandma found it easier to discipline children with a firm hand than does the modern mother filled with so many doubts and uncertainties and with vain longings that Johnny will outgrow his hateful ways and actions. Whoever said "The hand that rocks the cradle rules the world" must certainly have held the old-fashioned way of discipline in highest regard.

The building was of the simple one-room type. 

The door opened into a long entry; from there a door on the left opened into the woodshed, the one opposite opening into the school room. Here on the right was a long wooden blackboard beside the teacher's desk which was in front of a window. There was also another blackboard on the side of the room near the door. The erasers were just oblong pieces of wood covered with heavy cloth. The honor of cleaning these erasers after school was a special reward for the good child, thus advancing him further into the good graces of the teacher-sort of "an apple for teacher" impulse. This privilege was also coveted by the bad child with an eye out for mitigating the blows of the chastening rod when the one man or one woman jury demanded it.

Teacher's desk was on a platform in the center front of the room. It was just a long wooden chest 92

with cover, standing on wooden legs, and was kept closed except when necessary to get school supplies or the rod of justice from it. As imagination may have caught up with the idea that this rod might naturally be spirited away some dark night by one of 

God's chillun, I will clear up this point by saying that the first time that happened the culprit learned that "Discretion is the better part of valor." Becoming over-bold on the next day, and chuckling over the absence of this weapon from the desk, he was not only chastised by a rough knotted switch in place of the nicely smoothed one but had to cut it from an overhanging tree limb with his treasured jackknife.

The floor was made of wide pine boards, having hollows worn in it where restless feet stood most often over a period of long years. As there was no cellar, the drafts of cold air coming up through the floor would have been unbearable, had the children not been dressed to withstand the cold. The boys wore long heavy stockings and heavy rubber shoes while the girls wore heavy knit stockings and leggings. Resulting chilblains were an accompanying factor. How about that style of dress, nylon enthusiasts and bobby-soxers of today?

While children in the back seats were slowly congealing with added cold coming through the walls of the room, those down front might be sweating with the intense heat from the long iron stove. The whole front of the stove dropped down for a door to take in large sticks of wood. Someone had to go early to build the fire of blazing kindling wood, then 93

put in a huge chunk of wood and leave every draft open until the stovetop became nearly red hot. New chunks had to be put in during the day and this job was sought by all the big boys who, like the men with big mustaches gloating over them "which hath not," liked to show their great strength before the admiring eyes of their favorite girls.

This stove was the focal point of interest in many ways. For the sacrificial boy who went after the wood there was always the opportunity of stopping in the entry to get an extra snack which was put in the dinner pail for that purpose or, better still, a chance for the less conscientious one to look over the contents of the other pails and exchange his plain doughnut for a tasty sugared one. The extra time for this diversion could always be managed by toppling over a few chunks of wood with one's foot, for sound effect, until the doughnut had been forced down. 

The last gulp was generally attended with as much effort as that made by Jimmy's hen in trying to swallow a long string.

More mischief could be planned around this piece of equipment, as a roving eye considered its possibilities, than in any other article except the tin water pail. Every kind of smelly thing imaginable could always be thrown upon it by those in the front row while teacher was standing at the blackboard; and with one concerted motion, every big geography would be in front of everyone's face when the vile aroma began to steal through the air. I can still see the injured expression of the culprit as, in answer to 94

Pepper was the favorite powder as every one could have a part in the sneezing which followed and thus detract several minutes from the tiresome day. Everything from tiny snapcrackers through popcorn and other noise makers, even to a previously embedded bullet in a chunk of wood, played its part in adding zest and excitement to the long day—from nine A.M. to four P.M. An old rubber carefully placed under a big stick of green wood was especially fragrant, for by the time that it had melted to the offensive stage it had run down into the ashes where it would continue to give off fragrance for hours. Chestnuts, also, ranked high in poppable material. 

We have already considered the germ-laden water pail and common dipper as a good source of mischief for a boy to throw water on the girls at noon or recess; but it had continual use in school hours as well. 

At as short intervals as they dared someone would ask, "Teacher, may I get a drink?" Then several different things might happen. If teacher were back to the school the last swallow of water might be diverted from its regular channel, down the owner's throat, to the head of some studious child nearby, with the resulting squeak of surprise. Or a casual observer might notice a note slyly tucked under the edge of the pail, after a clearing of the boy's throat gave signal to that certain girl who suddenly became thirsty, also. I know from experience the amount of revenue that the government would have 95

been losing had postage been required for mailing these missiles.

Water from this pail was sprinkled on the floor before it was swept in order to lay the dust. Probably many dirty hands were dipped into the pail to add an extra quota of germs to those already in hiding from the day before. Many more germs must have settled there during the day with all the dirt and chalkdust flying around and, added to this, all the sneezing and assorted odors from the stove, where something was always cooking.

The seats had to accommodate the length of legs from the six year old children to those of the flock of older boys who had a sudden yen for learning whenever there was a pretty female schoolteacher. 

For those who sat on the long board on the back wall, running the whole length of the room and facing the three double seats, no water line of communication was needed. Sliding notes along the board seat was as easy as the so-called grapevine method of communication. And though teacher could usually tell by the absorption of back seat students when a note was somewhere in transit, she knew that it would be as difficult to find it as would be the case of finding the key in the game where a key is passed from one closed hand to the other, with all participants standing in a large circle. 

Another advantage of sitting in the back seat was the chance to look out the windows and watch all the passing objects. A teacher was judged good or bad according to the number or scarcity of faces peeking 96

out through the windows. Those children were unconsciously adding extra value to their booklearning -that of self-discipline. I got many a stiff neck in keeping my head at such an angle that I could observe what was to follow the first rumble of wagon wheels and the clicking of horses' feet on the hard ground. 

As I was obliged to wear glasses when I was thirteen years old, possibly this sidewise straining of my eyes whenever I was lucky enough to sit near a window was a contributing factor to my defect.

The seat par excellence was the one in the further corner, opposite the blackboard by the door. This faced the side of the teacher's desk but was a seat length behind it. From this vantage point one could face the whole school, munch apples behind upraised books and cut up any kind of capers so long as no noise was made to attract teacher's attention. This seat which was prized for its seclusion and mischievous possibilities was the object of all kinds of barter including jackknives, choice articles of food, and even from a lukewarm sweetheart, rights to one's best girl.

'But first, an explanation of the district school. The towns were divided into nine or more districts which were usually called deestricts, in Yankee vernacular, and there was always this one-room type of building. 

A school committee bought all supplies and hired the teachers, after a proper examination had been made of their qualifications for teaching. The older boys had to help with the farm work during most of the spring and fall terms, then they entered for the winter term. They cared little about booklearning, as a rule, since they seemed doomed for life to farm work to help their fathers support the large families which was the usual case. Thus there had to be a man teacher in this winter term where brawn and muscle were the chief requisites for a teacher, who expected to keep even a semblance of order. It is no exaggeration that if the boys did not like this man teacher or objected to his brand of discipline they would lug him out of doors and leave him sitting in the snow, while they went inside and locked the door. 

But as all leaders of youth know, a wise teacher who would play ball with the boys and tell them adventure stories with lots of zip could easily make these same giants as meek and submissive as lambs.

Now we come to the climatic point in this description of the country school-a trip down the flowery path of knowledge. This subject of knowledge had highest importance only in mature minds, for in the minds of the children it was a decided anticlimax. One writer makes mention of the birch stick always laying across three pegs driven in the upper part of the wall and says that it was often used to urge some tardy loiterer along this path of knowledge. Many of them preferred to loiter since they did not consider gaining knowledge of any importance whatsoever for they were too engrossed in finding new paths of mischief and fun for their wayward feet to follow.

A child was supposed to know all the letters of the alphabet before he was sent to school as well as be able to count up to ten or one hundred, according to the vision of his parents. The two outstanding features of the district school were the superintendent's visit and the closing exercises on the last afternoon. The superintendent visited the school twice during the term, and the second visit was an occasion of mingled joy and dread because he came to check on the progress made during the term. As his approach could be seen one-fourth of a mile away by the favored onlooker in the back seat, that scholar would obligingly inform the teacher, before his horse was hitched to the apple tree at the corner of the building. Blanche's long year of waiting was over at last and she will soon embark on the flowery path of knowledge at Bean schoolhouse. When this new method came into use the only thanks which a fond loving mother got for sending her child to school with the ability to print the alphabet was a polite note from the teacher telling her not to teach Johnny his letters as these were not taught any more.

Mention is made in the poem "The Barefoot Boy" of the valuable knowledge which Whittier derived from nature, the teacher who inspired him to write- "I was rich in flowers and trees, Humming-birds and honey-bees;

These two pranksters of Hilltop Farm were more or less rich in this kind of lore also. Now as larger chillun' of God, their feet were encased in larger shoes called by the poet "Prison cells of Pride." They should have availed themselves of the opportunity to use these as agents to help them delve deeper into the mysteries of nature which surrounded them on every side. As we learned in a preceding chapter, however, they were far more interested in the mysteries and plottings for mischief. In spite of their advanced knowledge, they clung with their usual tenacity, to their own version of Whittier's later advice which they preferred to interpret- 

Happy if their tracks be found

As their line of growth was fourfold, it necessarily followed that along with their increased capacity for intellectual growth came also a larger capacity for 139

acquiring less desirable traits like juvenile intrigue and the desire to defy the laws of God and man. It is conceded by judges of juvenile courts that the usual underlying cause of misdemeanors is the forced idleness of children and lack of constructive things to occupy their active minds. They concede the truth of the saying "Idleness is the Devil's workshop."

Since there were no banks to rob on the farm, no autos to steal or use for abducting stray women, and no beer joints to patronize, there was a lesser number of sins for older boys to copy from their elders. These apparent sins consisted mostly of profanity, smoking and becoming tipsy from overindulgence in hard cider.

As to profanity, a boy was usually torn between two conflicting emotions: either to emulate Dad and swear like a pirate with every sentence uttered, or to yield to mother's pleadings to be a little gentleman and use the same kind of refined language that his teacher used. A real conscientious boy would usually follow the common course of swearing like several pirates while out behind the barn whittling with other boys, and then revert to mother's little man as soon as he entered the house.

The smoking problem was much easier to handle as some inventive brain discovered that the large heavy ribs of rattan which kept an umbrella open made an ideal substitute for tobacco. It could be cut up in the same way, stuffed into a pipe bowl, and would give off smoke. It had no pleasant taste, but neither did tobacco, as children had learned by gagging over 140

slyly stolen pieces; so they were well content to merely go through all the motions of the smoking process.

No warning had to be given against tobacco chewing as they revolted against the sight of slovenly farm hands with nasty-looking brown juice always oozing from the corners of their mouths, excepting the more fortunate ones who had beards to catch the unsavory drippings.

The especial drawback to this smoking orgy by older boys was the desire to smoke real tobacco. 

Many wise fathers nipped this desire in the bud by slyly filling the boy's pipe with real tobacco, and in nearly all cases the victim would be so sick that he could never tolerate tobacco again. This was what happened to Jimmy, who never again smoked after this first sickness. And a father who, in answer to his son's pleading for a cigar, kindly lights one and passes it to him is not forgiven for a long time after it has been smoked.

Reverting to mention of the glorious Fourth—if you condemned the fairly civilized way in which it was celebrated a few years ago, with nothing worse than an ear-splitting racket for a week, and now and then a few fingers blown off or a few eyes put out of commission, you would have been satisfied that it was nothing compared to the celebration in the quiet town of Cornish.

The racket started with the vigorous pealing of the bell of the Methodist Church, lasting until daybreak. I do not need to be more explicit for those who took part in those terrifying doings, to which older boys and men assembled from the neighboring farms; for every such man reader is now chuckling at the remembrance of his favorite escapade. And I certainly would not dare suggest to the present gen- 163

But it wouldn't do any harm to mention how many missing gates, rocking chairs, and vehicles might be located by next nightfall, down to Limington village, nine miles away; or up at Hiram, six miles in the other direction; or how a farmer, after looking everywhere for his twowheeled gig for a quick trip to the store the next morning, would reluctantly hitch up the heavy Concord wagon. Then in making the turn at the foot of the hill, his eyes would pop at seeing the gig majestically astride the high stable roof. I nearly jumped into the street instead of bearing off to the left, until I saw a muscular lad of about twenty being dragged along by a string, hitched to the skunk's collar. Then 

I recalled an article in our Press Herald about unusual pets in our two cities, including baby lions, snakes, and other ferocious animals, whose owners were peeved because the public shied away to the other side of the sidewalk. They even remonstrated with the authorities because their man-eating pets were denied the freedom of the streets. Special mention was made of this skunk who was frequently seen holding priority rights among the crowds of shoppers.

After Luther had compared our own oxen with his own White-heads; and inquired how much milk each cow gave (especially Eliza's full-blooded Jersey, called Twilight) they went in to the usual Saturday night baked bean supper. These beans were baked differently and tasted far better than any others, because they were thrust way back in a previously heated brick oven; and with the brownbread, baked slowly all day. I remember the shovel, three feet long, which had to be used to drag them forward to the door. The children were content to follow their usual role, at the supper hour, of being seen and not heard; for they knew that their turn would come tomorrow to cook up a whole day-full of deviltry.

Furthermore, children really enjoyed listening to talk from out-siders, as it was their only way of learning 171

what was happening in the outside world, which held such fascination for them. After supper, the men would retire to the fireplace to swap yarns and smoke, followed by the three children. The mention of the towns of Hiram, Denmark and Bridgton held as much fascination for them, as do the words Paris and Rome to travelers of today. 

And if, perchance, anyone had performed the great miracle of riding to Portland on the train, they sat just spellbound to hear about the palatial Union Station with its marble floors and beautiful walls. This was a far cry from their own wooden station of one small room, with a pot-bellied wood stove in the center of it, which had a long black funnel running the whole length of the room.

Each one was, as befitted the times, a simple game for amusement at the end of a long, hard day's work; not a game, like our most popular one today, which is so hard to understand and execute, that it taxes our brain power to the limit and bars any conversation to help ease the tension. Although the children could play cards as well as the older ones, they preferred the more active games of checkers and back-gammon; especially the latter one, because it was more exciting to rattle the dice (and I mean rattle) than to toss out a card now and then. 

But, as I mentioned before, it was such a treat to have new faces in the home, that they usually preferred to just munch apples and listen to the excited remarks, as someone slapped down a coveted ten spot of trumps to help make game. The other three points were high, low and Jack of trumps.

The grown-ups sat up until late, because the next day was to them what the Bible commanded, a day of rest except for the necessary chores for the welfare of their livestock. Although none of the families attended church services, three miles away, they were respectful, God-fearing people; and religiously took care that no hay was mowed on Saturday nor anything else started, which would require extra work on Sunday. No games of any kind were allowed, no places of amusement tempted them; and they retired at the close of such a quiet day with a deeper feeling 173

of peace and contentment, than do our present day migrants, a majority of whom educate their children to feel that Sunday is just an extra day for sports, dancing, and running everything wide open. This laxity of Sunday observance usually drains the pockets of those who have worked so hard to save a week's wages.

Gilman and Eliza got up about five o'clock, so that he could get the cows off to pasture after Eliza had done the milking job. Then she would have half a day's work done, before the Cole faction appeared. 

Ella never seemed to realize how lucky she was to have the care of two children, and the heavy work of the housewife taken out of her hands by the smart mother, who did all of the cooking, knitting, mending, churning; she also took full charge of the milk and butter operations. When Eliza finally got tired of the unequal division of labor, she took over the ell of the house and kitchen, leaving Ella in the main house. This had its drawback, in depriving Eliza of her Saturday fun of tracking up Ella's clean floor; but it was a bonanza for the children. They would usually eat six meals a day; for Liza, whose chief aim in life seemed to be eating the richest food that she could cook, would turn them loose at her food cupboard, in case that she had eaten earlier than they had. Soon the neighbors began to drop in to see the Denmark folks. No matter how much each neighbor privately maligned the other, the same brotherly love existed in their hearts as the Bible commanded; so a guest became a responsibility for others in the neighborhood. 

Dinner was always of baked beans, pies, puddings, pickles, preserves and all the fixings. After eating, the leaves were taken out of the table to restore it to its original size; and everyone but the women, sprawled out on couches or slept in their chairs to get ready for the next big event-consuming four quarts of rich ice cream, later. Needless to state that Jane Cole was a very shrewd hard-working woman, else she would never have wrested a living for herself and six children from an old tavern and poorly worked farm. It was a very irksome task, and one not conducive to the best moral interests of her children having this tavern filled with uncouth, profane, tipsy teamsters who, as stated before, took advantage of these few occasions to break away from their wives' apron strings and indulge in riotous living. Among these ox drivers was a typical backwoodsman, Uriah Hitchcock, a giant of a man with enough good qualities to earn him the consideration of being called “a diamond in the rough.”

Although he was the typical profane, freely imbibing teamster, he was a hard-working man and very proud of the fact that he owned half of Watson's

Hill, a small mountain three miles above Freedom Village on the road to North Conway. Here he tilled the soil in summer and logged in the winter, working practically "around the clock." His one absorbing 186

interest was in raising and selling the best strain of White Head steers and oxen in that part of the country. As his uncouth ways and profane language concerned not at all his prestige with the cattle with whom he lived alone on this bleak hillside, he scorned every new-fangled notion and never conformed to any new ways of life or speech, at home or abroad. 

He was proud to show his defiance of those slaves of fashion who laid down their knives and took up forks at popular demand, by scooping up all the food that his knife would hold, and cramming it into his mouth. In spite of his oft-repeated reminder that fingers were made before forks, and his habitual use of them except when a fork was used to spear a piece of hard food while being cut, the neglect of Eliza to provide the old-fashioned three-tined fork was always met with a long string of oaths. If any new guests were present and seemed oblivious of the objectionable knife, he would let it clatter to the floor to attest its presence. As he had no mustache, he had the untrammeled joy of drinking his tea from his saucer, after noisily blowing it until it was cool enough to drink.

Uriah was the opposite type in every respect from the one that Jane would have married from choice; but her choice seemed to be that of expediency which was to prevent becoming a burden to Jim and Ella. 

So she solved that problem by selling the old homestead to Jimmy, and left all of her life-long friends. and relatives to marry Uriah and do life sentence on this isolated mountain home in Freedom. As so often 187

They absolutely refused to call him anything but by that name. When they knew him there was only one other family on the hill, his son Will, wife, and three children. Will was a chip off the old block, by nature and intent, reverting to the same old customs and language when in his father's presence. But the refining influence of his modern wife and Jimmy's dancing classes each week, added to the constant proddings by his children not to shame them in public, kept him in a middle course of culture. 

Jane and Uriah made two regular trips to Limington each year-one in the early spring before planting; and the other at the time of Cornish Fair in August. Here he exhibited his cattle as a preliminary to later sales and he usually got first prizes in some of the pulling contests. He could easily do this because he scorned anything so modern as a horse for doing the farm work, with the result that his oxen got continual exercise in pulling and straining at heavy loads; while his horse got fat and lazy, from lack of exercise. On the spring trip, they usually drove into the dooryard about five o'clock, to be ready for his customary five-thirty supper. Though the Cole family usually dined at six, they took special pains to have supper on the table at the appointed time, to forestall the lion's roar at any delay. 188

the scenery, first hand, rather than go flying by as the speed demons of today do and then spend money on some second rate picture to get a view of a lake, or of a majestic mountain with a sparkling river at its base. As Whittier eloquently expresses this thought- "Touched by a light that hath no name, A glory never sung, Aloft on sky and mountain wall 

Are God's great pictures hung. 

But beauty seen is never lost, God's colors all are fast; 

The glory of this sunset heaven 

Into my soul has passed."

Aside from responding to the glories of these pictures created by nature's paint brush, the occupants of the slow-moving wagons were freed from the hectic traffic problems of today, by the common sense of good old Dobbin; and they could enjoy the fun which could be created among themselves on this leisurely drive.

Though Blanche traveled over this road, during the long years of her life, this trip always stood out in memory, as the high spot, when she and Ned munched crackers, cheese and apples until they arrived at Kezar Falls. At this store that showed the first signs of having bought fresh chocolates in the dim past, Ned stopped the caravan, presumably to get some of those fragrant cinnamon cigars which

Blanche loved to smell; but with the real purpose of fostering the budding romance between himself and 193

Blanche, by buying a large box of chocolates to dissipate the lingering taste of the crackers and cheese.

The latter was strong enough to have walked along with Jimmy and the cow, as he loved his cheese strong and mouldy. The romancers ate candy, talked and laughed all the way to Cornish, accompanied by the squawking of the hens, the grunting of the pig and Jimmy's whistling which became less and less frequent as the miles seemed to lengthen with him walking beside the cow. The sequel to this romance, which grew in intensity in proportion to Ella's efforts to nip it in the bud, so to speak, will be given in a future chapter, if the second book materializes. Sufficient to state here, that where Jimmy started this favorite son of Cornish on his musical career, at the age of eighteen, he loved him as much as if he were his own son.

Ella viewed with alarm this budding attachment between the two young people; but though she nearly lost her robust health trying to go on every trip to keep Cupid at bay, he used a double supply of arrows when not watched by her eagle eyes. He had a strong ally in Jimmy, who, though not daring to openly promote the cause, would obligingly sleep on the back seat for the thirty-mile drives; while Ned drove the sympathetic old horse, whose instinct seemed to tell him how acceptable slow progress would be to the new driver. Usually an extra cigar or box of candy would be thrown in by the Newfield storekeeper. He, like all people attending the dances, resented Ella's tactics to keep the young folks apart; 194

for Ned and Blanche were their favorite young people, and she the most eagerly sought partner because she was the dancing-master's daughter and because she had inherited from him, her grace in dancing. 

Finally, due to the insistence of Ella; and to the fact that Ned and Blanche formed the growing habit of climbing down from the platform and becoming lost in the maze of waltzes, during the evenings, Jim did drop her from the orchestra. This proved to be a good break for Cupid; for Ned could always find a substitute whenever he would observe Ella offguard and indicate by a flick of his hand to Blanche, that he was claiming her for the next dance. It took some doing to brave the heat-lightnings of Ella's face, as courtesy demanded that he smile at her when waltzing by. The temperature was lowered so much here that he usually had a premature fit of sneezing, when approaching this chilly corner. Both Jim and the crowd inwardly chuckled at the series of charge and counter-charge between the opposing factions, which they gradually found to be more entertaining than the dance program. The fact that "All the world loves a lover" lined everybody up, silently or openly, on Cupid's side; and as Ella's keen eyes noted that public opinion held her in contempt, she shifted over to neutral to save the few remaining friends which she still held in the assembly.

While Blanche continued to zig-zag across the border with Jimmy on his business trips, which took him all over Freedom, Ossipee and to regular dancing schools as far away as Wolfeboro Junction, she got 195

special insight into the tricks and trades of the typical Yankee horse trader. This inherited love for dickering followed through her own life, leading her to ferret out ways to acquire four cottages at a nearby beach and to embark on other ventures, which would have made a more timid soul quake with fear. Since he traded mostly for the fun of it, and would take anything in exchange, once in a while his luck would turn and leave him aghast at some newly acquired possession. Such was the case in two special trades, which I am sure will amuse my readers. 

In later years, Jimmy made a deal with Bert Patch of Newfield, to take an old horse off his hands, if he could persuade a native to swap this old horse out to pasture, for an old Stanley Steamer owned by 

Blanche's husband. The day that Jimmy rode over in the Steamer and signed the agreement, the man died in his chair, after eating dinner. Hunt as they would, they could never find the horse in the large pasture; but weeks later someone found his dead body at the foot of a high ledge. It became almost a Supreme 

Court case to settle who owned the automobile; and who, the dead horse. Trading for a dead horse was not the original bargain, though Jimmy knew that it was just about an even trade, at that. 

The other outstanding joker was a trade for the patent of a new kind of ice cream freezer, with the freezer, of course, thrown in for demonstration purposes. This consisted of a large hollow aluminum utensil, which rotated through a long pan of ice cream mixture. As the round dish was filled with ice, a cer- 196

tain portion of the mixture was supposed to freeze on to the outside of it and then be scraped off by a metal slide. Enough of these scrapings made a dish of so-called ice cream; but the same flat taste could have been attained by setting the dish of mixture on ice to let it freeze.

The family, together with a popular bachelor cousin from Boston, Sidney Marr, could hardly wait for Jimmy to bring home and demonstrate the new freezer. For with the arduous task of turning the crank of the only kind of freezer then known, eliminated, they could vision themselves feasting on a dish of ice cream, every time that they dropped into a chair to rest. But try as he would, Jimmy could not get it to make ice cream such as the slick salesman had so deftly ground out, at one quick turn of the crank. Of course, every one of the seven people present knew exactly how it should work; and together with their wrangling as to who should try it next, and their exasperating remarks to Jimmy for being duped by the salesman, a perfect bedlam reigned. He finally got mad and was going to carry it off, when in turning the disk up to the proper point, he churned off a nice sample of frozen cream. To people who were used to dipping into three quarts of delicious cream, these little slivers of frozen stuff looked pretty silly. They packed the freezer back into its box, possibly to be traded later for another dead horse or something as bad; and everybody went to bed, mad with everyone else in the household. Sidney visited the family every summer until he died in 1940; and 197

never would they get to reminiscing, but what he would ask if they remembered the fight over the new freezer. Then he would lean back in his chair, slap his knees and roar with his deep-throated laughter.

It is not due to oversight that I have omitted any mention of the charming village of Freedom, itself, other than to skirt its borders with Jimmy's fabulous trading ventures. Rather, I chose all scenes of action with the mere mention of "Up Freedom Way", because I wished to present to the public the beautiful panorama which unfolded itself in Blanche's childhood, on the drive from Cornish to the summit of Watson's Hill. I, also, wanted to take time to describe the many unusual things of interest there as Jane had been forced to leave everything in the primitive state which Uriah had enjoyed. 

The beauties of the scenery changed with each season of the year-from the flowers of spring, with sound of bob-o-link and sparrow coming from the treetops; through the hot, hazy days of summer when a hay-cart creaks slowly down the road, with its driver fast asleep on top of the load; through the scarlet, crimson and gold of the forest brought out by the flaming glow of sunset; followed by the special beauties of winter with pure, white snow gleaming on the bushes and trees. 

The return visit to Freedom was made in the spring while nature was teeming with beauty and new life on every side. At the last minute, Gus decided not to go, as tomorrow's baseball game had more pulling power; so Lucy was asked to go along because Jim 198

had already hitched the horse into the new two-seated wagon. How often things happen, unimportant at the time; but which prove of highest value, later!

As in this case, I could never have described this trip so well if I had not gotten such a vivid description of it, through Lucy's appreciative eyes. I might have overlooked many important things, for as a small boy once said, "Memory is what we forget with." We will assume that she is telling about the trip. First among the attractions were the new harness, long tasseled whip and plush robe, with a spotted leopard or tiger of tawny hue against a rich brown and green background. Of course, these beauties were as nothing, compared to the trappings in which the winsome females were decked out; but they made a nice supplement.

Cornish village, past the cemetery (which was always the chief point of interest in a village), we had to travel over a four-mile stretch of shady woods to Kezar Falls. This lacked the charm of the gushing waterfall on the Denmark road and was merely an enormous half barrel, or hogshead, set in the rich brown earth and filled with quietly running water. Here at Hunt's Bridge, which was an old-fashioned covered bridge, we were confronted by Green Mountain, the first high mountain in the foothills of the Ossipee range. The two-mile stretch of road to Freedom was just a series of short hills, the only item of interest being two large farms owned by the Towle brothers. Freedom village was a very attractive little village, nestling among many high hills; and I mean nestling, for the encircling hills discouraged any idea of attempted flight, as so many sprawling villages seemed to be considering doing. Four streets converged to a large stone watering fountain in the center of the village. Here were two stores, a postoffice and town hall, where the dances and other public entertainments were held. The only level road, on the left, led to Effingham Falls whence the gracious Ossipee River had come to greet us as we entered its fair state. 201

While we were waiting for the horse to drink, nearly everyone in the village hastened over to talk with us.

They said that I hadn't seen anything yet! Jimmy walked up to ease the burden of the poor horse; while the three of us had to lean way forward to prevent sliding back into the road. For by the steady backward pull, it would seem that the force of gravity had stopped functioning there at the foot of the hill.

The hill ended as abruptly as it had begun. While we waited for Jimmy to catch up with us and wipe the perspiration from his throbbing forehead, I could only exclaim with admiration and feast my eyes on the view of the long Ossipee range of mountains, with misty blue Chocorua towering above the other peaks.

Then we came to Cash's Corner, a corner just about big enough to hold Cash and a small family. GRANDMA CLOSES HER MEMORY ALBUM 

"The book is completed 

And closed, like the day; 

And the hand that has written it 

Lays it away." 

Yes, a weary but satisfied Grandma dusts off her crutches, totters across the floor and sinks back into her rocking chair to resume her knitting. Spurred on by the assurance of some philosopher that, "It is better to try something and fail, than to do nothing and succeed," the resultant book is more of a surprise to herself than to the friends who were sure that she could do it if she would only try. Now she has discovered that her greatest worry about finding memories enough to make a book was groundless. 

Where she planned to cover Blanche's whole life in one book, she finds that with her not yet enrolled in high school, she could easily fill another big book about her later life as homemaker, teacher, active church leader and just plain Grandma. If this book meets with sufficient approval, she will write the sequel which should indicate that she has acquired the necessary 1% inspiration to give her the coveted title of genius.

I, as that weary Grandma, have penalized myself by reading this manuscript over and over, hoping to find some new way to improve it. I have, therefore, made many necessary changes until now it is with a clear conscience that I can write FINIS, after a few more pertinent thoughts. Aside from the hope that I 212

might help the young people of today to better understand old traditions and customs, and thus show more reverence for older people, my chief aim was an attempt to make many lives happier with my natural capacity for fun and logic. I quote: "If I can let into some soul, a little light, If some pathway dark and drear, I can render bright, If I to someone in gloom, can show the sunny side, Though no reward I win 

I shall be satisfied."

And I am satisfied, after so much effort in that direction, that many lives will be brightened in more or less degree.

The verse at the beginning of Grandma's collapse was from Longfellow's "Curfew," a poem which he placed at the end of his book of poetry; and the words seemed applicable here as this book is laid away with the same feeling of finality.

As this weary Grandma rocks slowly back and forth, watching the sun fade from sight and the evening star appear, she is reminded of another of his twilight reveries- "The day is done, and the darkness 

Falls from the wings of night, As a feather is wafted downward 

From an eagle in his flight."

Likewise at the close of this day and this book she slowly draws the curtain over these past lingering 213

memories, and further encouraged by Longfellow that- Something attempted, something done, Has earned a night's repose, she bids her patient readers a fond goodnight and wishes them many years of happiness as she hobbles off, on her crutches, to seek that welcome repose.
