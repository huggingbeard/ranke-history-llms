THE STAGE COACH PERIOD IN NEW ENGLAND BY JOHN G. WOOD 

In the early times transportation of men and goods was naturally by the ocean and by the rivers flowing into it. The conduct of the Revolutionary War, the selection of the site of the Capitol at Washington, its completion, and the occupancy of the White House by John Adams in 1800 were factors in bringing to the consciousness of the nation the inadequacy of transportation facilities. This realization served to stimulate effort toward the appropriation of moneys for the construction of lines of communication, the better to serve the population and to protect our frontiers from attack. How inadequate had been means of transportation will be realized if we recall that our New Hampshire soldiers had to drag cannon, ammunition, and supplies through the wilderness of Vermont preparatory to fighting the battle of Bennington. Their success after such arduous labors cannot fail to excite unbounded admiration.

It was in 1806 that the construction of the Cumberland or Old National Road, at Government expense, came up for consideration in Congress. The project was bitterly opposed in debate. Not till 1810 was any provision made. But beginning with this year a series of appropriations followed amounting in all to $5,794,919.33. 

This money was expended as rapidly as practicable. By 1820 the road had reached from the Potomac to Wheeling, and early in the thirties, to the Mississippi River.

More and better roads were to come with the advent of the stage lines. The stage coach period in our eastern country may be set between 1790 and 1840, the year that marks the coming of the steam cars to Exeter and to Newburyport. Thereafter the rapid extension of our railroad lines pushed the stage coach back to the frontier, and finally relegated it to the western part of the country, where it survived for many years.

Travel by land to the distant seaboard cities of the South was difficult and infrequent owing to the condition of the roads and the discomfort of making a long journey. In the last years of the eighteenth century lines were extended and connections made so completely that finally in 1802 a Post Road was published, running from Portsmouth, New Hampshire, to Savannah, Georgia, one thousand three hundred and forty miles in length. The route lay through Boston, Worcester, Springfield, Hartford, New Haven, New York, Newark, Princeton, Trenton, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Alexandria, Fredericksburg, Richmond, Columbia and Augusta. 

James Truslow Adams, in his "March of Democracy," says the stages took four days from Boston to New York, a day and a half to Philadelphia, fifteen from Philadelphia to Charleston, and two more to Savannah, or twenty-two and one-half in all. 

This route is given as twelve hundred miles from Boston and was traversed at a rate of fifty-three miles per day average, the hard journey costing $70.00 for transportation and about $25.00 for board and lodging on the way. Before this, John Wentworth, the last Royal Governor of New Hampshire, made a report of an interesting and extensive trip taken by him on his return to New Hampshire from England, after having been appointed Governor of the province and Surveyor General of His Majesty's woods in America. In preparation for his return home, he had purchased in England a number of fine horses and had engaged a retinue of servants. In order to investigate his Majesty's woods in the South, Wentworth decided to return home by way of Charleston, South 

Carolina. He reached that city on the 22nd day of March, 1767, after a sixty-day sea voyage, and a few days later started out with eager interest to examine the timber in various parts of the provinces. He made an interesting record of the forests of the South, including yellow pines, live oaks, and white oaks. His northward journey, owing to his position and the many introductions he carried, was pleasantly interrupted at the homes of the colonial aristocracy. He visited William Byrd at Westover, Va., whose seat surpassed all others on the James River. William Byrd was a delightful host and a man after Wentworth's own heart, being fond of horses and somewhat too fond of cards. Another stopping

Crossing the Potomac, the Governor came next to Annapolis, the capital of provincial Maryland, where he lodged with Governor Sharpe. Thence the road led him to Philadelphia, to Trenton Falls, and on to New York. Near

He reached New York in the month of June. From there three days by water brought him to Boston. Here he renewed old acquaintances, and then started on the last lap of his long journey from London to Portsmouth.

A record of his stable in Portsmouth is of interest as showing the style in which a Royal Governor of wealth and position was accustomed to travel. The stable contained sixteen blooded horses and numerous vehicles ranging from the stately coach to a little one-horse sulky for one person, a type which Wentworth had ordered made to his specifications on his journey through Philadelphia. He gave instructions that it should be painted a light straw color, with gilt mouldings, and should show his crest and cipher enclosed in a plain oval. The sulky was built on steel springs,* something new in vehicle construction, and was withal a very smart and sporty rig.

In the last two decades of the eighteenth century there was a great improvement in roads and a marked increase in the number of stage lines, and this increase continued for more than two decades into the nineteenth century. Wanzey in 1794 remarked: "Eight years ago the road from Boston to New Haven a distance of 170 miles could scarcely maintain two stages and twelve horses; now it maintains twenty stages weekly, with upwards of one hundred horses."

Another through route was established from Boston through Salem, Ipswich, Newbury, and Hampton, joining the other road at Greenland, and thence to Portsmouth and Dover. Over this road Bartholomew Stavers of Portsmouth inaugurated a stage line to Boston equipped with a small curricle drawn by two horses and carrying three passengers from his brother's tavern, "The 

Earl of Halifax," Portsmouth to Boston for 13s. 6d., including fare "at good inns on the way, where good entertainment and attendance are provided for passengers in the coach." His advertisement further states the stage will leave on Mondays of each week, stopping at Ipswich for the night and arriving at Charlestown Ferry on Tuesdays, returning on Thursday morning and arriving at Portsmouth Friday evening. Finally, it assures the public that "any gentleman may have business transacted in Newbury or Boston with fidelity and dispatch at reasonable cost."

Bartholomew Stavers was a pioneer stage driver in New Hampshire. He is described as a very energetic man rather below medium height, with a florid countenance. He was of Tory inclinations, and, having a strong love for his native land, looked upon the movements of the discontented colonists with growing disfavor till in 1774 he departed from this country, leaving his wife and unborn son behind. He never returned. After his departure for 

Europe his brother John, the respectable tavernkeeper of the "Earl of Halifax," continued the activity. How popular the line became may be gathered from an item in the Portsmouth Journal of that time. On May 15, 1831, as we find there stated, no fewer than forty passengers started from the Portsmouth hotel for Boston. 

To accommodate so many every kind of equipment must have been pressed into service.

While Benjamin Stavers was continuing his course of progress and development there appeared about 1770 a rival in the person of Benjamin Hart, who began running an opposition stage. The rivalry continued till 1772 and then gave place to a combination of the two proprietors, who announced on July 31 of that year that the stages of Stavers and Hart still continue to ply between

In the December following a third stage was set up by Theodore Davis, who had served as an apprentice to Stavers and Hart. Three stages, then, between the two cities probably resulted in better carriages, better horses, and possibly in a reduction of fare. These stages ran by way of Newbury, Ipswich, and Salem over the Post Road. As time went on roads were improved, traffic became heavier, and well established lines turned out to be profitable.

In 1818 the Eastern Stage Company* was chartered in New Hampshire with a capital of 425 shares at $100 each. It had a large business; profits were enormous, and stockholders gloated. over their incomes. The company was said at one time to have owned seven hundred horses all of good quality. Such prosperity, however, did not continue indefinitely. In 1828 the assets of this company were sold and, though the business was continued by others, the Eastern Stage Company, as such, went out of business with a heavy loss to its shareholders. It was on a coach of this line that Henry Clay rode from Pleasant Street, Salem, to the Tremont House in Boston in exactly one hour; and it was over this same route that Daniel Webster in 1842 was carried at the rate of sixteen miles per hour from Boston to Portland to sign the 

Ashburton Treaty.

In 1827 there was published in Boston every two months, as a supplement to the American Traveller, Badger and Porter's Stage Register. It contained a list of the principal lines of stages, steamboats, and canal packets in the New England states and in the state of New York, together with hours of departure and arrival, towns through which they passed, proprietors' names, fare, distances, etc. This booklet, indispensable to the traveller, was not sold separately, but was obtainable only through subscription to the paper.

The issue of March 6, 1827, furnishes details on two hundred and two stage lines, most of them starting from, or connecting with, Boston. This list shows the extent to which stage transportation had become a necessity to the public. Comparison of it with the earlier list, that of 1802, clearly reveals the increase in traffic that had been going on. Whereas in 1802 trips once or twice a week were deemed sufficient for traffic needs, in 1827 trips were in many cases made daily. It is from these two lists that we gather details of the lines most interesting to Exeter.

The line listed as Boston, Newburyport, Exeter, and Dover, New Hampshire, left Boston at 7.30 every morning except Sunday. 

It left Dover the following morning at 7.00 and arrived at Portland at 5.00 P. M. Returning the stage left Portland the next morning and arrived at Dover the same evening, where the night was

spent. The next day the stage left Dover at 7.00 A. M., arrived at Exeter at 10.00, Newburyport at 12.00, and Boston at 6.00 P. M. 

The fare to Exeter was $2.25, to Dover $3.25, to Portland $6.00. 

It was over this route that my grandmother, Nancy Gilman

Folsom, in 1822 travelled to Boston by stage. She wrote a very interesting story of her journey and of her visit to Cambridge to attend the Harvard Commencement exercises of her brother, George Folsom. She writes to her sisters in Exeter, September the first, 1822: "We rode from Exeter in company with Mr. Woodward, Mr. B. Lovering, Mr. Gorham and wife, and one stranger; stopped but a few minutes in Newburyport; dined at Mr. Wildes' hotel in Topsfield, a delightful situation, with a view of the country around. We left the stage but a few minutes in Salem, passed through Charlestown just as the sun was setting. I thought at first we were in Boston, but soon perceived my mistake, as on crossing the bridge, the city burst upon my sight at once. I never saw anything that equalled its grandeur or sublimity. This letter was carried by Mr. 

Hildreth of Exeter- a custom frequent in those days, as it was a means of saving ten cents in postage. 

The line from Boston to Portsmouth via Newburyport, sometimes called the lower road, made similar schedule with a connecting stage from Hampton Falls to Exeter. Thus a century ago 

Exeter ladies could go shopping in Boston seventeen times a week.

There were many other lines of interest listed; but we have not time to follow them here. Names such as Burlington, Vt., Albany,

Saratoga, Montreal, Quebec, Lake Champlain, Philadelphia, New

York, Buffalo, and many other cities and towns appear in the routing. Suffice it to say that for approximately five cents per mile one could in 1827 reach by stage coach all the important places in the country.

Travelling over these stage lines, while generally safe, was not always comfortable. A letter from Charles Sumner of Boston, dated February 19, 1834, describes his trip by stage and water to 

New York City. He started from Boston at 3.30 on a Monday morning in company with twelve other passengers and their full complement of baggage. The way was dark, so dark that, though he rode beside the driver, it was some time before he discovered that the coach was drawn by six horses. He breakfasted at Natick, sixteen miles out. From there some of the party rode for thirty miles in a crazy wagon, Sumner among them. After that he rode sixteen miles alone in a gig, driving the horse himself. He arrived at Thompson, the first town in Connecticut, about three o'clock in the afternoon, sixty miles from Boston. "Here," he says, "we dined and again started on our way with forty miles of heavy travelling before us; changed horses every sixteen miles, and arrived at Hartford, Conn., at three o'clock Tuesday morning, having been on the road twenty-three hours." 

The cold was numbing during the night, so much so that even the experienced driver complained. We may well believe, therefore, that no sound could have been more grateful to Sumner's ears than that made by the heavy tramp of the horses over the sounding covered bridge which led into Hartford. After a short nap at the hotel he enjoyed a good breakfast. At eleven o'clock in the morning he started from Hartford for New Haven a route of forty miles where he arrived at eight o'clock in the evening after a very pleasant journey, so far as a good driver and good weather could make it so. The following morning he attended prayers at Yale, and finding himself pressed for time ran till his wind was almost gone to catch the boat leaving at seven o'clock for New York. The remainder of the trip by boat was exceedingly pleasant. A postscript to this letter adds: "Travelling is very expensive. Thus far, thirty per cent above my calculation. This is owing to the delays, bad roads, and the season of the year."

It was not the custom for stages to run on Sundays. Routes were laid out to avoid this as far as possible, and travellers were expected to stop at some tavern over the day and to proceed on the following Monday. When traffic was not heavy enough to support a daily stage it was customary to proceed on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, the return trip being made on Tuesdays,

Thursdays and Saturdays. An interesting exception to this generalization is the Haverhill and Lancaster, New Hampshire, mail stage, Samuel Rose, proprietor, which departed from Haverhill 

Sunday and Wednesday mornings and returned on Tuesdays and Saturdays.

A century ago there were three public houses at Hampton Beach, supported by patronage drawn partly from the immediate locality, partly from outlying territory as, for example, Exeter. At this time Exeter, we find, was referred to as a summer resort, probably because of its nearness to Hampton Beach, whose patrons were increasing in number. Exeter and Hampton Beach were connected by stage coach. Advertisements show there were two rival lines, one maintained by John Moulton, Jr., the other by Robert P. 

Kimball. Kimball's stage left Mr. Gilman's hotel on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday at half past one in the afternoon; Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday it left at nine o'clock in the morning.

Returning the stage left Hampton Beach at half past five and arrived in Exeter at half past six in the evening, a journey of ten miles in an even hour. At Exeter this stage connected with the 

Concord, Dover, Portsmouth, Newburyport, Salem, White Mountains, and Poplin stages, and also with the Winnepesaukee Steam Boat Line. These connections reveal how widely distributed over 

New England travel by stage was in 1833.

A moment's reflection will suggest that the expansion and development of stage transportation must have called for help from many collateral lines of industry. For example, coach manufacture had to be developed; and this industry, in turn, required proper selection and preparation of lumber and skillful designing. 

Again, tanning, forging, iron-making were other subsidiary industries stirred to increased activity. Still other requirements followed, as the building of taverns for the entertainment of travellers, and provision for ample stores of grain and hay necessary to the feeding of many horses. Of the seven hundred horses once owned by the Eastern Stage Coach Company, one hundred and twentyfive were stabled at Hampton Falls, a station for changing horses. 

How much it must have meant to the farmers in that locality to be called on to furnish feed for so large a number throughout the year. This demand upon the farmers would account for the many large farm barns of that day. Again, the proper shoeing of these horses required much skill. Among the expert shoers of that day one reads the names of Richard and Alfred Marsh, brothers, of Hampton Falls. It is said to have been a rare instance that any horse shod by them ever went lame.

For detailed information regarding stage lines, patrons in New England turned to the Farmer's Almanac, published by Robert B. Thomas, the first number of which appeared in 1792. In addition to furnishing valuable information on all sorts of subjects, then as now, the Almanac of that day annually published a list of stage lines and thereby made itself well nigh indispensable to

New Englanders. The road list as published in the Almanac of 

1802 gives details of the roads leading from Boston, together with names of those who kept houses of entertainment, and the distances from place to place. There are twenty-five routes listed, the longer ones including Portsmouth to Savannah, a distance of one thousand three hundred and forty miles; from Philadelphia to Washington, one hundred ninety-three miles; from Boston to 

Albany and Quebec, five hundred sixty-eight miles. Here is ample evidence to show how extensively travel by stage had developed. 

The importance of the Almanac in these old times was recognized by Prof. George Lyman Kittredge, who deemed it worthy of extensive study, and in 1904 published the results in a most interesting and valuable book entitled: "The Old Farmer and His Almanac."

It is to the Old Farmer's Almanac that one has to turn for a description of the stage coach. Thomas Lovering journeyed from 

Philadelphia to Baltimore in 1795. Regarding the conveyance he says: "The vehicle was a long car with four benches. Three of these in the interior held nine passengers, and a tenth was seated beside the driver on the front bench. A light roof was supported by eight slender pillars, four on each side. Three large leather curtains suspended from the roof, one on each side and the third behind, were rolled up and lowered at the pleasure of the passengers. There was no space for luggage, each passenger being expected to stow his things as he could under his seat or legs. The entrance was in front over the driver's bench. There were no backs to the benches, which were most uncomfortable."

In 1806 John Mellish, describing his trip from Boston to New York, adds to our knowledge of the type of public conveyance then in use. "The vehicle," he writes, "is hung on leather traces running the length of the body, which has space for the nine passengers with one on each side of the driver, eleven in all." He adds that the conveyance is easy, and in summer very agreeable; but thinks it must be excessively cold in winter. 

Abdy in 1833 tells of his journey from Northampton, Mass., to 

Boston a distance of ninety-four miles. He left Northampton at 3.00 A. M. and arrived in Boston about eight in the evening;

The vehicle was on leathern springs; the boot and hind part being appropriated to the baggage, while the box was occupied by two passengers in addition to the conductor, and as many on the roof. 

Some of the trunks and packages were carried on the roof, which was surrounded by an iron rail. Inside were places for nine people -three on the rear seat, three on the center seat, and three on the front seat. This description tallies well with that of the "Concord Coach," so-called, which became the standard coach of the country for years, and even now is used in the western part of the United States. This type of coach took its name from our Concord, New Hampshire, where it was developed, built and sold for many years by the Abbot-Downing Company. 

The New England Gazetteer of 1839 records that Levi Pease, father of the mail stages in this country, was a native of Shrewsbury, Mass. He died there in 1824 at the age of eighty-six. Mr. 

Pease was a man of great enterprise. To him more than to any other man the public was indebted for its excellent mail establishment. At the time Mr. Pease started his first line of stages between 

Boston and New York in 1784, the mail between those places passed only once a fortnight on horseback, carried in a pair of saddle bags. 

Benjamin Pierce Cheney, an owner of vast railroad properties, founder of the rich Cheney Express Company, and one of the builders of the Northern Pacific Railroad and other great western lines, began his business life as a strong boy of seventeen, driving the coach from Exeter to Nashua. For six years he drove fifty miles every day. Then he became stage agent, agent in the Lowell and National Railroad, and from there advanced to become a large railroad owner. Chester W. Chapin, afterwards president of the Boston and Albany Railroad, ran a stage line between Springfield and Hartford. It was said by an old coachman of the Eastern 

Stage Company that all its men were given positions on the Eastern Railroad if so desired. "All who wished had something to do." 

Certainly much consideration was shown the old employees of the stage roads.

In addition to carrying passengers, luggage, etc., an important function of the stage coach was carrying the mails. In earliest colonial days letters were carried along the coast or to the few inland towns by chance travellers, or by butchers, who made frequent trips to buy and sell cattle. John Winthrop of New London sent letters by these butchers. In 1672 "Indian Posts" carried the Albany winter mail. 

Andrew Hamilton was made Deputy Postmaster General April 4, 1692. He established a regular post office in Boston the following 

May. He established the first post office in New Hampshire at 

Piscataqua and laid out a route for post riders running from Piscataqua through to New York and Philadelphia and then on to Richmond, Va.

The first regular mounted post from New York to Boston started 

January 1, 1673, with "port mantles,” which were crammed with letters, small portable goods and divers bags. It was required that the rider must be active, indefatigable and honest. He changed horses at Hartford. He was ordered to keep an eye out for the best roads, the best way through forests, for ferries, fords, etc., to watch keenly for all fugitive servants and deserters, and to be kind to all persons travelling in his company. During the month that he was gone the mail was collected in a box in the office of the Colonial Secretary. Journeys were often performed at the risk of life and limb, and the exposure to weather and the easily imagined hardships, with the necessity for speed, resulted in many thrilling experiences for the riders. Government fixed their compensation at three pence per mile, while inn holders were warned not to charge exorbitant prices for their fare and ferrymen were ordered not to delay them on their passage and to carry them free of charge. The Post rider was authorized to press into his service any horse or horses he might need in case of emergency. A fine for delay by the ferryman was imposed and the owners of horses pressed into service received six pence a mile for their use. 

This method of carrying the mails continued for many years. 

In 1753 Benjamin Franklin, by appointment of the crown, attempted to systematize mail routes. In 1775 he was appointed 

Post Master General, and his efforts became successful,

The Federal Government early saw that mail transportation was one of its necessary functions and accepted the responsibility, naturally turning to the stage coach as the means already provided. 

No doubt the additional revenue was gladly welcomed by the stage lines. In time the "mail coach" on many of the more profitable routes became the "Pullman" service, with better coaches, faster time and extra fare. An example of this is seen on the Eastern 

Stage Line. The mail stage ran express, carrying passengers only from places where horses were changed. The fare from Boston to Portsmouth was four dollars and the time eight hours, while by the accommodation the fare was three dollars and the time twelve hours.

Rates of postage always presented to the letter-writer a complicated problem. The charge was based on two factors, the weight of the letter and the distance it was to go. The basic charge was for a single letter of half an ounce; double letters at double rate. 

In 1798 the charge for carriage of thirty miles was six cents, 150 miles 122 cents, and 450 miles 25 cents; other distances in proportion. In 1800 the rates for short distances were increased, and in 1816 they were again sharply advanced, with the result that in that year the charges were from fifty to one hundred per cent above those prevailing in 1798. It was not the custom at this time to use envelopes for inclosing letters. Instead, the letter was folded over and fastened with sealing wax. Neither postmarks nor stamps were then in use, but the amount paid was written on the address side by the postmasters. Letters, however, could be sent prepaid or collect.

It was these high costs that led to the custom of sending letters by a travelling friend, and to the practice of employing stage drivers as messengers for receiving packages and letters at destination and undertaking commissions of purchase, inquiry, and the like. One stage driver, whose route was between center New 

Hampshire and Boston, states he always satisfied the ladies on bonnet purchases for no two were ever alike. 

The practice of carrying mail by the drivers was stopped as far as possible by the Government agencies. The other activities of the driver were permitted as largely his perquisite. Yet the prac-

tice was at times annoying to the company as is shown by the following quotation from a notice of the Eastern Stage Coach 

Company to the public under date of May 14, 1832, at Newburyport. "We give notice that all bank notes delivered to any driver of Company's stage coaches, or other person in the service of the Company, to be carried from place to place, will be carried for the sole remuneration and for the sole responsibility of such driver or other person, and the Company will not derive any benefit or be liable for any loss on account of the same."

While directing attention to stage routes we must not overlook the importance of bridges as being necessary parts of the highway. 

No through route would be worthy of the name unless comfort and speed were gained by the elimination of by-passes, fords, and ferries. Bridges were the means of accomplishing this desired end. 

During the latter part of the eighteenth century, and again in the beginning of the nineteenth, we note activity in the building of bridges. In some instances the undertakings were carried through by private capital; such usually were the more important. In other instances they were carried through by town and county. 

The bridging of the Merrimack River was important as linking Massachusetts and New Hampshire and connecting highways both at Newburyport and at Haverhill. In the construction of bridges many interesting engineering problems were worked out. 

We shall confine our attention to Newburyport.

The early crossing of the Merrimack was by Carr's Ferry to Carr's Island, a short distance up the river from Newburyport, and thence by bridge to the north shore of the Merrimack in Salisbury. This crossing was in use for many years until superseded by the Essex-Merrimack bridge. It was on November 26, 1792, that the new bridge was opened to the public. As a matter of fact it consisted of two bridges resting on Deer Island in the middle of the river. When finished its length was 1030 feet, its width 

34 feet; and its height above high water mark, 37 feet; 6000 tons of lumber had gone into its construction. It was built under the direction of Timothy Palmer of Newburyport, a native of Boxford, and seven months were required for its completion. It served to

replace the ferry at the mouth of Powwow River, running from this point to what had been the ferry landing opposite. It was rebuilt in 1810 and was the first chain bridge in New England. For seventeen years it served its purpose without any evidence of undue strain. But in 1827 on a cold morning in February, at one o'clock in the morning, as David Jackman and Frederick Carlton were driving a heavily loaded wagon drawn by four oxen and one horse over this bridge, the chains broke, and the wagon with its load, the draught animals and their drivers were all precipitated into the river. Men and horse were saved but the oxen drowned. 

The morning was very cold and the bridge had on it a large amount of snow and ice. It was replaced a few years later by the chain bridge familiar to some of us older citizens. 

On November 26, 1795, a covered bridge was erected at Holt's Rocks between Newbury and Haverhill called the Rock's Bridge, and was opened for travellers. It was 1000 feet in length, had four arches, a draw, and was supported by five piers and two abutments. In 1818 this bridge was swept away by ice. A new bridge, 900 feet long, and similar in construction to the previous one, was finished in the fall of 1828. This was maintained as a toll bridge until 1868, and was then made free.

The question of a bridge across the Merrimack to Salisbury direct was discussed for twenty-five years but without result, because the objection was raised that a bridge would be an obstruction to navigation. Finally on March 4, 1826, on petition of James 

Prince, John Wood, Steven Frothingham, and associates, authority was given to build a bridge between Market and Kent Streets, Newburyport, to some convenient point in Salisbury, and also to lay out and make a road four rods in width on the Salisbury side. 

The bridge was built in 1827 and was supported in part by chains. 

Thus another important link was added to the Turnpike and the line itself improved, forming a part of the present Lafayette Road from Newburyport north.

Another means of meeting the need for better transportation was the building of private roads, known as "turnpikes." On account of their importance turnpikes deserve more than a passing notice.

The development and maintenance of good roadways was expensive for the towns through which they passed, and in order to meet this situation turnpikes were chartered by the state government. Eighty-two such charters were granted in New Hampshire, fifty of them between 1793 and 1833 and thirty-two from 1833 on. The customary provisions were that a new route was to be laid out differing from the old one, the grantees were to have the privilege of erecting turnstiles or gates at proper locations and to collect monies or tolls to reimburse them for the expense they had been put to for construction and for the further outlay and maintenance, for which they were duly obligated. Toll rates from gate to gate were regulated by the mileage or distance between them. The rates granted to the first company per mile were:

The authority to build and maintain a road was expressed as follows: "That the said Corporation are empowered to survey, lay out, make, and keep in repair a Turnpike or highway of four rods wide in such route or tract as, in their best judgment and skill, will combine shortness of distance with the most practicable from point A to point B."

The straight line requirement does not appear in any of the New Hampshire charters. It was common in Massachusetts. 

The First New Hampshire Turnpike was granted to "The Proprietors of New Hampshire Road." The petition for incorporation. set forth that communication between the seacoast and the interior parts of the state might be made much more easy, convenient, and less expensive than hitherto by a direct road from Concord. to the Piscataqua Bridge; but that the expensiveness of such an

undertaking would render it difficult of accomplishment "otherwise than by an incorporated company," to be indemnified by a toll for the sums that should be expended by it. 

The road was promptly completed a distance of thirty-six miles, passing through Durham, Northwood, Epsom, Chichester, Pembroke and Concord, about as route number 4A now runs. The eastern terminus was at the Piscataqua Bridge, connecting Durham with Newington over a half mile of water, considered in those days a marvel of bridge building. This bridge was carried away by ice and high water on February 18, 1853.

No tolls were to be collected on any mile until six hundred dollars had been expended thereon, or a proportionate amount upon several miles covered by one gate. Proposals for construction were invited on October 3, 1800, and in March, 1803, public notice was given that the required sums had been expended, and that the company would avail itself of the privilege of collecting tolls on April first of that year. The cost of construction was nine hundred dollars per mile.

The company erected gates where it saw fit and it appears there were three in the limits of Durham, one in Lee, one at Oyster Bridge, and at other places on the route. 

By an act of June 14, 1824, the corporation was permitted to sell its road to the towns through which it passed. Concord bought its part for five thousand dollars, Portsmouth paid four thousand dollars, and on January 28, 1825, the road became free to all. 

I have gone into details on this Turnpike because it was an important road near Exeter and was expeditiously completed. It was typical, too, of other charters that followed it soon after. The traffic over this road to tide water was heavy in summer and winter. 

At Durham several brick warehouses were built on the north bank of Oyster River opposite the residence of General Sullivan. It was the custom to fill these warehouses with goods from Portsmouth by packet boat in the summer time, and in the winter frequently long lines of sleighs, pungs, sleds came down from "up country" with their produce of pork products, furs, corn, hay and so forth, ending at the sea coast. After bartering for their needs, they returned laden with fish products, salt, dress goods and so on.

The Second New Hampshire Turnpike was chartered Decem ber 6, 1799, and lasted until 1837 when its charter was repealed. 

Its route was from the "lottery bridge in Claremont to the plain in Amherst near the Court House." For twenty-five years it carried an enormous traffic of farm products and timber to Boston, the teams returning with loads of rum and store goods. 

The Third New Hampshire Turnpike was incorporated December 27, 1799, to build from Bellows Falls in Walpole on the Connecticut River through Keene to the Massachusetts line. In 1801 

Massachusetts permitted an extension of four miles to Winchendon, in that state, to improve the way to Boston. 

Dearborn Emerson started a line of stages over this road, running from Boston to Bellows Falls; fare four dollars and fifty cents a reduction from previous rates. 

In 1824 the corporation was allowed to surrender its charter and turn its road over to the municipalities. 

The Fourth New Hampshire Turnpike was founded in 1800 with authority to build "From the east bank of the Connecticut River in Lebanon to the west bank of the Merrimack River in Boscawen." The estimated cost was six hundred dollars per mile, but the road actually cost a little over twelve hundred dollars per mile. The Andover, New Hampshire section, was built by 

Capt. Stephen Harriman of Vermont, who had a remarkable contractor's outfit. A light frame house was built on trucks for the accommodation of himself and family, and was moved as the work progressed by eight yoke of oxen. The twenty men composing his force lived in tents, and two daughters of the boss prepared their meals by means of a large iron-hooped brick oven built on a solid platform on wheels. This road was opened in 1804. It was needed as an outlet for products of "up above" and annually each fall and winter one might find parked at the Common, east of Moulton's Tavern in Andover, sleighs and sleds of many varieties, ranging from the little one-horse pung loaded with butter, cheese, poultry, etc., to the huge van drawn by eight horses; all in search of a market "down below." A line of freight wagons was run by Balch, each team being composed of eight well-groomed horses, one seat being occupied by a stalwart Negro, something unusual at the time.

In 1840 the road was made free, Andover paying five hundred sixty-six dollars for its portion of the road. 

The Tenth New Hampshire Turnpike, chartered December 28, 1803, brought the north country into communication with the seaports and stimulated settlement and development to a greater extent than any other road. Slowly before 1785 the people had been creeping up the Connecticut as far north as Lancaster, where they found themselves without communication except via the river, and then mostly during the winter months. The White 

Mountain Range seemed to cut off outlet to the southeast. The 

Crawford Notch was known and that an old Indian trail ran down through it; and so gradually a rough pioneer road had developed, very difficult to keep in repair; and hence, in 1803, the corporation was formed, to improve the same as a turnpike. 

The description of this route reads "from the upper line in Bartlett through the Notch in the White Hills, containing twenty miles." Through the grandeur of the White Mountains the tenth 

New Hampshire thrust its road and it became an avenue of great importance over which a heavy stream of traffic flowed for many years. It connected southeasterly with the public roads through 

Fryeburg to Portland and the sea coast. In winter often lines of teams over a half mile in length might be seen going and returning laden with wares of all kinds. 

The Tenth New Hampshire was planned to connect Portland with the Lake Champlain region, but this was not accomplished as in time the public roads served to do this. 

In 1803 on the site of the present Fabyan House was built the first real hotel in the mountains. The storm of 1826, which destroyed the Willey family, was almost a vital blow to the turnpike, the road being washed away in many places and all but two of the bridges destroyed. Tradition says that the city of Portland, appreciating the importance of the road, contributed in cash substantially to its reconstruction. The road was freed to the public about 1876.

I have given you the history in detail of a few of the turnpikes of New Hampshire, all of which were incorporated between 1799 and 1803. If all the remainder were plotted on the map, one would

see lines crossing and recrossing up and down, so that the state would show itself pretty well covered with good roads where needed. Bear in mind, please, that these are privately incorporated toll gate roads distinct from public thoroughfares.

It is interesting to note how skillfully these routes were laid out, for in most cases they are today, though much improved, the routes of our main automobile travel.

Thus we have been wheeling along the highways of New England. We have at length reached the point where another means of transportation was about to emerge and grow, a means which was destined either to relegate the stage coach to the western part of our country or to confine its use to such parts of the east as could not easily be reached by rail. The advent of the railroad marks the termination in 1840 of our stage coach era. For forty years, then, the stage coach had served us well. The Andover and Wilmington Railroad, connecting with the Boston and Lowell, opened May 8, 1836. It provided a schedule of two trains daily in each direction with a fare of one dollar. Stage connections to

Haverhill and Exeter were advertised. It was in 1840 that the 

Boston and Maine reached Exeter, the same year that the eastern railroad reached Newburyport. And so our coaching days are over.

We have wheeled many miles and one needs to remind one's self that the bigger the wheel, the longer the tire.
