SIXTY-FIVE AND OVER 

Grow old along with me!

The best is yet to be, The last of life, for which the first was made. 

Our times are in His hand 

Who saith, "A whole I planned, Youth shows but half; trust God: see all, nor be afraid!"

Rabbi Ben Ezra - Robert Browning QUAKER BACKGROUNDS 

Since its origin the Society of Friends has had a concern for the poor and a deep sense of responsibility for the aged who were no longer able to manage their own affairs nor to take care of themselves. In the late seventeenth century social conditions in 

England were greatly disturbed and to be old meant beggary. In 

1660 Thomas Lawson, Quaker schoolmaster and botanist, sent "An Appeal to the Parliament Concerning the Poor that there may not be a Beggar in England." Quaker work for the aged was thus part of their pioneer social endeavor. To us too, old age is not only an economic problem and a social responsibility, but is as it was for the early Friends, a deep personal and spiritual concern. 

Their Monthly Meetings, which had charge of humanitarian activities in behalf of individuals, did their tasks quietly and modestly. 

They held with John Bellers, friend of William Penn and social pioneer, that "our loving kindness should be all-embracing as the love of God." However their charity was so often sought that it was found necessary in 1737 to establish membership qualifications because many asked aid of Friends who had little in common with them or their way of life. But in the time of George Fox and 

William Penn, they had to offer aid to many of their own members. 

An examination of records of the day shows that Friends were artisans and seamen, tradesmen and masters, with a small proportion of unskilled laborers and gentry. The larger number were then in very moderate or humble circumstances. 

From the very beginning it was taken as a self-evident principle that everyone should support himself by his own work, but in order that persons in need should not suffer, the elders and overseers and sometimes a visiting committee especially appointed for the purpose had the duty of seeking out and reporting those who in their judgment needed help. The Meetings not only helped "poor Friends" but also others, "the world's poor people." Friends contributed to the public poor funds, but rarely appealed to the custodians of these funds for their own members.

In work for Friends and others, the Society's effort was based on the prevention of poverty and the encouragement of thrift to insure provision for times of difficulty, the proverbial rainy day, and for old age. To prevent poverty, Friends were given guidance on management of home affairs and in improved business methods. 

The motto was "help toward self-help." 

As early as 1669, George Fox urged an almshouse "for all poor Friends who were past work." He envisioned it as a house where "an hundred may have room to work in" and shops of all sorts of things to sell.

In 1696 a workhouse was erected at Bristol for "willing Friends to work in and for the aged and feeble to live in." In 1701 the famous Quaker workhouse at Clerkenwell, London, was established. In addition to the aged, the jobless and children, those who could pay for care, and pensioners of the founders were admitted. Both institutions, originally places of labor for old and young, became places of residence for the aged and the incapacitated. Clerkenwell institution continued in existence until well into the nineteenth century. It became a school in 1811. 

Friends' almshouses and workhouses, it can be seen, were not the poor-houses of the past which were so feared by the poor and unemployed. They embodied, it is true, a philosophy of thrift, sparing and simple maintenance, well-spent funds, but they were administered in tenderness and love.

Friends brought with them to America their practice of benevolent care for those in their declining years. John Martin, a tailor in humble circumstances who died in Philadelphia in 1703, left two lots on the south side of Walnut Street, between 3rd and 4th Streets, to the Monthly Meeting for an almshouse for the care of older people. It was not until 1729 that the institution, a stone building, was built fronting Walnut Street. 

Chroniclers tell us that among those members of the Society who found a comfortable home there, were several who were able to carry on in a modest way some skilled trade. One of the earliest and most expert of the Swiss watchmakers who came to Philadelphia in the eighteenth century spent his last days in Friends' almshouse and while there turned out many specimens of his craftsmanship.

This was not a place of degradation but a residence for the poor and aged, -- "a peaceful haven affording not only shelter but opportunity for study and meditation." A decent privacy in homelike surroundings was provided for the "happier poor" and "the

3 old and helpless." "Two or three of the old women conducted little schools, another made molasses candy, and sold herbs."

Within its sheltered enclosure "all was still and the air was filled with the perfume of roses, bees were humming, quaint placid old men were sitting smoking their pipes." Under green arbors ancient Quaker ladies were bending over beds of sweet marjoram and lavender. The thrifty little community was a very human one; they had their traditions, their stories and their aristocracy. 

It was in its sunny rooms that Longfellow, the poet, laid one of the scenes in his poem "Evangeline." For here his heroine visited her lover Gabriel when he lay dying of yellow fever. In 

1841 it was found that there were too few Friends needing care and the building was abandoned. Quaint Walnut Place in Philadelphia now occupies the historic site. 

Friends were also active in the establishment of public almshouses in Pennsylvania and petitioned the legislature for their creation. A city institution for the poor and homeless of all ages was erected "in a green meadow."

The duty of visiting the aged in their homes and ministering to their needs, spiritual and social, has always been part of the activities of members of the Meetings. In England Elizabeth Fry established a District Visiting Society and in 1825 founded a National Guardian Society which attended to the wants of the aged as part of its program. 

In the nineteenth century Friends in America set up and joined others in creating female societies for the employment of the poor, sewing rooms and houses of industry for the unemployed or for those too feeble or old to do heavy labor. They also became members of boards of directors of public and private agencies and institutions.

In later years as their economic status grew, there has been little need to extend assistance to the members of the Society themselves. The entire concern for assistance to the poor and the aged became a matter of helping to develop services for those outside the Quaker fold. The problem of poverty among Friends which faced George Fox and his successors has practically vanished.

IMPORTANCE OF OLD AGE TODAY 

Why is old age important today? What aspects of it should we be concerned with in any special sense?

4 

We are rediscovering old age because we have found there are many more old people. Social change is having a profound effect on what old people can do and on what we can do for them. 

We realize that it is necessary to think of better ways of meeting the new and increasing problems of old age.

To most people the aged seem a small group and their problem a simple one. It may be therefore as surprising to our members as it is to the public to know that the question of older people in the population is becoming a major one in the United States. 

The population of our country is rapidly aging. People now can expect to live longer. The lengthening of the life-span makes it possible for more of us to hope that we will reach old age. A look backward through time makes clearer what that means. Let us examine briefly some figures authorities give us. 

In ancient Rome, it could be expected for an infant born that he would (on the average) live to the age of 23 years. By 1850 in 

New England this life expectancy for an infant had risen to 40 years. In 1900 for the whole United States it became 48 years. 

Today, it has grown to sixty-three years, for the average person. 

Those who reach fifty now, can look forward to another score of years of life.

Since most of this improvement is due to better health and better social conditions among infants and the young, those who belong to more fortunately circumstanced groups, such as ours, can hope for greater increase of years. 

Not only does the average child and young person live longer but the proportion of older persons already in the population is greater. More than one-fourth of the people in the country now are above forty-five. Looking ahead we can foresee increase in that ratio. Students of the subject tell us that by 1980 two out of every five will be over forty-five years of age. 

Why do we live longer now? 

Why has our proportion of older persons increased?

People live longer because we have been conquering the diseases of infancy, childhood, youth and early middle-age. Social illness and the diseases of poverty are waning. This has allowed more people to reach old age. The limitation of the size of families and the consequent decrease in the number of children born have also been among the factors in the reduction in the proportion

of children in the population and the increase in the proportion of old people. The change in our immigration policy has also altered this proportion. A number of years ago, European immigration brought us a host of young people. Later sharp curtailment of immigration by Congress changed the picture. While those immigrants already settled here were growing older and adding to the aging elements in the population, there were no further increments of young people from overseas. What the war and its aftermath will bring, we do not yet know.

OLDER PEOPLE AMONG FRIENDS 

Information collected by the Social Service Committee concerning the number of members of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting who are over sixty-five has been analyzed and interpreted. Data as to those above sixty-five and those below have been obtained from all the meetings from which reports were requested. These facts have been analyzed geographically in order to correlate them in a general way. It must be remembered by those who study this material that for statistical purposes the individuals involved are too few to admit of other than general interpretation and inference. 

However, the facts are illuminating and can be roughly related to what we already have said about the increase in the proportion of the aged in the general population. This last fact was already noted by the Social Service Committee in its report to the Yearly Meeting of 1944 and was given as one of its reasons for studying the number of older Friends above sixty-five in Philadelphia 

Yearly Meeting. 

Our survey shows that sixty-nine Monthly Meetings of the 

Philadelphia Yearly Meeting reported 11,069 members: 6,173 women and girls and 4,896 men and boys. Most of the membership was in Pennsylvania where forty-five Meetings reported 7,895 members: 4,420 women and girls and 3,475 men and boys. In New 

Jersey seventeen Meetings reported 2,367 members: 1,323 women and girls and 1,044 men and boys. In Delaware three Meetings reported 540 members: 292 women and girls and 248 men and boys. 

In Maryland two Meetings said they had 103 members: 44 women and girls and 59 men and boys, while California gave for one 

Meeting 164 members: 94 women and girls and 70 men and boys. 

For ready comparison see tables in the appendix.

In all the Meetings except in Maryland, women and girls outnumbered men and boys. When the total membership was considered we found that 55.6 per cent were women and girls and 44.4 per cent men and boys. There were, therefore, roughly six female members for every five male members.

The information on older Friends in the membership revealed that Philadelphia Yearly Meeting had a comparatively large group of older Friends among its members, except for the meetings at Swarthmore, Pennsylvania, and Princeton, New Jersey. 

Both of these are located in college towns and showed a large proportion of young people. For the over-all membership our reports revealed that 2,080 Friends or 18.8 per cent, almost one in every five, of the total membership of 11,069 were sixty-five years of age or over.

In all the states but Maryland older women Friends outnumbered older men Friends. Of the 2,080 older Friends, 63.7 per cent or five out of every eight were women. Of the 6,173 women members, we discovered that 1,324 or 21 per cent were sixtyfive or older while among the 4,896 men members only 756 or 

15.4 per cent were in that age grouping.

In the general population of the sections of the states in which Friends of the Yearly Meeting lived, the ratio of the aged (men and women sixty-five or over) was six to nine per cent according to census figures. The fact that we found the ratio of old people among Friends to be so much higher cannot be considered proof or indication of longevity among Friends. It may suggest itself that Friends are economically favored and have in their environment and mode of life, conditions advantageous to longer life. It may also connote a lower birth-rate in our Society. 

It seems that the figures collected show clearly that Friends have a responsibility and a Friendly concern for a large group of older Friends for whom they very likely will be afforded wider opportunities of service as the situation is studied and illumination obtained for the proper exercise of care. 

WHEN OLDER PEOPLE ARE ILL 

Many years ago Edward T. Devine, author of "Misery and Its 

Causes, pointed out that while we were indeed succeeding in our attack on the diseases of poverty, we were failing in our attempts to deal with degenerative illnesses. The medical profession has still to cope with the diseases peculiar to aging persons: organic heart disease; arterio-sclerosis (hardening of the arteries); hypertension (high blood pressure); kidney diseases (Bright's disease, etc.); diseases of the lungs, such as, chronic bronchitis, emphysema, and asthma; ulcer; cancer; adiposity (fatness); diabetes; rheumatism and arthritis; neurologic and mental conditions. They make old age difficult and sometimes very hard to endure. These are the great problems for medical pioneers.

The lack of success on the part of the medical profession in dealing with them (and apparent lack of interest also) has prevented the prolongation of the life-span in the group above fifty or fifty-five.

However, the shocking picture which writers, even medical authors, too frequently give of old age is that of sickness and senility. Despite the saying, that Cicero wrote of the beauty of old age but not of beautiful old people, there are many who have serenity, sweetness, and beauty of spirit and of person too. The fact that advanced age may be a time of infirmity and illness makes it necessary to think of good doctor's advice, plans for care, attention, and service at home and in the institutions where our old relatives and friends live. One does not like to believe, as one authority says, that so little research has been done in the medical treatment of the aged "because they are economically worthless." Research in pediatrics, the illness of children, has been considered of worth to society because children have increasing economic value with added years and their maturity can be influenced and developed while old people become less valuable to society as they grow older. However, as the beginnings of research and the work of longevity clubs and clinics show, we have overdrawn the disvalues in old age. It makes it the responsibility of all of us to encourage medical research and more specialized work.

One medical authority says with deep regret the subject of old age has never been popular with doctors. But the attitude is changing. A new science, gerontology, the study of old age and a new medical art, geriatrics - the treatment of its diseases, is becoming a specialty of physicians. The new conception is that old age is not a time for putting people aside. The older person is no longer thought of as a possible invalid or valetudinarian just because he has attained fullness of years. It is considered harmful and conducive to ill health, in fact, to retire. Thus the once prized objective, retirement from business or work, has become undesirable unless the person is ill. The aim to "drop out of active life" at forty which we inherited from our European forebears has been changed to continue work or at least activity until seventy and later. But as we grow old we must, physicians say, let down and shorten our working hours interspersing them with periods of rest and recreation. Occupational therapy and continued active and interesting work has become part of the program of institutions and clinics for older people. Even the aged sick are given 'activity' and recreation.

There is a new mental and spiritual atmosphere for the older people of our day which enables them to remain part of the world

in which we and they live. There is developing beɩter care for the illness of the old and a more intelligent health program and approach which will lead to the discovery of better methods we are told.

In the inquiry sent to the Monthly Meetings for this study, the only questions which were asked which would elicit information as to physical or mental health of those Friends of sixty-five years and over dealt with institutional care. We learned that approximately one-quarter of these Friends, 38 out of 177, were under special care. There were twenty-one in mental hospitals; fourteen in nursing homes and three in sanatoria. 

The need for a concern for the health of older members of the Society is evident. It may be profitable to broaden the inquiry to discover whether adequate care is provided for Friends in commercial homes and institutions where standards are so often poor. 

Perhaps we should also look into the care given our aged who are living alone as invalids.

A CHANGING WORLD 

The greatest problem with which older people must cope is that of swiftness and variety in the changing social and economic situations today.

Changes have become so great and so many in modern times that it becomes difficult even for the young and for vigorous folk of middle years to meet life's tempo and to adjust effectively to altering times. These adjustments deeply trouble those who have developed patterns of behavior and who may be "set in their ways". 

It becomes a harder pull when old people are without associates of their age and without ties remaining from the childhood and youth which they so fondly recall and when memory for far-off things becomes clearer and that for the recent past is confused. 

Life today presses down more than before on the old man or old woman who is house-bound or incapacitated or in poor health. 

Old people today must live in a new world. For they have not the same position or place which once they had. In the days of the 

Greeks a graybeard was of great importance and much respected because there were so few. It was rare for a person to reach venerable years in that ancient time. In the four-generation or patriarchal family which existed not so long ago, the elders, grandfather and grandmother as heads of the family, were leaders,

mentors, and advisers. They took care of the young, they directed and guided the middle-aged.

Homes were large once. There was plenty of room for old people even when they became dependents. Our houses are smaller today. There has been a change from life in the home to life away from it. It began with the increase of transportation facilities and wider area of residence--with the apartment which replaced the house. Old people lost their function in the circle of kin.

Other modern ways of life have altered the background of the home. Women go to work. Young daughters take jobs. The decline of the home as a family center has been due also to the increased diversity of community life. Members of the family no longer remain in its enclosed circumference nor seek their social life there. Mothers, fathers, daughters, sons--all leave it for work, pleasure and companionship. 

The jobs of the old people in the family home have disappeared. And their responsibilities and tasks have vanished. At the same time the corollary of their responsibilities and tasks-their privileges and expected rights--likewise have waned. 

When old people are ill or disabled there may be no one to take care of them. There is often no room for them with relatives in the new compact quarters. So, even when they need care most, they must leave the shelter of the home and the family. 

Then too, an aging population in a rapidly changing civilization brings new problems for all of us.

The information supplied by the Monthly Meetings enables us to present some interesting figures of value on the effect which changed social and economic conditions have had on the care of 

Friends over sixty-five. A relatively large number, 281 of 2,080 older persons or approximately 13.5 per cent, almost one out of every eight, were reported as either living alone or in congregate institutions and boarding homes. There were 177 in places of care and 104 living alone. Apparently, most of them were able to pay their own way. Only nine in institutions and seven living alone were aided by their Meetings. Twenty-six others were said to be in need but not receiving assistance from Friends. Whether these were living alone or with their families or elsewhere was not stated. Nor do we know how many Friends now in congregate homes might have continued their own living arrangements with satisfaction had they funds to do so.

Perhaps we can liken these times of profound social upheaval to those of George Fox and his immediate spiritual heirs in the late 1600's and early 1700's. While Friends now have no problem of want or unemployment there are social and spiritual relations involved, which must deeply affect our older Friends. As one 

Friend has put it, "the most important problems of old age are not financial at all and are not limited to any one social class. 

They are the need for somewhere to live, something to do, some one to care."

II CARE AND SERVICES FOR THE AGED TODAY 

How do we care for the aged today? Our older people now have become so clearly and definitely part of our national responsibility that we have enacted Federal laws for old age assistance and old age and survivors insurance. The care of old people in need has become practically a matter of public support, For the needy who live at home or plan for themselves old age assistance has largely replaced private benevolence. For others of this group who require institutional or special care in shelter the public welfare home has replaced the private almshouse of old and the public poor house. For older people with income or funds of their own, care in private institutions for the aged, boarding homes, etc. is · available in many places.

Federal old age and survivors insurance provides the missing link of independence in old age to the superannuated employee and his family when he can no longer work at his job. 

In many communities advisory services are offered to older people by welfare agencies regardless of their financial status, to guide them in the social and personal problems of advancing years. 

OLD AGE ASSISTANCE 

Public old age assistance is not a pension. The Federal Government makes the following requirements as to individuals receiving grants:

The applicant must be sixty-five years of age or over. 

He must be in financial need and the state in determining this must take into consideration any other income or resources which the individual who requests old age assistance may have.

He may not be an inmate of a public institution. 

He must have been a resident of the state for at least five of the nine years immediately preceding application for old age assistance and continuously for one year before application.

In addition the Federal Government makes the three following stipulations: 

No person may be excluded by any citizenship requirement. 

A hearing must be given any individual whose claim for old age assistance is denied.

All records and information concerning applicants and recipients must be confidential. 

One-half of the cost of old age assistance is financed through state taxes; the other half through funds provided by the Federal 

Social Security Board. Although the Federal Government shares one-half of the expenditures, it does not contribute to that part of the grant which is above $40 per month. It shares in the cost of administration fixing such cost at 5 per cent of expenditures. The state must make reports to the Federal Social Security Board. 

Under the Federal Social Security Act, first enacted in 1936, a state plan for old age assistance must apply equally to all divisions of the state, provide for financial participation by the state, and be administered or supervised by a single state agency. State employees administering old age assistance must be appointed under the Merit System.

The large sum which the Federal Government puts into old age assistance in the states has made for the establishment of high standards and has given stability and dignity to old age assistance programs. This is reflected in the attitude to those who obtain old age assistance and in the attention given to their needs. 

In most states, those aided must be without income or property resources to meet immediate needs and have no legally responsible relatives to support them. The needs and resources of the family are taken into account in determining whether the person should be given assistance.

Since an old age assistance grant is an unconditional money payment the state or the local assistance unit usually takes the position that it makes no decision as to where the individual may or may not live, but determines only whether or not he is in need. 

The aged person may, therefore, decide for himself to live at home, or board in a private family home. When other regulations of

the state do not bar it, he may use his grant to pay board in a private institutional boarding home.

The amount of grant is determined by calculating requirements in accordance with the standards of the public old age assistance agency and deducting therefrom the available resources; the difference between the two is the amount of the grant. 

It may interest Friends to know that in September, 1944, old age assistance payments in the amount of $57,893,729 were made to 2,070,393 individuals. THE COUNTY HOME 

Humanity in distress has traveled a long hard road from the public poor house to the modern county home or welfare institution. These are now often equipped for hospital care and for the treatment of the infirm and feeble. However standards vary widely and treatment ranges from the indifferent and callous to the kindly and humane. Some of the institutions are excellent, with service comparatively as good as that in private boarding homes and institutions for the aged. Most of the inmates are usually aged persons with many chronically ill and seniles among their number. 

New Jersey has developed a good county program for the care of the aged and chronically ill. Some of the county welfare homes have hospital and nursing equipment of a high order and are staffed with competent physicians and nurses. The county welfare 

Board has power to administer the old age assistance program; to manage the welfare house; to approve care to be rendered either in the client's own home, family boarding home, commercial

nursing home, or welfare house, and to supplement out of county funds old age assistance care for the chronically ill in licensed nursing homes or by visiting nurse service. When an aged person enters a nursing home which is approved, the grant may be as high as $70.00 per month.

In Delaware there are no county homes. A State Welfare 

Home is operated by the Old Age Welfare Commission. This is reported as complete with operating room, hospital facilities, trained nurses, a resident physician, and a superintendent. There are also provision for those "who just need a home".

In Maryland there are no public institutions for aged persons and the state at present lacks resources for the chronically ill. 

A chronic hospital is being planned when it becomes feasible to build.

In California most counties maintain homes for the needy aged usually operated in connection with the county hospital. The type of care is described as custodial. Persons are admitted who are not considered eligible for old age assistance.

In Pennsylvania there are county homes to which aged persons are admitted. They vary widely in character in the sixty-seven counties. A relatively small number have hospital and medical care facilities and are equipped with good staff services for the inmates.

It would be well for Friends who have a concern for the aged to visit their county institutions in order that they may be informed as to the character of care given. It would be helpful to encourage the establishment of good medical and health programs for the prevention of illness among inmates and for the care of the senile, incapacitated, and sick or chronically ill. 

OLD AGE AND SURVIVORS INSURANCE 

The Federal Old Age and Survivors Insurance law was enacted in 1935, set up by the Federal Government in 1936 as part of the Social Security program, and is now in force as amended by the Congress in 1939. However it is not only an old age pension program for the individual but is a system of protection for the entire family and those dependent upon the wage earner. 

I. Monthly insurance benefits are payable to the insured wage earner when he reaches sixty-five years, to his wife, and to his unmarried dependent children if they are under eighteen as follows:

Beneficiary 

1. To the wage earner (the insured). 

2. To the wife of the insured after the insured reaches sixty-five years and the wife also reaches sixty-five years. 

3. To the dependent unmarried child or children under eighteen of the insured after the insured reaches sixty-five years.

Amount 

Full monthly benefit. 

One-half of the monthly benefit payable to the insured (the husband). 

One-half of the monthly benefit payable to the insured (the parent of the child or children).

II. Monthly insurance benefits are payable when the insured wage earner dies to his widow, unmarried dependent child or children under eighteen or to his dependent parents as follows:

Beneficiary 

1. To the widow when she reaches sixty-five years. 

2. To the widow who is under sixtyfive years if she has in her care a child or children under eighteen of the deceased insured. 

3. To the unmarried child or children under eighteen of the deceased insured. 

4. To the dependent parents of the deceased insured after they have reached sixty-five years if there is no widow or no unmarried child or children under eighteen.

III. Lump Sum Payments Amount 

Three-fourths of the monthly benefit which would have been payable to deceased insured (her husband). 

Three-fourths of the monthly benefit which would have been payable to deceased insured (the husband). 

One-half of the monthly benefit which would have been payable to deceased insured (the parent). 

One-half of the amount which would have been payable to the deceased insured (the son). 

When there are no survivors entitled to benefits as listed above, then a lump sum benefit equal to six times the monthly benefit which would have been payable

to the deceased insured is paid to the widow, widower, child, parent of the deceased in the order named. If there are no such surviving relatives the person who defrayed the burial expenses is reimbursed proportionately. 

No monthly benefit may be less than $10 per month and the maximum amount payable to a family may not be more than twice the benefit payable to the insured wage earner and in no case more than $85 a month.

If an insured wage earner or any member of his family who receives monthly payments earns more than $15 a month at any occupation covered by the insurance, his benefits are then suspended for the month during which he was so employed. This does not prevent employment for remuneration at any occupation which is not covered by insurance. 

The monthly benefit payment is calculated as follows: (a) 40 per cent of the first $50 of the monthly average wage plus (b) 10 per cent of the next $200 of the monthly average wage plus (c) 1 per cent of the amounts computed under (a) and (b) for each year of total annual wages equal to $200 per year or more.

A wage earner who had earned $100 per month for seven years would be entitled to $26.75 per month benefit when he reached sixty-five. His wife at sixty-five years of age and with no old age insurance of her own would receive half of that amount per month, etc.

The law provides that an aged person can only claim benefits if he has worked for a specified time at an occupation covered by the Act. There are certain requirements as to how long the aged person must have worked. Taking the present year, 1945, the requirements are that his employment must have been for any eight out of the sixteen quarters which have elapsed since the insurance system was established. In addition, his earnings must have been at least $50 during any one of these quarters.

The insurance benefits are definitely weighted in favor of low income groups and older wage earners. Although there is deliberate loading of the monthly benefit in favor of those who have paid least into the national insurance fund there should be no question that it is a sound policy as a matter of aid and relief to give

more liberal treatment to those with the smallest incomes. Every person in the classes covered by the insurance from the humblest employee in the industry to its highest paid director is insurable and eligible for benefits for that part of his earnings which is $3,000 a year or less. The monthly benefits from survivors' insurance are, therefore, a definite item of income for all persons who have been insured and have reached the age of sixty-five no matter what their financial status may be. They can be used for care either at home or in a family home or in a congregate or boarding home for the aged.

The funds for the payment of these old age benefits are provided through a system of payroll taxes shared equally by employer and employee. Wage earners are covered except those engaged in agricultural labor, in casual labor, at domestic service in private homes, on ships' crews, Government services--city, state, and municipal--those employed by religious, scientific, literary, or educational organizations and operated without profit; self-employed persons or those employed by members of the family are also excluded.

When Old Age and Survivors Insurance was established it was with the hope that it might eventually include most wage earners and that as it became more inclusive in the future it might obviate the need for public assistance for a great block of old people, thus placing old age protection more and more on a non-relief basis. 

For a number of sessions the Federal Social Security Board has urged the Congress to extend Old Age and Survivors Insurance to groups not now included. 

PRIVATE HOMES 

Private institutions or homes for the aged have been established by philanthropic individuals and groups concerned for the care of older men and women. In the order of number of homes in each of the following groups, they include religious homes; private benevolent homes; homes established by fraternal organizations or trade unions or nationality groups. Most of them admit men and women and married couples. Some are for men or women only. There are more homes for aged women than for aged men. 

There are sixty-six homes in the Metropolitan Philadelphia area: 

43 for men and women, 18 for women only, and 5 for men only. 

With few exceptions the standard of care ranks far above that of the average county home. Almost all of them are full to capacity and have long waiting lists. Their total capacity is said to be not much more than 1 per cent of the total number of persons sixtyfive years of age and older.

Private homes often require advance payments for care, contracts as to responsibility and financial arrangements, the making over of the applicant's monetary resources and require devises or bequest of the applicant's estate. 

More recently the boarding home plan has been developed. 

This has the advantage of leaving the older person and the institution free as to length of stay and gives independence to the individual resident. The modern private home stresses concern for health, individual interests, program, activity, recreation, social and spiritual welfare, and respect for the personality of the resident. 

FRIENDS BOARDING HOMES 

It was about fifty years ago that a hitherto unrecognized need was discovered by the women Friends of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting. Boarding home care for older Friends had been started in 1891 with the opening of "Friends Boarding Home, Established by Concord Quarterly Meeting" in West Chester. The idea of this home had its source in the minds of two women, Sallie A. Sharpless and Lydia H. Hall who had a concern for "a Home for friends of limited circumstances, where they might have the comforts which they could not have unaided."

In 1895 a concern appeared in the minutes of the Yearly Meeting of Women Friends, brought to it by a valued Friend, Mary Thomas, of Norristown, expressing the desire for homes for persons who needed quiet, tranquil, protected residences in which to enjoy declining years. A committee of the Women's Meeting was appointed to explore the matter. In 1896 the committee reported that there was a need to raise funds for the purpose. 

In the Yearly Meeting of 1896, Anna T. Jeanes transmitted a letter and a check for $200,000 to the Yearly Meeting in order that it might establish the needed facilities. The sum was donated from the estate of Joseph Jeanes to assist in the establishment and maintenance of boarding homes for the aged and infirm amongst Friends "and those in sympathy with us". The donation was to be placed under the control and care of a committee of the Yearly Meeting with the number of representatives from each Quarterly Meeting being equal. In 1911 a bequest under the will of Anna T. 

Jeanes was made in the amount of $250,000 for infirmary purposes. Only homes maintaining infirmaries are eligible to share the income from this bequest. 

In order to qualify under Anna T. Jeanes' will, the established homes either added to their buildings or set aside certain rooms

for sick patients. Some of the homes have been using their infirmary facilities for regular boarders when pressed for room. It has been questioned whether this is the proper use of these facilities and also whether it is to the best interest of the residents. In 

1922 Stapeley Farm, part of the Jeanes estate, was sold and the proceeds became available for the support of both Friends' homes and infirmaries in accordance with the terms of the will. 

The proceeds from this fund are distributed to the Quarterly Meeting homes by the Jeanes Committee on a pro rata basis in accordance with the instructions of the donor. The Committee does not supervise the homes nor does it arrange admissions. It cannot assist members financially who live in them or desire to enter them. Admissions are entirely under the control of the administering boards of the Quarterly Meeting homes as are the charges for board.

The will of Anna T. Jeanes also provided a modest fund, the income of which is used for older women Friends who are in Friends Boarding homes and in need of assistance. 

Eight homes are conducted by as many Quarterly Meetings: in Kennett Square, Pennsylvania, by Western Quarterly Meeting; in Newtown, Pennsylvania, by Bucks Quarterly Meeting; in Norristown, Pennsylvania, by Abington Quarterly Meeting; in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, (Stapeley Hall) by Philadelphia Quarterly 

Meeting; in West Chester, Pennsylvania, (Hickman Memorial) by Concord Quarterly Meeting; in Moorestown, New Jersey, (The 

Greenleaf) by Haddonfield Quarterly Meeting; in Trenton, New 

Jersey, (Pennlyn) by Burlington Quarterly Meeting, and in Woods town, New Jersey, by Salem Quarterly Meeting. The Half Yearly 

Meetings, Caln, Millville, and Southern, have no homes. Their members may use the homes of the Quarterly Meetings.

Friends boarding homes are modern in principle and are considered to be well conducted institutions. Infirmary and other facilities for members unable to manage for themselves, gardens or open spaces, activity programs, work therapy, and recreation which some of the homes have are all desirable features of congregate homes and are recommended by experienced administrators and advocates of good programs for aged persons needing sheltered care or institutional service.

The homes are at present used for the most part by those Friends who either can themselves provide the moderate boarding charges or do so with the aid of relatives or friends. Monthly 

Meetings are contributing for nine of the residents only.

The board of each home determines its policy and boarding rates. There seems no definite minimum age of admission to most of the homes. Acceptance appears to be based rather on the health and physical condition of the applicant. 

Our study showed that at the time it was made there were 139 members of our Yearly Meeting in congregate homes. Nearly all of them, 134, were in Friends boarding homes, five were in other denominational homes for the aged. The total number in our Quarterly Meeting boarding homes reported to us in March, 1945, is 253. One hundred seventy-five were Friends.

We do not know how many Friends there may be on the waiting lists of private institutions nor how many would desire to enter them had they means. Nor do we know how many Friends in commercial nursing and boarding homes would prefer to be in Friends boarding homes. It was noted that Friends reported in need in their own homes was greater than the number being assisted in the boarding homes.

SOME PROBLEMS OF INSTITUTION CARE: 

VIEWS OF AUTHORITIES AND OFFICIALS 

A national authority on the subject of the chronically ill, Miss Mary C. Jarrett says, "There is a growing trend among the voluntary homes for the aged to convert part of their facilities into homes for the chronically ill....Homes for the aged as a rule refuse admission to an applicant who has a chronic disease that is likely to incapacitate him. But as a resident many become ill at any time after admission, about half the residents of these institutions are usually found to be patients of the type suitable for a home for the chronic sick. Usually the governing board does not recognize this fact, and the provision for medical care is inadequate....The more progressive homes accept old people suffering from infirmities or chronic diseases that make institutional protection imperative and seek other ways of providing for the ablebodied aged. Homes with this policy plan their medical and nursing services accordingly."

New Jersey administrators tell us that nursing homes and institutions for the chronically ill are badly needed for the care of the aged. Very few of the homes for the aged have developed the infirmary type of care which state officials deem to be their most useful contribution to the problem of the care of the aged. 

As a result many homes have a rule that the applicant must be in good physical condition at the time of admission and some require

the removal of the resident when he becomes physically disabled. 

After the war it is planned to develop good infirmary institutions for the chronically ill under county auspices. 

Maryland lacks resources. The Legislature has, however, made provision for building a hospital for the chronically ill after the war.

In Pennsylvania nursing homes are state licensed. 

There are important questions and problems brought out by letters of inquiry and personal conferences with officials of public welfare and assistance services in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Maryland, Delaware, and California. These deal with the relation of public assistance to boarding home care; facilities which are lacking and matters of policy. 

Except in Delaware an individual receiving old age assistance may use his grant to pay for boarding home care in a private or commercial home or institution at the per capita rate it costs the institution to give such care. However it is not possible to pay more than the maximum set by the state for the grant. This maximum is $50 per month in California, and $40 a month in the states of Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Maryland. (In Delaware where the old age recipient cannot use his grant for institutional care, the maximum grant is only $25 per month.) The individual's resources and any contributions made by relatives or Friends are deducted from the amount granted.

In some states exceptions providing for a higher rate than the old age assistance grant are made for individuals requiring special care in nursing homes.

When an individual is in a home for the aged on a life contract basis, he cannot be considered to be in need and, therefore, cannot make application for old age assistance.

A resident of a private home who decides to leave a congregate home in order to establish a home of his own or live with friends or relatives usually may make application for old age assistance.

In California persons living in homes maintained by fraternal, benevolent, or other non-profit organizations may receive assistance. If the institution provides board and room or other support without charge, the resident is eligible to receive aid in the amount needed to meet his other requirements. In that state anyone who takes an aged person into his home is required to have a license from the Department of Social Welfare.

In New Jersey in general, old age assistance is not available to persons in "private charitable homes for the aged" with limited exceptions. These are for the most part for those requiring nursing care in homes which have infirmary service. The feeling is that if homes need help to take care of individual need, this should come through a direct appropriation from the state to the home. 

In Pennsylvania, Maryland, New Jersey, and California, appropriations are made by the states to private benevolent institutions for the care of aged persons. These sums are given to help the homes take care of individuals who cannot pay the cost of their care. Therefore, institutions wishing to serve older persons in need can make application for a state subsidy. 

In Pennsylvania homes under religious auspices may not receive appropriations from the state treasury. Those private homes for the aged which receive state aid are supervised by 

State Department of Welfare. 

In Maryland the amount received by private institutions from public funds is applicable on a daily rate basis to the care of old persons who are eligible under state law.

Some authorities believe that we should not depend on congregate institutional care completely for old people who cannot live at home, with their families, or board with a private family. 

For such persons it is suggested that we should have substitute facilities midway between institutional care and family home care. 

One idea advanced by a former welfare official is that church groups establish simple, homelike boarding houses. These could be run for very little money beyond what the old people can pay since the overhead would not be high and the furnishings simple. 

The residents would come and go as { y pleased; any individual who could not fit into the group would, of course, be asked to make other arrangements. Homes, each housing ten to twenty, would probably be a reasonable size. Perhaps such places should not be run by one denomination but by several in cooperation and be open to men and women of all denominations. 

Others who have had experience in the field recommend that old people be established in pleasant rooms in family homes in the vicinity of a good congregate institution and be permitted to come into the institution for meals, companionship, social and religious life. Furthermore, congregate institutions might "place out" or board their residents in such rooms when they wish to live alone, rebel against institutional life, or become difficult as members of the group.

COMMUNITY SERVICE 

Most old people are not in institutions, public or private, nor in boarding homes. They are with us as our neighbors in the community. When old people need advice and guidance they must have those to whom they may turn, with whom they can plan. Even their relatives and friends seek counsel and advice at times. In the past that advice and counsel came from the church group altogether. With Friends it was the Meeting. In our more complex life today the experienced social worker trained for his or her task meets this need and takes the place of the minister, the church worker in dealing with these special problems. 

There is a quaint entry in the minutes of Friends Meeting at Banbury, England for the year 1715 concerning a man and his wife. 

The record reads, "they desire one to be assistant to 'em to make their bed and sweep their house and wash their dishes, the old woman being uncapable of performing the same." We can imagine that the Friends of that day complied lovingly and efficiently. In some communities a visiting housekeeper does just what the aged couple in Banbury, England wanted done for them. There are also visiting nurses, public and private, to serve sick people in distress and to provide nursing care in city and rural communities. 

The Banbury Meeting records an earlier case in 1708, "Two women Friends are recommended to the Monthly Meeting for 1s apiece instead of 9d, they being weakly and aged and can do but very little; and work being scarce for that little they can do." 

Today in cases like this Friends can still be helpful by calling in the social worker of the family welfare society, or the old age assistance visitor, when needed to help plan. "Social service as a vocation can best be undertaken by those especially qualified by training," to quote the Book of Discipline. "But there remains for every willing individual, an opportunity for service in daily life at special tasks": giving of counsel, personal service, social and religious visiting in the home. 

A new development is the old age clinic or the guidance clinic for the aged set up by the older people themselves in some of our cities. In the metropolitan centers are planning groups to study and help promote services and programs. The Philadelphia Council of Social Agencies has a special division for the care of the aged and a worker for this purpose. Its information and referral service provides information as to resources for older persons in Delaware, Montgomery and Philadelphia Counties in Pennsylvania. Some social and health agencies have special bureaus to

deal with the individual problems of older people. Departments of 

Public Assistance and Welfare sometimes have specialized services for the aged in their local units. There is no longer the conception that old age is a single problem, one that can be dealt with by one formula: institutional care or financial aid. 

The new attitude to old age that it is not the end of all interest in active life, now requires more of us in the way of community and personal service. This has led to the setting up of hobby shops and clubs, work shops, etc. for the aged where they can "tinker" and make themselves of use by making things for their friends, for children, etc. Then, too, the formation of Golden Age Clubs to supply social life to old people is a new service which the Philadelphia Council of Social Agencies is stimulating. These are groups in which old people can meet under leadership to chat, play games, meet friends, participate in group activity, or take leadership if they can.

The central idea of modern old age service is that the older person must help in the planning. As some one lately expressed it, "the best plan is the older person's own plan if he is capable and well enough to think out a plan." In the future more people when they reach sixty-five will be able to make the choice whether they wish to remain in the community or seek institution care. 

This becomes possible by those in need through old age assistance and by the employed through the benefits of old age insurance. 

Our examination of older people above sixty-five among Friends living in our communities, either by themselves or with their families, is of significance. Of the 2,080 older Friends 1903 or 91.5 per cent lived in the community. Only 104 of that number or 5 per cent lived alone in a home or lodging of their own. 

This is probably as it should be according to the modern conception. Old people should remain in the community as part of its life stream as long as they are able and can manage and think of shelter care only when they come to need it. Among the large number of Friends above sixty-five in the community, there should, therefore, be opportunity for greater personal service and ministry.

Stephen Crisp of Colchester, England, a Quaker leader, wrote in 1690 that the sick, the lame, the impotent, and aged were the peculiar care of the Meeting. We see in that expression of apostolic concern not only an injunction to give protective and spiritual care to older people in our Meetings and in our communities but also as has been the custom of the Friends to those in the wider world outside.

It is our hope that the results of this study of the problems of old age may aid Friends to discover opportunities for themselves of service and assistance in filling the needs of older people.

Since age is indeed, "the last for which the first was made," the crown of life when the individual spirit may be most luminous to show the way, older men and women because of their matured judgment, wisdom, and creative imagination have also a part to play in what is suggested, in what is planned, and in what is done in the Society and out of it for the aged. 

III WHAT FRIENDS CAN DO FOR OLDER PEOPLE 

A. What can we do as a Yearly Meeting of Friends? 

B. What can our Monthly Meeting do? 

C. What can an individual member do?

A. The Yearly Meeting 

In the field of social betterment, the Yearly Meeting through its Social Service Committee or some other special group can assume responsibility for those programs which, we hope, will affect the care given the aged. These might include: 

1. Uniting with others in the community for the development of scientific methods for prevention of disease and promotion of good health among the aged in the spirit and with the zeal with which we participated in the conquest of the diseases of childhood and poverty.

2. Seeing to it that our counties and our state have the facilities for medical treatment, nursing and hospital service, and special institutions required for older people who have become acutely or chronically ill. 

3. Establishing contact with the public agencies for the aged, such as, the old age assistance and insurance services in order to assure a level of performance that will best meet the present and future problems and requirements of older people. 

4. Keeping in touch with public institutions in order that good standards of treatment may be observed and to see that those in them are not neglected. 

5. Encouraging good modern standards and progressive methods in private institutions and boarding homes for the aged. 

6. Having an interest in and concern for the conduct of boarding and nursing homes operated for profit in order that aged persons living in them may not be neglected or exploited.

B. The Monthly Meeting 

1. In the field of more direct responsibility our Monthly 

Meetings can give attention to the questions concerning members which were not thoroughly taken up or answered in the general inquiry of the Social Service Committee. 

These include: (a) Friendly inquiry among members as to the kind of service they require or wish and help in carrying through the practical plan made for the most suitable care whether that is continuance in own home, care in a family home, Friends boarding home, or other congregate home, care in a home for the mentally or chronically ill, nursing home, provision for financial assistance, etc. (b) The extent to which the Monthly Meetings have established cooperation with the medical and social agencies of the community, such as, the family society, the visiting nurse, council of social agencies and other community agencies serving individuals and families.

2. A Monthly Meeting might also help in cooperation with other groups in the community to establish or encourage the establishment of local facilities and services which are lacking and which may be helpful to older people for their welfare, health or therapeutic requirements. These might include, when practical, local, medical and nursing facilities; opportunities for mental hygiene guidance; work activities especially adapted for older people; hobby shops; tinker shops; clubs; social groups; light recreation; book service.

C. The Individual Member 

The individual member can do a number of things for older people. He can: 

1. Express concern for them and see that they receive the care they need--in their own homes, in family homes or in institutional and congregate boarding homes. 

2. Visit them in their own homes, boarding houses, in institutions, in Friends Boarding Homes, etc. 

3. Invite them to his home. 

4. Become acquainted with the public systems established and maintained for those who cannot afford to pay for care. 

5. Visit the county home, the welfare home, etc. 

6. Be concerned for the correction of abuses in public and private institutions, and in commercial boarding and nursing homes for old people. 

7. Assist in the development of good standards of care for older Negro men and women. 

8. Take an interest in the leisure time life, activities and recreation of older people. 

9. Learn to understand older people and be interested in and hear from them about things that interest them or trouble them.

Dear Friend:

Of late years, questions concerning the assistance given to members of Friends Meetings in need of financial aid, boarding home care, and advice, have repeatedly come before several of the Yearly Meeting committees. The changes in the economic system which we are all experiencing, and the rapid rise of the percentage of persons 65 years old and over in the total population and consequently in all probability in our Meetings, bring increasing problems to the attention of every 

Monthly Meeting.

In a desire to be helpful to the Meetings, a committee has been appointed by our Yearly Meeting to inquire into the problems facing the Meetings. 

The names of the members of this committee are listed below. The Yearly Meeting of 1943 passed the following minute and gave it to the Social Service Committee to carry out: "Much interest was shown in the concern of the committee for administering relief in needy cases among Friends. The responsibility for any further study of this problem need not be assumed by the Joseph Jeanes Committee but rests directly on the Yearly Meeting. "The Social Service Committee is encouraged to make a study of the conditions which exist at present among our members who may need financial assistance. The committee should look forward to the possible needs of Friends which may arise as conditions of the world change in the years to In this study the committee should inquire about the methods and ability of each Meeting to care for its own members. The Social Service Committee is empowered to form a committee composed of its own members or others to carry forward this survey." come.

The attached information sheet was drafted by the committee, whose members will appreciate your cooperation and any suggestions you may have of specific problems and ways of meeting them.
