Hand Composition-Slug-Cast 

25 25 25 SR SR SR of HE efficient linotype and monotype machines clicking away in our busy composing rooms of today sound a far cry from the laborious "sticking" type in small sizes that for hundreds of years was identified with typesetting. These wonderful machines have entirely solved the problem of setting small size type in large quantities. They have practically made the daily newspaper with its frequent editions a possibility, instead of a dream of laborious and tedious work.

Until recent years, however, there remained with newspaper and job composing rooms everywhere an other problem which, though closely related, was not solved by either of the composition machines which had thus far been developed. These machines were not efficient or adaptable for setting display type in sizes larger than 14 or 18 point. Foundry type or monotype [7]

sorts cast in the composing room were still set by hand from the cases. We still had with us the old annoyances of broken and worn type, insufficient fonts, picking sorts and other such well-known evils which any system of machine composition does away with entirely.

In the most progressive newspaper and job plants all over the country, however, this has been changed since the advent of the Ludlow. This latest composing room machine combines the art of the hand compositor with the efficiency of a machine. It is the modern method of rapid and economical display composition. 

By the Ludlow system, matrices are set by hand in a specially designed stick, and the slugs are cast on the machine. The matrices are distributed by hand while the slugs are being trimmed type high and ejected. 

The answer to the inevitable question as to why this is quicker or more economical than composition with single foundry types or cast sorts must wait upon a more detailed description of the characteristics of the system.

The matrices for hand composition are kept in orderly arrangement in cases. As each line is distributed as soon as it is cast, there is no necessity for large fonts of matrices and, therefore, the cases are small and compact. 

Up to and including 48 point, the matrices are all of a uniform size regardless of the point size of the type, [8]

except of course that they vary in width according to the requirements of each character. Different sizes may be set to align in a special stick designed for this purpose. 

Above 48 point, for the 60 point and 72 point sizes, a special stick and larger size matrices are used. 

The Ludlow stick is a hollow rectangle marked off in picas. As the matrices are dropped into the stick, casting face down, they rest on the edges of the stick by virtue of flanges on their opposite sides. Spacing is done on much the same principle as in hand composition except that the justification need not be "tight" as a set screw locks the matrices firmly in place and takes up any slight play. After the line of matrices has been thus locked, the stick is immediately placed in the machine, a lever is pressed and a second or two later the stick of matrices may be withdrawn for distribution while the slug is being trimmed and automatically ejected.

The slug itself, whether the size be 12 point or 48 point, is cast in a uniform mold which produces a slug whose shank is of 12 point body. The face and neck of the slug, representing the counter-sunk design in the matrix, overhang the shank on both sides. Thus a 24 point slug would have a shank of 12 point body with 6 points overhanging on either side for the face. This overhang is approximately 11 points thick (the thickness of an electrotype). In making-up, the overhang is sup- [9]

ported by blank slugs which are cast in spare time, or on any other strip casting machine. 

Ludlow slugs are cast either 21 or 22 1/2 picas in length, the former size being more adaptable to job work and the latter to newspaper. When wider mea sures are being set, the slugs are butted in a manner which eliminates any possibility of a❝river." The division is made in a stick, which is long enough to accommodate matrices for two or more slugs, but in casting the break does not necessarily have to occur at the end of a slug. A character may overhang from the first slug to a blank space on the second just large enough to accommodate it (this being automatically determined) and vice versa. 

The machine itself is exceedingly simple, small, and has comparatively few operating parts as contrasted with keyboard and other casting machines. It is no larger or higher than a small flat table. The metal is kept at a uniform temperature by thermostatic control. 

Obviously, the advantages of such a system over foundry type are the advantages that are common to other slug casting systems which compose the smaller sizes of body type. Ease of make-up and lock-up are pre-eminent among these advantages. Non-distribution of type is another prime factor. Small forms of display type can be duplicated easily for printing more than one up by simply casting two slugs from the same line [10]

of set-up matrices and making up two forms. Type can be stored against the possibility of future reprints without depleting type cases, and there can never be any shortage of display type through being tied up with standing jobs.

Using the Ludlow system, a printer may always be assured of typefaces that are clean, new and bright. 

Furthermore, the variety of matrices offered at the present time leaves little to be desired for composing rooms whose clientele demands modern, attractive composition of the quality kind. 

The Ludlow uses the same metal as the linotype, which is an important advantage, as it eliminates maintaining a separate metal supply for this machine. As compared with the use of sorts-cast type for display composition, the Ludlow equipment occupies surprisingly less space, as there is no need of keeping a large reserve of type. Similarly the large investment in metal represented by the sorts in cases and in reserve is elim inated, as the only metal in use is live metal in use in jobs or held standing.

A unique feature of the Ludlow system as contrasted with other slug casting systems is the development of italic faces. Hitherto a non-kerning italic has been the only italic possible with any system employing matrices for casting in sluglines. The Ludlow, however, employs [11]

italic matrices which are slanting and they may be justified in the stick or used in combination with roman by the simple expedient of inserting wedge-shaped spaces before and after the italic characters. By the use of such matrices a full kerning italic may be designed and—what is even more of a boon-there are no kerns to break off. 

The Ludlow system also provides special matrices for cross-rule set-ups that, by a surprisingly simple interlocking principle, eliminate the breaks and insure alignment. Not only that, but a Ludlow ruleform can be set up completely while a compositor working with rules and slugs is still cutting his materials. 

Of course the Ludlow shares the disadvantage of the linotype in that even the slightest correction involves resetting a line. The only other disadvantage that it has been possible to find in the Ludlow system is that the slugs in the larger sizes are apt to have mechanical imperfections. This, however, is a factor that is controlled by the experience and skill of the operator. in The Ludlow is not to be considered as a competitor of the linotype or monotype for setting body type small sizes. It is essentially a machine for setting display type in slugs as against the use of foundry or type produced on sorts casters. In actual operation a number of conditions make it possible for the Ludlow operator to handle the same display composition job a great deal [12]

faster than his fellow compositors using foundry type.

In the first place the matrices are always neatly arranged in the cases, flange side up, so that they may be picked up without turning or fumbling. The uniform size of the matrices makes for speed in handling. The ease of justification is another important element. The use of universal spaces for all sizes is not only a time saver but a space saver. The flanges on the spaces are wider than the flanges on the type matrices, making them easy to pick out when respacing, as contrasted with type spaces which are lower than the type and consequently hard to get at. Almost 100% accuracy assured by the fact that the character represented by each matrix is stamped on the back making it possible to read the line from left to right, in normal reading position. Furthermore, engraved font distinguishing marks make it possible to immediately detect turned characters and wrong fonts. is

Comparative tests have definitely substantiated the claims advanced for the Ludlow system that these advantages make for speed and economy. In competition with foundry type, similar advertisements set on the 

Ludlow have taken from but 1/2 to 1/3 the time, and correction time has been cut down to practically nothing. 

The non-distribution feature, of course, clinches the argument for the Ludlow as against single types. [13]

When compared with hand composition, with types produced on sorts casters, the non-distribution element takes on a different aspect. True enough, sorts-cast type may be dumped after the job is finished, but how many job composition houses dump their sorts in sizes over 18 point? They find it more economical to distribute the larger sizes than to keep casting sorts to replenish the cases; and this casting time item is one that is not to be overlooked. So we come back to virtually the same condition that prevails with foundry type. 

The writer, after a thorough investigation of the Ludlow system and considerable experience with it, is convinced that it deserves consideration from busy composing rooms no matter what their special problems may be. The Ludlow is undoubtedly the closest approach to the adaptation of machinery to artistic hand composition-the modern answer to an age-old problem. [14]
