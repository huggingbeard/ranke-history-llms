THE BECK-LINTHICUM PROHIBITION AMENDMENT. 

The recent debate in the House of Representatives arising in connection with the Beck-Linthicum proposal for a modification of the Eighteenth Amendment clearly shows that marked progress is being made in the movement for relieving the evils springing from the present ineffective attempts to enforce nation-wide prohibition. 

The merits of this specific measure were not at all passed upon by the House, for the vote excluded all consideration thereof or of the form in which any proposed modification should be framed. Action has been now practically remitted to the national conventions and the next Congress. As the wording of such a constitutional amendment is of supreme importance, it may be deemed fitting and of public interest to analyze the modification proposed by Messrs. Beck and Linthicum, and to state what seem to the writer to constitute grave objections to its present form, although the purpose and substance of the proposal, in so far as it intends to vest in the several States control of the liquor traffic within their own borders, is deserving of fullest support. 

The Beck-Linthicum proposal in modification of the Eighteenth Amendment is a composite one. In its first part, it incorporates substantially the language of the conditional recommendation No. 11 contained in the report of the Wickersham Commission, which would confer power on Congress to regulate or to prohibit traffic in, and the manufacture, transportation, importation and 686140

exportation of intoxicating liquors. In its second part, it provides that this federal power "shall not be construed or applied to abridge or deny the right of any State to authorize and regulate the manufacture, sale, transportation, or use of such intoxicating liquors, wholly within the borders of such State", which is in substance the Raskob proposal and which is avowed to be designed to permit each State to "take over complete control of the manufacture", etc., of intoxicating beverages within its own territory. Its third part prohibits the shipment or importation of intoxicating liquors into a dry State in violation of its laws, and embodies with certain omissions the proposal contained in the writer's article entitled "Constitutional Aspects of National Prohibition", wherein modification of the Eighteenth Amendment was urged so as to limit its language and operation to the protection of the Prohibition States against the violation of their laws by the shipment of intoxicating liquors in the course of interstate or foreign commerce. 

The Beck-Linthicum amendment in its totality would vest power in Congress to "regulate or to prohibit" the liquor traffic, and, at the same time, vest or preserve power in each State to "authorize and regulate" such traffic. The field of power, duty and responsibility for regulation would be thus occupied exclusively neither by the Federal Government nor by the States, but would be divided between them in an uncertain and ambiguous manner. It would undoubtedly require an indefinite period of litigation to resolve the questions and doubts which would with reasonable certainty arise thereunder as to the intent and meaning of the language of the pro- 2

posed amendment, as well as the innumerable questions and uncertainties in practically applying such constitutional language to specific statutes and states of fact. 

While thus saving to each State power to "authorize and regulate" the liquor traffic within its borders, the wording of this proposed amendment, under any interpretation, would leave the Federal Government as an exceedingly important, if not indeed a dominating factor, in the situation-exactly how important and how dominating being dependent upon the construction placed upon the language of the amendment by the Supreme Court, the extent of the exercise by Congress of the power conferred, and the action taken by the several States. In any event, the power of Congress would be extensive and far-reaching, and it would in all probability be actively exerted, and not left unexercised or dormant or merely an incidental one. On the one hand, Congress would be under continual political pressure from various interested groups, as was pointed out on behalf of President Hoover in connection with his refusal to approve the similar and conditional recommendation in the Wickersham Report. The vesting of power in Congress to regulate or prohibit would force the wet and dry question into politics every two years in the election of members of Congress and perhaps every four years in our presidential campaigns. On the other hand, few of the dry States, of which it is well to remember there were thirty-three in 1919, would be able to resist the constitutionally offered temptation of avoiding the heavy expense and the duty and responsibility of liquor regulation and law enforcement by shifting this burden, duty 3

and responsibility in whole or in part upon the Federal Government and by calling upon the latter to furnish adequate police forces. It would furthermore be an easy compromise for legislatures and politicians, in States where sentiment was divided, to take no independent affirmative action at all and have no enforcement law (as in New York), but leave the regulations of the Federal Government in sole force and effect with the likelihood that enforcement by federal officers would be no more effective than it is at present. 

In spite of the provisions for home rule or local selfgovernment contained in this proposal, the result of adopting its wording would, in all probability, be to establish a system of exclusively federal police regulation and enforcement in some parts of the country and of concurrent and perhaps conflicting police regulation and enforcement in other parts. Either alternative would present grave evils, and would offer little improvement over present conditions.

The objections to federal enforcement have never been better stated than by ex-Secretary of the Treasury William G. McAdoo, who, although himself an ardent advocate of Prohibition, declared in his address on "Prohibition Enforcement", delivered in Richmond, Virginia,

February 1, 1928 (reprinted in his book entitled "The 

Challenge; Liquor and Lawlessness versus Constitutional 

Government", at pp. 243-288) as follows: "The Federal Government is not equipped to perform the duties of ordinary police enforcement. 

It has not the necessary agencies, nor could it equip itself with them without radically altering its character. Police enforcement, on the other hand, is the

normal and principal function of the state governments . We must start with the proposition that the effectiveness or ineffectiveness of enforcement is in direct proportion to the extent to which the state governments cooperate or fail to cooperate If it (the Federal Government) should undertake directly the task of prohibition enforcement throughout the country it would have to increase its staff of enforcement agents to a veritable army-it would have to post thousands of these officials in the cities and towns and villages of the land and establish a great network of supervising and supporting agencies unlike anything which it has ever attempted before in any field of its activities. The wisdom of such a step is doubtful . . . It might well be represented as threatening the balance of our political system were the Federal Government required to exclusively employ the great number of police agents necessary to enforce the prohibition laws. It would lend weight to the hitherto unwarranted charge of bureaucracy and excessive centralization. It might create a weapon of patronage capable of wide abuse, and which conceivably, in dishonest hands, might aid in the formation of a Federal political machine of sufficient strength to dominate the internal politics of the States."

Surely, experience has demonstrated that the evils of federal enforcement or attempted enforcement are neither eliminated nor essentially alleviated by the existence of a nominal "concurrent" power in the several States, but that the situation is confused and bedevilled by the division of power, duty and responsibility. Further to quote from Mr. McAdoo (at pp. 280-283): "One of the chief obstacles to effective enforcement at the present time is the absence of a clear division of responsibility between the Federal Government and the States. Both are nominally purporting to perform the same task of direct enforce- 5

ment against offenders 

The fact that the

Federal Government is supposed to be enforcing the law tends to lull the state governments into a feeling that there is no need for them to put forth the full measure of effort which they might devote to the task. More and more the States thus rely upon 

Federal authority and feel less and less responsibility for their own part in enforcement. The present situation is a standing invitation to the practice, so fatal to efficiency, popularly known as 'passing the buck' . . . [S]o long as the Federal Government undertakes to duplicate the work of the States and engage through its own agencies in the task of direct police enforcement . . . where the States themselves. are seeking to enforce the law, the Federal Government seems to the average citizen to be taking over state functions. Conflicts and uncertainties are always bound to arise where two sets of agencies are thus seeking to perform the same task, and the result is to create a wholly unnecessary jealousy and suspicion between the two jurisdictions."

The third part of the amendment embodied in the resolution or modification proposed by Messrs. Beck and 

Linthicum is based upon the proposal contained in the writer's article above mentioned; but in the final clause of the amendment which they propose and which reads "any such shipment or importation shall be subject to the laws of the State upon its arrival therein", the concluding language of the writer's draft is omitted, viz., "both before and after delivery to the consignee, whether in the original package of shipment or otherwise". This language was included because of the decisions of the Supreme Court of the United States in the Bowman, Leisy and Rhodes cases (Bowman v. Chicago, etc. Railway Co., 125 U. S. 465; Leisy v. Hardin, 135 U. S. 100; 

Rhodes v. Iowa, 170 U. S. 412). Thus, in the Rhodes

case, the Supreme Court construed the language of the Wilson Act of 1890, "upon arrival in such State”, as meaning not merely arrival in the State, but actual delivery therein to the consignee; and this interpretation in effect nullified the Wilson Law, and permitted the wet States to flood the dry States with shipments of intoxicating liquor. The omission in the Beck-Linthicum proposal of the language specifically guarding against such a result might well cast doubt upon the incidence and scope of other parts of the proposal, and might open the door to the very evils perpetuated by the decision in the Rhodes case and which required the enactment of the Webb-Kenyon Bill to overcome after fifteen years of struggle. Similarly, the language "whether by the postal service or otherwise," which is also omitted in the Beck- Linthicum proposal, was inserted in the writer's draft to cover the possibility of its being held that the power of Congress over the postal service was particular and independent of the power to regulate interstate and foreign commerce. The contemplation of such a possibility in the realm of "judicial statesmanship" is no more unreasonable or technical than the ratio decidendi in Rhodes v. Iowa.

Some of the above objections to the wording of the Beck-Linthicum resolution apply likewise to the Wickersham and Raskob proposals, which are in a sense constituent, though mutually modifying, parts of the former. 

In the case of the conditional Wickersham proposal, indeed, the evils of federal regulation and enforcement would be aggravated by the absence of any provision for home rule or local self-government, or any other re-

straint upon the power of Congress to regulate or prohibit on a nation-wide scale, irrespective of the great diversities of custom, tradition and social and economic conditions obtaining in the various parts of our country. 

The Raskob proposal, on the other hand, while conferring power on each State to establish its own system, leaves the Eighteenth Amendment in its present form unaffected and in force as to all States not taking affirmative action. It would undoubtedly continue federal enforcement or attempts at enforcement in the States that preferred to make no change, and it would continue the present rigid prohibitions of the Eighteenth Amendment permanently in force and effect, beyond the power of Congress in any way to modify, in the District of Columbia, Alaska, Porto Rico, the Virgin Islands, Hawaii, the Philippine Islands and Guam, and, to an indeterminate extent, would constitutionally guarantee the persistence of the very evils of federal ineffective administration or interference and divided responsibility which the present system of enforcement has invited and produced. 

Furthermore, notwithstanding the provision in the Raskob proposal vesting "complete control" in each State of the "importation", as well as of the manufacture, transportation and sale of intoxicating beverages, it would raise a grave question as to the rights of the individual States to regulate importations from foreign countries, the regulation of foreign commerce being a subject hitherto, and rightly, under the exclusive control of the Federal Government.

It is also to be noted that the Beck-Linthicum proposal has been criticized because it contains no express

prohibition of the saloon; but with this criticism the writer cannot agree. The authors of the resolution, learned as they both are in the principles of constitutional law, showed a true respect for and understanding of the nature and scope of our federal system and the Constitution of the United States when they refused to insert any such provision in their draft amendment, as well as a true grasp of the complex problems created by the saloon. Past history has shown that, at the best, the problem of effectively restricting, or even defining, the saloon, is a very difficult one, and that such an institution has to a great degree the power of adapting its form so as to evade the strict terms of any rigid legal definition, leaving the essential evils and vices unabated. 

Thus, in the State of New York, where the law sought to close saloons on Sunday and restrict the sale of liquor on such days to hotels, the only result was the rise of the numerous and notorious "Raines Law hotels", which were hotels in name only, but in reality became centers of vice and prostitution and as such adjuncts to the lower class of saloons. The requirement that no liquor should be served except with meals was generally evaded with similar ease by pretended or perfunctory orders of food. 

In practice, it proved impossible to restrict the sale of liquor to bona fide hotels, serving bona fide meals, as clearly intended by the statute. Broadly to forbid the drinking of liquor on any premises where liquor is sold, moreover, would operate oppressively with respect to all legitimate hotels and restaurants and ultimately result in continuing the many thousands of speakeasies now scattered all over the country. The suggestion, which has

also been made, that hotels and restaurants should not be allowed to sell liquors, but that customers should be permitted to bring their own liquor with them and consume it on the premises, would, in the opinion of the writer, be futile and impracticable, and bring about many forms of subterfuge.

It must be obvious that details of enforcement and definition, such as the above, would be wholly out of place in the Federal Constitution or even in an Act of Congress. The difficulties in the way of effective regulation of the saloon can be dealt with successfully only by careful and competent action on the part of the various States, in accordance with local conditions and practical requirements, and guided by local sentiment and history. 

However desirable the object, the chief effect of a sweeping, nation-wide fiat, prohibiting the "saloon", with all the ambiguity of that term, would be the encouragement and perpetuation of the illicit speakeasy. All thoughtful men and women must agree that whatever its evils, the saloon, especially when highly taxed and competently and honestly regulated (as in England), is preferable to the secret speakeasy with its flaunting of the law, its debauching of youth and its constantly demoralizing influence. 

As will be readily recalled, the Wickersham Commission, in its fifth Conclusion, stated as follows: "The Commission is of opinion that the cooperation of the States is an essential element in the enforcement of the Eighteenth Amendment and the

National Prohibition Act throughout the territory of the United States; that the support of public opinion in the several States is necessary in order to insure such cooperation.” 10

There can be no reasonable doubt that this conclusion is fully supported by the facts. It seems reasonably to follow from this experience that any provision in an amendment to the Constitution of the United States which expressly attempted to prohibit the return of the saloon might be found just as difficult, if not as impossible, of nation-wide enforcement as the Eighteenth Amendment itself because of the lack of public support and cooperation by the States. The multiplication of federal prohibitions which are in their nature essentially matters for local police regulation and which would depend upon State public opinion and cooperation for effective enforcement would only tend to perpetuate the present evils of the speakeasy and hence to prevent any real improvement or reform. The problem of the saloon is of vital importance to the States, and it will have to be effectively solved and regulated by them. 

It is submitted that any modification of or substitute for the Eighteenth Amendment should be so framed as clearly and effectively to afford protection to the Prohibition States against the violation of their laws by shipments in interstate or foreign commerce as well as to restrict and confine the Federal Government within its traditional and legitimate sphere of interstate and foreign commerce. This would make it incumbent upon each 

State, not only to determine its own system of liquor regulation and enforcement, according to local sentiment, needs and public policy, but to enforce its own police. laws within its own borders. It is always highly desirable to foster a local sense of public duty and responsibility both in the enactment and in the enforcement of 11

liquor or other police legislation, and without this sense of responsibility all plans for dealing with such important local governmental problems must inevitably fail. 

It may, in conclusion, be helpful to recall the stand taken by Senator Morrow which was overwhelmingly approved in New Jersey and so largely endorsed throughout the country. Among other things he said: "I bring to you no panacea for this deplorable condition. I know of no magical solution. I cannot, however, stand before my friends and neighbors and ask for their suffrage without having them know my attitude toward the problem. "First: I believe that the way out of the present difficulty is to recognize clearly the fundamental difference between the nature of the Federal Government and the State Government. I believe this involves a repeal of the 18th Amendment and the substitution therefor of an Amendment which will restore to the States the power to determine their policy toward the liquor traffic, and vest in the Federal Government power to give all possible protection and assistance to those States that desire complete prohibition against invasion from the States. that do not. If I should be elected to the United 

States Senate I should be prepared to vote for a resolution submitting to the several States an amendment to the Constitution in any form that would accomplish the foregoing purpose. I do not believe that the fundamental difficulty can be reached by an amendment of the Volstead Act."

The writer has not the slightest doubt that Senator Morrow would not have approved vesting any power in the Federal Government with respect to prohibition enforcement other than that of protecting the Prohibition States against the violation of their laws in and by interstate or foreign commerce. And if it be urged that 12

he advocated repeal of the Eighteenth Amendment and the substitution of a different amendment, instead of modification thereof, the answer is that nomenclature, method and form are alike immaterial when the end sought and the result would be the same. However, it is submitted that tolerant Prohibitionists, without whose help there is little chance of ultimate success in the thirty-six States necessary to obtain due ratification, are much more likely to approve or acquiesce in a modification of the Eighteenth Amendment than in its out-andout repeal with the then possible return of the conditions that led to its adoption.
