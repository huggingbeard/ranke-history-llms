EVERED by men and adored by women, acclaimed by the world of science and virtually deified by the masses, the successful hypnotist enjoys an accolade that is beyond the reach of even great conquerors and the masters of empire.

Nor is it at all difficult to understand how this priceless gaud was cast in the crucibles of human adulation and bestowed upon the modern disciples of such dauntless pioneers as Braid, Char cot and Tucky. The hypnotist, however, can neither be analyzed nor catalogued; in the valley of mankind, he rises like the topmost peak of Everest; his ability to control the human mind and his power to relieve disease-ridden mortals of mental and physical maladies are among the miracles that defy human comprehension. 

Is it any wonder that the hypnotist—yes, even the tyro-is looked upon with awe? Is it any wonder that he or she who watches the master hypnotist perform becomes enamoured of this science and longs to walk in his footsteps?

Nor is it the experience of the authors of this work that such desires are in vain. Time was when men thought that the hypnotist was a man with a certain God-given power that was his and his alone. 

Experience, however, has shown that the power to hypnotize is within the reach of all who are willing to study its phenomena and carry to the altars of the master-hypnotists the lamps of diligence and patience.

What we do know-and what it is important for any rising hypnotist to know-is that there lived in Austria in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries (1733-1815) a man named Franz Anton Mesmer and that he held the world spellbound with his demonstrations of animal magnetism or "mesmerism."

Mesmer was born in Weil, Austria, on May 23, 1733, and after attending common and preparatory schools he went to Vienna to study medicine. While thus engaged, he became so deeply interested in the influence of planetary bodies upon the humans of the earth that he wrote as his graduation thesis a dissertation on magnetism. It was Mesmer's idea that the human hand could exert a similar influence over human bodies, and he conducted a series of

He later attempted to practice as a mesmerist, but the Viennese authorities branded him a charlatan and ordered him to leave the city. His attempt to practice at Spa met with like opposition and he was driven, in 1778, to Paris. Here he gained many disciples and hippodromed mesmerism until a demonstration of it looked like the feature attraction of a three-ring circus. Chroniclers of the time record that Mesmer and his followers held hands as they formed a circle around a group of "magnetic" tubs. These tubs contained sulphide and other ingredients and were provided with iron conductors, the terminals of which were made to contact the hands of the patients by means of a dangling metal ring. It was Mesmer's idea that this arrangement of "magnetic" forces would draw from the bodies of the patients all signs of nervous tension and cause each individual body to be shockproof, healthy and strong. 

Most scientists merely laughed at Mesmer and branded him as a fool. A few, however, felt that mesmerism was the foundation rock upon which a new branch of medicine would be established. One of these was John Elliotson, M.D., and another was

Dr. James Braid, however, was a man of a different turn of mind. A brilliant English surgeon and phrenologist, he did not condemn Mesmer, but he felt that mesmerism was only the shell of a science. THE GIRL WHO WAS HAUNTED

Indeed, it was impossible for her to reason; her mind was paralyzed by fear. At the root of the trouble is the story of "Hansel and Gretel", engraved on the subconsciousness in early childhood.

He walks or rides a block or less from his home, and suddenly he is gripped by a fear that almost kills. The result is that he has to rush home as quickly as possible. Always there is that fear that he will be without a home-an unconscious fear that now rises to consciousness and paralyzes his reasoning powers.
