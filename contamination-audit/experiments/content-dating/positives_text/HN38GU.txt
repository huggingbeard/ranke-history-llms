PREFACE 

Had this little book been published in 1933 it would have been necessary to explain what the Club des Arts Gastronomiques represented and the purpose for which it was organized. Since that time the Club has received much publicity from the Press and has acquired an international reputation in the realm of gastronomy. In a series of articles under captions such as "Dine and Wine Like Royalty" and "Vests of Velvet at Royal Feasts" the affairs of the Club were described by Mr. Padraic King, a Boston journalist well known for his wit and epicurean tastes. Foreign publications have called to the attention of their readers the existence in Boston of a society which promotes an interest in wines and food. 

During the early years of Prohibition a small group who made a hobby of collecting wines would meet occasionally at dinner in order to preserve the oenophilic traditions for which Boston was famous. 

In 1925 when the grim reality of Prohibition had finally convinced certain gourmets that the days of freedom would never return, together with the realization that supplies of wines and spirits were being rapidly depleted, it became the custom to reserve the old stocks for special occasions in order to make them last as long as possible. 

To carry out this purpose, Mr. Sohier Welch and I organized a drinking society known as the Club des Arts Gastronomiques. The titular head of our group was Mr. Philip Dexter whose interest and influence in these matters were largely responsible for the Club's high standards. The first official Club dinner was given February 20, 1925, in honor of Serge Koussevitzky, the new conductor of the Boston Symphony Orchestra. Since that date the Club has flourished and our vintage dinners have been a source of enjoyment and social intercourse for members and guests.

It is not the aim of this book to advise readers what to drink or how to drink. A host of Post-Repeal literati whose knowledge was gleaned mostly from the works of others or whose contacts with wines were limited to a few Paris restaurants have deluged the public with instructions ad infinitum (and it may be said ad nauseam) to the confusion of countless Americans who were receptive to the idea of ex-

PREFACE 

perimenting with wine, but who in despair at the abracadabra of these wine pundits resorted to Gin and Whisky. Owing to the constant urging of friends who have honored me with their company at vintage dinners this edition is published. They emphasized the fact that as I was a co-founder of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques and furthermore a person whose hobby of collecting wine extended over a period of two decades, I might furnish information to those having fewer opportunities in this field and who want particulars regarding wines and spirits.

Wine collecting has been one of my avocations for many years and since Repeal a vocation. In my capacity as wine consultant for an old New England firm of importers it has been most interesting to study the wine business from the point of view of the grower, the purchaser, and the consumer. It is not within the scope of this work to discuss various aspects of the trade. My intention is to present a background of the principal categories of wines and spirits; to describe and illustrate some of the vinous activities of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques, referred to as an "earthly Elysium". Our recommendations may be helpful. However, as in the other fine arts like music, painting, sculpture, etc., experts often disagree in their opinions concerning the merits of wines. Knowledge and appreciation of wine are indications of culture, so let us dine with wine, ever mindful of Baudelaire's admonition- "Tous les buveurs d'eau sont des méchants, C'est bien prouvé par le déluge."

MADEIRA 

It is customary for members of the Club who are still the fortunate possessors of old Madeiras to reserve these rarities for special occasions and to serve them before dinner. No other aperitif can compete with this noble heritage.

Members and guests having arrived at the appointed hour, the host displays his bottle of Madeira. The old container, with or without label, well crusted, hand blown, unusual in shape, is an antique which bears a history as ancient as ancestral portraits. The contents are carefully poured into suitable glasses. Each person in silence communes with his wine. Glasses emptied, comments are exchanged and the Madeira's virtues extolled. Seldom do we receive a poor wine, the host assuring himself beforehand that the quality will pass muster. 

If the Madeira offered for the delectation of the members was a Welsh 

1842, a George IV 1811, a John Hubbard 1780, an Endeavor 1832, all from the cellar of President Welch; or, from the cellar of the writer, a Verdelho 1866, a Brahmin 1824, a Perkins 1816, a Monteiro 1844, or the Nautilus Madeira owned by Professor Morison, it is safe to state that these wines represent the finest types of well preserved pre-phylloxera Madeiras of bottle-age existing in the world today. There are probably no better old Madeiras to be found than among the unique collections belonging to the members of the Club. 

New York, Baltimore, Philadelphia, and Boston were traditional centers of Madeira drinking and there are private cellars in these cities still containing old vintage wines. Last year in New York city,

I was invited to inspect and pass judgment upon a lot of about one hundred bottles dating from 1806, 1811, and 1824. These wines were of historical interest but the average quality was mediocre. I have tasted many old Madeiras from various cellars and my interest in making researches in this field has never been dampened by disappointments. It must be confessed that the majority in existence at this time are distinctly on the down grade due to age and improper storage conditions. Neglect is a principal cause of deterioration. 

Madeiras of any famous vintage year such as 1816, 1834, 1844, 11

Recently I examined and recorked a mixed lot of Madeiras from a private cellar in Boston, salvaging about four dozen bottles, many of which were over one hundred years of age in the original bottles. In my cellar are several types more than one hundred years in the original bottle and their condition is superlative. Mr. Welch also possesses many similar wines. At the end of this chapter are listed some of the great Madeiras which have been served at Club dinners.

Throughout this chapter reference is made to old Madeira. 

What is meant by old? From the standpoint of a Madeira drinker who has the good fortune to own many vintage wines prior to the year 1873, all wines vintaged before that date are old: those of post date, being different in character, do not rate the distinction of being called old. In other words, the year 1873, when the dreaded scourge of the Phylloxera Vastatrix made its entry into the vineyards, is the turning point in the history of the Madeira industry with the result that the wines of the pre-phylloxera period, which we call old, differ intrinsically in character from those of the postphylloxera era. In order to comprehend this situation, familiarity with the entire background of the Madeira trade is essential. 

The island of Madeira was discovered in 1419. A few years later a general conflagration consumed considerable parts of the forests which covered the island, clearing the way for the planting of vines. The volcanic soil of the island together with the rich mixture of wood ash resulting from the fire provided favorable viticultural conditions. The original stocks brought from Spain and 

Portugal were probably of the Malvasia type. Records show the exportation of wines before 1460. Early trade was mostly with the 

West Indies and American Colonies, as the island became an important stopping place for all ships en route from Europe. Every vessel usually carried some casks of Madeira wines in its hold for trading purposes and later it became a practice to ship casks at sea as the long voyages improved and hastened the development of the wine. 

Many old Madeiras are named after the ships which nursed them along to maturity. During the middle of the sixteenth century, the wines of Madeira were in high favor at the court of Francois Premier of France. Shakespeare refers to the good quality of the wine. In

13 1813, more than twenty-two thousand pipes of wine were shipped from the island. War conditions in Europe and the closing of wine ports, together with heavy consumption in the West and East Indies, caused the industry to flourish. During the American Wars, Madeira wines became increasingly popular in England as British officers in the Colonies acquired a familiarity with the wines and upon returning to their homes, introduced the various types. One of the earliest traditions relative to wine drinking in the American Colonies resulted from trade with the island of Madeira. The colonists imported wines in exchange for American oak which was sent to the island. and converted into pipe staves by Madeira shippers. An intimate association developed between the two countries and from the latter part of the eighteenth century until the year of the phylloxera in 1873, all cellars of consequence on the Atlantic seaboard contained quantities of this famous wine. Certain shippers in Funchal still carry in their files original bills of lading to the ancestors of some of America's prominent families. 

An infinitesimal quantity of these old wines is still preserved; wines of special character, refined flavor, with remarkable softness, either dry or sweet, whose exceptional keeping powers have made them antiques today.

These qualities will presumably never be reproduced as the methods employed in making the wines before the phylloxera period differed fundamentally from those of the present day. Formerly 

Madeiras were laid down as vintage wines and preserved as such. 

The general practice now is to treat them on the Solera system as in the case of Sherries. Wines are often designated according to the respective grapes from which made, such as Bual, Sercial, etc., and occasionally the label bears the name of the district; for example, Cama de Lobos or Sao Martinho. 

The important wine districts of the island are as follows: In the Southern part of the island the best vineyards are found in the parishes of Cama de Lobos, San Amaro, San Antonio Campanario. 

In the Funchal basin, Sao Martinho, Sao Roque, Sao Joao, and on the North side, Seical, Sao Vicente, and Porta da Cruz. The principal grapes from which vintage Madeiras were formerly made and now used for Soleras are the Malmsey, Bual, Sercial, and Verdelho; the two latter grapes chiefly used for Soleras owing to their dry and semi-dry characteristics. These are the main types; other varieties

Jesuits imported the finer varieties of vines and the wines from their vineyards (Fajaa dos Padres the most famous) excelled all others.

In 1852 most of the vineyards were destroyed by the Oidium. 

Vines were replaced with sugar cane. No wines were made until 

1863, the vines being replanted in 1860. Two great vintage years were recorded, 1866 and 1868. The phylloxera appeared in 1873 causing immense damage until 1883 when the plague was controlled by grafting with American stocks. Good wines are now made by the Solera process but to repeat my former statement, they differ in character from those of the pre-phylloxera era. 

The best Madeiras are made from the Malmsey grape, producing a sweet luscious wine, almost like a fine liqueur; the Bual grape, also rich and sweet but with a more delicate flavor; the 

Verdelho grape, high quality, oily and delicious, with pronounced bouquet; the Sercial grape, yielding the driest, choicest, with delicate and exquisite bouquet, requiring much age and very rare. Rainwater Madeiras, so-called, formerly of great popularity in America, are blends of pale, delicate, soft wines to which are added 

Palhetino wine.

The vines, planted along trellises, are carried to a height of seven feet and across so as to lie in a horizontal position, thereby absorbing the full warmth of the sun. In February and March the vines are pruned and begin to flower two months later. September is the vintage month, except for the Malmsey which is later. Most of the vineyards are small, each growing several varieties of grapes which generally are crushed together. However, the mostos of the principal grapes, i.e., Bual, Sercial, Verdelho, and Malmsey, are kept separate. The grapes are pressed at the vineyards and the resulting mostos are brought to the wine stores at Funchal for fermentation. The first quality wine, called pingo, is made from the treading and pressing of grapes by bare-legged peasants. Gypsum is added to the must, as in the method of making Sherry. Fermentation lasts about six weeks, after which period brandy (not over three per cent) is added. The new wine is racked and sent to the estufa stores. It is a modern equivalent of the old method of shipping casks at sea in the hold of sailing vessels where the heat and motion were supposed to complete the homogeneity of the wine and amalgamate its component parts. Common wines are placed in the estufas for three months at a temperature of one hundred and forty degrees; intermediate qualities, four months at one hundred and thirty degrees; superior wines for six months at one hundred and ten degrees; the highest class for a longer period at ninety to one hundred degrees. Vinhos do Sol, formerly high quality wines, were heated only by direct action of the sun, but are no longer made, all Madeiras being submitted to the estufa treatment. 

I have tried briefly to describe the methods employed in making Madeira wines and to indicate the fundamental differences in character between what I call the old and the modern wines. It is not my intention to apotheosize the old wines and to infer that post phylloxera Madeiras are not worthy of attention. Excellent wines are available and remarkable Solera stocks have been accumulating during all the years when Madeira drinking went out of fashion. 

The oidium plague causing higher prices due to diminishing stocks, together with the dissolution of the East India Company, also the opening of the Suez Canal, after which time fewer ships stopped at the island, were important factors in the decreasing popularity of Madeira. Sherry was rapidly becoming a substitute and has held sway ever since. Madeira shippers, however, can now offer well blended wines from ancient Soleras at prices in keeping with equivalent qualities in Sherries and Ports.

SHERRY 

There is a Spanish proverb, "Wine that has been cursed is better than holy water." Sherries have been famous the world over for more than three centuries, and the vast bodegas in Jerez, San Lucas, and other localities, contain innumerable Soleras capable of producing countless varieties of flavor. Therefore, if we are to accept the truth of this adage, we must examine the evidence. Certain bodegas in Jerez contain wines whose records of origin have been lost in antiquity and such wines are not found in any other viticultural district of the world. 

Both are

There is a kinship between Sherry and Madeira. made by the Solera system, both are fortified wines, both improve with bottle-age. Their respective processes of vinification differ, and although there is a more extensive blending of wines in the making of Sherry, as well as differences in the species of grapes used, there may be a marked similarity of flavor between the two. This resemblance is noticed particularly in Sherries of great age in bottle. 

At dinners of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques, we have tasted Sherries possessing bottle-age of fifty years or more whose flavors were essentially the same as those of an old pre-phylloxera Madeira. 

The special nutty flavor, which is the rarely found desideratum of Sherry, only develops after years in bottle. Probably one of the best known de luxe Sherries in America is Harvey's Bristol Cream.

Lately I compared the flavor of this wine, a recent importation with only a few months bottle-age, with that of a Bristol Cream bottled in 1880. The difference between the two was amazing. The former, well known to Sherry drinkers, is a rich, full-bodied sweet wine with slight dry finish. The latter was entirely transformed, possessing a far greater concentration of bouquet, a rich mellow dryness and had lost the obvious saccharine quality of the former. A far superior wine in every respect and a most interesting example of what might be called a metamorphosis due to bottle-age.

In my cellar are various Sherries possessing age in bottle up to the half century mark. It has been my policy to put aside a few 19

VINTAGE DINNERS 

bottles from every case purchased and not to open them for at least five years. In this manner, "adde parvum parvo magnus acervus erit," to quote from Ovid, I have been able to build up a reserve of oldbottled Sherries. As a regular consumer of Sherry, it is interesting to sample newly-bottled wines and when a brand is pleasing to the taste and possesses qualities inherent in good breeding and blending, I lay down a small stock which through bottle-age will acquire a marked improvement.

Sherry drinkers who want to enjoy the increased gustatory pleasures derived from consuming old wine should follow this modus. operandi. One's patience will be well rewarded in the short space of three to five years. There are London wine merchants, notably 

John Harvey & Sons, who have adopted this system and from them the Sherry advocate can obtain some wonderful specimens. Naturally, they bring higher prices to cover storage, interest, and other carrying charges. American importers should build up reserves of bottleaged Sherries and Madeiras which eventually would contribute to the prestige of their cellars. 

A word of caution to the owner or purchaser of old-bottled Sherry is pertinent in this chapter. Before serving this kind of Sherry, decant same several hours before drinking. The purpose of decanting is primarily to evaporate or lose the so-called "bottle stink" which sometimes emanates from bottle-aged wines. This "stink" in no way detracts from the quality of the wine-on the contrary, it is proof of age. However, you will enjoy the wine to a greater extent and its bouquet will be enhanced by allowing first of all the "stink" to disappear.

It has been said that Sherry is the most useful of wines and that the trade can provide flavors to suit every taste. This is quite true. 

An entire scale or complete alphabet of flavors from the light dry Finos to the rich, sweet Olorosos or dark browns are made in a multiplicity of types. No dinner of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques would be complete without some sort of Sherry. We usually drink 

Sherry before dinner, unless an old Madeira is offered, in which case, the Sherry is reserved for the soup course. A rich type may be served at the end of a meal. Generally speaking, there are no rules. for Sherry drinking. It can be enjoyed with practically any course or appreciated by itself at any hour of the day. It is the easiest and quickest medium for the offering of hospitality, and, in my opinion,

I have previously stated that Sherry is a fortified wine. However, there are few Sherry drinkers who comprehend the significance of this fact, or who understand the complicated processes required to make their favorite brand. It is fitting now to outline briefly the manner in which Sherry is made, also where it is made. It is a common error to assume that all Spanish wines are Sherry. There are many natural wines, both red and white, produced in Spain. Some of the red wines can be of such high quality as to compete on even terms with many of the "crus classés" of the Medoc. I have in mind a Rioja served at a Club des Arts Gastronomiques dinner which was certainly a close runner-up to a good Premier Cru. The white wines generally are heavier, richer types, and lack the breeding and delicacy of their northern brethren of Bordeaux, Burgundy, or Germany.

Sherry as known in England and the United States is a blended and fortified wine, the product of a very complex method of vinification called the Solera system. Practically all the Sherry shipped from Spain is made in the districts of San Lucas de Barrameda, Jerez, and Puerto de Santa Maria.

The San Lucas district is famous principally for types of Sherry known as Manzanilla. The vintage is usually two weeks earlier than that of Jerez and lasts about sixteen days. The grapes after being picked are spread on mats and dried in the sun for twentyfour hours (forty-eight hours in Jerez) and the pressing is done in the evening to avoid the excessive heat of the day, which might precipitate the fermentation of the must. The pressing takes place at the vineyards and the must is sent in butts to the bodegas for fermentation in fresh casks. As in Jerez, the must of Manzanilla wines is sprinkled with yeso. The fermentation lasts until February. In April the wine is racked from its lees and fortified with grape brandy; dosage one to two per cent. It is now used to replenish the lowest scale of the Solera. The Manzanilla wines mature more rapidly than those of Jerez, ten years being the extreme age of the bulk of their Soleras. It takes about four years to develop the aromatic floral bouquet and bitter flavor typical of the best Manzanillas. The fragrance and freshness of many of these wines resemble those of certain Rhine wines. The Manzanillas are made

from the Listan grape. Some of the famous vineyards of the district are those of Santo Domingo, Torre Breva, La Punta del Aquila and Miraflores la Baja. The Manzanillas are not generally known in the United States, which is a pity, because these pale, delicate, dry wines have a great deal of character and breed and can be used to advantage in many ways.

A region about twelve miles long and ten miles wide with the town of Jerez in the center is the heart of the Sherry trade. The best vineyards are located northwest of Jerez in the districts of Carrascal and Macharnudo; some eminent vineyards of the former are those of Amoroso, Romano, Ducha and Vina de la Paz; of the latter, Majuelo, A. B., Matamoros, and Almocaden are prominent. 

West of Jerez are the well known vineyards of Cerro de Obregon, San Julian, Tula, Leon, and Las Canas. There are other notable vineyards east and northeast of Jerez. They are supposed to be the most ancient as reference is made to them by Jusuf the Arab in his account of the siege of Jerez in the year 1285 A.D.

The principal grape used for the best Sherry is the Palomino; others are the Mantuo Castellano, Vidueno, Mantuo de Pila, and the Pedro Ximenez (the latter for a special wine of the same name).

The best soil of the district is called Albariza, a white clay consisting of lime and chalk. This chalk content is responsible for the ultimate delicacy and quality of the wine. The Albariza soil is similar to that of the Champagne district and the Grande Champagne of Cognac. 

It seems to be a viticultural fact that the vine is at its best in sterile soil. Anyone who has seen the Riesling vine thriving along the terraces of the Mosel river where the soil is mostly chunks of slate will not confute this statement. Soils of lesser quality are called 

Barros and the poorest Arenas.

The Jerez vintage opens during the first week of September and lasts for one month, during which time the vineyards are picked over two or three times. The grapes are spread on mats to dry in the sun for forty-eight hours, then placed in wooden troughs called lagars and crushed by bare-legged chaps, the pisadores, who dance upon them until all the expressed juice has flowed out of the lagar into vats. Tiradores are the lads who heap the grapes, after the crushing, into a large cone around a screw in the middle of the lagar. 

The cone is surrounded with bands of esparto and by means of this screw pressed until the last drops of juice have been extracted. The

VINTAGE DINNERS 

23 must is taken from the vineyards to the bodegas in Jerez for fermentation.

A most important feature of Sherry vinification takes place at the time the grapes are crushed. The marc is sprinkled with yeso or gypsum (calcium sulphate). It is a traditional custom called plastering and has a chemical importance in that the high sugar content of the must may contain a shortage of acids. The gypsum increases the tartaric acid in solution, thereby encouraging good ferments. It probably also favors the development of the wine flower-more is said about this phenomenon later. 

The primary fermentation lasts about three months and the wine is racked. A tasting at this time is made to determine the probable future characteristics of the wines. The wines are now called 

Mostos and are classified into three groups, indicated on the butts by means of marks or strokes— Raya (one stroke)—the most delicate, with breed and aroma. Receives small dose of brandy, one percent, i.e., one to two gallons to a butt of approximately one hundred and eight gallons. 

Dos Rayas (two strokes)—wines of second quality. Receive larger dose of grape spirit, three gallons; they are big wines of 

Oloroso type and may be kept apart as vintage wines. 

Bastos-other wines of same type, but coarser and lacking flavor. Good for blending. Redondos and Abocados milder types. 

Tres Rayas (three strokes)-sound wines lacking bouquet. For cheap Sherries and heavily fortified.

After the first racking, as described, the casks are left on ullage, i.e., never filled to the top, but exposed to the air. The wine enters into a period of secondary fermentation and during the early stages the mycodermi vini or wine flowers make their appearance. In this short article it is impossible to describe the biological or chemical reactions of these bacteria whose influences are of such importance in establishing the ultimate Sherry type. It suffices to say that these micro-organisms establish the character of the Sherry and affect its final taste and aroma. For those who are interested in further research pertaining to this subject, Pasteur's "Etudes sur le Vinaigre et sur le Vin" will provide abundant material. His experiments on

VINTAGE DINNERS 

the wines of Arbois, especially the famous Chateau Chalons, which under normal conditions will produce the same flowering as in Sherry, lead to important conclusions relative to the value of this particular phase of the Sherry's vinification.

The Fino Sherries which flower the most will develop the greatest dryness and nutty aroma, whereas the Olorosos, due to a higher brandy content, usually flower the least and lack the delicacy of the former. The flowering lasts about eighteen months and the wines are racked again. They cease to be called Mostos and now become Vinos de Anada. A fresh classification of the partially finished wines is made in the following categories:

Palmas-these are the Fino Sherries; driest and lightest in body; with age the best develop into Amontillados. 

Palo Cortado-less dry, more body, medium wines such as Amorosos and lesser Olorosos.

Rayas-highest quality of rich wines; deeper color, mellower; types of Golden Sherries, Brown and East Indian.

There are many categories of inferior wines such as the Bastos, Redondos, Entre-Finos, Abocados, etc., but why bother with them. 

There are four classes of wines made in Jerez: 

1. Natural wines, requiring about five years to mature and seldom found in the United States. 

2. Vintage wines—also rare, from special vineyards. Some of the vintage wines of Dos Rayas type are kept for twelve years for future blending with Oloroso Soleras. Vintage wines receive small dosage (one percent) of grape spirit. 

3. Soleras to be explained in detail further on in this chapter. 

4. Blended wines-these comprise the bulk of the Sherry trade and there are countless types. 

I find that most people have a very hazy conception of the term Solera. Wine merchants themselves are apt to err in their understanding of its significance and it is a one hundred to one bet that Sherries labelled Solera this or that are erroneously named. I shall attempt to describe briefly the process which makes a Solera. We have now followed the course of the wine from the time that the first

VINTAGE DINNERS 

25 fermentation produced the various Mostos and the secondary fermentation, associated with the flowering of the wine, resulted in bringing forth the Vinos de Anada. It must be emphasized that this entire operation is unique in the making of what we call Sherry wine and that in no other viticultural region is this process employed; also that wines made from the same grapes and from the same vineyards will develop entirely different characteristics in their respective casks. This is one of the unsolved mysteries of fermentation. The wines are now sent to the Criadera or nursery for further maturation until ready for blending with the young wine of their Soleras. 

What is a Solera? The Solera is a scale, or a system, of building up young wines on the foundation of older ones in order to maintain a definite standard of quality. Actually wines of different ages but of the same character are grouped together in such a manner that the stocks are never depleted. The casks of a single Solera are arranged in tiers, the oldest wine at the bottom, the youngest on top. When a certain amount is withdrawn from the bottom cask, it is replaced by the same amount from the next cask higher in the tier, and that one from the next above, and so on throughout the entire scale. Careful attention is paid to wines replenishing a Solera, thus each Solera has its Criadera where wines are matured until their types are definitely established before introduction to the Solera system itself. Wines are often kept for twelve years before entering a Solera. Their qualities should be delicate with vinosity, aromatic yet soft, free from pungent flavors, and well aged. It must be realized, therefore, that a Solera represents an expensive investment and that a Sherry shipper to be in this business must own tremendous stocks as every Fino, Amontillado, Oloroso, etc., has its own. definite Criadera and Solera. A Solera is the stock in trade of a merchant and he only parts with it for high prices. In Jerez there are Soleras whose basic or mother wine dates back two centuries. 

Thus a true Solera is seldom obtained commercially and when a bottled is labelled Solera of any year, it means that an infinitesimal proportion of that wine has been drawn from the mother wine of that particular year.

There are other kinds of wines used in the making of Sherry. 

A sweet wine, called Pedro Ximenez, or P. X., from the grape of the same name, is employed in small quantities to give softness and roundness to young or crude wines. It is expensive and very

VINTAGE DINNERS 

sweet, more like a cordial, as the grapes are dried for two weeks until they reach a raisin-like condition before pressing. The extracted juice is placed in butts containing brandy (six to seven gallons of grape spirit to a butt of one hundred and eight gallons) to prevent fermentation so that all the sugar is retained. Even many 

Amontillados receive a dosage of P. X., perhaps only one percent, as it will impart to dry wines a softness only acquired after five or six years in bottle. P. X. also has its own Solera system. 

Vino de Color, used for sweetening but more to impart color to young, undeveloped wines, is made of nine parts of white wine Mosto and one part Arrope. Arrope is made by boiling for sixteen to eighteen hours unfermented must until reduced to one-fifth of original amount. It becomes a deep brown saccharine fluid. It is added to the Mosto which ferments in the regular way, but the finished product is of low alcoholic strength and sweet. Sometimes

Pajarete is used for darkening rich Sherries. It consist of Vino de 

Color blended with P. X.

Let us now analyze the component parts of commercial Sherry. 

Young Solera wines of several types are blended and given character by introducing to the mixture a small percentage of an old Solera. 

A small amount of Vino Dulce is added to subdue the pungency of the older wine and a touch of Vino de Color to improve the flavor and to apply the desired tint. After proper amalgamation, the wine is ready for shipment.

In addition to San Lucas and Jerez, there are other districts of lesser importance. Rota, northwest of Puerto, produces a wellknown wine called Tintilla made from a variety of red grape. The grapes, separated from their stalks, are exposed to the sun for eight days, and placed in butts with an addition of Vino de Color. The fermentation lasts six weeks, producing a deep colored wine. 

Chiclana and Chipionia are other districts. Between Seville and

CHAMPAGNE 

There are few places in Europe of greater historical and archaeological interest than the City of Reims. The story of monarchy in France is closely interwoven with the history of Reims and yet factors, overlooked by historians, which contributed to the centralization in this place of the early monarchical, ecclesiastical, and cultural growth of France were the local vineyards. This statement may cause my readers to smile, but I will point out the economic importance of the vineyards and the prominent part they played in the development of the community. 

The wines of Gallia Narboniensis were celebrated by Roman writers prior to the Christian era and had it not been for Domitian's Edict which for nearly two hundred years prevented the culture of the vine in Gallic territory, the vinous memorabilia of Reims would have been that much older in richness and tradition. However, the enlightened Emperor Probus in 282 A.D. revoked the edict and granted permission to cultivate vines. At the end of the fourth century, the district was well mantled with flourishing vineyards. 

These soon became objects of envy and aroused the cupidity of barbarian tribes along the border. Reims was successively invaded by the Alemanni, the Vandals, and Attila with his hordes. The attraction of the vineyards was a principal cause of these invasions and not until the Alemanni were destroyed by Clovis in 486 A.D. was the spread of viticulture on a more permanent basis. When 

Christianity became the established religion of the Roman Empire, grape culture increased throughout Gaul and it is interesting to note (W. C. T. U. and others of same ilk) that viticulture and Christianity were closely associated and grew side by side. There was a retardation during the rule of the Merovingian Kings when a state of barbarism existed. During the seventh century, conditions were much improved. It was the period of the establishment of abbeys such as Hautvillers, and Epernay. We owe a great debt of gratitude to the good monks who showed a proper solicitude for their vines and who tended them with infinite care. The vineyards in due course of time were largely controlled by religious denominations. 28

29 Many of the famous European vineyards owe their origin to the church and are steeped in ecclesiastical traditions. 

Under the rule of the Carolingians much progress was made in the cultivation of the grape, particularly the making of wine in the Champagne district. At this epoch the distinction is made between the wines of the river of the Marne and wines of the mountain of Reims. If any person deserves a posthumous award of the green, yellow, and red ribbon of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques, it is the great Charlemagne. He was probably the first to differentiate in the making of ordinary and Auslese wines, and he is accredited with planting the original vines at Rudesheim. 

The era of coronations greatly increased the popularity of Champagne wines. The tenth century was a bad period for the vintners, internal strife and foreign invasions raising havoc with the vineyards, while Reims and Epernay were sacked many times. The eleventh century brought prosperity to the vine growers. The schools of Reims, coronations, ecclesiastical councils, etc., caused Reims to become such a center of activity that it was called Little Rome. The crusades of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries played an important part in the development of the vineyards. Crusaders made large gifts of land to the monasteries of Reims which increased their vineyard holdings.

As previously remarked, the vineyards were of great economic importance. They were the most valued possessions of the church; the principal source of revenue, through levies, for municipalities. 

The growth and culture of France was directly influenced by the merits of her wines and especially is this true in the relationship of 

Reims with its neighboring vineyards.

Champagne as we know it today, i.e., a sparkling wine, was probably first made in the latter part of the seventeenth century. 

Before that time, peasant, knight, and prelate drank still wines. To trace the history of Champagne through all the centuries until the discoveries of Dom Perignon and Chaptal would require a monumental work. Suffice it to say that time increased the importance of this product. The gay parties of Louis XIV and those of the Regency as well as the royal repasts of Louis XV drew European attention to Champagne. What is modern Champagne? It is an effervescent wine made by special process to produce a balanced product. We are concerned only with first zone Champagnes, as all the finest wines are made from grapes grown in this territory. This district is sub-divided into two main divisions: a. vineyards in vicinity of Epernay, called vineyards of the river. b. vineyards in vicinity of Reims, called vineyards of the mountain. 

The best Champagnes are made from wines blended together whose grapes, both black and white, are grown in various districts of the first zone, each district supposedly imparting certain qualities which harmonize to produce a perfect entity. Briefly, Champagne is made by the following operations. The vintage is usually at the end of September or early October, white grapes two weeks later than black. Grapes gathered with precaution and carefully cleaned. Juice of first pressing only used for best

31 wines. Fermentation period in casks about six months. During this period wine is racked three times. Assemblage is blending of same districts after first racking. Coupage is blending of different growths after second or third racking. Here lies the ability and experience of the firm to maintain an approximate standard of flavor for its different brands by the selection of various qualities of good growths. After the operation of Coupage, the must should show the proper percentage of alcohol, degree of acidity, tannin, dry extracts, etc. Otherwise, the blend is toned up with stocks of older wines. In a good vintage year, when the must possesses the correct balance of its various elements, little wine of old stocks is added. 

A vintage Champagne generally indicates that most of the cuvée consists of wines of that year only, although a small proportion of old is usually added. The wine is bottled in the spring following the harvest. Liqueur de tirage is added, a syrup which causes a secondary fermentation within the bottle and which gives the wine its sparkling character. The amount of sugar added for this operation is approximately four grams to a litre of wine, which will give a pressure equal to one atmosphere (15 pounds per square inch). 

Champagne bottles are made to withstand a pressure of six atmospheres, so that the percentage of breakage during the period of secondary fermentation is negligible. The bottles are now stacked on their sides during this fermentation process for three to four years. 

Next they are "mise sur pointes," i.e., placed in special racks, bottle neck down at an angle of thirty degrees. The operation called "remuage" follows, consisting of oscillations to which the bottles are subjected to bring all the sediment into the neck of the bottle to be deposited upon the cork. The last process, "dégorgement," whereby the sediment is removed, the final corking completed, and, most important, the "liqueur d'expédition" added. It is this liqueur which imparts the degree of dryness or sweetness to differentiate types of Champagne such as Brut, Sec, Demi-Sec, etc. This liqueur is a dosage of old brandy wine combined with syrup of sugar cane. It would be presumptuous to attempt in a single chapter of this treatise to enumerate or describe all the wines from "le vignoble du monde". A legion of able scribes, authors, and publicists have recorded all the wine-producing districts of France, vineyards of note, vintage years and other relevant bits of information; rather would I consider this article a narration of personal experiences and indicate some of the wines which have left delightful memories. It is doubtful whether any wine collector, regardless of his means, could acquire specimens from each individual classified vineyard of the Bordeaux district not to mention all the great Burgundies and other sections of lesser importance. The oenophile ambitious to taste every Bordeaux wine has a life-long task. Literally hundreds of good Crus Bourgeois Supérieurs can be found in Bordeaux, but few ever reach the United States. A notable exception is the Chateau Paveil de Luze.

Since the decree of 1855 the five categories of Crus Classés of Médoc red wines have received no official change of rating. The original classification in most cases bears an actual present day relationship to the respective merits of these wines. Other red wines of the Médoc are classified in ascending order as Crus Paysans, Crus 

Artisans, Crus Bourgeois Ordinaires, Bons Bourgeois, Bourgeois 

Supérieurs.

The latter supposedly rank in quality below the five 

Grands Crus Classés. Occasionally certain Crus Bourgeois Superieurs are equal in quality to some of the fourth and fifth Crus Classés.

One of the most prominent of the Crus Bourgeois Supérieurs

VINTAGE DINNERS 

is the Chateau Paveil, in the ownership of Monsieur Alfred de Luze, head of the well known Bordeaux wine firm. Chateau Paveil de

Luze, as it is now named, is a very old vineyard situated in the Commune of Soussans close by the famous Chateau Margaux. The 

Chateau was constructed during different periods, the oldest section dating back to the seventeenth century, and was originally a pavilion or sort of one-story tower which gave the Chateau its name- 

Paveil being derived from pavilion.

During the eighteenth century a Monsieur Bretonneau was the owner. His son, an officer of the king, married the daughter of the 

Chevalier de Rauzan. This youth, always in financial straits owing to the expense of maintaining his military equipment in the time of the Seven Years War, lost his property. The Bretonneau family disappears during the French Revolution and there are no records of the property for this period. In 1820 under the reign of 

Louis XVIII a Monsieur Minvielle arrives at St. Nazaire from Porto 

Rico where he had amassed a considerable fortune. Placing his worldly goods upon mules, he sets out for Bordeaux but on the way bandits relieve him of part of his fortune. However, the remainder enables him to purchase the Chateau Paveil which he cultivates until his death in 1861. From his heirs, in 1862 Baron Alfred de 

Luze, the grandfather of the present head of the Bordeaux firm, purchases the property.

Three generations of the de Luze family have successively owned and cultivated this vineyard, bringing to it their viticultural experience and knowledge so that today the quality of the wine from its vines ranks with many of the Grands Crus Classés. The best qualities inherent in a fine Médoc are found in this wine, viz., a beautiful ruby color, light body with refinement, a finesse of bouquet possessing delicacy and distinction. 

For persons who prefer a light-bodied soft wine, it is an excellent Claret. Although Chateau Paveil is a bourgeois by decree, its breed ranks with nobility.

In connection with this wine it is interesting to observe the role played by tradition which has enabled lovers of Claret to obtain this charming wine. For three successive generations a distinguished 

Boston family controlling one of the largest and oldest American importing houses has been directly associated with three successive generations of the de Luze family of Bordeaux. In the course of

Before leaving the red wines of Médoc and Bourgogne, a few words on our domestic reds are pertinent. Vineyards cannot be planted, cultivated, the grapes from same fermented, properly vinified, and the wine matured to produce a sound palatable wine in the space of a year. American vintners after Repeal made the mistake of trying to earn a quick dollar by mass production of wines similar

VINTAGE DINNERS 

to the manufacturing of automobiles. Wines were "aged" in a few months by processes including all the tricks of the laboratory from chemicals to electricity. The output, of course, was terrible stuff unfit for consumption; net result, a large public eager to experiment with wines disillusioned and reverting to liquors. At present the trend is towards domestic wines of better quality. Good wines can be purchased, but our costs are still too high. Imported wines of superior quality are obtainable at relatively lower prices. 

At the Club's eleventh anniversary dinner, February 1936, a

Pre-Prohibition American wine was offered as proof of our native ability to make a domestic wine first class in every respect. It was by name a 1917 Zinfandel, Mountain Claret, Castle Rock Vineyard,

Napa County, California. The vineyard formerly was operated by 

Mr. Rudolph Jordan, class of 1886 Harvard. This wine was tried against a Chateau Latour 1929 and showed up remarkably well. On the occasion of its fiftieth reunion, the Harvard Class of 1886 drank the 1917 Mountain Claret. If in the past American growers could turn out a wine with lasting properties such as this Mountain Claret exhibited after nineteen years in bottle, there must be a successful future for our vineyards if their owners will adhere honestly and strictly to the accepted and time proven principles governing viticulture and vinification.

This chapter under the plea of brevity can not include a description of the remaining French districts. We have enjoyed the wines of Saumur and Vouvray. A sparkling white wine which deserves a gold medal is the 1904 Vouvray bottled by P. Fleury. A few remaining bottles which I own are treasured. Throughout Prohibition this delicious wine carried off many honors at dinners of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques. Fleury's 1915 Vouvray is a worthy successor. 

With regret our thesis on French wines is brought to a close; a topic with so many ramifications that to do it justice tomes should be written to give fitting recognition to the products of the world's greatest vineyard-La Belle France.

GERMAN WINES 

If you believe in the adage that clouds have silver linings, then the clouds of the recent economic depression have had gilded linings for American oenophiles of Post Repeal with respect to German wines. 

Let me explain. Prior to Prohibition, practically all German wines consumed in the United States were of regional varieties such as ordinary Hochheimers, Niersteiners, Rudesheimers, etc. They were light, dry wines (a great many sour ones) of no particular character. 

Many suffered the indignity of Hock and Seltzer and they deserved no better fate. Rarely would a bottle with the words "Auslese" or "Original Abfullung" appear on a label, and I doubt whether the significance of these marks would have been understood. 

These high-grade wines were virtually unobtainable outside of Germany and even within their native borders seldom found in the trade. Wines of Auslese and Beeren-Auslese quality found their way into the cellars of the aristocracy and rich merchants able to pay the high prices which the wines brought. Since the economic crisis of 1923 in Germany, these wines can be bought in small quantities. 

Today in England and in the United States a few importers offer Auslese wines from the famous vineyards of the Rhine and Mosel districts. There are many wine drinkers of considerable training who until recently have had few experiences in tasting, let us say, a great Auslese from the Rheingau of the 1921 vintage. Those who have are compelled to revise their ratings of the world's viticultural products because in the category of white wines, the German wines are head and shoulders above those of any other country. Their superiority from the standpoint of bouquet, body, and flavor places them in a separate class. No other wine can approach the overpowering richness and magnificence of a topnotch Trocken-Beerenauslese from the Rheingau, or compare with the brilliant vinosity of a superlative Beeren-Auslese from a blue ribbon vineyard of the Mosel. I have yet to find a wine drinker who after tasting a Rhine or Mosel of high rank does not willingly and whole heartedly ascribe to its virtues a dominance over all other white wines. 58

59 Since Repeal a new field has been opened to Americans, and the abundant variety and never tiring charms of the German wines offer many avenues for exploration. There has been a marked yearly increase in the consumption of German wines during the past three years and their popularity is spreading rapidly. The amazing varieties of flavor to be found in these wines adapt themselves to the American palate. Refreshing, vigorous, and clean, they may be dry or sweet in flavor, light or heavy in body, flowery and aromatic in bouquet, and they harmonize with all foods, are easily assimilated, and require no formality in serving, so necessary with red wines. 

The best German wines are expensive. Most people do not understand why a bottle should cost so much because the word Auslese is inscribed upon the label. With typical thoroughness, German vintners use a distinctive terminology in describing their wines. 

It is opportune to comment upon these terms. The following definitions may be helpful to purchasers of German wines. These terms: are governed strictly by law and guarantee to the consumer pure natural wines.

Auslese-wine made from selected bunches of fully ripe grapes. 

Damaged or inferior grapes are removed resulting in a superior wine, but necessarily quite expensive. 

Beeren-Auslese-made from single grapes, each selected for its uniform ripeness and condition. Generally the best grapes. from the Auslese bunches. Involves much labor, producing a small quantity and usually very expensive. 

Spatlese-made from grapes gathered at the end of the harvest. In general a heavy-bodied and rich type of wine.

Trocken-Beeren-Auslese-Perhaps once or twice in a decade, exceptional climatic conditions will allow the postponement of the vintage until late October or even early November.

The grapes will have acquired an over-ripe raisin-like condition producing an incredibly rich wine. Only infinitesimal quantities can be made and the prices which they command are truly fabulous. In Europe these wines. are reserved for great occasions and are considered royal gifts. It is impossible to exaggerate the magnificence and overpowering majesty of body, flavor, and bouquet which these wines combine, and the experience of tasting one of them leaves an indelible impression of pure delight. No

vineyards of the world other than those of Germany can yield such sumptuous wines.

Wachstum or Gewachs-Few German vineyards as in Burgundy are under single ownership. Certain proprietors bottle their wines, others do not. Likewise, the cultivation and care of the vines in different parts of the same vineyard will vary depending upon the owner's interest in his property. 

Wachstum followed by a proper name indicates the growth or section of a vineyard of the particular proprietor from whose grapes the wine was made.

Original Abfullung or Kellerabzug-A wine bottled by the proprietor at his estate. It is the equivalent of chateau bottling in the Médoc.

My readers might deduce from the preceding chapters that the members of the Club des Arts Gastronomiques are interested only in wines and that to suggest the drinking of alcohol would arouse a feeling of disdain. In these days, one seldom encounters such spirits except in cellars of men who have made a hobby of collecting over a period of years. Considering the tremendous annual production of liquors in the United States, the British Empire, and France, as compared with the much smaller output in wines, it sounds paradoxical, yet true, nevertheless, to state that it is far more difficult to find and to acquire rarities in distillates than in wines. One can buy the best Clarets, Burgundies, or Rhines at moderate prices with little effort, and there are plenty of kinds from which to choose, but a great deal of research is necessary and, if successful, very costly to purchase really fine brandies, rums, or whiskies.

The Charente vineyards are ancient. Documents exist showing that wines were exported to England from La Rochelle in the early fourteenth century. Prior to that period, the wines had quite a reputation in far away Denmark and Norway. Philip III, King of France, in 1260 reminds the King of England that payment is due to the town of La Rochelle for wines previously delivered to Henry III. In the early seventeenth century, the wines of the Borderies were those in favor. Dutch traders plied a heavy traffic in the wines of the Charente for distribution throughout the northern countries. The demand became so great that new vineyards had to be planted and the present great vineyards along the left bank of the Charente owe their origin to these conditions which existed in approximately 1620. A few years later, overproduction caused an abundance of stocks which could not be sold. In order to preserve unsalable wines for future markets, the idea was conceived to distill wines, thereby conserving them in reduced volumes. The approximate date of distilling Charente wines into brandies was in the year 1630. This method failed to dispose of surplus stocks; meanwhile, the new brandies were ageing in casks awaiting buyers. It was in this manner that discovery was made of the remarkable improvement in quality of the spirits due to age in wood. A transformation had taken place,

79 and by process of oxidation and evaporation, the raw, crude spirits had acquired color, taste and aroma. An era of prosperity followed with few vicissitudes until 1875 when the phylloxera plague ravaged the district and exterminated most of the vineyards. Three years later, after many experiments, the vineyards were replanted with American stocks grafted to the European species. The hybrid developed successfully for this purpose was the Vinifera X Berlandieri 41 B. Cognac experts claim that there is practically no change in character between post and pre-phylloxera Cognacs. 

The Folle Blanche, St. Emilion, and Colombar are the principal grapes used in making high-grade brandies; others of lesser importance are the Balzac Noir, Le Jurancon, Balzac Blanc. The 

Folle Blanche, which closely resembles certain grapes of the Rhine, and the St. Emilion, mixed in a proportion of twenty-five percent for the former and seventy-five percent for the latter, produce the finest Cognacs.

A clear understanding of the process of distillation is necessary to appreciate the fact that the making of good brandy is an art. The harvest, held about the first of October, is over by the end of November. Distillation begins early in the spring and is a double process. 

This important fact, namely, a double distillation, is not generally realized. In the Cognac district, the traditional pot still is the type of apparatus employed by nearly all distillers. The first distillation is called "brouillis". The alcohol in the wine, together with other substances, is extracted and redistilled to obtain a higher concentration. The operation of brouillis takes about eight hours and the amount distilled is equal to about one-third of the original contents. 

The strength varies from twenty to twenty-five degrees of alcohol. 

The brouillis is then redistilled. This process, called the "bonne chauffe" is a form of regulated rectification and this operation must be considered in three phases. The first product of the bonne chauffe is called the "produit de tête" consisting of ethers, aldehydes, etc., and is of high alcoholic strength (eighty-four degrees). The second liquid called "produit de coeur" contains the finest alcohol and is the real Cognac. When the alcohometer registers about fifty degrees, the distillation of the coeur is completed and the last product, called "produit de queue", is obtained from the remaining spirits. There are no hard and fast rules or exact timing to separate the three distilling phases of the bonne chauffe, and to know just when to begin

VINTAGE DINNERS 

and to end the extraction of the produit de coeur is the distiller's art. 

The coeur is placed in oak casks for ageing. 

Let us assume that the brandy was made from wine suitable for the purpose and that the distiller obtained a perfect extraction of the coeur. It is just as important to select new clean casks, preferably made of oak, from the forests of Limousin for the period of ageing as any of the preceding steps. Only in wood does brandy develop its quality. The process of oxidation, evaporation, and absorption of tannin, quercin, and quercetin from the cask causes a series of complex reactions producing the flavor and special bouquet of the spirit. Unlike whisky, which increases in strength in cask, brandy gradually loses its strength.

The amount of ageing necessary to develop the best characteristics of brandy cannot be determined with any precision; at least twenty-five to thirty years and not more than sixty years in wood. 

It has been conclusively and irrefutably proven that brandy will not improve in bottle. Many times have friends produced bottles, proudly proclaiming that they were forty years old or more, inherited from fathers or grandfathers. When I inquire as to probable age in wood before bottling, the question is treated as an irrelevant matter- "Oh," says the host, "this is a vintage of 1848 and I've had it for years in my cellar." The proof of quality, however, is the liquid itself, and usually the so-called 1848 is a young brandy toned down with some older stocks and judiciously doctored with burnt sugar and prune juice. I repeat that it is a certainty, and every producer of brandy with whom I have discussed this subject will corroborate my statement, that brandy will not improve in bottle.

The quality of brandy depends upon soil, climate, cultivation of the vine, distillation, and ageing. As conditions vary each year, no two brandies are produced similar in every respect. In order to provide the trade and drinking public with a constant flavor year after year, the practice of blending is carried out in a manner resembling the making of cuvées in Champagne or the Solera system in Sherry. All brandies when newly-distilled are white. Custom requires coloring (caramel) and young brandies are softened in flavor by addition of prune juice, sugar, etc. Pure Vintage Cognacs of age as previously stated are difficult to find owing to the system of blending. Merchants do not care to part with these rare treasures as they are needed to improve the younger spirits. Peasants either

81 sell their new stocks to the large brandy houses or keep for their personal use unblended vintages which are passed on as heirlooms or better yet reserved for dowries.

Since Repeal there have been practically no pure vintage brandies available in the United States. It is not an exaggeration to say that considering the price, the 1900 Cognac offered by the S. S. Pierce 

Company is the best commercial vintage brandy at this time in America. My readers may consider these words an expression of professional bias or a motive for a brazen attempt at salesmanship. 

I have been very particular all through this document not to allow business interests to influence my opinions; otherwise, I would be defeating the purpose of this work, i.e., to present a brief graphic picture of my oenophilic experiences during two decades mainly unrelated to my new vocation of the past three years. My remarks on the 1900 Cognac, endorsed by other experts, are made simply to designate categorically a perfect example of a vintage brandy. 

How to drink brandy is a subject well covered by other writers. 

It is not pertinent to discuss in this treatise the hygienic or gastronomic values of brandy, but Cognac "c'est l'âme du vin" and the dégustation of a pure old spirit will produce on the olfactory nerves sensations which from physiological and aesthetical points of view are unequalled in any other manner.

It is difficult to cover the subject of Whisky in a few words, but this pamphlet would be incomplete without some reference to alcoholic beverages. In ancient Egypt five thousand years ago the brewing of ale was an important industry. With the growth of the Roman 

Empire, wine replaced ale as the beverage of civilization. In England malt liquor again took precedence when Rome declined. The conquest of Britain by the Saxons and later by the Danes and Normans brought few changes in the drinking customs of the country. 

Drunkenness was an honorable pursuit through the medium of mead and ale. Old King John, who died of excesses, in his Magna Charta provided standard measurements for the sale of wine and ale. The spread of inns throughout England coupled with the brewing efforts of the monasteries increased the popularity and consumption of the national drink. Some wine was consumed in "Merrie England”, probably from the juice of the apple rather than of the grape. About 

1154 when Henry II married his French lady, Bordeaux wines were brought to England. Later the wine merchants or vintners were incorporated by Henry VI in 1437 and held royal charters until James the First.

In early times the brewing of ale was a household occupation performed by women, hence the term Ale-wife. So long as it remained a domestic industry there was little interference upon the part of the Government or local authorities. Gradually this human enterprise, the object of St. Adrian's patronage, passed into the hands of men. Guilds were formed to conduct the business but it was not until 1445 that the brewers received their first royal charter from Henry VI and from that moment brewing became big business. As in the case of all sizeable commercial undertakings, the Government

During many centuries ale was the universal beverage of the English. Ingredients necessary for the making of this drink were considered national commodities to the extent that malt, corn, etc., could not be exported without royal permission. Heavy drinking was in vogue among all classes and we learn that good Queen Elizabeth in traditional fashion enjoyed daily at breakfast her full quart of strong ale which she referred to as "an excellent wash".

Drunkenness was a virtue evidenced by an epitaph in the churchyard of Winchester Cathedral which reads, "A gallant soldier never is forgot

Perhaps the ale consumed by these jolly fellows in olden times was purer than the stuff which is adulterated and dosed with chemicals under modern conditions; nevertheless, the law imposed restrictions as to quality and strength. The first Licensing Act in 1552 marks an important date when local jurisdiction was passed to a national control and attempts were made to suppress the growth of ale-houses. 

With a population of less than five million people, there were over 13,000 licensed establishments. From the reign of Edward VI (15471553) to James I (1603-1625) the prevalence of inebriation was on a national scale. Parliament was engaged in passing restrictive legislation with little effect. The Act of Charles I placing penalties upon licensees for permitting drunkenness together with several other Acts

It must be remembered that the Great Protector, Cromwell, was a brewer and in spite of represssions under his regime, like all politicians, he had an eye on his purse.

The drinking habits were worse than ever during the Restoration (1660-1685). Englishmen had been contented to use ale as a principal intoxicant until the Revolutionary period of the seventeenth century. The practice of drinking brandy had spread in 

Europe at the close of the fifteenth century. Brandy was probably introduced into England by troops which acquired the taste of spirits while campaigning in the Low Countries. Eventually the influence of distilled spirits resulted in petitions to Parliament to prohibit the importation of brandy as interfering with the consumption of homemade products. We note the introduction of the Excise system with the advent of spirituous liquors. The first imposition of duties about 

1643 may be said to have changed the drinking mores of the nation with far-reaching consequences. The early imposts were levied on ale, beer and cider, but additional taxes were heaped upon coffee and tea by Charles II. The following reigns of James II and William III 

found excises upon nearly all beverages together with extra customs duties. Needless to say smuggling, corruption, and frauds comparable to our own bootleg days were practiced on a large scale. The abolition of feudal dues replaced by taxes on ale further aggravated a deplorable situation. The climax arrived when Parliament to provoke France prohibited all importations of brandy. With a tax of three d. per quart on ale between 1690-1692, the output of London breweries dropped over thirty percent, at the same time the Government was encouraging home distillation from native grains. The masses of people naturally turned from wholesome ale burdened with heavy duties to the cheap substitutes in form of liquors. England of today can thank its monarch, William III, for converting the nation from drinkers of healthy malt beverages to addicts of gin. 

In his book, "Drinking Customs of Old England", Mr. Hackwood describes the "gin fever" which debauched the morals of the nation for several decades. The Gin Act of 1736 was an attempt to ameliorate the conditions of evil and crime resulting from excessive gin drinking. In London one could buy gin so cheaply that signs

A parallel can be drawn between gin-sodden England of the eighteenth century and twentieth century Prohibition in America. In both cases blundering legislation increased the consumption of spirits, developed a disregard for law and linked the criminal elements with politics. There has been no complete recovery from the effects of the gin policy established by England's legislators of the seventeenth century. Many of our drinking conventions are of English origin. Our pioneers drank pretty crude stuff. Whisky was never aged by the maker until after the Civil War. The expenses of this conflict made it necessary to raise additional revenues, with the result that bonded warehouses were established to enable a more accurate collection of taxes. This new system, incidentally, allowed the ageing of stocks. The conflict between the distilling industry and government in England had its counterpart in our country. The Whisky Rebellion of 1791 in Pennsylvania may not have been carried to the same extremes as the Gin Riots of 1736 in London, but the causes and effects of both were not dissimilar.

To define Whisky is a problem. Scotch Whisky as a straight beverage was introduced into England about 1825. It was made from barley malt and produced by the simple pot still method of distillation. Pure Whisky should be made from barley malt alone, insisting upon the barley being a native-grown cereal; kilned by peat fire; the wort after proper fermentation the resulting beer distilled by the pot still method. The process of making Scotch is as follows. The barley is passed through screens to eliminate inferior grains. Let it be understood that imperfect cereals should never be used. In order to produce good Whisky, the best grains must be selected. The barley after being cleansed is soaked in water and then scattered over the malting floor where it sprouts. During the period of germination the starch is partly converted into sugar by the action of a ferment called diastase. Next the malted barley is kilned or dried. In the making of Highland Scotch the malt is dried by peat fires which impart a smoky flavor to the ultimate liquid after distillation. The dried malt is ground and passed into the mashing tubs. Stirred with water, preferably from peat marshes, the temperature of the mash is raised sufficiently (not over one hundred and forty degrees Fahrenheit) to convert the remaining starch into sugar. The liquid, now called the wort, is cooled and run into fermentation vats. Yeast is added at this stage. The wort after fermentation is known as the wash or beer which in turn is distilled. 

The first distillation of the beer produces the low wines. These are redistilled into what are termed the high wines and feints. The feints, similar in nature to the dunder of rum making, are returned to the low wines for subsequent distillation. The high wines are placed in sherry casks. Here they mature and after suitable ageing are blended to become what we know as Whisky.

87 I cannot enter into a detailed or technical discussion of Whisky distillation. Suffice it to say that many factors must be emphasized. 

The selection of the grain, kind of peat, nature of water, type and shape of still, location of distillery, local atmospheric conditions, methods of distillation, ageing, and finally blending, each and all combined affect the final product. In the making of commercial 

Whisky, the methods of distillation and blending are of vital importance. I believe that the pot still will turn out a finer spirit than the rectified Whisky of the patent still. Blending consists of a proper mixture of Highland, Lowland, and Grain spirits. These ingredients vary in proportion to the desired blend. There are only about one hundred and twenty-two distilleries including those of the Lowland, Highland, Campbeltown and Islay districts, whereas there are thousands of brands offered on the market. The answer is blending. 

Irish Whisky, like brandy, is the product of a triple distillation (double for Scotch). Compared with Scotch there are fewer blends. 

Irish is made either from malted barley or part malt and other unmalted cereals. It requires longer age in wood than Scotch. A straight Irish made of barley malt and aged twenty years in wood can develop ethereal qualities and delicacy of flavor difficult to duplicate.

The Whisky trade has been accused of "opening door after door to commercial vandalism". It is true as well as unfortunate that the Royal Commission on Whisky aided and abetted these conditions by legally stretching the definition of Whisky so to eliminate practically all specifications which heretofore had distinguished the intrinsic character of Whisky.

In America the situation is no better. The old fashioned method of distillation by means of the pot still has been supplanted by the rectification process. In England a single syndicate or holding company controls over eighty per cent of the liquor distilling industry; in the United States two companies hold a virtual monopoly of the trade. People drink what is handed out to them with little thought or care of the ingredients used in their favorite brands or the methods by which their beverages are made. High pressure advertising can cover a multitude of sins. 

American Whiskies are of two varieties, corn and rye with added malt. The F. A. A. regulations number five define the types of whisky and prescribe how they shall be designated. However, the public

little realizes what the congeneric or allied principles are in Whisky or how to select a type which will be the least injurious to the human system. There is much difference between a pot still Whisky wellaged in oak and the rectified, neutral-spirit blended stuff which the trade is chiefly making at present. Let us consider the former. When the fermented mash is ready for distillation it contains many associated alcohols. The principal one is ethyl alcohol and the more important others are amyl, butyl, and propl. These alcohols together with about fifty substances are the main constituents of Whisky. All these ingredients exist at the moment of distillation but it is the process of ageing that changes the balance or proportion of the various substances which in turn give Whisky its flavor and character. In the process of oxidation in cask volatile products or ethers are formed, esters and acids developed. The undesirable aldehydes which exist in small amounts after distillation decrease with age in wood. In other words, ageing produces combination of substances, development of ingredients, which change and improve the aroma, taste, and character of the distillate. In rectified Whisky blended with neutral spirits many of the so-called impurities which impart to Whisky its delightful features are lost during rectification. 

The Whisky collector within the next few years will be confronted with a dearth of aged spirits if by age we mean Whiskies comparable to the present sixteen to eighteen year old bottled-in-bond types. It will not be long ere they become rarities, obtainable only at fancy prices.

RUM 

Rum, a potable spirit distilled either from the fermented molasses of sugar cane or from the fermented sugar cane juice itself, is one of the best known and oldest of liquors. Generations ago when

VINTAGE DINNERS 

the early settlers of New England due to the rigours of the climate and the hardships of life required fortifying and stimulating beverages, Rum was the most popular and beneficial drink. In the eighteenth century the Rum industry was well established in New England and a flourishing business was carried on between the West Indies and in particular the States of Massachusetts, Connecticut, the Rhode Island. The Rum trade played an important part in the economic and social life of early New England. Molasses imported from the 

West Indies was converted into Rum in the many New England distilleries not only for local consumption but large exports of Rum were sent to the African coast in exchange for slaves who were transported to the West Indies for labor.

Today this traditional beverage is becoming more and more popular among discriminating consumers of fine distillates. From the highly fragrant Rums of Jamaica to the lighter and more delicate types of New England Rums, there is a wide range of flavors to meet every demand. A well-aged New England Rum can be served as a cordial whereas for a punch or similar purpose there is no substitute for a high-grade Jamaican Rum. Other West Indian islands, notably those of St. Crois, Antigua, Grenada, Barbados, and Haiti, produce very fine Rums.

It was a very warm day for the time of year and the guests lingered on until well into the late afternoon. As the sun began to set it was realized that our thirst was still unquenched. For our edification the Commodore made a mint julep and it was agreed that no person was better qualified to offer a recipe than the Commodore who was well versed in the lore of the South pertaining to this delicious beverage. 

Recipe for Mint Julep by Gerard B. Lambert

Using a silver mug (very important in order to obtain condition of proper frosting) place one-half teaspoonful of granulated sugar in the mug. Add enough water to make a paste. Grind fresh mint leaves into paste. Fill mug up to the top with finely scraped ice. Add Bourbon, pouring it through the ice. Stir with spoon until mug is frosted. Add sprigs of fresh mint.
