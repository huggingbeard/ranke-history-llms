FOREWORD 

These are true sketches of "Maestro," Principal of Casa Blanca School, and his interesting work in this foreign settlement. 

The qualities which I have noted in Mexicans are, their appreciation of kindness, love and appreciation of color and rhythm, devotion to family, and deep spirituality. 

The Author.

MEXICAN MINIATURE 

Enrique loved to "skeep" to the entrancing music in the kindergarten. He liked, too, to sing the songs which the gay little Maestra taught.

He did both with abandon. He also enjoyed drawing pictures, his subjects ranging from a large "piojo," which he depicted perched on Salvador's hair, to a gorgeous western sunset done in crayolas. There were large opportunities to study each from life. 

Enrique would enlarge upon the life in the home. How "de papa drink de vino," distending his round little stomach, in imitation of "de papa,' and how "de mama make de tortilla," patting and working with his small brown hands the imaginary staff of Mexican life.

Nature talks, too, were a joy, and Enrique, with much gesture, told the Maestra how "de leetle ant he build heem a house," and also, naively suiting the action to the word, how "de beeg peeg fa' down on de groun' and speel de lettle peeg on de grass!"

Time to close the period abruptly and play games. 

Enrique was of the earth-earthly. Consuela, the next younger of the sizable Ortiz flock, was spiritual. She, too, loved to sing and to "skeep,' but with more restraint than Enrique. She dearly loved the Maestra, ever terming her "bonita," and once she felt for her "leetle weengs," which 32

Often in the mornings, before the settlement school convened, Consuela might be seen, leading the very old grandmother to mass. A striking picture! Tiny shabby Consuela, with her great serious brown eyes fixed on the jagged rim of the mountains, and the grandmother, in long, rusty, black garments-the ever-present mantilla on her white hair-feebly tottering toward the church.

There they would kneel, with what thoughts, the limited vocabulary of Consuela might never express, but one could fancy those of the old Señora, who had seen much of birth, life—death, and for whom little now remained but the blessed hope of the Hereafter.

Life is inscrutable. The aged Señora lives on, day by day growing more feeble. Now sitting long hours in the sun, on the bare little plot, where rests the squalid hut of the family of Ortiz. 

No longer does Consuela lead the Señora to mass, for despite the efforts of the free clinic and the ministration of the nurses at the rambling county hospital, Consuela, at last, lay in state before the altar of the crude yet churchly church. Tall candles shed a glow over the little body. The sweet voice no longer sings the gay songs of the kindergarten nor the stately sacred music of the Church. 

The wistful eyes are forever closed. Consuela "skeeps" in fields Elysian. The happiness of her days was furnished by the "Maestra," whose efforts were not in vain.
