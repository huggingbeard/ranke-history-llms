PROPOSITION: Resolved, That a substitute for trial by jury should be adopted. (The following suggestive brief was prepared for the Debaters Research by L. W. Courtney and P. E. Teeling.)

BRIEF I. 

II. 

INTRODUCTION The origin and history of trial by jury are as follows: 

A. The exact origin of trial by jury is not agreed upon by all who have studied the subject. 

B. It is agreed that trial by jury does not owe its existence to any positive act of law.

C. In its early stages the jury was composed of men who knew the facts in the case as well as the customary laws. 

D. Another stage in the development of trial by jury was the use of men who admittedly knew nothing of the facts or the law in the case.

E. In America the right of trial by jury is guaranteed in the 

Federal Constitution and in practically all our state constitutions.

F. The present interest in trial by jury arises from the fact that many attacks are being made on the system today. 

The following terms require definition: 

A. "Trial by jury," as generally understood today, is a trial by a body of men selected according to law, impaneled and sworn to try any matter of fact, and render a verdict according to the evidence legally adduced and the law as instructed by the judge.

B. By a "substitute" is meant any method of trial which dispenses with the present system of trial by jury; it means "putting in the place of," not a change or a modification.

III. The following matter is irrelevant: 

A. Any question dealing with the constitutionality of the proposition is irrelevant.

IV. It is admitted that: 

A. There are defects in the present system of trial by jury.

B. The purpose of trial by jury or by any substitute for the present system is the highest efficiency in the administration of justice. 

The affirmative contends that:

A. The defects in the present system of trial by jury are such that a substitute is necessary. 

B. Some substitute for the present system of trial by jury would promote a higher efficiency in the administration of justice. 

VI. The negative contends that:

A. The defects in the present system of trial by jury are not such that a substitute is necessary. 

B. Some substitute, that proposed by the affirmative, for the present system of trial by jury would not promote a higher efficiency in the administration of justice. 

VII. The issues in this discussion are: 

A. Are the defects in the present system of trial by jury such that a substitute is necessary?

B. Would some substitute, that proposed by the affirmative, for the present system of trial by jury promote a higher efficiency in the administration of justice? 

BRIEF PROPER AFFIRMATIVE 

A substitute for trial by jury should be adopted, because I.

The defects in the present system of trial by jury are such that a substitute is necessary, for A. The average juror under our present system of trial by jury is incompetent, for 

1. There are few legal qualifications for jury service. for a. Only the following qualifications are generally required, that the juror be: (1). Twenty-one years of age, (2). Citizen of the county, (3). Sound in mind, (4). Of good moral character,

(5). Able to read and write, (6). Neither under indictment nor formerly convicted of a felony, (7). A qualified voter. b. Jurors are not usually challenged on the above points. 

2. The trained citizenry is largely exempt, for a. The following classes of persons are usually exempt: (1). Persons over sixty years of age, (2). Civil officers of the state and of the United States, 

(3). Overseers of roads, 

(4). Ministers, (5). Physicians, (6). Attorneys, (7). Publishers of newspapers, (8). Schoolmasters, (9). Druggists, (10). Undertakers, (11). Telegraph operators, (12). Railway station agents, (13). Ferrymen, (14). Millers, (15). Presidents, vice presidents, conductors, and gineers of railroad companies, (16). Members of the militia, (17). Firemen, 3. In practice, jury service is not compulsory, for en- a. Those summoned for jury service are not generally penalized for non-appearance. b. Those who appear may be excused upon various pretenses. 

4. He is incapable of judging correctly disputed questions of facts, for a. There are many technical rules of evidence which the juror is called upon to apply. b. The average juror has had no technical training in the rules of evidence. c. Multiplicity of facts and arguments of counsel confuse the average juror. 

5. He is not qualified to decide criminal cases, for a. In criminal cases the juror must apply rules of law to findings of facts, for

(1). The jury must render either a verdict of "guilty" or "not guilty." (2). In deciding the question of the guilt of the defendant, the juror must apply the rules of law to the findings of facts. b. The average juror cannot discriminatingly apply rules of law to findings of fact, for (1). He has practically no knowledge of the technical rules of law. (2). A thorough knowledge of the rules of law is essential to their proper application.

B. The present system of trial by jury causes great waste, for 

1. It is a waste of time in the administration of justice, for a. Much time is required to select a jury, for (1). A jury list of all the qualified jurors of the county must be prepared. (2). The jurors for the term of court and for each week of the term must be selected from the jury list, (3). The veniremen must be summoned. (4). The jury for particular cases must be selected from the summoned jurors. (5). It is frequently necessary to summon a special venire, for (a). Objection by the counsels may be made on many grounds. b. Much time is lost by the veniremen who serve as jurors, for (1). No real service is rendered until after the trial jury is impaneled. 

2. There is much waste from the purely economic viewpoint, for a. The machinery used in selecting the jury requires much time of the paid officials. b. Much time is lost by the veniremen who are excused, or dismissed, for (1). They have to report to the court and remain idle until they are properly excused or dismissed. c. Time is lost by all the veniremen from their private business.

d. The loss is also great for the litigants, for (1). They must pay for the time lost by their employed counsels.

The following substitute, which the affirmative proposes-that five expert jurors, selected according to provisions of law, be substituted for the present system of trial by jury-would promote a higher efficiency in the administration of justice, for A. It would guarantee competent jurors, for 

1. The legal qualifications of the juror under this plan would require: a. That he have legal training, b. That he be of mature age, c. That he be of good moral character. 

2. The expert juror would receive an adequate salary for his services. 

3. The expert juror's mind would not be distracted by other duties, for a. Hearing and judging evidence would be his vocation, not his avocation. 

4. The expert juror would be capable of judging disputed questions of fact, for a. There are many technical rules of evidence which must be applied in judging facts. b. The expert juror would be trained in technical rules of evidence. c. The multiplicity of facts and arguments of counsel would not confuse the expert juror. 

5. The expert juror would be qualified to decide criminal cases, for a. In criminal cases he must apply rules of law to findings of fact, for (1). He must render either a verdict of “guilty" or "not guilty" in each case. (2). In deciding the questions of the guilt of the defendant, rules of law must be applied to findings of fact. b. He would be able to apply intelligently rules of law to findings of fact, for (1) He would have a technical knowledge of rules of law and their application.

B. It would eliminate the waste caused by the present system, for 1. There would be no delay in the administration of justice, for a. The time necessary to secure the trial jury under the old system would be saved, for (1). The jury is already selected. 

2. It would eliminate the economic waste, for a. It would save the time of the public officials who have to select the jury. b. It would save the time lost by the veniremen excused or dismissed. c. Time would not be lost from private business, for (1). The business of the expert juror is the trial of cases. d. The expenses of the litigants would be reduced, for (1). The time required of the council would be reduced. I.

CONCLUSION 

Since the defects in the present system of trial by jury are such that a substitute is necessary, and II. Since the substitute proposed by the affirmative would promote a higher efficiency in the administration of justice, we conclude that a substitute for trial by jury should be adopted. I.

NEGATIVE 

A substitute for trial by jury should not be adopted, because The defects in the present system of trial by jury are not such that a substitute is necessary, for A. The defects are not inherent in trial by jury, for 

1. The intellectual status of the average juror can be raised, for a. The qualifications for jury service can be changed by legislation, for (1). The present qualifications for jury service are fixed by statute. b. Many exemptions now allowed may be abolished, for .

(1). In the case of educators, ministers, persons over sixty, presidents, vice presidents, and engineers of railroads, and other classes exemptions are not essential, for (a). It is not essential that members of these classes be on duty every day. c. The laws which compel jury service can be strictly enforced, for (1). Fines for contempt of court orders can be assessed. 

2. The argument that trial by jury is defective because the average juror is incapable of judging disputed questions of fact is fallacious, for a. The average juror is incapable of judging disputed questions of fact due to the technical rules of evidence. b. The technical rules of evidence which the jury is called upon to apply are not essential, for (1). The judge may be given full freedom in directing the jury in considering and weighing the evidence, c. Judgment of disputed questions of fact rests ultimately upon personal opinion, for (1). Even appellate judges differ in judgment of disputed questions of fact.

B. The fact that jurors have to apply rules of law to findings of fact in criminal cases is a merit rather than a defect in our system of trial by jury, for 1. The average juror interprets facts and applies the rules of law by common sense rather than by technicalities, for a. Unlike the judge, he is not bound by technical precedents. 

2. In criminal cases it is better that rules of law be applied to findings of fact by common sense than by technical precedents, for a. In criminal cases each case should be decided upon its own merits.

C. In the present system of trial by jury there is no real waste in the administration of justice, for 1. When any specific case is called for trial, the material for the trial jury is ready at hand, for a. The venire is selected before the case is called.

b. In case a special venire is summoned no time is wasted, for (1). The proper official is instructed to bring in any qualified jurors at once.

D. There is no waste from the economic viewpoint, for 

1. The time required of the official in selecting the venire and the time consumed by the veniremen who are not used on the trial jury should not be considered wasted, for a. These steps are necessary in the promotion of justice, and b. The cost in dollars should not be weighed against justice. 

The substitute that five expert jurors, selected according to law, be substituted for the present system of trial by jury-would not promote a higher efficiency in the admministration of justice, for A. It would not necessarily guarantee competent jurors, for 

1. Qualifications for expert jurors cannot be fixed by statute, for a. Legal training does not guarantee competency in an expert juror, for (1). The juror determines only questions of fact. (2). Legal training is not necessary in determining questions of fact, for (a). The judge may have full freedom in directing the juror in considering and weighing the evidence. b. Maturity in years does not guarantee that a man will be a competent juror. c. The requirement of good moral character by law is not a requirement in fact, for (1). A man's reputation is not an infallable index to his character. d. Qualifications other than legal training, maturity of age, and moral character are essential for an expert juror, for (1). An expert is one who has special skill in the performance of a given task, and (2). Skill in jury service cannot be fixed by statute, for (a). Skill is a matter of experience.

B. Defects inhere in the substitute proposed, for 

1. It does not provide for selectivity, for

a. There is no method by which an admittedly prejudiced juror may be eliminated. b. In some instances it might require a juror to become a witness, for (1). It is not impossible that an expert juror should be a witness to a crime or a trespass. 

2. Difficulties would arise in the case of new trials, for a. In case of a new trial, due to admission of improper evidence, the same expert jurors would have to decide the case, and b. Improper evidence heard on a previous trial would fluence their decision.

CONCLUSION 

I. Since the defects in the present system of trial by jury are not such that a substitute is necessary, and II. Since the substitute proposed by the affirmative would not promote a higher efficiency of justice, we conclude that a substitute for trial by jury should not be adopted. 

NOTE: 

By the phrase "applying rules of law to findings of fact" is meant the final process by which a case on trial is decided. The judge in his charge to the jury sets out the rules of law which govern the case. The jury, with the Court's charge as its measure, determines its verdict from the evidence. In arriving at the verdict the jury must necessarily interpret the Court's charge and the evidence, and by applying both reach its general conclusion of "guilty" or "not guilty" in a criminal case, or "for the plaintiff" or "for the defendant" in a civil case.

In some jurisdictions upon the request of either party in a civil suit, the verdict of the jury may be in the form of answers to direct questions of disputed facts. In such cases the instructions of the 

Court include only such matters as definitions of terms or burden of proof.

THE RIGHT OF TRIAL BY JURY IN CIVIL CASES SHOULD BE ABOLISHED By The late O. L. Stribling, Attorney-at-Law 

(Reprinted by special permission) During the past decade, there has been a constantly growing sentiment, not only among the masses of the people, but within the ranks of the legal profession, favoring radical reforms in our court procedure, rendering more simple, speedy and certain the adjudication of the personal and property rights of litigants.

The sentiment has become so strong in recent years that the Bar Associations of the several states of the Union, at each annual meeting spend much of their time in discussing proper and necessary reforms in the Court procedure of their respective States. 

This feeling of dissatisfaction, with present conditions as to the administration of our laws through the courts, as I said before, is not confined to Texas, but is widespread.

It finds strong expression in a recent report made by the chairman of the committee appointed by the "New York County Bar Association," charged with the duty of making inquiry into the proper reform of law and procedure in New York State, in which report, made on behalf of the entire committee, he says: “A system which requires us to look back into past centuries for legal guide is wrong. 

We are older and should be wiser than our great grandfathers who knew nothing of existing conditions and never dreamed of our growth and progress." Proceeding further, he says: "The people are entitled to know their rights and duties within a reasonable time, with reasonable certainty and at a reasonable expense. Any system which produces such unnatural results as the present one is fundamentally wrong.' 

In this connection I desire to state that I believe that the right of trial by jury in criminal cases should remain inviolate. 

Without undertaking to discuss at length the reasons for this, I will say in passing that I believe the issues of life and liberty should be left as near as is consistent with good government, in the hands of the masses of the people. It is a matter that concerns the community as a whole, while the determination of personal and property rights under the form of civil suits is in nearly every instance a matter of individual interest, and should be determined largely by the rule of mathematical certainty, valued in dollars and cents; while in determining the rights of life and liberty, a broad catholic spirit should prevail the tribunal sitting in judgment upon the person charged with crime, tempering upon all proper occasions its judgment with mercy.

It has long been recognized both by the State and Federal Courts that the provisions of the Federal Constitution reserving the right of trial by jury, only apply to trials in the Federal Courts, and that the several States have the right to regulate, restrict or abolish the same in the State Courts.

It is also familiar knowledge, that it is not a right protected by the Fourteenth Amendment of the Federal Constitution. Upon dis cussing the nature of trial by jury as given by the Federal Constitution, the Supreme Court of the United States in a case reported in 190 U. S. Rep., holds: “The right of trial by jury is not a fundamental right which goes wherever the jurisdiction of the United States extends. But concerns merely a method of procedure." ...

Our Courts are established for the purpose of adjudicating the property and personal rights of the citizens according to the prescribed rules of law, and this should be accomplished without any unnecessary delay or expense to the litigants.

Will it be contended that a trial by jury is essential to the accomplishment of this purpose? Experience teaches us the contrary. 

Under our present system, many civil cases are tried before the Court without a jury and proper judgments rendered on the law and the facts.

It will not be denied that the jury system as a part of our judicial machinery is unwieldy and cumbersome. From the time of the selection of the Jury Commissioners to the rendition of a verdict, it is hedged about with inconveniences, obstacles, delays, expense, speculation, and disappointment. I am speaking now of conditions and not theories.

The first delay comes upon calling the regular panel summoned to serve as jurors for the week for the purpose of organizing the jury. Usually about one-fourth of the panel will be absent, then the sheriff must either bring in the absentees or summon a sufficient number of qualified persons to make up the requisite number. This done, the Court proceeds to hear under oath any reasonable excuse of a juror for not serving at that time. Most of these excuses are found reasonable, and doubtless properly so, and the jurors are released and the proceedings are further delayed by the necessity of bringing in more jurors to take their places. 

After having the case submitted to them, under consideration for a day or more, the jury may report to the Court their inability to reach a verdict, and the Court discharges them from further duty in that case, and then it is, that the judge, jurors, parties and frequently the attorneys, all experience a feeling of disappointment and disgust at a system which not only makes possible but probable such a useless waste of time and money in the attempt to settle controversies through the Courts under the forms of law.

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

It is true that this does not occur in a large per cent of the civil cases tried before a jury, and that satisfactory results are often obtained in jury trials, but it serves to emphasize the serious defects in the present system and to inspire every unselfish citizen, lawyer and layman alike, if possible, to find a remedy. . 

It may be said that much of the dissatisfaction resulting from trial by jury may be attributable to the fact that the personal equation as to the parties and their attorneys enters largely into the selection of jurors, which also necessarily involves the bias or prejudice of each individual juror. In addition to this the average intelligence of a majority of nearly every jury as it is constituted under our present system is below the standard of experience and education necessary to a comprehensive understanding of the issues of fact to be determined from the mass of testimony presented, and such jurors must, of necessity, look to the few of the better informed members of the jury for guidance in this as well as in a proper understanding of the charge of the Court, and its application to the facts as found by them. 

I am not mindful of the criticisms I invite from certain quarters in taking this view of the question. There is a large per cent of the members of the legal profession who honestly believe in the jury system as the best mode that can be devised for the determination of disputed facts in the trial of civil cases; and there are a few who profess to believe that the jury is their client's only salvation from incompetent and favor-seeking judges. 

But I have yet to find one of these who, having an abiding faith in justness of his cause, both upon the law and facts, is not willing 'to waive a jury and submit his case to the court for decision. 

It may be urged by some that this is not an argument against the jury system in civil cases, but only furnishes a reason for repealing the law making the exemptions, and a more strict enforcement of the present law as to those not exempt. 

In reply I think it can be said, that the fact the Legislature has deemed it necessary to exempt the classes mentioned, is proof that the State recognizes that jury service is not compatible with the business and professional interest of a large class of our citizens, and only those not within the classes mentioned should be charged with jury duty.

Conceding that the exemptions are wise, then can anyone give a sound reason for exempting the railroad president and vice-president engaged in the active discharge of their duties, and not exempting the president, vice-president or cashier of a bank while engaged in the active discharge of their duties; both classes are engaged in a business absolutely essential to the commercial interests of the country. . . 

So we might continue through the list of exemptions. Why require one class of citizens to sacrifice their business interests in order that

litigants may have their business difference settled, and exempt another class not more worthy, from a like duty? Jury service in civil cases is not, in my opinion, in the true sense a service to one's State; if so, then the duty should fall equally upon all classes possessing the necessary qualifications. It seems to me, the proper conclusion to be drawn from this is, that as it is shown to be inexpedient to maintain the jury system for the benefit of private litigants, without arbitrarily exempting a large class of qualified citizens from jury service, which in connection with the well known inclination of a large class not exempt, but well qualified, to shirk jury duty, which seriously impairs the efficiency of the jury system, that it would therefore be a wise policy on the part of the State to abolish the right of trial by jury in civil cases, and substitute in its place a tribunal charged with the sole duty of determining all issues of fact as well as of law arising in the trial of such cases. ...

I am persuaded that a District Court composed of three judges, by reason of their experience, training, and deep sense of responsibility, would more nearly reach correct conclusions on all issues of fact submitted to them than the average jury selected from the circumscribed class of citizens as herein indicated. 

This is a practical question and should be met and dealt with in a practical way in view of known conditions. We can never hope to reach that point in our civilization where a perfect administration of the law is attained.

So long as men are born with the selfish instinct, and must compete for the necessities of life, and the accumulation of wealth, equal and exact justice can not be looked for at the hands of our fellow man; the best will always be only an approximation. It is for this we should strive.

What are the essential qualifications for the proper determination of issues of fact? Mr. Starkis, in his treatise on evidence, says: "The only true guide to truth, whether the object be to explore the mysteries of nature or unravel the hidden transaction of mankind, is reason aided by experience." Putting it in another form, Solomon says: "The simple believeth every word; but the prudent man looketh well to his going"..

In every other field of human endeavor we know that intelligence and experience is essential to the highest degree of success. 

In many of the vocations of life peculiar mental training is necessary to properly fit the person to successfully follow it. 

This is true of all the professions, and many of the commercial pursuits.

A former Justice of the United States Supreme Court is quoted by a law writer as declaring "that the difficulties in determining questions of fact are greater and more common than those that occur in determining questions of law."

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

Mr. Justice Holmes has said: “A judge who has long sat at nisi prius ought gradually to acquire a fund of experience which enables him to represent the common sense of the community in ordinary cases far better than an average jury." Chief Justice Peters, of Maine, is quoted as saying: "No class of men know better than judges how much interest may unconsciously warp an honest mind." Chief Justice

Beasley, of New Jersey, is also quoted as saying: "Those who know from experience and reflection, the laws which regulate the human memory, are they whose duties constantly require them to use such knowledge, namely, the judges of courts." Again the same author, quoting from a magistrate of renown, says: "Memory is a thing so marvelous and so complex, manifesting its activity in so many diverse ways, that the trier of facts can never study it enough."

Is it not more reasonable to presume that the high standard of intelligence and experience called for in the opinions quoted is more apt to be found in a Court composed of three average District Judges such as we elect to 'that office in this State than in the average jurysuch as we usually select to try civil cases? ...

I am not unmindful of the fact, that many distinguished members of the legal profession have in the past expressed a preference for a trial by a common law jury upon issues of fact. It would not be proper to attempt an impartial discussion of this question without quoting from some of them; among the more prominent of these may be quoted Judge Dillon, who, in one of his lectures at Yale University, (Lecture IV, page 122), said: "I have tried literally thousands of cases with juries, and the instances are few where I had reasons to be dissatisfied with their verdicts." (He doubtless won most of his cases.) 

Again, Judge Caldwell of the United States District and Circuit 

Court, in Myers' vs. Chicago Ry. Co., 95 Fed. Rep., says: "The constitutional mode of ascertaining the sense of reasonable men on disputed questions of fact in common law actions, is by the verdict of twelve jurymen, and not by the opinion of the judges. It was because the people knew the judges were poor judges of the facts, that they committed their decisions to a jury, and every day's experience confirms the wisdom of their action."

Upon inquiry I find there is a strong sentiment among the people where I have heard the question discussed in favor of abolishing the right of trial by jury in civil cases; not only because they desire to be relieved of the duty of jury service, but they believe the business of the Court can be more expeditiously and satisfactorily transacted; that the business man's lawsuit can be more satisfactorily determined in a business like way, according to the law of the land, by three experts trained for that particular work than by a jury composed of men from the different walks of life, varying intelligence and experi-

ence, some of whom are perhaps thinking more of getting through and back to their own business than of the merits of the case on trial. 

With three capable District Judges sitting as a Court to try both law and facts, the judgments of that Court will be looked upon as possessing a finality not now given to them by litigants or lawyers. 

Many litigants who have had the benefit of a trial of their case before such a Court, who have had the chance of seeing the witnesses and hearing them testify, both on direct and cross examination, will more quickly submit to an adverse decision.

The defeated litigant will feel that he has had his real day in court, and that he does not have to go to an appellate court to get it. 

Then would our appellate courts be relieved of the duty of deciding many questions of procedure about which they have already written volumes.

Much of the judge-made law on the subject of reversible error on account of the admission or rejection of evidence would become obsolete.

The decisions as to reversible error in the giving or refusing of charges to the jury, such as those on the weight of the evidence, undue prominence to some particular issue, contradictory charges, failure to submit certain issues; measure of damages, and many others, could be forgotten for the "weightier matters of the law." 

Errors upon trial, as to the incompetency or disqualification of jurors would be eliminated, also errors as to conduct of the jury as well as of the parties and their attorneys before and in relation to the jury, could not rise.

Errors upon the deliberations and manner of the jury in arriving at their verdict would be forgotten lore. Errors on account of irregularities and defects in the verdict and findings on special issues could not rise; besides many others, which will readily occur to each of you, could be eliminated from our law of procedure. 

With this system in operation, the supreme and appellate court judges could attend the annual sessions of this Association free from the task of telling the bar of the State why they are so far behind with their work, and praying for both general and special relief.

THE JURY ON TRIAL By Harold H. Corbin 

Member of the New York Bar (Reprinted by special permission of the publishers, American Bar 

Association Journal) 

The jury is said to be cumbersome, inefficient, incompetent, unintelligent. Only recently, one of our most able judges expressed himself as favoring the complete abolition of the jury system in contract cases.¹ 

His characterization of it was "a wasteful, inefficient and outworn fetish." In his opinion: "We observe daily the spectacle of twelve perfectly honest jurors, untrained in the analysis of evidence, ignorant of the subject matter of the litigation, inexpert in that art peculiar to the lawyer by which he quickly absorbs and assimilates as his own that which is primarily the business of others; we see these twelve men sitting through a long complicated trial, with scores of documents and letters and accounts in the evidence, vainly endeavoring to interpret that which they barely understand. We know the waste of time consumed in reading to a jury hour after hour and day after day written evidence which can be handed up to a trial judge and absorbed by him in a few minutes; and we know the frittering away of time in openings and summations. This is a spectacle which must strike dismay into the heart of every lover of justice. There is no more practical reason today for persistence in jury trial in this type of case than there would be for the continuance of trial by battle."

A well-known metropolitan lawyer writes in similar vein,2 listing the faults of juries, charging their unfitness to pass on the merits of controversies, both civil and criminal, and anticipating the argument of partisans of trial by jury founded upon the "historical basis" of the process by analyzing the Magna Charta to mean something entirely different from our present method of trial by jury. He advocates the trial by one or more judges as the panacea of the ills he catalogues. 

I want to record an emphatic dissent from, these views. I believe in the jury. I believe in its present day efficiency. I have recently tried a series of cases involving practically the same evidence and witnesses in each, seven with and three without a jury, and not one of the jury trials lasted as long as those tried without a jury. Much might be said of the time spent upon trials of equity cases before judges and upon trials before referees and masters; but I venture that the experience of the average lawyer and jurist will bear me out in saying that the argument against the jury trial as a time-waster is without merit. After all, the balancing of hours and minutes consumed in trials with and without a jury is quite as futile as it is inconsequential; the real question is which form is most conducive to a just, true and satisfying result. 

The second count of the indictment against the civil jury is that it lacks the experience and ability to understand and fairly determine the cases tried before it. It is said that we must have a mind trained in the science of sifting fact from fiction and that hte legal mind of the jurist is best equipped to meet the requirements of an able and impartial arbiter. Some writers urge the extinction of the jury in contract cases alone; others in all classes of civil cases; others in both civil and criminal cases. I am not concerned here with the abolition of the jury in criminal cases. It has stood and always will stand as the only bulwark between the security of the individual and the oppressive use of power by the state.

The distinction drawn between actions upon contract and actions for torts or wrongs mirrors a weakness in the position of the criticsfor if the jury be competent to determine actions for injury to person or property, it is equally competent to decide issues of fact arising from broken contracts. Judge Proskauer says that he does not refer, in his criticism, "to jury trial in the type of civil case like the negligence case where a public demand for the judgment of the average man may still have logical basis;" but it is in such cases more than any other that the jury is called upon to sift the opinions of experts, to reach a conclusion from medical testimony as to what diagnosis or prognosis is the more accurate and to decide between opinions of engineers and scientists in various fields. Surely, if the jury be qualified to pass upon such issues, it is qualified to determine veracity of non-experts and facts arising out of ordinary business relations and contract dealings between man and man. If public demand for the jury has logical basis in the class of cases mentioned, it should follow that the demand in contract cases is also logical.

But let us see, first, what the true function of the jurors is in both classes of civil actions. They determine facts-nothing else. They

They do not ordinarily interpret documents nor construe the meaning or effect of written instruments. The judge does that. Where the language and meaning of documentary evidence is clear, he construes or instructs as the case requires. Where the evidence is without dispute, the judge directs the verdict or takes the case from them altogether, save in a comparatively small number of cases where undisputed evidence may give rise to diverse inferences of fact and then the case may be given to the jury to determine what inferences are the most reasonable and probable. In all cases it is the task of the judge to define the issues of fact which he leaves to the jury to determine and to limit their consideration to those issues. There is always the supervisory control of the judge over the verdict which he may exercise by setting it aside if it be contrary to, or against the weight of, the evidence. And now, in this state, he may direct a verdict in a case where he would set aside a contrary verdict on the latter ground. 

The comparative infrequency with which verdicts are set aside is some indication of the respect in which verdicts of juries are held by trial judges.

It will thus be seen that in both classes of civil actions the province of the jury is to decide disputed questions of fact, and this means that in practically every case their sole function is to judge the witnesses and to say who is giving the most truthful or probable version or opinion. They are, essentially, judges of men. Shall the trained legal mind of the jurist supplant twelve lay minds as the judge of men and facts? 

The best key to the lay mind and lay action is the layman; there is no better judge of the veracity of witnesses than twelve men representing a cross-section of the community whence the witnesses come; the best trained and most expert judge of witnesses is the man who is in daily business, social and personal contact with the average run of the witnesses being judged; the composite judgment of twelve laymen drawn from all walks of life is far more likely to represent the truth. than the judgment of one or more judges. 

In one of the articles above referred to the observation is made that juries are unfit to pass upon questions of negligence, intent and fraud. 

So great a jurist as James Kent, afterwards Chief Justice of the Supreme Court and Chancellor of New York, has said:4 "The constructions of judges, on the intention of the party, may often be (with the most upright motives) too speculative and refined, and not altogether just in their application to every case. Their rules may have too technical a cast, and become, in their operation, severe and oppressive. 

To judge accurately of motives and intentions does not require a master's skill in the science of the law. It depends more on a knowledge

of the passions and of the springs of human action, and may be the lot of ordinary experience and sagacity." What is true of the determination of issues of fact involving questions of motive, intent, fraud and like operations of the mind is also true of contract cases. 

Such cases almost invariably turn, so far as the jury is concerned, upon whether a conversation or any incident occurred as recounted by one witness or by another-issues usually more simple of decision than cases necessitating the determination of mental operations of a party. . ...

Of all the objections to trial by jury, the one most often urged, and with perhaps the best foundation, is this point that the jury does not possess the art of the judge of analyzing evidence. But I maintain that, of far greater value than an extended courtroom experience with the analysis of evidence, is the ability, which so many judges and lawyers lack, of comprehending the mental situation of a witness, drawn, like the jurors themselves, from a world outside courtrooms and away from the harassing formalism of the legal field. Such understanding alone affords the true basis for proper investigation and decision. The mere fact that the judge sees more witnesses on the witness stand in the course of a year than does the jury does not qualify him, in my opinion, as the better judge of witnesses. In fact, that very experience sets up in the judge's mind a particular code by which he judges all witnesses. 

His work becomes, of necessity, systematized. Each judge will develop a system of his own which, to him, becomes orthodox-his method a rule. What that standard or rule shall be depends upon his individuality, his personal contacts and associations, and upon the particular school of thought to which he belongs. His acceptance or rejection of the testimony of witnesses of a certain class, or characteristic, or appearance, or mannerism, grows naturally, but certainly, into a habit by which all are tested. The eternal question with him will be whether he should depart from this or that classification in a given instance. 

His object will be consistency with his established rule. He has been taught the worship of precedent from his first day in the law school. 

I say there is no rule of thumb by which to judge a witness. His veracity may be determined by a glance, an expression, or an otherwise trivial incident which may escape the notice of a judge-which may be observed by some of the jury and pass entirely unnoticed by the others. To borrow again the language of Judge Kent concerning the constructions of judges, "Their rules may have too technical a cast and become in their operation severe and oppressive."

SOME OBSERVATIONS ON THE JURY SYSTEM FROM THE STANDPOINT OF A PRACTITIONER By W. W. Naman Attorney-at-Law 

(Written especially for the Debaters Research) To the lawyer who finds the law in its aspect of a science particularly attractive the jury element in trial of civil cases is disconcerting and disheartening. The whimsical and uncertain result of a trial before a jury renders it as open to speculation as a fling of the dice and, accordingly, much more appealing to those who have a flare for the "green table" than to the more conservative brethren of the "green bag." 

The good lawyer will bring his case to trial with thorough and comprehensive preparation. With great labor and ingenuity he will have gathered, marshalled, and sifted the facts germane to and supporting the legal principles upon which the case is predicated and presented them with a discriminating comprehension of the rules of logic and the laws of evidence, and yet as the judge in his smug complacency delivers the case to the jury, as one who in utter exhaustion at last surrenders and throws virtue to the powers of evil, and as the jury rises to retire and consider of its verdict, the most seasoned practitioner will stand uncertain and trembling, shot through with a thousand fears, like a compassless captain on a rudderless ship. Complete mastery of his case is gone in apprehension of the verdict of the jury which no one can foretell.

In our system of jurisprudence we draw a very sharp line of demarcation between what we denominate the law of the case and the facts. In the first field we have made great progress in applying scientific methods. With great precision trained and intelligent judicial minds have ascertained, classified and determined legal principles, as witness the great system of laws known as the "common law." 

A lawyer who is willing to devote himself faithfully to the task may indicate the legal rights and duties in the realm of the common law under stated circumstances with almost the same accuracy as the scientist in the laboratory in the physical realm can predict a certain chemical re-action with given factors. Indeed, the law is truly a science.

Of course, with the most trained judiciary the law takes on here and there unscientific principles brought about by the deflecting force of a strong and prevailing public opinion or by the personal views or

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

predilections of individual judges. Against this tendency the great body of the judiciary is at work intelligently, faithfully, constantly, conscientiously, and dispassionately in an effort to keep the body of the law a harmonious, scientific, exact, and precise system of legal principles. The lawyer may enter the courtroom with reasonable assurance that by the time his case has run the gamut of the trial, intermediate, and appellate courts the issues will have been tried and the rights of the parties will have been determined on the whole according to sound principles of law.

Under any system of jurisprudence the method of determining the facts is more likely to be unscientific than the process of ascertaining and determining the legal principles. Sure and exact method for the ascertainment of the facts of any transaction is very difficult to devise. The common law rules of evidence have gone far in the direction of reducing to a scientific and logical basis the discovery and determination of the truth of a controversy. The rules with reference to relevancy, materiality, and competency of evidence, including the rule requiring the best evidence and the rule excluding hearsay and the rules fixing the qualifications of witnesses all represent the contribution of English and American judiciary to the formulation of a science judicial procedure designed to lessen the element of chance in the processes of justice.

The very greatness of the difficulty in perfecting a scientific basis for the discovery of the facts renders all the more necessary the selection of a competent body to perform this function. Yet we could not create any body for this purpose less qualified than is the jury in civil cases. As a matter of fact, the jury system as it exists today was not designed or created to function as it now does. Those who have looked into the origin of the institution report that in the beginning a jury consisted of a group of persons who themselves had witnessed a transaction and were, therefore, called upon to determine the facts on account of their personal information concerning them. 

So far has the modern jury changed from this original idea that it is the first qualification of a juror for service in any case under the modern system that he have no personal knowledge or information whatsoever about the facts in the controversy in which he is called upon to sit as a juror.

It must be conceded that a body best designed to discover the real facts in any controversy should be composed of men of the maximum intelligence, broad and comprehensive knowledge, trained in the observation of human nature, accustomed to weigh facts, able to sift out the relevant facts from a mass of evidence, accustomed to form a decision upon their own judgment, and capable by reason of education and mental discipline of overcoming their personal bias and

prejudices. Of course, the selection of such an ideal group of persons is impossible, but still there is no reason why a system should not be constructed that would provide men whose qualifications would approach these requirements as nearly as possible. As presently constituted, the juries, speaking from the writer's personal experience, reflect the very opposite of the above qualifications. In the first place, it rarely happens that the most competent and intelligent men in the community serve on the jury; in some way or another they manage, generally speaking, to get excused from jury service, particularly in the state courts. Few of the men who serve on juries have the education or training to understand and weigh the facts of a complicated civil case. Moreover, few have had any experience whatsoever in forming judgments from a group of facts submitted to them and few among the rank and file of the jurors have schooled and disciplined themselves against being carried away by the forces of sentimentalism and prejudices that whirl around the trial. 

The picture is not overdrawn. It is true, of course, that it sometimes happens that by chance the influential and dominating factors in a jury are unusually intelligent men. It may be also conceded that in the Federal courts, where the jurors are selected by commissioners appointed for that purpose, a more intelligent class of jurors are selected. These instances relieve the situation, but do not change the fundamental objections. The lawyers, of course, are familiar with this fundamental weakness of the jury system. It is the rarest thing that a lawyer waives a jury where sentimentality and prejudices would be likely to throw the case in his favor if permitted to operate. The plaintiff in a personal injury damage suit will never use his challenges on the weakest members of the panel. Think of a system that renders it possible and desirable to select as arbiters of a controversy the weakest minded. One of the usual and customary questions propounded by lawyers in the examination of prospective jurors is whether they are acquainted with the attorneys for either side. No inquiry could better indicate the general opinion of the bar as to what trivial circumstances might influence the juror, and the innumerable cases in the law books where the appellate courts have set aside verdicts of juries on the ground that there was no evidence to support them are abundant proof that the attorneys eliciting this information about the jurors were justified. For the benefit of the laymen, it may be said that when an appellate court reverses a case on the ground that there was no evidence to sustain the verdict it simply means that there was not a single solitary fact established in the record justifying the conclusion at which the jurors arrived.

A  IN CIVIL CASES 

By 

John Maxwell Attorney-at-Law 

(Written especially for The Debaters Research) The right of trial by jury is so fixed in the Anglo-Saxon mind that it is doubtless futile to discuss a substitute for it. In criminal cases, unquestionably such a right should be zealously guarded. In civil cases, however, some substitute for the jury in a majority of cases is now desirable.

In any discussion of this matter it should always be borne in mind that the purpose of Courts in civil matters is to administer justice. 

This should be done impartially, expeditiously, and at a minimum of expense to parties and the State. 

We have advanced far in our social and business relations. Points of contact have many times multiplied and the lines have become intricate and complex. Yet, in adjusting the differences that arise out of these complex relations, we are, to a large extent, using the machinery used by our fore-fathers.

In some matters, such as public utility regulations, workmen's compensation, and in arbitration agreements, we have already substituted expert fact finding bodies for the old method of trial by a jury, but in personal and private controversies we still retain this system.

The result of this is a wide spread complaint from lawyers and laymen as to the failure, or at least the burden, of administering justice and settling differences between men. The principal complaints relate to the delay, the expense and the uncertainty of appealing to the Courts to settle disputes. Many good men sacrifice actual rights rather than undergo the burden of a trial in the Courts. 

Trial by a jury contributes in part to all three of the above complaints, but the later―uncertainty-is its chief weakness. A case can be tried before the Court in much less time, with less expense and with greater finality than before a jury. 

There are several reasons for this. In the first place, the jurors are inexperienced in analyzing the facts. These men are gathered from all walks of life and grades of intelligence. They listen several days to conflicting testimony from many witnesses. Oftentimes these witnesses are vague and indefinite in their statements. The jurors then hear the charge of the Court and the argument of Counsel, and, when they retire to consider their verdict, the whole thing is largely a cross-word puzzle. Consequently, in a vast number of cases the verdict rendered is determined by the impressions made on the jury by the attorneys in the case, or by some attractive and skillful witness, or by one of the litigants themselves. The real issue is lost in the maze, and important differences are settled by the jurors on sentiment or emotion. The actual justice to be administered becomes secondary. It is no surprise then, to an experienced trial lawyer that freak verdicts and mistrials are common.

The most deplorable thing, however, in our present system is the hazard of error on appeal. Present day litigation is largely a game of skill between two groups of trained lawyers—somewhat after the fashion of a game of chess. Attorneys give much time to setting traps for the opposing Counsel and the trial judge.

A study of the standard digests will surprise the laymen as to the number of new trials and reversals that are granted in jury trials. not one of which would have been granted in trials before the Court. 

These are mainly due to the following grounds:

First, improper remarks of the trial judge in the presence and hearing of the jury.

Second, prejudicial statements of Counsel in his argument to the jury.

Third, asking improper questions of witnesses while on the stand.

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

Fourth, admission of improper testimony before the jury. 

Fifth, errors in the Court's charge to the jury. 

Sixth, misconduct of the jury itself, either during the trial or in considering its verdict. 

Under proper objection at the time a case is tried before a jury, same may be reversed or a new trial had for any of the above errors, if in the opinion of the trial Court or Appellate Court such error probably affected the verdict of the jury. 

If the same case, however, were tried before the Court without a jury under the identical record, the Court would affirm the judgment. 

In a non-jury case the Appellate Court will confine its inquiry largely to two questions:

First, did the trial Court correctly apply the law to the facts? 

Seceond, are the facts in the record sufficient to sustain the judgment? 

Remarks of the Court and of Counsel during the trial, improper evidence, the charge of the Court and conduct of the jury, are well eliminated as error and justice between men is more nearly approximated in a more certain, expeditious and less expensive manner. 

Before this can be done, however, the judiciary itself should be reorganized. The judges should be removed from the influences of politics and public opinion. Their terms of office and their manner of selection should be such that no fear will enter their minds when considering a case. The judiciary should be made independent and therefore impartial. When that is done, the office of judge will again become a place of dignity and honor, one to be desired to such an extent that capable and ambitious men will prepare for it as for any other career.

The writer therefore believes, that with a recreated judiciary, as a substitute for jury trials in civil cases a Court composed of from one to three judges, depending on the public importance of the questions involved, would be an improvement on the present expensive and intricate method of trial by jury.

WHAT IS WRONG WITH TRIAL BY JURY? 

By 

Philip E. Teeling, M.A., LL.B. 

Members of the Waco, Texas, Bar (Written especially for the Debaters Research) 

What's wrong with trial by jury? What are the inherent flaws of this cog in our present judicial machinery? In the following lines it will be my purpose to point out rather summarily the particulars in which jury trials fall short of the objective of all courts, which is certainty and speedy and equal justice for all. 

There are certain faults which inhere in human nature itself; these cannot be eliminated from any court which is under the direction of man, the only hope is to minimize their presence. Bribery, deceit, infidelity, erroneous judgment, and prejudices are common to humanity. 

Though these evils of human nature exist in practically all the social classes, they differ from individual to individual. The issue then presented is whether or not the average juryman, if such a subject may be conceived, is more susceptible to the so called evils of human nature than a person or persons who are selected to fill the place of the jurors based upon other qualifications. The source for members of a petit jury is generally the tax rolls. The beginning may be acceptable as a cross-section of society, but after being chosen for the venire come repeated eliminations. The professional men along with many others utilize their statutory exemptions. Next the men who are successful in business-indicative of the best material for a fair and intelligent jury-have no time to spare from their business and beg to be excused. What can be done other than grant the request when a man says he cannot pay attention to the evidence because of pressing business problems and engagements? Finally the attorneys get a chance to quiz and challenge the jurors which is an opportunity to get a prejudicial body of men rather than an impartial and more intelligent body. For example, where the plaintiff must prove his case through technical accounts, the defendant would attempt to scratch off the jury list any prospective juror who appeared likely to be able to comprehend the evidence. As a result of this threshing process, often the men who actually compose the petit jury are far below the average citizens. It will be assumed as a rule of sociology, that the aforenamed evils of human nature abound more frequently in the less intelligent classes of men than in the educated and successful business men.

From the preceding remarks concerning the selection of a jury it is obvious that an undue amount of time is necessarily required in getting ready to present the facts of a case. Delays in jury trials are occasioned more, however, after the testimony commences. 

The pres ence of a jury consisting of laymen to decide the issuable facts is responsible for the creation and existence of the multifarious rule of evidence. A layman not knowing proper from improper evidence must have his ear closely guarded by the repeated objections of counsel. 

Each objection must be then supported with explanatory remarks and argument. It would be hardly incorrect to say that as much time is ordinarily used in making objections to evidence as is actually used in giving evidence.

Delays due to jury trials are occasioned after the trial is over. Material misconduct of the jury necessitates a new trial. Jurors not knowing what constitutes misconduct frequently go outside the record to decide their verdict and fatally taint the whole proceeding. The jury may consider that the attorneys must be paid and assess the damages accordingly; or the damages may be fixed by averaging the amounts fixed by each of the twelve jurors. Such verdicts must be set aside and held for naught. If a prejudicial and improper remark comes to the hearing of the jury, a mistrial should be ordered, and if not, the case would no doubt be reversed by the appellate court. When an improper ruling is made on the admission of evidence the same is reversible error.

The final considerable fault of trial by jury is the inefficiency of the layman to fulfill the task to which jurors are assigned. The theory of our present system of jurisprudence is a separation of issues of fact and issues of law: allow the jury to find the facts and let the judge ascertain the law. The theory breaks down on most questions which arise during the trial of a case because most issues are mixed questions of law and fact. It therefore invariably becomes the duty of the jury to apply the law as charged by the judge to the facts. It is in this particular-weighing evidence and applying the law thereto-that the jury shows its greatest weakness. In practically every damage suit the issue arises as to whether or not the alleged wrongful act of the defendant was a "Proximate cause" of the injuries to the plaintiff. "Proximate cause" is a legal term with a technical and definite meaning. The jury must ascertain its technical meaning in order to make the finding. Since it has taken the courts years to fix the meaning of this phrase and about which volumes have been written, it is only natural that a layman cannot comprehend its meaning after but a single reading of the technical definition submitted in the court's charge. Here is a frequently used definition of the term: "By the term Proximate cause is meant that cause which in its natural and

1 = continual sequence, unbroken by any intervening agency, cause the event which but for the cause would not have happened, and which in the light of attending circumstances, ought to have been reasonably foreseen." 

The courts have recently criticized the definition because of the use of the term "intervening agency," and have held that it is reversible error not to define that expression upon timely objection since it has a technical meaning unknown to the layman. This is only a most common example of the legal phases of the juror's work. There is only one logical conclusion: a knowledge of the legal principle is indispensible in applying the legal principle to facts. Jurors usually do not possess a knowledge of the legal principles. 

The lack of experience in the jury box is an impediment to a thorough analysis and determination of a case by a jury. It is axiomatic that experience is necessary in order to perform ably any difficult task. Jurors being inexperienced in weighing conflicting evidence do not qualify even as good fact finders.

A DEFENSE OF OUR JURY SYSTEM 

Thos. E. McDonald, J.D. 

Professor of Law, Baylor University (Written especially for the Debaters Research) Governments are instituted among men in order that life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness may be rendered more secure. The perpetuation of these rights in the past has ever depended upon trial by jury as such trial is known and practiced in England and the United States. The conception of human liberty may have been born in the brain of learned students of government, but such conception was nurtured, protected, humanized, and transmitted to our generation by English and American juries. The jury box is the agency which gives expression to the people's idea of justice. It furnishes a powerful bond of faith uniting the people with their government. 

The jury system therefore adds stability to organized society by giving the people confidence that justice will prevail. If we should abolish trial by jury, the cause of anarchy and communism would be served because distrust and suspicion would be increased in the ranks of the common people. Trial by jury is synonymous with free government according to the belief of millions of American citizens. 

We cannot abolish trial by jury without endangering that wholesome respect for law and order which lies at the foundation of democracy.

SO THIS IS JUSTICE 

By 

Charles P. Taft, Second (From The World's Work, May, 1928. Reprinted by special permission of the publishers.)

On the day set last October for the hearing of his wife's divorce suit, Remus intercepted her on the way to court and with one pistol shot reached four vital organs. He said afterwards that he used his, medical knowledge to make her die slowly-and she did. He asserted, and I think believed, that he killed her for society's sake and that his act was a legal crime but not a moral wrong. 

Within three hours, Remus told our alienist that he would not set up the defense of insanity because he was not insane and never had been crazy. He said that he was justified in doing what he had done. 

Ten days later he found he could do nothing else except plead insanity. 

At first the claim was transitory maniacal insanity, as Remus described it. He got this, together with his fondness for Lombroso, from his legal experiences in criminal practice in Chicago and in particular from his own defense, as a lawyer, of Bill Ellis, a former Cincinnatian who murdered his wife in 1914. . . .

The rebuttal for the state reached its climax with the testimony of these three alienists who had sat throughout the case, heard all the testimony, and made a number of thorough examinations of Remus. 

Alienists are now agreed, and several testified at the trial, that there is no such thing as transitory maniacal insanity. They say that in certain types of manic-depressive psychoses, a person may have lucid intervals, during which he is criminally responsible. With this exception, every psychosis (the modern technical term almost synonymous to insanity) is continuous, and the only types from which Remus by any possibility could have been suffering are not curable. Furthermore, the alienists is one of the clearest and most satisfactory reports ever presented by expert witnesses in a murder case, said that Remus

was criminally responsible; as did many lay witnesses. On December 

20, 1927, a jury of his peers, after nineteen minutes of perfunctory deliberation, brought in a verdict of "Not guilty" on the sole ground of insanity. . . .

The case presents primarily three problems of our criminal situation that call for consideration and action. Remus was acquitted by the newspapers, and after he was acquitted they turned on him and sent him to the insane asylum. Judge Lueders, of the Hamilton County 

Probate Court, probably would have committed Remus to Lima under any circumstances, for he is a man of mature common sense, of soundness of judgment, and of the greatest courage. But unless he had been backed up by the tremendous reaction of public opinion, not only in Cininnati but throughout the state of Ohio-a reaction produced principally by the newspapers-I venture to think that Remus would not have been within the walls of the Lima State Hospital a month after the verdict

It might be useful first to recall that the jury system does not consist of the submission of question of fact to a group of the more ignorant citizens, full of the prejudices of lower than ordinary human nature, and without the guidance of any experienced intelligence. The jury system that our constitution seek to protect is the system whereby an experienced judge calls in a group of conscientious citizens to give judgment on the facts under his direction after his instruction as to the law, and after his wise comment upon the evidence and the credibility of the witnesses who have presented it. 

The English common law calls for a trial not BY a jury BEFORE a judge, but BY a judge WITH a jury. In England, for instance, in an important capital case, the judge may say to the jury (as he did in the Butler case, last summer): "Gentlemen, this is a simple case.

The lawyers have presented to you the youth of the defendant, as they properly might, and which you properly may consider, but that is no excuse for crime. I hardly think it is necessary for you to retire to the jury room. The front row may turn around and consult with the back row, and you may render your verdict from the box if you can agree upon it there."

Instead of the common-law jury trial, we now have a jury trial created by state legislatures, which, in turn, have been controlled by those more interested in the defense of the criminals and in mushheaded sentimentality, than in the protection of the public. Our state legislatures are almost invariably controlled by the rural bloc. In smaller communities, the leading members of the bar are frequently called in to defend criminal cases. A state prosecutor in one county

may defend murder cases in adjoining counties. These leading members of the bar are always involved in politics. The legislators from these communities reflect their attitude It is this situation that has resulted in the outburst of popular resentment against criminal procedure and has created the many crime commissions and crime surveys that have been so helpful in recent years. 

Pressure of popular opinion must continue, for if it does not, then a fine piece of work, like the Missouri Crime Survey, can be thrown in the wastebasket by the state legislature, as was done last year. 

To the situation that I have just described must be added the political features of municipal government. Jury statutes generally provide that names shall be put into the jury wheel by impartial individuals who shall be approached by no one. But one of the most effective favors by which local political control can be wielded, is to get people on juries. It is amazing, therefore, how the contents of the jury wheel can become restricted to certain types: elderly men retired from business; women whose children have grown up or who have no children, and who are looking for variety and a little pocketmoney; and straightout professional jurors, who cannot earn $3 or $5 a day in any other way. What goes up must come down, so we say, and it is equally true that what goes into the jury wheel must come out. 

When you add to this situation a practice that may grow up, by which judges fill out exhausted panels by naming persons who apply to them as additional jurors, the situation may become disgraceful. This latter practice, of course, cannot affect a special panel drawn for a first degree murder case, but it may apply to a jury selected for a term.... 

Another most important element in a jury trial is its control by the judge. So long as the judge sits as an umpire and must let the jury do its will, so long will a fair trial be impossible in cases where public prejudice is arounsed for the defendant, as in the Remus case, and against the defendant, as in the Hickman case. The judge must have power, as he has in the Federal courts, to sit before the jury as the representative of law and authority and justice, and, by his attitude in the course of the trial and his comments at its close, overcome the ordinary prejudices of human nature. It is amazing that, when we have a splendid example of the successful working of the jury trial in our Federal courts, we should need to go to England and to other states for advice. Yet the kind of influence that has produced our vicious laws of criminal procedure can ever introduce bills into Congress to emasculate our Federal jury trial. The jury system as praised by our forefathers can and does work, but one may rest assured that they were not talking about the ordinary criminal jury trial in our state courts. What success it has there is in spite of the handicaps.

CRIME-A CRITICAL ANALYSIS By Cornelius F. Collins Judge of Court of General Sessions, New York 

(From North American Review, July, 1928. Reprinted by special permission of the publishers.) "The very heart of the criminal process," says Arthur V. Lashlay, one of the able contributors to the Missouri Crime Survey, “is the prosecuting office." The District Attorney is the counsel for the people of the State. He is the pivotal force between crime detection and crime punishment. If this office is ill equipped either in number or capacity, to say nothing of integrity, disaster from the standpoint of public safety is inevitable. By reason of improper or inefficient legal management, a criminal case can fail before the Magistrate, the Grand Jury or the Petit Jury. The compensation provided for the prosecuting attorneys has been so small as to make it impractiable for lawyers of experience and ability to accept the office. No progress can be made toward improvement in the administration of justice until effective attention has been given to the better organization and equipment of the agencies of the police and the District Attorney, those indispensable legal ouposts for social protection. 

The immemorially cherished institution of trial by jury has been attacked severely by those critics who find in our administration of law the causes for the alleged crime increase. Nothing, it would seem to the writer, could be more unjustified than affirmative action on the drastic recommendation of jury abolition. An institution so highly prized by our fathers as to be classified among the most important provisions of our fundamental law, Federal and State, and frequently boasted of as "the bulwark of our liberties," could not be interferred with without dire results.

At times there have been miscarriages of justice by reason of the action of juries. On the whole, however, trial by jury in America can be said to have stood the test of time as an institution making for the administration of justice to the satisfaction of a free people. Judges of experience in criminal cases know that on the whole substantial justice is done both to the people and to the defendant in trial by jury. 

There is, however, room for improvement in the jury system. Many citizens, often those who are too ready violently to condemn the shortcomings of government, are shamefully neglectful of their obligations of jury duty. The calibre of our juries would be immeasurably improved if some of our worthy citizens would serve upon them instead

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

of evading this sacred responsibility. Judges and lawyers would welcome any measures tending to improve the jury system, but the lessons of history teach us that it is a safe and sound instrument of justice.

The trial of a defendant charged with serious crime should be characterized by dignity, fairness and a calm pursuit of the truth, not only that substantial justice may be done, but that public confidence in our tribunals of law may be safeguarded. In recent years some of our most important criminal trials have become public spectacles. 

The press intrenches itself in the court-room en masse. The camera man, the special writer, the popular author, the prominent actress, a notorious divorcee, or almost any person in the popular eye, take upon themselves the responsibility of informing the public of their opinion on the guilt or innocence of the accused, and of the conduct of the case. It is a truth as old as Beccaria and Howard, over a century ago, that it is not the severity but the celerity and certainty of punishment that give it its powers to deter and repress criminal action. A fatal mistake is made when there is an attempt to utilize cruel severity as the sole weapon to combat criminal action. In the history of crime and punishment here is written in letters of fire the truth that savage punishment defeats its own ends. No one prescription such as long term sentence or severity will cure the evil. The question is not so much whether we are coddling criminals as it is of the extent of our realization of the magnitude of the problem, and the necessity of bringing to its solution humanitarian principles and scientific methods. Mcre important still is the aim at prevention which is the latest development in society's war against crime. In the meantime the best things we can do are to organize and equip better the police and the offices of the District Attorney and to provide the courts and all of our institutions dealing with delinquency with clinics and an adequate staff of well trained probation and parole officers, and give to those who administer these agencies greater powers of scientific discrimination in order to bring about individual treatment, not falling back to the old way governed by rigid laws and "feelings."

FUNCTIONS OF JUDGE AND JURY IN THE DETER- MINATION OF PRELIMINARY QUESTIONS OF FACT 

By 

Edmund M. Morgan, Harvard Law School 

(Reprinted by special permission of the publishers of the Harvard 

Law Review and of the Author.)

To say that the relevancy of A depends upon the existence of B is only to say that it requires a combination of A and B to produce a relevant factor in the case. No argument can be framed for taking from the jury the question as to A which will not be equally applicable to the question as to B. If when evidence upon A is offered, an objection of irrelevancy puts the determination of B into the province of the judge, by the same token when B is offered, an objection of the same sort will produce a similar result as to A. If both A and B are not thus to be taken from the jury, at least the proponent can at his pleasure determine which shall be decided by the judge by first tendering evidence of the other. In no case, it is believed, where the analysis can be so baldly made, has any court held that, under the guise of ruling upon the admissibility of evidence, a judge may exclude the jury from determining an issue otherwise properly within its field. 

But such an analysis is not always so obvious. 

Where the competency of A depends upon the existence of B, it is not a whit more difficult for the jury to determine the existence of B. It can as easily determine the state of mind of the dying declarant as the intent or motive of the defendant. The same is true of any fact upon the existence of which the admissibility of otherwise incompetent evidence depends, as, for example, the circumstances undr which a confession was made, the availability or unavailability of an original document, the status of marriage or non-marriage between a witness and a party, or the relationship of client and attorney between a witness and another. Consequently, if such questions are to be taken from the jury, it must be on some other ground than the incapacity of the jury to determine the facts. 

On the other hand, if these preliminary questions are to be left to the jury, and if at the same time the objects designed to be accomplished by the exclusionary rules are to be attained, it will be necessary sharply to distinguish and separate the preliminary question upon which the admissibility of the challenged evidence depends from the evidence itself. In many cases, this could be done by making a special issue of the preliminary question, confining the testimony strictly

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

thereto, and taking a special interlocutory verdict. If the verdict found the preliminary basis, B, the jury would then get the evidence as to A; otherwise not. It needs no argument to show that the time, already too great, which a jury trial consumes, would under such a procedure be substantially increased. Moreover, in many cases, it would be a practical impossibility so to divorce the evidence of the preliminary fact from the challenged testimony as not to indicate the tenor or content of the latter to the jury.

If evidence as to both A and B is submitted to the jury, and the jurors are instructed to cast out that as to A unless they find D, several very practical objections at once obtrude themselves. Such a procedure will enormously complicate the work of the jury. It will not do to disregard realities. The jury is a casual group taken from the body of the citizenry. Jurors are compelled to perform their duties in rooms in which vile ventilation, inadequate acoustics, and limited light make keen, accurate work especially difficult. They are usually required to rely upon their memories unaided by memoranda. A trial may occupy days or weeks or even months. It takes great faith to expect jurors to distinguish between bare assertions, argument, and objections of counsel, on the one hand, and evidence on the other, and to rely only on the evidence as a basis for their verdict. To require them at the outset of their deliberations to separate the questions of admissibility from the ultimate questions, to apply to the former the testimony touching their foundations, and to reach rulings thereon prior to a consideration of the merits, is to demand the practically impossible. 

Furthermore, the only method of ascertaining whether the jury really went through the form of complying with such instructions would be the submission of special questions. Even assuming that the answers to these questions were made final, such a process would add enormously to the opportunities to assign errors on appeal or motion for new trial.

But a more fundamental objection exists. If the jury be credited with the capacity to perform the task of ruling intelligently upon such a mass of testimony-a capacity which every judge and trial lawyer knows that it does not possess can it be credited with the capacity to wipe its mind clean of the objectionable testimony which it has heard or seen, and rest its decision upon the admissible evidence only? 

It is a familiar fiction that the trial judge in equity cases regularly performs this psychological feat. But there is no trial lawyer who does not rejoice when he gets such a judge to admit de bene a piece of evidence which is emotionally potent in his favor, even though it may later be formally stricken, and no trial lawyer against whom it is received who is not convinced that the trial judge is purporting to strain out the water of prejudice from the milk of legitimate evidence

through a totally ineffective mental sieve. A mind trained to sift evidence may substantially accomplish even so difficult a task; but to expect the unskilled minds of jurors to do so is little short of ridiculous.

In many instances, moreover, the chief objective of the exclusionary rule would be destroyed. Where the exclusion is based on a policy of protection of an interest, nothing could be more absurd than to violate the interest and then to instruct the jury to repair the damage by disregarding the wrongfully extracted evidence. If a lawyer is compelled to repeat in open court the confidential communications of ' his alleged client, and the jury is told to disregard them in case they find the relationship exists, the harm of disclosure is beyond remedy. 

In this respect certainly, the only sensible option must be between a decision of the preliminary questions by the judge, and an interlocutory verdict thereon by the jury. ..

On theory, then, where the relevancy of A depends upon the existence of B, the existence of B should normally be for the jury; where the competency of A depends upon the existence of B, the existence of 

B should always be for the judge. . . .

It often happens that the only evidence which a litigant has to support his case is incompetent either by statute or by a rule of the common law. Where its incompetency does not depend upon the decision of a preliminary question of fact, no court allows itself to be influenced by this consideration. Granting the validity of the exclusionary rules, there is no plausible or substantial reason for discriminating between such cases and those where a preliminary investigation is necessary. The accepted theory of jury trial requires that there be submitted to the jury only matters in dispute as to which reasonable men could differ, and that in resolving the dispute the jury shall hear only legal evidence. There never was a time when every question of fact arising in a lawsuit was to be decided by the jury; nor has there been a time since the jury has been required to base its findings upon evidence submitted in open court, when it has been privileged to hear and consider all relevant evidence. There is nothing inherently objectionable in a judge-made decision.

It has also been said that no greater showing should be required as a basis for the admission of evidence than for a finding on the merits by the jury. This is merely a persuasive phrasing designed to hide the real issue. The hypothesis of the pertinent exclusionary rule is that the jury cannot evaluate A unless B exists, or that policy demands the rejection of A in the absence of B. The fact that B is in dispute is no reason for assuming that B exists, or that the policy of the law has changed, or that the capacity of the jury has been thereby increased.

Where B coincides with an alleged ultimate fact on the merits which must be submitted to the jury, additional arguments have been adduced. Since the jury must finally determine the existence or nonexistence of B, it is immaterial whether it is done as an interlocutory matter or finally. If the jurors can be trusted to decide on the merits, they can be trusted to decide on the procedural preliminary. This overlooks the very gist of the question. To decide B on evidence exclusive of A is quite a different thing from deciding B with the helpful or harmful influence of A. If A without B is improper provender for the jury, its prejudicial effect is not at all reduced by the circumstance that B is one of the issues on the merits. But, it is objected, the jury in its ultimate finding may disagree with the judge's preliminary finishing; and it may well be that the judge will destroy proponent's case by excluding A, when the jury would have found B, or actually did find B. Well, what of it? Suppose an interested survivor to be suing upon a contract which he alleges was made with the decedent through the decedent's agent, X. The survivor is by statute a competent witness if the contract was made through X, but is incompetent if it was made directly with the decedent. 

When the survivor presents himself as a witness, the court must, as a preliminary matter, determine whether the contract, if made at all, was made through X or directly with decedent. If he decides the latter, then the survivor cannot testify, and if he has no other evidence, he must lose. If the judge decides the former, then the whole question is for the jury. Certainly the jury is not bound to agree with the judge; it may well find, (1) that no contract at all was made, or (2) that a contract was made with decedent directly, or (3) that a contract was made through X. If the judge admits the survivor as a witness, he certainly cannot make the jurors in their deliberations on the ultimate fact accept his finding that the contract was made through X. All he can do is to make them hear the survivor's testimony. 

They may give it such weight as they see fit. By the same reasoning, if he decides the survivor incompetent, the jury has nothing to do with that decision, and the survivor must make his case with other testimony or not at all.

Under our system, the trial tribunal has two parts, each with its separate functions. The judge determines what witnesses and what evidence the jurors shall hear; he directs them as to the law, and in some jurisdictions he may advise them upon the facts which are ultimately in issue. But as to those facts, the jury is not bound to agree with him. Indeed, in most of our states the jury is not privileged even to hear his advice or opinion on them. And nothing is more common than to find a judge declaring on a motion for a new trial that, had he been in the jury's place, he would have found differently.

There is, then, no requirement at all that judge and jury shall agree on ultimate facts. Consistency or inconsistency between a preliminary finding by the judge and a final determination by the jury would therefore be entirely immaterial, even if the two findings were made for the same purpose. The judge, however, is ruling for the purpose only of determining whether the jury shall hear the evidence, and the rules which govern him in reaching his conclusion may be quite different from those governing the jury in its deliberation. For example, a person of normal intelligence is prima facie competent as a witness. 

He who objects has the burden of persuading the judge of the specific incompetency which he alleges. Hence if the mind of the judge is in equilibrium on the question, he must decide in favor of competency. 

Thus he may let in the alleged interested survivor's testimony because he cannot make up his mind whether the witness falls within the prohibition. The jury, however, will have to find by a preponderance of the evidence that the contract was made, in order to find for the plaintiff. And if plaintiff's only evidence is that the contract was made through X, then assuming the court and jury to be in absolute accord on the facts, their findings would have to be different. 

Finally, it is suggested, whatever may be the logic of the orthodox practice, it must give way to considerations of convenience. Since the jury must ultimately decide B, it will be a waste of precious time for the judge to make the preliminary inquiry. The short reply is that no great waste is necessary. If the judge is alert and energetic, he can so direct the order of proof that almost no time will be lost. Furthermore, if the value of the exclusionary rule is conceded, time necessarily so spent will be well spent. The time consumed in such inquiries, if proprly supervised by the judge, will be almost infinitesimal as compared to the time wasted in useless examination of prospective jurors, in purposeless cross-examination, in frivolous objections to evidence and specious arguments in support of them, and in forensic disputes as to matters not bona fide in issue. If the judges are to conduct a time-saving campaign, they had better begin in situations where the time consumed is wasted.

JUDGE AND JURY By Richard Washburn Child (From The Saturday Evening Post, May 10, 1930. 

Reprinted by special permission from The Saturday Evening Post, copyright 1930 by the Curtis Publishing Company, and with the permission and approval of the author.)

It requires no very exhaustive survey to convince us, particularly where the system of judges is an elective system and where the population is crowded, that we often have not only a badly organized judiciary but one which is stripped of power by our American practices and by legislation, and one which is sometimes dominated by the political machine. "No one can deny that in many states there has been improvement in the organization of the machinery of justice," wrties one of the most distinguished of my recent correspondents. "Massachusetts, Maryland, North Dakota, California and several other states have done a great deal to make their courts efficient. In other jurisdictions constitutional provisions stand in the way. In others, the legislature, as usual, attempts to dictate the procedure and control all things which affect our judge and jury system of trials. Legislatures usurp the privilege of the judiciary to organize itself. But you are quite right in presenting the general picture of the disorganization of our American legal machinery, the impotency of our judges and the almost utter degeneration of our jury system." 

Another says: "No doubt you will be assailed for telling the truth, but be certain that the protest will come from those lawyers who fail to concede, as their more enlightened brethren have to admit, that legislative control of courts has meant lawyer control of courts, and that lawyer control of our courts has made of our courts an arena for the jousts of attorneys before juries selected for their unfitness and befuddled by the whole bout they are made to attend, and a judge whose powers and inclination to help the jury are throttled by law or by personal caution because disinclined to be overruled, and even sometimes by political dictation from some city machine."

Everyone understands that any indictment of our judiciary is aimed more at its disorganization and its helplessness than at its corruption; and everyone knows that principles applying in one jurisdiction or district may not be applied in another. Everyone knows, too, that in spite of the fact that elective judges may be above political intrigue, there are plenty of bad spots, particularly in the lower courts.

The fitness of the average voter to choose between politically nominated candidates for the bench is one of the delusions of selfgovernment, and yet in many states the election of judges will surely continue for many years. Furthermore, the substitution of appointment of judges by the executive will not, in fact, completely eradicate the political taint in the selection.

Everyone who has investigated not only by statistics but by application of common sense and intimate observation knows that the largest hole through which leaks out our respect for law is the relation and the alliance of politics and the prosecutors and the judges. However great the stupid solemnity archaic practices, verbosity, hair-splitting, chicanery and oratory of lawyers in the arena of our courts may be, however fanatic, useless and outworn the laws themselves may become the loss of respect for judicial machinery is greatest when a citizenry realizes that just herself is not straight, that politics has involved her in a secret marriage. 

What, then, is the goal any good American must ask for? Obviously to remove, so far as possible, from all prosecutors and judges all political nominations, obligations, pressure, persuasion. After many months of investigation, I am inclined to believe that there is only one road to this goal, and that gradually, after public awakening and public determination, this goal may be attained by the proper kind of nonpolitical nominating machinery.

Cowardice hiding under the robe of caution and under the tin halo of conservatism, sloth, defense of "things as they are," have characterized many of the attempts to have anything better in most of our states.

Those who know the backstage facts about the failure of justice know that reform cannot be had wherever it is needed without breaking all the alliances between politics, legal chicanery, organized crime and the administration of law. Nor is that reform to be had overnight or without a battle. If a large part of the system of interdependence of politics and court weakness is to be wiped away, not much assistance is to be expected from those who are a part of the system or find it a convenience. Just as it is difficult to get legislatures to release their hold upon rules of procedure and control of the judiciary, so, also, we need not expect that political judges will be battling for a nonpartisan system of nominations. Nor will shyster lawyers, who profit because weak judges or judges who are hog-tied by the legislature's regulations give court orators and windbags and charlatans a long tether, be found campaigning to put an end to their own profits. 

It is only the enlightened members of the bar and the steady pressure of public opinion which will constitute the force to inaugurate nonpartisan nominations in the election or appointment of judges and prosecuting officers.

WHY A SUBSTITUTE FOR JURY TRIAL? 

By 

Howell Ward, Attorney-at-Law 

Member of the Corpus Christi Bar (Written especially for Debaters Research, Baylor University, Waco, Texas.)

Upon those who advocate trial by jury as it is found in Texas today there rests a twofold burden; first, to demonstrate that the jury should be retained in any event, and secondly, to affirm that it is, as administered under our procedure, a more efficient instrument than the system of jury trial as it exists today in other jurisdictions. 

It is not enough for the proponents of the system to answer that the jury trial is one of the bulwarks of our liberty, recognized from the time of Magna Carta and firmly embedded in our Constitutions. Men of eminence and ability have revered it solely on the ground that it is so ancient as to have become a fixed part of our political institutions. No one doubts that civilization moved forward when Henry II proclaimed the Grand Assize, granting to all his subjects "recognition of a jury."2 Might was no longer right, and the progress toward evnhanded justice was begun. No one will deny that it has protected peoples from the wrath of an arbitrary king and the whimsical injustice of the judge, a minion of a tyrannical sovereign. That it has been long a primordial, however, alone cannot justify its continued existence. 

If the conditions which created it have changed and reflection suggests that without it we can simplify and better judicial procedure, we should not hesitate to remove it root and branch from our jurisprudence.

It will be granted readily that the petit jury had its origin in times when commerce was small, land was the standard of wealth, and the social relations of the community were not perplexed by the problems which today render association so complex. Agricultural then were the questions presented to the courts-cases involving the fields of Blackacre and the rights of the owner of a horse to recover from the farrier for his negligence. Mechanical now are the controversies before our tribunals-suits involving the dominion of the air and the duties of the corporation to its stockholders. One who glances through the 

Year Books must be impressed by the difference in the character of the facts presented in those cases as compared to the ones seen in our modern reports. The will of the people has displaced the whims of kings. The elective means of judicial selection in our country has become so common that it may well be called universal. From the people do the judges come, and to the people are they responsible. 

Since those causes which gave the jury birth no longer are found, we may examine the institution to determine its efficiency. Let us see whether there are reasons for the abolition of the jury sufficiently convincing that we may put aside our veneration for the ancient and sweep it into the discard. 

Professor Willoughby, of Brookings Institution,3 tells us that: "If a people were starting anew, uninfluenced by historical attachments and sentimental considerations, free to devise that system for the administration of justice which it believed would give the best results in practice, it is hardly conceivable that it would deliberately create such an institution." 

Who would expect the butcher, the baker, and the candlestick maker to diagnose a case of dementia praecox, to apply correct accounting practice so as to determine the tax due for the good will of a partnership, or to say whether A has good or marketable title to land? If we cannot have a seaman to hear the facts in admiralty, a physician to sit on issues involving the science of medicine, we can at least better our procedure by permitting the cases to be determined by one who is trained in arriving at the truth. 

Nor is the juror's lack of technical training alone an objection. To find out the verities as they come from the mouths of witnesses requires what is sometimes called the judicial temperament. The prosecutor who has repeatedly seen the jury acquit the avowedly guilty but too well knows that the jury does not possess this judicial temperament. 

That the sympathy of the jury is often carried away by the picture of poverty's claim against the rich is so universally recognized among the legal profession as to have evoked the rule that it is error to inject into a case either expressly or impliedly evidence that the defendant. carries insurance against his liability.

How far short do our talesmen fall in that scale! Concentration and memory are essentially gifts of the educated and disciplined mind, characteristics poorly developed and in any but the mental work. 

Neither is the juryman better at weighing evidence. When witnesses' testimony conflicts, the juryman seldom weighs the probabilities of the different tales to determine which is credible. The very statement of the requirement that one apply the law to facts found presupposes legal training and judicial temperament.

Not only do jurors lack the necessary training to render fair and impartial verdicts, but there is no sense of responsibility resting upon them. True, the juror is sworn to try the case only upon the law as given by the court, and the facts as determined by the competent evidence adduced. That juries often are influenced by extraneous evidence is recognized by bur law, for the judge must retire the jury before he reprimands counsel in the case lest the jury might believe that the judge leaned away from that cause, and it is customary to retire the jury during the discussion of proposed testimony to which the opposition object. The juror often will unwittingly consider evidence not properly admissible and base his verdict upon it. Wilfully he occasionally does. Yet there is no physical punishment nor moral scourge to stay him in his conduct. The jury's verdict is unimpeachable and no opprobrium attaches to the juror for his conduct. After a short service he returns to his place in the community, to be followed by one of his neighbors. The judge, on the other hand, is answerable to the people, and should he for long fail in his duty the bar will see to it that the electorate are informed in terms sufficiently strong that the voter soon will thrust the judge from his position. 

Furthermore, the jury as it exists today is a cumbersome thing, expensive to the taxpayer and a burden upon the citizen who must serve. The cost of transporting a score or more of men from their homes to the scene of the trial, thence again to their abode, is no inconsiderable part of the budget of our courts. The pay of the juror is so meager that it will not compensate him for the loss due to absence from his usual duties.

As administered in Texas, the jury is deprived of the guidance of the only disinterested participant in the trial. WHY TRIAL BY JURY? 

By Dr. Leon Green, Yale Law School Published in The American Mercury, November. 1928 

(Reprinted by special permission of the author.) 

In early England, and in the pioneer days of our own country, the sheriff, as the chief officer of the shire or county, could be depended upon to summon twelve or more good men and true for jury duty without any undue embarrassment of justice. But the political possibilities of his office were too great. He aligned himself offensively and defensively with influential lawyers and all character of other interests, so that it required generations of reform to separate him from the power to dispense favors and protection to, as well as take vengeance upon, juror, litigant and lawyer alike. ...

Assume that the machinery of justice is now set up for the particular case. The next case, of course, must provide its own jury; each party has his own set of peers. But to the merits! The parties are nervous, the judge is impatient, the witnesses are jumpy, the members of the jury are eager to be about their business. Counsel are not so impatient. 

They know who must do the chief acting in this drama, and the time required in order to act to advantage. In the examination of the jury they have introduced themselves. Whether serious and dignified, smiling and friendly, blustering and bullying, suave and clever, blunt and brutal, or quiet and cautious, they have chosen their roles. They are now ready to make their second appearance in the opening statement to the jury.

The opening statement is for the purpose of preparing the jury for what is to follow by way of proof. It is an outline of the theory of the case from the opener's standpoint, together with a statement of what he expects to prove and how. It offers no mean opportunities to a skilled trial lawyer. There is no argument so telling as artistic statement, and here the plaintiff's counsel has the advantage of talking to the jurymen while they are still eager and fresh. The lawyer who knows his jury neither overdraws nor blurs this first picture, and its lines are ineradicable. The opening statement gives the plaintiff his first great chance to tell his story. He will have few others so dependably favorable. But when the plaintiff is through, the jury is whetted to hear what the defendant can say, and his story must be even more greatly told if he is to overcome the advantage of his opponent.... 

In a recent rape case the judge mistakenly gave the defendant the benefit of the defences of self-defence, adequate provacation, assault

with a deadly weapon, and sudden passion! Nevertheless the defendant was convicted. (Johnson v. State, 267 S. W. 713.) Only in those 

States in which the judge still has the Common Law power to comment upon the weight of evidence and advise with the jury is there any real place for this function. Yet the United States Senate has only recently passed the Caraway bill designed to deprive Federal judges of the power to advise and instruct juries. Thus, in the face of the most urgent appeals from all quarters for a better administration of the law, our highest legislative body would take away the power which has given trial by jury its chief, if not its only, dependability!

Closely associated with the charge of the court is the argument of counsel-one to six hours to the side, depending upon the importance of the case. Cases are won and lost at this stage. Counsel may overcome handicaps of prior errors, handicaps of poor witnesses, handicaps of every sort if he has skill in jury argument. Likewise counsel may lose the advantage of good generalship, the advantage of good witnesses, the advantages of just cause, if he lack skill at this point. No one knows what the factors are that enter into a jury's conclusions. 

No one can guess what a particular jury will do. The least that can be said is that trial by jury is a process of strategy, of matching wits, a battle of surprises and emotional struggles at best, and that in this sort of combat the point of merit is apt to be lost. ...

The argument on the issues is brief and pointed. There are no instructions to prepare, no verdict, no motion for a new trial except in the rarest instance. The judge either announces his conclusion, or else takes it under advisement for further study and later announcement. He may then file the findings which support is decision. The whole process is deflated until there is little left to do save get down to business. The trial of the same case before a judge and before the same judge with a jury, with the same lawyers, reflects, the most startling differences; but the differences are those of time and technique and errors. Judge and jury are generally in accord if the jury is not brought under some improper influence. If they disagree, it frequently results from some error or mistake which necessitates a new trial. 

The differences come in those close cases in which there can be no certain opinion, and in which two juries or two judges would as likely as not reach opposite conclusions.

Time is the most dependable of defences. It can be used both by meritorious and unmeritorious litigants. If a meritorious litigant does not ask for jury trial, his opponent usually does, and probably thereby secures his most effective defensive weapon. He can afford to wait and prefers to do so. His success depends upon adventitious factors. 

Anything may happen. Witnesses may scatter, death may intervene,

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

the memories of claimants and witnesses may grow dim, opponents may lose interest, counsel may become absorbed in more promising litigation, the feeling of injury and injustice may subside. 

But aside from all the handicaps that flow from mere delay, and from the prostitution of procedural devices, the chances for reversible error due to the treacherous steps of the extended process of jury trial are so great that no counsel for the defence can overlook the favorable hazards. This is contrary to the prevailing impression that the jury is the plaintiff's or poor man's friend. No doubt the individual juror is very favorable to the injured person, but despite that fact all available data show that defendants demand more juries than plaintiffs, and this is true even though a plaintiff normally exercises the first choice, thus relieving the defendant from making any choice at all. 

The reason is clear. At every turn a defendant may legitimately lie in ambush if he so desires, and most defendants do. 

The very favorable attitude of jurors for plaintiffs is justification enough for claiming all the protection the offsetting advantages afford. But there is in fact little basis to warrant the belief that jurors are more favorable to claimants than are judges. In a recent survey of 8800 Connecticut cases desposed of by the Superior Courts in 1925 and 1926, it was found that of the cases disposed of by jury trial plaintiffs were given judgment in only 50%, whereas in cases tried without juries plaintiffs received judgment in 75%. Approximately the same ratio held in negligence and contract cases, the two most important classes of jury litigation. In bona fide cases, judges seem to have more sympathy for a plaintiff's claim than juries. I have heard defence counsel frequently express the opinion that to a bona fide claimant the judge will be more intelligently liberal than a jury, and this, plus the fact that a judge can protect his conclusions better than a jury, accounts for the defendants claiming jury trial. ...

What is the situation with jury cases alone? How much of normal jury business is disposed of by jury trials? A report of the Special

Calendar Committee appointed by the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court of New York, First Department, made June 20, 1927, gives about as good an answer to this question as can be found. The report in part says:

A brief reference to the business pending in the three courts just before the January (1927) call will make this clear. There were pending in the Supreme Court of New York County 29,466 cases triable for jury; and a case could not ordinarily be reached for trial for 22 months after it was at issue. There were pending in the 

Supreme Court for Bronx County 9,562 cases triable by jury; and a case could not be reached for trial for 24 months after it was at issue. There were pending in the City Court of New York County about 18,000 cases triable by jury, the delay in reaching a case for trial being 16 months. At the same time there were pending

in the Municipal Courts 59,086 cases triable by jury, and a case could not be reached for trial in crowded districts for a year and a half. 

And further:

It is a significant circumstance that a large proportion of cases placed on the calendar are not disposed of by inquest or jury trial. 

Taking the year 1924, for example, as showing the recent trend, it appears that in that year out of 15,923 cases disposed of, 12,147 or over 76% were disposed of without trial, by discontinuance, abatement, reference or otherwise.. In the years 1919 to 1923, inclusive, the cases disposed of without a jury trial varied between 70 and 75%, except in 1923, when it rose to 80% of the cases which disappeared from the calendar. The presence of the cases on the general calendar undoubtedly caused uncertainty and delay in reaching those of later issues which were awaiting an opportunity for trial. ...

The recent survey made by the Yale Law School covering approximately 8800 civil cases disposed of by the Superior Courts of Connecticut during 1925 and 1926 disclosed: (1) That juries were had in only 38% of the cases tried in which juries might have been had; (2) That approximately only 13% (232 in number) of all the cases tried (excluding uncontested divorces, uncontested foreclosures and suits for change of name) were tried by juries. 

No doubt these two facts account in very large part for the comparatively uncongested condition of the dockets of these courts. 

The figures are remarkable in that they disclose that jury trial is little more than a bad habit, and yet one that serves to clog our courts so that only a fraction of the jury business can actually be done by juries. It is significant that we hear little complaint about delays in non-jury cases, despite the fact that there is small time for them, since the time of the courts is taken up very largely by jury cases. It is a safe conclusion that were it not for trial by jury, our present judges could clear their dockets and dispose of all the new business as it came to them, and that the number of judges could be reduced. Clearly it would be impossible to enlarge the judiciary sufficiently, or furnish enough courtroom facilities, jurors, attendants, sheriffs and the like, to give prompt attention to all the jury cases docketed without disrupting the general business of the community. Our court machinery is already extravagantly large, so much so that it uses up much of its energy in the mere operation of the machine. 

What of the cost? Little need be said here. Anything that prevents the machinery of justice from functioning properly is expensivemore so than we can calculate. Assuming honest and most effective administration, the time consumed in jury trials, as shown by the Connecticut survey, is more than twice that required in trials by judges. The difference in getting to trial is even greater, the cost of obtaining a jury and keeping it until a verdict is reached, the time of

the judge and court attendants, the additional time of the parties, lawyers, and witnesses, the greater facilities offered by trial before a judge in allowing adjournments and accommodations to parties and witnesses, the reduction in appellate reviews incident to the more informal court trial, all make the cost of jury trial anywhere from three to eight times that of any other mode of trial now employed. . ...

The litigation of the earlier centuries, as I have said, involved simple issues, simply fought out under relatively simple theories of law, and the fight was in the open and the methods of counsel were obvious. 

All this has changed. Elaborately trained counsel now have at their command the zeal of the publicist, and the machinery of the press, supported by the skill and daring of the sleuth, and the laboratories of the chemist, physicist, engineer, psychologist, psychiatrist, biologist, and all the other scientists. It is no longer only the hypothetical question that gives the courtroom its odor of science. It is no longer merely the ingenuity of one smart lawyer at work; a staff of specialists is at his command.

Clients are demanding and receiving all the aid the scientific world can give their cases. Time and money pile up enormously. The data not infrequently assume aspects as far above the understanding of the every-day citizen as modern science is beyond the science of the Fourteenth Century. Under these conditions, honesty and ability to read and write are no longer enough to meet the demands of a modern law-suit. The average jury in any case of difficulty is about as helpful as it would be in solving a problem in the higher mathematics, in industrial finance, or in electrical engineering.

As a formula for administering justice trial by jury is merely a societal antique. But it typifies something back in the growth of society which has been gripped by man's emotions and they will not let it go. 

Its processes radiate a flavor of popular justice and a flourish of democracy. Those are still stout words. But the fact is that in the organism of society, as in the organisms of all life, there are structural parts which no longer serve useful functions. They reappear nevertheless in succeeding generations. They are sometimes removed from the physical organism seldom submits. To this the social organism seldom submits. Moreover, the intelligence that would do so in this instance would not stop with the jury's removal; it would demand more cutting.

NOTES ON JUDICIAL ORGANIZATION AND PROCEDURE 

By 

J. A. C. Grant (From "The American Political Science Review," February, 1930, edited by Walter F. Dodd, Yale Law School. Reprinted by special permission of the publishers and the author.) 

Although to Blackstone the trial jury seemed "to have been coeval with the first civil government" of England, more scholarly research has shown it to be of Norman origin. Such research has likewise pointed out marked changes in the character of the jury itself. Whereas we think of it as a group of individuals having neither knowledge of the facts nor bias as to the parties, who are to hear the evidence and decide according to its weight, the early jurors were chosen because of their knowledge of the facts. They did not hear witnesses; they were the witnesses. Glanville tells us that the first function of the court was to ascertain, by their oath, whether any of the jurors summoned were ignorant of the fact in issue. "If there be any such, they are rejected and others chosen." How remarkably different is the situation today, when we have need of appellate decisions to settle the right of jurors to avail themselves of their "common knowledge and experience" in passing upon the question of whether a fact is logically deducible from the circumstances in evidence! And how strangely out of keeping is the Illinois decision, reversing the conviction of a forty-four-year-old confessed criminal of statutory rape because the only evidence showing that he was over sixteen years of age, aside from his confession, was the fact that the jury had seen him, and "the law does not allow the jury to fix his age by inspecting his person." One similarity, however, remains: except under extraordinary circumstances, the jury must be chosen from the county in which the crime was committed or the action brought. 

Having abandoned the requirement that jurors should be personally acquainted with the facts of the case, the common law still clung to the desire to secure jurors whose special training and experience would fit them to decide the particular type of questions involved. The books abound in examples of such special juries, e. g., those of “cooks and fishmongers" to hear accusations of the sale of bad food, of "attorneys of Common Bench and Exchequer" to pass upon technical questions of law. A similar desire for specialists is to be noted today in the civil law countries that have adopted the criminal jury. A few years ago, a murder case in which the defense was to be emotional

insanity was before the French Court of Assizes. On the evening before the trial was to open the press announced that everyone would be pleased to hear that one member of the jury was to be a professor of diseases of the mind at the College de France, and one of the leading experts of the world in this field. If such a juror were called in an American court, he would be eliminated by challenge, since "the American jury lawyer, where insanity is an issue, does not want anyone on the jury who knows more of the subject than he does." The same is true in fields other than medicine, with the result that the special jury has long been moribund.

The special jury soon crystallized into its modern form of a struck jury. As described by Blackstone, the clerk, in the presence of the parties, selected the names of 48 freeholders. Each party then struck (crossed out) the names of twelve, and the remaining twenty-four were returned upon the panel. By this time the motive for requesting such a jury was more often a desire to escape the absolute control of a biased sheriff than to secure a group of experts. 

Whatever may be said for the effectiveness of the common law method of jury selection during the earlier period of its use, there can be no doubt that it outlived the period of its sufficiency. Blackstone praised it, to be sure, as “avoiding. . . . frauds and secret management" through electing the twelve jurors by lot out of a panel chosen by an "indifferent officer." But what of the panel? And there was room for doubt as to the "avoidance of frauds and secret management" in the selection of the twelve, as well as to the "indifference" of the sheriff. The professional juror was an accepted fact. Packed juries were easily possible, which naturally caused a good deal of suspicion; and experience was even then showing that the personal followers of the sheriff are not always of the highest type. In more progressive jurisdictions, where a jury commission or jury judge with investigative powers is employed, the list of eligible persons would only be a preliminary step in the preparation of a final list. 

The Milwaukee and Baltimore systems may be taken as typical examples of such methods at their best.

The Milwaukee commission consists of three electors chosen by the judges of the Circuit Court. Although appointments are for three years, the present commissioners have served for ten years, seven years, and one year, respectively, the newest member succeeding her husband, who had served for thirteen years. Starting with the poll lists, each name is checked against the city directory and the files of the commission to eliminate those who are exempt by occupation or prior service. Candidates are then selected at random from the various wards and notified by post-card to appear before the commission. If the card is ignored, a subpoena is issued. When those summoned

appear, about sixty each evening, they fill out a short questionnaire giving information as to age, occupation, education, period of residence, interest in pending litigation, prior service, etc. This is done under the eye of the bailiff. Claims for exemption by way of occupation .or sex must be entered at this time; otherwise they are waived. Each candidate then appears individually before the commissioners, who ask further questions to test intelligence, mental alertness, hearing, eyesight, ability to understand English, etc. He is then excused, without being told whether or not he has been accepted. This will never be known by any but the commissioners until such time as he is actually summoned upon a venire. If not summoned, it may never be known.

The Supreme Bench of Baltimore has eleven departments, of which seven are ordinarily engaged in the trial of jury cases. Each year one member of the court is designated as jury judge, with the duties ordinarily devolving upon a jury commission, in addition to those of summoning and impaneling jurors. Once each year this judge devotes from three to four weeks in preparing the jury list. Candidates are summoned from the various districts, their names being picked at random from the tax lists of the county, to appear for examination on their choice of two or three named days. Upon appearance, they go through much the same process as in Milwaukee, including the filling out of a questionnaire, followed by an oral examination by the judge. When a sufficient number of candidates have been accepted, they are divided into groups according to the time of the year in which they prefer to serve. Every three weeks 400 names are drawn by chance, and these candidates again appear before the judge, who selects 175, or twenty-five per court, as the panel for the period. 

There seems little to choose between the Milwaukee and Baltimore systems from the point of view of efficiency. However, it is evident that were it not that the panel chosen by the Baltimore judge is to be divided by chance among seven judges, the wisdom of such complete control over the selection of the final panel might well be doubted. 

Others will be of the opinion that if the first examination is all that it should be, the second is merely a fifth wheel to the cart. There is also no reason why a jury commission should not be as efficient and impartial as a judge, leaving the latter free to spend more of his time in the trial of cases. But the facts being that many jury commissions are neither efficient nor impartial, many jurisdictions will find it to their advantage to copy the Baltimore rather than the Milwaukee sysem. ...

In the great majority of jurisdictions jurors are still summoned by a writ of venire facias directed to the sheriff. There would seem to be no reason for this, save that it has always been so. The system is

inefficient as well as costly, the proportion of names returned non est inventus (not found) often being large. The Illinois Crime Survey found that "deputy sheriffs frequently serve jury summons by leaving them in a mail box or under the door of the juror's supposed residence," and concluded that service by registered mail would have all of the advantages and few of the disadvantages of service by the sheriff. 

This has ben the experience of the courts now following the practice. 

Among other advantages, Chief Justice Powell, of the Cleveland Court of Common Pleas, states that "if a juror has changed his residence. . . the post office forwards the letter to him and we reach many in this way who could not be reached by service of the sheriff." In addition, such evils as the excusing of jurors by deputy sheriffs and charges of partiality in the returning of the panel are avoided. 

Service by mail is a relatively new development and has been very favorably received. We may look for a rather rapid adoption of the practice as it is brought to the attention of other courts by our judicial councils, although in most states legislation will be necessary to allow its use.

North Carolina has authorized summons by telephone as well as by mail; but the writer is not informed as to what advantage has been taken of the statute. The telephone would seem to be a logical supplement to the post in many jurisdicitons. • .. 

As each case is called, the names of all jurors who are present and not already engaged are placed in a box, and the first twelve drawn are presented to the parties for examination and challenge. Challenges are spoken of as to the array (entire panel) or to the polls (individual juror). The former are based upon some serious irregularity in the selection of the panel, and their sole result is delay the case until a new panel is summoned. Challenges to the polls may be either peremptory (without cause stated) or for cause, such as bias, relationship, or dealings with the parties, knowledge of the material facts of the case, or a lack of some legal qualification for jury service. 

Perhaps no other single cause has had greater effect in bringing jury trial into disrepute than the abuse by counsel of the voir dire examination, which is the questioning of prospective jurors for the purpose of establishing grounds for challenge. Although the magnitude of this abuse has been over-emphasized, extreme cases are entirely too frequent; and it is such cases that catch the public eye. Too frequently the questioning has no legitimate motive, but is purely for purpose of delay, or even to prevent the selection of really competent jurors. 

The last few years have witnessed a decided change in attitude toward the proper degree of control by the judge. One of the first recommendations of the federal Board of Senior Circuit Judges was that the "examination. . . . shall be by the judge alone. If counsel on either side desires that additional matter be inquired into, he shall

state the matter to the judge, and the judge, if the matter is proper, shall conduct the investigaion. Such had long been the practice in many jurisdictions, and with the prestige added by the support of Chief Justice Taft and the senior circuit judges it has been adopted rather widely by federal courts. Granted proper confidence in the judge, there is no reason why the rule should not meet every possible requirement; and its efficiency cannot be doubted. But if any one is laboring under the illusion that this practice is about to become universal in our American courts, let him look into the results that followed its suggestion by the judicial council of Kansas for the courts of that state. Before such a change can come about, there must be a wholesale repeal of statutes, and also the development of an entirely new attitude toward the proper function of the judge in the conduct of a jury case.

Many courts have adopted the practice of having the judge examine all prospective jurors, but allowing the counsel thereupon to ask such questions as may appear proper. This can be done without any change in the law, and its result is generally the same as under the rule sug gested by the Board of Senior Circuit Judges. Chief Justice Powell states that in the Cleveland Court of Common Pleas, where this practice has been followed for some years, the usual time in qualifying a jury is from five to ten minutes. Under such circumstances, no attorney feels justified in conducting a fishing expedition, if for no other reason than because the reaction of the jurors toward this type of conduct would place him at a disadvantage. If the attorneys for both parties are required to question a given juror or waive examination before passing to another, an additional saving results in the elimination of the unnecessary repetition of questions. ...

Although in many states special juries are still provided for by law, they are virtually never used. To the antipathy of the bench for the expert juror and the prohibitive expense involved in summoning separate jurors for each case must be added the practical difficulties illustrated by Bruce V. Beall, where the specialists in "iron and wire cables" summoned by the court "were each. . . . found to have a good, valid reason to be excused from serving." In our search for expert fact-finders we are turning to commercial arbitration and administrative justice, which would seem to be the logical solution. 

The struck jury has been remodeled into a refined method of handling peremptory challenges, and in this form it is receiving wide acceptance. In the practice of the Baltimore Supreme Bench, twenty jurors are presented to the parties. The judge or clerk questions them, and if any are found disqualified they are replaced by others. Each party then strikes four from the list, and the twelve remaining are sworn to try the case. In many jurisdictions all juries are chosen in this manner;

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

elsewhere, as in Philadelphia, it is adopted at the request of either party. A fairer or more expeditious manner of handling peremptory challenges would be hard to devise, but as yet the plan does not appear to have been applied to the class of cases where the peremptory challenge is of greatest importance, i. e., the trial of felonies. 

Another modern use of the struck jury, where its purpose is more akin to that of Blackstone's day, is in the court of the justice of the peace. Since a jury is seldom required, no regular panel is in attendance, and until such time as the justice is authorized to borrow a jury from a neighboring court the only alternative seems to be to fall back upon common law methods. To prevent too great a degree of control from resting with the justice or the constable, it is commonly provided that the court shall present a list of some eighteen names to the parties, each of whom shall strike six, the remaining six being summoned as the jury for the case. If talesman are required, they are chosen in the same manner. Needless to say, the practice is not a satisfactory one.

One of the most efficient methods of handling juries in a large court is in use in the Cleveland Court of Common Pleas. Only 24 more jurors than are needed to give each judge a jury of twelve are summoned on a given venire. These names, close to 200 in all, are placed in a jury wheel in charge of the assignment commissioner. When a case is called and sent to a given department for trial, 18 jurors, drawn from this wheel, accompany the parties. The trial judge examines these on their voir dire, counsel being permitted to ask additional questions if they desire, and ineligible candidates are replaced by others. Each party then strikes three names, and the six whose services are not required return to the assembly room, their names being replaced in the wheel ready for the drawing of the next jury. 

If a jury is not secured from the first eighteen, additional names are drawn from the wheel.

The Cleveland method has a number of very obvious advantages. 

Fewer jurors are needed, yet talesmen are almost unknown. Jury tampering in advance of the trial is next to impossible. As one jury retires to consider its verdict another is chosen for the trial of the next case. Justice Fritz, under a similar scheme in Milwaukee, has on several occasions had two juries out deliberating while still another was hearing evidence in a third case; and the writer understands that similar experiences are quite common in Baltimore. When Presiding 

Judge Ward, of the San Francisco Superior Court, copied the Cleveland plan in 1925, in an attempt to enable his court to catch up with its work, the results were so striking that the system was adoped on a permanent basis, and it is now provided for in the rules promulgated by the judicial council. The writer knows of no instance in which this practice, once adopted, has been abandoned.

WHY A SUBSTITUTE FOR TRIAL BY JURY? 

By 

Frank G. Guittard, B.A., LL.B., Member of the Bar, Victoria, Texas (Written especially for The Debaters Research) Whenever reformers seek to do away with a time-honored institution, the chief argument which they use against it is that it has become outworn and that it has served its purpose. When the friends of trial by jury say that its long service has proved its worth, opponents of the jury system counter by saying that the age of an institution is no criterion by which to judge its value, and that we should forget the past and build anew. These would-be destroyers of the jury system try to draw a parallel between trial by jury and the ox cart and buggy, saying that the latter have been supplanted by more modern means of transportation, and that likewise trial by jury is the relic of a past civilization, an impediment in the path of judicial progress. By this reasoning the age of trial by jury, which shows its worth more than anything else, is actually used as an argument against its continuance. 

If an institution has been able to function for some thousand years, under all sorts of civilizations, despite changes in government, under kings and under republics, in the middle ages and in modern times, has not this long and honorable career of service proved its worth? 

There are substantial reasons why trial by jury has held its place so long, and why it has been able to exist and function under such varying conditions. It is not a mechanical, changeless thing such as the ox cart and the buggy, which have been unable to survive in the advance of modern life. A jury is composed of men, of average men who reflect the spirit of the times and who represent the mass of people from whom they are drawn. Human beings are infinitely capable of adapting themselves to new situations and new civilizations. For this reason trial by jury is able to keep abreast of modern progress.

No institution not fundamentally sound can long survive. The fundamental soundness of the principles underlying trial by jury explain its survival. One of these principles is that the average man is a better judge of his neighbour and knows more about him than anyone else. We may be able to deceive judges and elected officers, people of a different class from us or all of one class, and we may be able to conceal our motives from people who come from different

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

walks of life and whose interests are not our interests, but our neighbours understand our motives and can explain our actions because they have the same interests and the same motives. 

Another principle that makes the jury an ideal fact-finding body is that the average jury does not represent any one class or profession. 

When it is correctly constituted, the jurors are drawn from all walks of life and represent a cross-section of the populace. They have the average sense of justice of the whole people. With twelve men the various emotions, prejudices, and standards are balanced. The jurors examine the facts from a composite viewpoint, and not from the viewpoint of case-hardened and legal-minded judges. 

It takes no training to judge of facts and to decide the issues in a controverted question of fact. Legal training is of no use here. In fact, legal training is even a drawback. Our system of jurisprudence is based upon the fundamental distinction between law and fact. The law is stated in the abstract, without reference to the facts of any particular controversy. The jurymen take the facts as they come from the mouths of witnesses and from other evidence, and draw their own conclusions. And the ability to draw conclusions and analyze given state of facts is not acquired by training; it must be inborn and innate. 

The judge takes the law, and applies it to the facts in the case. If either the jury or the judge is given too much share in this process of determining the facts and applying the law, a maladministration of justice is sure to result. This distinction between law and fact is so deeply imbedded in our legal system that to entrust the determination of both to the same person or persons could only result in confusion. 

One or two examples will show how unsatisfactory this arrangement would be. When the judge decides both law and fact, it is rather hard for the appellate court, in reviewing the case on appeal, to decide whether the judge of the lower court has committed an error in his ruling on the law, or an error in his finding on the facts. The fine, but important distinction between law and fact is apt to be lost sight of. Then there is another inconvenience which would result in case the determination of both law and fact were entrusted to the same person or body. Under the jury system, when an attorney asks a question which may lead the witness to give testimony which is inadmissable as evidence, the judge may ask the jury to retire from the room and then have the witness give his answer. From the answer the judge can determine the admissibility of the evidence out of the hearing of the jury. This is impossible when one person or body determines both law and facts. The jury makes possible a division of judicial labor. Our law is based upon precedents, upon the doctrine of stare decisis. To destroy the division of judicial labor and unite law and fact would overthrow a long train of well-settled precedents, and could only result in confusion.

There is now in use in some of the states, Texas, for example, a method of determining the facts called the special issue verdict. By this method, which is used solely in civil cases, the judge merely propounds questions to the jury. These questions are for the purpose of deciding material and controverted issues of fact raised by the pleadings and the evidence. As a rule the questions are so phrased that the jury can answer "Yes" or "No," or in figures or dollars and cents.

Although the system is rather recent, it has secured excellent results, and when it is better understood, the results will be even better. By the special issue verdict the jurors are usually kept in complete ignorance of the effect of their answers to the questions. This is recognized by the appellate courts of Texas by decisions to the effect that if a lawyer in his argument to the jury tells the jurors what the effect of their answer to a question will be, the case must be reversed. 

So the jury does not know as a rule whether they are rendering a verdict for the plaintiff or the defendant under the special issue verdict. 

Surely bias and prejudice could have no part in the decision. 

Even when the general charge and general verdict is used, the judge defines the law so clearly that the jury has no difficulty. Usually there is little actual definition of legal phrases. The general charge consists mostly of instructions to the jury that if they find a certain state of facts to be true, they shall render their verdict in a certain manner. 

The judge in his charge states the facts which would authorize a verdict for the plaintiff, or for the defendant, setting them forth in a hypothetical manner. The jury has only to look to the charge, to determine which state of fact is true, and to render verdict accordingly. 

Because of its composite character, a jury is able to administer standards of ordinary conduct. The law of negligence is based upon such standards. In defining negligence to the jury the judge says, "Negligence is the doing of some act that a person of ordinary prudence would not do, or the failure to do an act which a person of ordinary prudence would do, under the same or similar circumstances." A jury of ordinary men is better able to administer and apply such standards than are judges. Negligence suits are an ever-increasing part of our litigation, and the jury is needed in them to apply these standards of ordinary conduct.

The jury is able to render justice when the universality of the law would in many cases result in injustice. The law is written in the abstract and cannot fit every set of facts. According to cold and hard legal rules many a guiltless man might be oppressed, and many a guilty man might get less than his full measure of justice. The jury takes the cold, hard, abstract principles of the law and tempers justice with mercy. The jury takes cognizance of moral justification for acts which would otherwise be crimes.

DEBATERS RESEARCH 

Because the jury is a temporary body, it is free from political influence. No political boss can dictate to it, and the jurors need obey only the dictates of their own consciences. Because of its temporary nature, the jury is hard to bribe. Not until the very hour of the trial is a jury selected, and then it is kept under constant surveillance until the verdict is rendered. On the other hand, if the case is tried before a judge or a few judges, bribery is much easier. Without a jury, the judge to try the case would be known to all persons long before the trial, and there would be more time to exert pressure of various kinds upon him. There is no danger at present of the judge willfully making an error in the law in order to favor one party because the rulings on the law become a part of the record and are reviewable by the appellate court. But rulings on disputed questions of fact are not capable of being reviewed by the appellate court, because they are largely matters of opinion and because there are many things which induce belief or disbelief, such as the attitude of the witnesses, which cannot be brought up in the record for the appellate court to pass upon. For this reason it would be simple for the judge to side with one of the litigants on the facts and avoid being reversed. Of course findings of fact are equally conclusive when the jury and not a judge is used as a fact finding body. But because of the conclusive character of fact findings, it would be extremely unwise to entrust the decision of fact to a person or body more accessible to bribe than the jury. It must also be realized that although one bribed juror may hang a jury, bribing only one will not change the verdict; all twelve must be bribed. And that is much harder than bribing one or two judges.

The critics of trial by jury have pointed out many supposed defects in the system, but close reflection will show that these defects are not inherent, but are due to the inefficiency in administration of the jury system, or may be traceable to other parts of our legal system. It has frequently been said that the jurors are of low intelligence. This defect has been remedied in many states, and can be remedied in ours, if it exists. The federal courts have much better juries than the state courts. If the names of intelligent, responsible citizens are put in the jury wheel, good jurors are bound to be selected. By means of a jury commission, the standard of jurors can be raised. Likewise, the occasional delay in selecting a jury, which obtains in some states, is not a fundamental defect of the jury system, for this delay has been obviated in the English system and also in many of our federal courts. 

The jury has been criticized for rendering wrong verdicts, but the proponents of a system of judges to displace the jury have only to pick up a volume of reported decisions of appellate courts to see that judges are just as prone to err as jurors. The frequent reversals and new trials today are due chiefly to errors of the judge in his charge,

in ruling on the admissibility of evidence, or in making other rulings. 

Very few new trials are caused by the jury. Whatever system is substituted for trial by jury, it will be some human system. And whatever defects trial by jury has, these defects are the inherent defects of human nature, and would obtain in any human system. 

Trial by jury is the last means of active participation in the affairs of the government which the people retain today. It is their last remnant of pure democracy. They have entrusted the executive power to governors, presidents, and other officials. They have delegated the legislative power to representatives. Only this last phase of the judicial power, that of deciding questions of fact in a lawsuit, remains as a means of direct expression of the will and privilege of the people. To deprive the people of this important phase of government would rob them of their last protection. The juries now afford a protection against usurpation of power by the judges. The opponents of trial by jury will say that no protection is needed in this day of democracy, that trial jury was originated to protect the people against the despotic power of kings, and that today there is nothing to protect against. 

Special privilege, monopoly, wealth, and the abuse of governmental authority today present just as urgent a necessity for protection as did the tyranny of the kings of old England in days past. Although in some states an accused man may have the option of being tried before a judge alone or before a jury, no free man who calls himself an American citizen would be willing to surrender his right to be judged by a jury of his peers. The right of trial by jury should remain forever inviolate.
