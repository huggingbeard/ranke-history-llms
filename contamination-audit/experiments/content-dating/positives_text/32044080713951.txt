II. REPORT OR CONTROL CENTERS A. PURPOSE OF REPORT CENTERS 1. Report Centers are the vital headquarters in cities and towns; through them Air Raid Warnings are received and needed assistance dispatched. 

2. They are generally operated by City and Town Committees on 

Public Safety, and are usually established by the Chief Air Raid Warden and directed by him. B.

PLACE OF REPORT CENTERS IN THE AIR RAID WARNING SYSTEM 

1. Army Observation Posts every 32 square miles pick up approaching enemy planes. 

2. Posts notify Army Filter Stations; three in New England. 3.

Filter Stations notify Army Information Center in Boston, only one in New England. 

4. Army Information Center notifies State District Warning 

District Warning Centers sometimes notify Sub-District Warning Centers. Centers. a.

Each District Warning Center is commanded by a Con troller, appointed by the Local or City Defense Chairman where the District Warning Center is located. District Warning Centers are not the property of a City or Town authority, although located in cities and towns. Since their responsibility for distributing "alerts" crosses local boundaries, they are usually stateoperated and owned.

The terms Report Center and Control Center are synonymous.

It is necessary that there be discipline among the Wardens in order that cooperation between the public and the Air 

Raid Precaution Services be insured.

C. GENERAL DUTIES 1. Reporting Damage. The Warden is the connecting link between the citizen, his home and the "services." It is only through the 

Warden's report form that these services are sent out by the Report Center. The report must be brief, correct and clear. Constant drilling in filling out the Warden's report forms cannot be over-emphasized. 

Detailed instructions will follow on filling out these forms but it is important to remember to state what has happened, rather than to ask for any service. These forms are written at the scene of the incident and then telephoned in to the Report Center.

Warden does not have police powers and, therefore, in an emergency usually must request police assistance. 

6. Give assistance and advice to householders on blackout, refuge rooms, protection against incendiary bombs and how to control them and the equipment of first aid boxes.

3. How to Choose and Equip a Refuge Room. Briefly in regard to this subject, the following should be emphasized:

In most houses a room in the cellar will serve the purpose of a refuge room. It is essential that the ceiling be propped up so that in case of a direct hit it will be able to hold up falling debris. It is desirable to have two exits in case one is blocked. These may be either windows, doors, bulkheads or even an enlarged coal chute. Any windows in the refuge room not being used should be sand-bagged and those necessary for light and ventilation should be carefully screened. It is preferable to have a room which faces soft ground because the bomb will penetrate deeper and the trajectory of blast is higher.

Equipment for a refuge room should include a crowbar, pickaxe and shovel so that the occupants of the room can aid the rescue party by starting to dig their way out from the inside. It is necessary to have warm clothes and blankets because the heating of a refuge room is often difficult. Fresh food and water should be stored in the room in case it is necessary for the occupants to stay a long period of time. There should be some sort of sanitary arrangement, flashlights, candles and matches in case the electricity is knocked out. A first aid kit should be equipped with not only the standard materials but also those necessary for first aid for gas casualties. A sterno stove, portable radio and material for recreation are also desirable. A strong kitchen table is an extremely good thing under which to take refuge in case the ceiling starts to collapse because of the weight of debris above it. Firefighting equipment may also be kept here but as a rule it is better to have it near the top floor in case of an incendiary bomb attack.

Warden. It is important that the Warden become acquainted with all the people in his sector and that they have faith in his judgment. householder should be trained to report to his sector post any change Every in address or, in fact, all his comings and goings. Each household

9. Precautions in War Time. The Warden must explain to his householders the need for getting into public shelters if it is impossible for them to get home when the air raid warning is sound- This may best be explained by percentage of danger. ing. 

WOOD WALL 

RELATIVE DANGER DURING AN AIR RAID BRICK WALL SAND BAGS 

100% danger exists for a person walking on the street; 50% danger for one lying in the gutter; 25% danger for one taking refuge by squatting in the vestibule of a house where there is no glass; 

15% danger inside a house and 5% danger in a prepared refuge room. 

The householders must be told to keep their bathtubs and all other available receptacles constantly full of water in case they have to fight an incendiary bomb and water mains have been broken. They should not wait until the alarm to do this. It is advisable to keep windows and doors on the top floor closed, so there will be no draft in case an incendiary bomb penetrates the roof and starts a fire.

One member of each family should be assigned to watch for incendiary bombs. If this is not done, it is possible for an entire house to be gutted by fire while the family are in the refuge room in the cellar. 

It is important that the householder be instructed to stay away from windows during a raid because of the danger from blast, machine gun fire and flying glass. 

10. Car Parking. At the alarm, cars should be parked in an orderly fashion at least 10 feet apart and out of the way of the services. They should be left unlocked in case they have to be moved, but with the ignition off. 11.

Importance of Looking for Delayed Action Bombs. Whenever possible the individual householder should be taught to search his grounds for delayed action bombs after a raid. This will relieve the Wardens of having to cover the entire area and will save their time for other duties. 

12. Anti-aircraft Shells. The civilian population should regard anti-aircraft shells as if they were small delayed action bombs and should never touch them under any circumstances. 13.

Draw up and have typed and put on the bulletin board in the Warden's post the method of local gas alarm and all clear and the positions of first aid posts, decontamination stations and hostels for the homeless.

REPORT FORMS 

These forms must be brief, correct and clear. This is simply a statement of what has occurred and should not be cluttered up with useless information.

Usually the report form should be written at the scene of the incident and then telephoned in from the Warden's post. 

However, if the Warden deems it necessary, he may in extreme cases act on the incident and write the report form afterwards. In any case, a report form for every incident must be handed in to the sector post. 

1. When telephoning, start with the words "Air Raid Damage" to identify the call as the report of an incident. 

2. The sector number is given. This is also a means of identification and should give the Warden's precinct and sector in the precinct. 

3. The location of damage is given by common street and number address. If it is on the corner of two streets, it should be indicated by giving both names, example: "corner of Boylston and Gloucester."

The "location of damage" is the first of four places in the report form in which liquid poison gas must be mentioned. 

4. Type of Bomb. This is simply to check whether it is a high explosive, an incendiary or a poison gas bomb. If it is liquid poison gas, the street should be closed to traffic and only the decontamination squad allowed to come in. This is the second place on the report form where liquid poison gas is mentioned. 

5. The approximate number of casualties. If any are trapped under wreckage an attempt must be made to estimate the number. The household registry is useful here. The Warden should know his sector well enough to approximate how many people are in each house.

I. GAS ATTACK 

When a Warden receives the gas signal from an adjacent sector, he should put on his gas mask at once. 

1. If up-wind, contact the sector from which the alarm is heard in order to find out what kind of gas it is. Then give the local gas alarm, especially if it is mustard (H.S.). Do not under any circumstances report unless it is your incident. 

2. If down-wind, give the local gas alarm before you contact the next sector to find out what kind of gas it is. 

If gas is discovered in your sector, put on gas mask, having first tried to identify the gas. Give the local gas alarm, test the

wind direction, then write out the report form. Be very careful to mention the gas in the four places indicated on the report form. Then contact the senior Warden of the sector to see if the gas alarm should be relayed to the next sector.

J. HOW TO CHOOSE A SECTOR WARDEN'S POST 

The basic unit of Civilian Defense is a Sector containing the homes of about 500 people. This is controlled by a Warden's Post, staffed by a Senior Warden and three or more Assistant Wardens. 

1. Wherever possible the Sector Warden should select a room in an existing building, preferably in the central part of the sector; it may be a room in the basement or on a ground floor of a house in a residential district. 

2. This post is the point of assembly for all Wardens, messengers, and personnel and must be large enough to accomodate them. 

3. An attemat should be made to gas-proof the Warden's post. 4.

Post must be plainly marked for the public, as it serves as an information booth. A self-luminous sign of some sort is necessary in the dark. 

5. A post will ordinarily be manned by 3 to 6 Wardens, not including specialists. There should be 6 on duty and 18 off during a "stand-by". All Wardens report when the pre-arranged signal is received.

M. WARDEN'S DRILL 

Once again it should be stressed that only about 40% of what should be taught can be included in this manual. The other 60% of training must be done by demonstration and drill in cooperation with the other Air Raid Precaution Services, and by familiarizing the householders with necessary procedure by means of constant drills. 

The unit drill starts with the Warden's Sector Post. The 

Chief Air Raid Warden, the Zone Warden and the Sector Warden consult as to the position of incidents in the sector, planning them on the sector map and executing their plans by putting down small bags of sand of different colors to represent different types of bombs, namely: black for high explosive, red for incendiary, and yellow for poison gas. The description of the incident is written on a tag and attached to the appropriate bomb. The Wardens of the sector are told to meet in their posts at a certain time, synchronize their watches, and at zero hour go out to their ordinary patrols.

When they find the bomb, they write out their Warden's report form, dispatch it to the Sector Post to be telephoned to the Report Center. It increases the interest of the Wardens if the Chief Warden can arrange for simulated services to arrive on the scene, being dispatched from the Report Center in cars suitably labeled as "Fire Engine", "Ambulance", or whatever unit the Warden has indicated that he needs. 

The Warden should follow the same procedure as he would in an actual raid.

The first exercises should always be done in daylight hours. 

When several sectors have practiced these exercises separately, they should then combine and have incidents staged in six sectors simultaneously (all sectors being in the same zone), following the same procedure of having cars dispatched from the Report Center. Zone Wardens and 

Sector Wardens from other parts of the town, taking part in the incident, should be asked to act as umpires, one stationed in the vicinity of each incident to report on the general action of the Warden. He must not give any hints or suggestions to the Warden, but should simply make a report. That is the only sure way that Wardens' training can be completed.

When these exercises are over, all the Wardens and their officers who have taken part in the exercises and also the umpires, should have a conference and discuss how it can be improved. Later on, as training has taken in more and more posts and sones, there should be a complete test of the whole town and the actual services should be called out. 

It can then be arranged to have some individuals act as casualties in each incident, with a tag attached to say where they are injured. In that way Auxiliary First Aid Stretcher Bearers and Ambulance Corps can be exercised.

When it comes to town exercises and practicing with the city services, the heads of each of these services should be called to a preliminary conference to work out incidents. The entire town organization then takes part in the exercises, mock casualties being brought into the appropriate First Aid or Main Casualty Station. The emergency organization in each one of these places should go through its routine.

Supplementary Information on The Incident Officer, New Material Added November 11, 1942 

This service was not in operation at the beginning of raids over England but gradually evolved and is now essential for the efficient handling of large incidents during air raids. 

While each service (fire, rescue, medical and utility repair squads) worked efficiently at its own work, there was a lack of cooperation which led to overlapping of effort and waste of time and equipment. Each service tended to see the incident from its own standpoint, with insufficient regard for the requirements of the other services, such as vehicles drawn up by fire hydrants, delay in obtaining additional reinforcements, the controller in the control center not being given essential information, and services not released promptly when their work was completed. 

Post Wardens could handle small incidents in their sectors but when large areas are involved, with three or more services needed, the warden had neither the training nor the authority to act as liaison officer and general representative of the controller. 

The general qualifications of an Incident Officer are: 1. 

A thorough knowledge of the Civilian Defense setup--local, group and regional. 

2. A thorough grounding in anti-gas precautions and identifications. 

3. Knowledge of all types of Incendiary Bombs and fire fighting. 

4. Knowledge of types of High Explosive bombs and bomb reconnaissance.* *See 0. C. D. Handbook, "Bomb Reconnaissance", August, 1942

A general knowledge of the duties and requirements of all the essential services with which he will come into contact. 

Ability to keep calm under trying and difficult circumstances. 

Resourcefulness to enable him to deal with unexpected difficulties.

A complete knowledge of wardens' duties. 

When the controller considers an incident too serious to be handled by the sector warden, he sends an incident officer to take charge. 

When the Incident Officer arrives at the occurrence he contacts the sector warden and receives all information available. The warden remains as his assistant and messenger. In incidents covering a large area it is necessary for several wardens to act in this capacity. 

The Incident Officer sets up a point of communication with Control Center and gives controller the phone number. Then he makes a quick reconnaissance of the damaged area, fixes a rally point for the services if the warden has not done so, and remains at his communication or Central Point until the services arrive. He explains the situation to leaders and gives them necessary local information. they want any streets blocked off, he requests the police to do so.

The Incident Officer is required to keep in constant communication with the leader of every service present so that he has at any one time a complete picture of the incident. 

Also he must keep in close contact with the Controller, who is thus conversant with the incident and any major development. Each service is required to regard the Incident Officer as the link between it and every other service, and with the Controller.

The service vehicles are parked on the instructions of the Incident Officer with a proper regard for the duties and priority of working of each class of service. The duty of the Incident Officer, therefore, can be summed up as co-ordination of services.

INCIDENT CONTROL 

Incident control means the co-ordination at an incident of the efforts of the various services (police, fire, wardens, first aid, medical, rescue, etc.) by a trained and responsible officer with a view to saving life, preventing injury, and clearing up the incident as quickly and safely as possible. 

Whoever acts as Incident Officer or co-ordinating officer at an occurrence is, for the time being, the representative of the Controller on the spot, and the effectiveness of a local civilian defense organization is largely measured by the efficiency of control at the scene of air raid damage.

The Incident Officer is responsible for maintaining communica tions with control center. All reports to control center must be made by him or have his approval. The Incident Officer is not bound by the confines of the warden's report form. He can, if he wishes, speak by telephone directly to the Controller in the control center. 

All communications from Control Center to the scene of the damage must be made to him. He must be fully informed concerning the strength and work of all services on the spot. He must know what wants doing, where and when it wants doing, and who best can do it. 

The Incident Officer is the axis around which revolve the functions of several services at the scene of air raid damage. He is the agent of the controller, issuing instructions for and on his behalf, sending all information to the control center.

VI FIRE FIGHTING 

Fire is an enemy which does not confine its power to destroy, war alone.. The peacetime fire record of our country shows excessive and unnecessary loss of life and property. A large proportion of the fires which cause such destruction could be avoided if people were properly educated in the procedure to take to eliminate fire hazards and thus prevent fires; also to know how to control incipient fires and extinguish them without becoming excited and allowing them to get out of control.

In an incendiary raid, householders will not be able to place entire dependence upon the fire department. If an important war plant is hit and the safety of the plant is endangered by ensuing fire, all fire fighting forces will be concentrated at the plant in an effort to save it. It is, therefore, important that all civilians learn all they can about fire prevention and the control of fire. 

FIRE PROTECTION 

1. 

In planning protection for the home, first consideration should be given to the amount of water available. Keep all available receptacles filled with water. It is possible that public water mains might be broken by high explosive bombs or pressure lowered by the draft of fire department pumpers fighting a fire in some other part of the community. 

2. There should be at least one stirrup pump, or equivalent piece of apparatus for putting water onto a fire, in every household. 

3. Members of every household should work out a plan of action in the event of fire. Drills should be carried out at frequent intervals in order that every individual responsible for certain duties would, in an emergency, execute them quickly and effectively, without losing his head.

FIRE PREVENTION 

1. 

Clear attic, cellar and cupboards of all unnecessary combustible material. Such material is not only a fire hazard, but seriously hinders fire fighting.

2. 

Attic floors can be covered with dry sand, if the floor is sufficiently strong to hold it, to a depth of 2 to 2 in.* 

3. Application of a fire retardant paint to roof timbers, trusses, partitions, etc., can be made inexpensively, consisting of Sodium 

Silicate (water glass), Kaolin (China clay), and water. The amount needed and the correct proportions of ingredients can be computed by any painter. This paint is only a fire retardant, and fires in such areas must still be dealt with promptly." 

4. During an air attack, keep all windows and doors closed tightly to prevent drafts from spreading fire should an incendiary bomb penetrate the house and start one. 

5. Eliminate fire hazards such as frayed insulation on electric wires, sooty chimneys, dirty furnace flues, oily rags, etc. Maintain good house-keeping at all times. 

FIRE FIGHTING 

1. When attacking a fire, direct the stream of water from the hose into the heart of fire, that is directly upon the material which is burning. It is important to get as much water as possible immediately upon the burning material to cool it sufficiently to stop combustion. 

2. Great care should be exercised when entering a burning room. 

If the door opens outward, place the foot in such a way as to control the swing of the door should it be blown outward by the pressure of the accumulated gases of combustion within the room. When entering a smoke-filled room, keep as close to the floor as possible without allowing the body to get into a helpless position. The air near the floor is clearer and facilitates breathing. 

Always attack the fire first with a straight stream of water and get it satisfactorily under control before turning attention to the bomb. The technique for attacking the bomb will depend upon the type of incendiary it is and the procedure should be followed as given for the type of incendiary bomb encountered. 

3. After extinguishment of the fire, it is of the utmost importance, in all cases, to investigate carefully the entire area involved. It is possible for fire to smoulder on the underside of the floor, behind the wainscoting or elsewhere and thus present the danger of fire breaking out again. As an added precaution it is wise to make a periodic check of the area for several hours after extinguishment of the fire has been accomplished, To determine the possibility of the existence of smouldering fires in concealed spaces, a simple test may be made by placing the palm of the hand against partitions or floors. If after a reasonable length of time in which they should have cooled off they still remain abnormally hot, an immediate investigation should be made by the removal of the part of the partition or floor at which the heat is concentrated. 

Note: *These two preventive measures would be effective only if the 

2-1b. magnesium incendiary should be used.

4. Fire burns on all sides and care should be taken to attack it from the side which will allow for the quickest and easiest control of it. 

5. When fighting a fire out of doors, stand on the windward side of the fire, that is, on the side exposed to the wind, with the flame and smoke blowing away from the fire fighter. If the fire is brought under control, advance slowly toward it, continuing to apply water into the heart of the fire. It should be remembered also that fire travels and every effort should be made to cut it off in the direction in which it is traveling. 6.

If a person's clothing catches fire, he should clap hand over nose and mouth, quickly lie down and roll. If the clothing of another person is burning, make him lie down, with burning parts upward; protect his eyes, nose and mouth, then smother the flames by covering with a blanket, rug or heavy coat and roll him on the floor. 7. 

When attempting to leave a smoke-filled room, do not grope aimlessly about, but use the hose line which has been taken into the room, and hand over hand, follow it out of the area.

Electron. In the fire raids over London the so-called "Electron" or hilogram bomb was used effectively. The tail fins are light sheet metal and guide the bomb's flight. The body is a hollow tube of magnesium nine inches long; two inches in external diameter and with walls about one-half inch thick. In the center is a thermit core, and at one end of this next the fuse is a starting first mixture.

When the bomb strikes, the fuse ignites the starter which in turn kindles the thermit. Thermit is a mixture of iron oxide and aluminum which reacts at temperatures in excess of 4500°F. Due to the fact that it contains its own oxygen (in the iron oxide) and the extremely high temperature, it cannot be extinguished. Fortunately, the reaction is completed in 40 to 50 seconds but in this time it will melt and ignite the magnesium which continues its burning at above 3000°F for 10 to 12 minutes.

Since effective counter measures have been developed, it is to be expected that relatively fewer of these will be used. Thermit. Is streamlined with pointed nose for penetration. Weighs from thirty-three to one hundred and ten pounds and consists of a metal shell filled with thermit. It cannot be extinguished because thermit contains its own oxygen.

A combination high explosive and incendiary bomb weighing 110 pounds has been used and sometimes called the "Fire Pot". New Type Incendiary Bomb (Explosive) 

The new type of fire bomb contains a heavy explosive charge. 

It must be fought from behind strong cover (brickwork at least 3 inches thick or the equivalent). Doors, tables or chairs such as have been recommended as sufficient cover when approaching a 2-lb. magnesium bomb, will not give adequate protection against this new type of explosive incendiary. When and if incendiary bombs are used in this country, it will be wise to treat them all as explosive and take cover under heavy protection while fighting the fires. It is important to continue to emphasize the necessity for fighting the fires first at all times.

Every warden should be thoroughly familiar with the most dangerous fire hazards in his sector so that he will know where to direct the fire fighting forces and thus minimize the danger of the spread of fire to conflagration proportions. 

At the time of a raid, fire fighting is going to be the major problem. The highly combustible nature of construction in densely populated areas in this country makes it imperative that every ablebodied citizen learn and do all he can to eliminate the hazards which cause fires to spread and to be able to combat and extinguish minor fires before they reach the proportions of a major catastrophe.

IX BLACKOUT A. FOREWORD 

Blackout is one of the most important phases of passive defense. 

It is also one of the most difficult to achieve successfully. It requires painstaking planning and advance preparation as well as full cooperation from the people at large. 

Already, a part of our country---the west coast---has experienced living under blackout conditions.

This The purpose of this chapter is to describe methods and procedures of blackout so that plans can be initiated forthwith and Civilian Defense Services informed of their duties and responsibilities. chapter also complements "Blackouts" prepared by the War Department and published by the Office of Civilian Defense prior to the Declaration of 

War. 

B.

GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS 

An area is said to be effectively blacked out when no light or reflection of light is clearly visible from the air. To accomplish this result, all exterior lights or sources of light must be extinguished or screened, or controlled in both intensity and direction, and all interior lights must be either extinguished or prevented from showing by obscuring openings such as windows, doors, or skylights. If blackouts are ordered by proper authorities, no source of light of any intensity or color must be seen directly from above. Reflected light of very low intensity and in fairly large area may be allowed. 

The purpose of blackouts is to deprive enemy airmen of all possible reference points which might aid them in locating definite targets, such

as military areas, air fields, utilities, industrial plants, centers of population. It hides the characteristic pattern of lights of a city, thus preventing the easy identification of an area as a guide to further objectives or to vital points within the area. 

To be sure, blackout is not a foolproof defense. 

Railroad tracks, rivers, shorelines, cement highways, etc., can be seen by starlight alone. Explosions, flares or fires may also briefly illuminate a target. Nevertheless, blackout has proved to be a successful means of reducing the effectiveness and accuracy of bombing.

To accomplish its purpose, blackout must be uniform over a fairly large area and must be enforced not only in vital centers, but also in rural areas which might serve as reference points and help hostile bombers toward these vital centers. It must therefore be enforced without laxity or exception. Penalties should be provided by law for noncompliance of blackout regulations and carelessness or negligence must be avoided at all cost.

C. RESPONSIBILITY FOR EXECUTION OF BLACKOUT 

The decision to blackout an area, large or small, and during long or short periods, is centralized with the Army Command, today the First Interceptor Command. This decision involves tactical considerations, such as strength and location of enemy forces and vulnerability of the area to effective enemy attack. It will be transmitted to the population only through the regular channel of Civilian Defense organization, by the Chief Warden of the local Report Center.

It is the duty of Civilian Authorities, on the other hand, to execute a blackout plan for their respective areas and to enforce blackout regulations through the police with the assistance of deputized auxiliaries or wardens, if necessary. Statutory regulations should be promptly enacted.

Full responsibility for complying with blackout regulations should be placed on the occupants, permanent or transient, owner or tenant, of all premises, whether house, apartment, hotel room, office, factory, shop, or farm. There is no exception to this rule. In hotels, apartments, office buildings and places of multiple occupancy and use, managers or superintendents should be made responsible for all lights, whether in public spaces or in tenant's rooms. 

Owners or those in control of illuminating signs and exterior private lights should be made responsible for observing blackout regulations. 

D. BLACKOUT PROCEDURES

Enemy plane courses have by then been checked and projected more accurately. Blue signals are given over a smaller area than the yellow.

England and on the Continent. It implies complete restriction of all exterior lights, obscuration of all openings, equipment of all vehicles necessary for transportation of vital necessities and war production with special devices. This type of blackout seriously slows down production, creates hazardous conditions to life and limb, and affects morale. It may, however, be made absolutely necessary by tactical considerations. 

The order for permanent blackout will be given by the First Interceptor Command. Air raid signals do not affect it. Permanent blackout deeply influences all aspects of cicilian life and requires careful and detailed planning to prevent undue harm. Most publications on the subject have only described this permanent blackout and based their recommendations on British experience. This has been somewhat misleading as other blackout procedures better fitted to our situation may be involved. 

Blackout may be of an emergency nature. An order to effect an

emergency blackout may issue from the First Interceptor Command.

Or, in the event of an air raid during the night, and in the absence of a previous blackout order of any sort, emergency blackout operations must then proceed in a carefully predetermined sequence set in motion by the various air raid warning signals.

The objective to be achieved by blackout procedures is to have all exterior lights so controlled that they can be immediately extinguished upon the red signal or upon the issuance of a command so to extinguish them. This may require certain blackout procedures to be set in motion prior to the occurrence of the red signal upon the yellow or blue signals. 

Street lights, traffic lights, illuminated signs, etc., may be difficult to extinguish and the initiation of measures to extinguish them may have to be taken on the yellow signal. Similarly, factories and large industrial plants, where sudden blackouts may endanger employees or when control of lighting is decentralized or slow, will have to begin blackout preparations before the red signal. The control of lighting on vehicles is particularly important. On the public alarm, all cars must be made to stop and park close to the curb. Lights must be off, but provisions must also be made for essential vehicles to circulate during the air raid. Their equipment with blackout devices is thus necessary. 

It is impossible to prescribe in detail all those precautions that must be taken on the various warning signals. But the goal should be clearly kept in mind, viz: that all exterior lights should immediately be extinguished upon the public alarm. 

This emergency blackout procedure, by putting out public lights on the yellow signal, does, in fact, indicate publicly the imminence of an air raid and therefore interferes somewhat with the confidential nature of the yellow signal, but no other procedure is at present practically

possible. The goal of the emergency blackout is to extinguish or hide all lights immediately after the public alarm. 

Public lights and other exterior lights in most communities may not be controlled instantaneously, and thus operations must be started on the yellow in order to be complete before it is too late. 

Speed of execution is the main requirement of an emergency blackout, and all planning must tend toward this aim. An emergency blackout only lasts as long as the air raid or as long as a command is in existence. 

It must be recognized, however, that instantaneous or sudden blacking out on the public alarm of private dwellings, factories, commercial buildings, and structures of all sorts, particularly in large communities, presents obvious if not insuperable difficulties, and that it may be advisable soon to equip with blackout devices all openings of buildings where lights are controlled from individual switches. 

Procedure regarding the extinction of exterior lights will, however, remain the same. But because they are not controlled from central switches, and can be put out or dimmed that much sooner, they need not be extinguished as early as might otherwise be the case. Vehicular traffic, nevertheless, must stop and extinguish lights on the public alarm signal. E.

F. TESTS AND PRACTICE BLACKOUTS 

Since emergency blackouts depend on speed of execution, tests are necessary to perfect procedure and show up any deficiencies. 

Practice blackouts, however, should not be conducted until plans have been made, personnel recruited and instructed, responsibilities assigned, and the general procedure been written and filed with the 

State Defense Authorities. Practice emergency blackouts need not be longer than time allowed to check on completeness of blackouts, delinquencies, and other difficulties. Tests should be conducted mainly for the purpose of demonstrating speed at which it may be enforced; hence, it should be restricted at first to small parts of the community, such as groups of streets, individual houses, or factories, business quarters, or residential areas. Industrial plants--particularly those engaged in production of defense materials--should not be asked to participate more often than is necessary to demonstrate that their blackout plans are workable and satisfactory.

The Chief Warden should be responsible for conducting practice blackouts.

G. METHODS AND MATERIALS 

There is no legal or "approved" method of obscuring an opening or

of screening an outside light. Choice of methods and materials is entirely left to the ingenuity of the owner, tenant, or occupant responsible for blackout as long as he follows the blackout procedure and meets the standard of obscuration set by the Authorities. The best and only way to test the effectiveness of method and materials used is by actual observation from the outside. 

The choice of blackout methods will depend on several factors: 

1. Ease of installation and removal. 

2. Permanency of installation. 

3. Location. 

4. Type of occupancy. 

5. Ventilation.

For instance, in dwellings, blackout devices should be light and easily removable because light, air, and sun should be admitted during the daytime. In factories, on the other hand, where most processes are done under artificial light, the method chosen may be permanent. A large factory producing defense materials and in itself an obvious target, will not blackout in the same way as a house in a rural area. 

Blackout measures will inevitably interfere with natural ventilation, may have serious effect on the health of occupants, and may cause undue condensation. This is less a problem in dwellings than in factories where the services of a ventilating engineer might be retained if necessary.

Openings for ventilation purposes, whether existing or new, must be provided with a "light trap". In designing it, use may be made of the simple principle that light rays travel only in straight lines, while air flows around corners. Almost any air passage with two rightangle bends will be in some degree effectively obscured. The inside of light traps should be painted a flat black to absorb reflected light.

Light locks can be made of overlapping curtains for use in dwellings, but care must be taken that wind does not push the curtains away from the window.

The same principles should be applied to the construction of light locks in front of entrances of essential services such as hospitals, which may be used extensively during the air raids. By means of screens at right angles to each other and overlapping in front of the entrance, light can be stopped without hindering traffic. A darkened vestibule large enough so that inner and outer doors are not opened at the same time, may also become a suitable light lock. 

Flying glass splinters from explosion blasts have been responsible for a large percentage of British casualties inside of factories. Other causes of casualties are flying debris, bomb splinters, and falling pieces of anti-aircraft shrapnel. Protection against these, if desirable or necessary, must be effective in daytime as well as at night, and therefore must not be confused with blackout methods. Some blackout methods and materials give effective protection against splinters or debris but most do not. Best protection consists of half-inch wire mesh screens fixed about one inch from the inside face of the glass window or skylight. 

The mesh allows the air blast to blow through and yet stops the splinters or debris. Muslin if dipped in asphalt and glued to the glass is an effective means of stooping splinterage, particularly in skylights. Plain white muslin is less effective against splinterage, but allows daylight. 

There are many materials suitable for blackout of openings. They need not be black or expensive, but they must be opaque. Paints are among the simplest and quickest to apply, but indiscriminate use of black paint may seriously affect inside temperatures and should not be used where temperature or moisture need be controlled for manufacturing process

unless an engineer is consulted. Black paint may also cause breakage of glass because of increased contraction and expansion from temperature It must also be noted that it is the final coat of paint that determines the heat-reflecting characteristics of a surface. variations. solution. 

Casein water paints and alkyd resin emulsion paints dry quickly, are flat in texture, and may be easily removed with an alkaline water The first are only good for inside application. The second may be used on the exterior work but will deteriorate within five or six months. Best results are secured with one coat on both sides. Varnish and linseed oil paints are permanent but more expensive. They can be had without gloss and should have a fairly high pigment ratio. 

Outside shutters also offer some protection against blast and splinters and can be kept open in the daytime. Joints, slits, and edges should be covered with building papers or slats of wood to make them lightproof.

Inside screens made of a variety of materials ranging from building and corrugated paper, to the whole series of building boards, must be built with a light wood frame and securely fastened to the window trim. 

They can be used in most buildings and are cheap to build. Some materials ere fire resistant and some are not. Care must be taken that the screens do not warp. 

There are too many types of curtains to be described here. Even blankets and carpets may be used in an emergency. Two layers of thin textiles like sateen will be sufficiently opaque. 

Venetian blinds and roller shades, if used with an over curtain, will be adequate. The critical point of all curtains is at the edges. They should be fastened securely at the edge, overlap at the bottom, and be covered with a valance at the top. Theatrical materials treated with fireproof solutions

are suitable for many installations where fire risks should be avoided. 

The variety of usable materials is such that no shortage should develop. The Office of Production Management has officially discouraged the purchase of new material as blackout cloth, suggesting as substitutes, articles found in the average house. No new yardage should be used for blackout in homes, as a wave of buying would seriously hinder the war effort.

Intensity, color, and shading or direction of lights are important factors in blackout. Reducing the intensity of lighting, particularly in general areas, such as hallways or entrances where no actual work is done, is desirable and will facilitate blackout. Many materials are opaque to a weak light. Red lights are effective and can be used during a blackout if not seen directly and are of very low intensity. They are particularly useful in entrances, garages, etc. Lights should be so shaded that they fall away from blacked out openings. Directions of inside lights should always be thrown on the floor, and light as small an area as possible.

As stated earlier in this chapter, street lights and traffic control lights reveal the pattern of an area and in most cases will be extinguished during emergency or partial blackouts. Cloth hoods with slits of colored

Fifteen miles per hour is a high limit. To equip all motor vehicles with such devices would cause many accidents and congest traffic to a standstill; hence, emergency or partial blackout procedure calls for use of regular lights until public air warnings when all must stop and put out lights. Only essential cars which must proceed during the air raid will be equipped with blackout devices and proper means of identification. 

Street cars and buses will observe the same regulations as other motor vehicles on the street.

Control of interior lights for blackout purposes is mainly a problem of obscuration of openings. As far as the lights themselves are concerned, a few simple rules should be followed in all premises.

2. Where multiple switches are used, directions regarding their use should be plainly posted to prevent mistakes and avoid fumbling.

3. 4. 5. 6.

In rooms equipped with blackout devices during an air raid, use a minimum number of lights, and assign someone to the switches.

Material used will depend on type of production and may call for such characteristics as fireproof and insect-proof qualities.

XII HIGH EXPLOSIVE BOMBS: 

THEIR PROPERTIES AND EFFECTS 

The terror inspired by high explosive bombs is due, in measure, to the fact that people in general have never been instructed in the proper practices to be followed should a raid occur. Since the beginning of the war the English people have been using the trial and error system to learn what practices can best be followed in such raids. 

They have now developed a standard of conduct for civilians which has cut down casualties to the point less than those caused in this country by the use of the automobile. 

There seems to be no reason, unless conditions change for the worse, why we should expect heavy raids of the type under which Plymouth, Coventry, or London suffered. Such raids as may come in this country must, from the nature of things, be of a "nuisance" type and will not continue for appreciable periods of time. While it is natural to suppose that the larger types of bombs and aerial mines will not be used since the difficulty of bringing such bombs and mines to this country outweigh their usefulness as morale destroyers, we should not count upon it as larger types of bombs may well be used.

Since all of the bombs have certain properties in common, it may be well to consider those properties. High explosive bombs may be roughly divided into these classes: 1. 

Impact bombs. 4. 

Armor piercing bombs. 

2. Delayed action bombs. 

5. Fragmentation bombs. 

3. General purpose or demolition bombs. 6. 

Aerial mines.

Impact bombs are intended to cause damage primarily through fragmentation, blast, and suction. Delayed action bombs produce injury to property and persons largely through cratering and mining effect. Impact bombs are designed to detonate on hitting the first substantial object with which 

The case they come in contact and do not penetrate to any great degree. of such bombs is relatively thin, and the percentage of high explosive in them may be as high as 60% of the total weight. When detonation of such a bomb occurs, a terrific, rending, outward force which tends to rise from all parts of the bomb, is loosed; this force is called blast. 

It is so great that it pushes the air out from the point of explosion and so causes a partial vacuum and is immediately followed by a lesser returning force called suction. Before high explosive bombs detonate, the fuse must be activated, the high explosive turned from a solid to a gas at high temperature and pressure until the casing has swollen to such size that it bursts. When it bursts, the fragments of the casing fly from the bomb in all directions, and they tend to rise as they fly. 

Because of the tendency of blast to rise, and of the fragments of casing to rise with it, the practice of persons caught out in a raid where high explosive bombs are used, is to throw themselves to the ground. It is preferable to throw one's self on soft ground rather than on the paved surface of a street or hard ground, because fragments of bomb, or falling anti-aircraft shells in hitting a hard surface tend to ricochet. 

Since blast may cause injury to the eardrums if one's mouth is closed, it is advisable to keep the mouth part way open. It is not advisable to keep the mouth fully open since high explosive bombs in exploding raise substantial clouds of dust. If possible, carry an eraser or some other

soft object to put between the teeth. In this way, you may avoid injury to your teeth. As you lie down, extend your arm, and pull your helmet down to protect the back of your neck.

The sketches below show the folly of running since, to do so, exposes you to the full effect of both blast and fragmentation. If you lie down, both blast and fragments of casing pass over your head. Some of the largest delayed action bombs penetrate as deep as fiftyseven feet into the ground. All such bombs, in exploding, give off forces laterally and upward. The forces given off laterally may undermine buildings and cause their fall, in some instances, a substantial distance from where the bomb explodes. The upward movement of force breaks the earth's surface because it is easier to break the crust of the earth than it is to move the soil laterally. In this way, so-called craters are formed. The size of a crater depends on many things, including the angle at which the bomb strikes the earth, the size and shape of the bomb, and the nature of the soil in which it falls.

The different classes of building construction are: 1. 2. 

First-class construction--steel and cement--all fire resistant. 

Second-class construction--steel frame and cement--not fire resistant throughout. 

3. Third-class construction--wooden frame--not fire resistant.

No building of third-class construction is strong enough to withstand the impact and blast of a direct hit although many of the buildings of second and first-class construction may do so. Since most homes in this country are of third-class construction, it is wise to consider how best to protect one's self from direct hits, near misses, and the effects of blast, suction, and fragmentation from the larger caliber bombs.

For capacity to resist the forces released by exploding bombs, buildings may be further classified into two groups: those having steel or reinforced concrete frame structures, and those which are wall supporting. 

In the latter group, the walls support the superstructure, and destruction of one or more walls generally leads to building collapse. In the frame structure buildings, the walls are carried by the frame and serve only to keep rain and weather out. Such buildings may suffer severe mechanical shocks from explosions, undergo localized distortion, seldom disintegrate or collapse.

The proposition, simply stated, is that there is no perfect place to be during a high explosive bomb raid. The best place to stay is in your own home, either on the first floor or in the cellar. If a competent architect, after an examination of the first floor timbers of the house, is satisfied that there is a sufficient safety factor in the construction so that these timbers will sustain the weight of the rest of the house, if it falls or is blown down, the cellar is the best place. If the cellar is used, however, there must be two exits provided. If, on the other hand, such examination reveals that the first floor timbers will not carry the load, the first floor near or under the staircase is the safest place. 

In large buildings the middle are usually the safest floors, but it is wise to get the advice of a competent architect or builder concerning

each such building. Be sure to keep away from windows, doors with glass in them, and elevator shafts, and skylights because these are the weakest portions of construction in a building.

XV. GAS DEFENSE AND DECONTAMINATION DEFENSE AGAINST CHEMICAL ATTACK A. GENERAL 

1. Obiect of Instruction. The object of instruction and training in defense against chemical attack is to: 8. 

Minimize casualties from enemy attack. b. Prevent undue interruption of normal life during and after attack. c. Prevent panic. 

2. Principal Chemical Agents. Definitions: 

8. b. c.

A chemical agent is a substance useful in war which, after release and acting directly through its chemical properties, is capable of producing a toxic effect a powerful irritant effect, or a screening smoke.

A persistent agent is one which will maintain an effective vapor concentration in the air at point of release for more than 10 minutes. An effective concentration is one which necessitates protection of any kind.

A non-persistent agent is one wherein the effectiveness in the air at point of release is dissipated within 10 minutes. d. A casualty agent is a material of such physical and chemical characteristics that a dangerous or killing concentration can be set up under conditions encountered in the field.

A harassing agent is one used to force masking and thus slow up enemy operations. Lacrimators and irritant smokes are the principal agents of this type. f. A lung irritant is a chemical agent which, when breathed, causes irritation and inflammation of the interior portion of the bronchial tubes and lungs. 

Its primary physiological action is limited to the respiratory tract. в. h. 1. 

A vesicant is a chemical agent which is readily absorbed or dissolved in both the exterior and interior parts of the human body, followed by the production of inflammation, burns, and destruction of tissue. 

A lacrimator is a chemical agent which causes copious flow of tears and intense, though temporary, eye pains.

An irritant smoke is a chemical agent which can be disseminated as extremely small solid or liquid particles in air, and when so disseminated, causes intolerable sneezing, coughing, lacrimation, or headaches followed by nausea, and temporary physical disability when breathed in very low concentrations. j. A sternutator is an irritant smoke. k. A screening smoke is a chemical agent used to hinder hostile observation.

With the exception of burning action of particles of phosphorus or liquid FS on the skin, smokes have little or no injurious effect on personnel.

GENERAL INSTRUCTIONS 

The importance of proper first aid for gas victims cannot be overemphasized. The following are general rules which apply in all cases. 

A. Act promptly and quietly; be calm.

B. Put a gas mask on the patient if gas is still present or, if he has a mask on, check to see that his is properly adjusted. If a mask is not available, wet a handkerchief or other cloth and have him breathe through it. 

C. Keep the patient at absolute rest; loosen clothing to facilitate breathing. 

D. Remove the patient to a gas-free place as soon as possible.

E. Summon medical aid promptly; if possible, send the victim to a hospital.

F. Do not permit the patient to smoke, as this causes coughing and, hence, exertion.

PROTECTION AND PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT 

1. 

Classification of Protective Measures. Protection against chemical attacks presents two classes of problems for civilians, individual and collective. a. b. 

Individual protection includes not only proper distribution, use, and care of protective equipment, but also the ability to recognize the presence of a particular chemical agent and, by knowing its characteristics, to utilize equipment in such a manner as to avoid being a casualty. 

Collective protection is the utilization of measures and unit protective equipment for the protection of personnel, animals and materiel. This includes the posting of gas sentries, operation of gas-proof shelters, preservation of animals, and equipment, and the use of protective covers. 

2. Gas mask. The gas mask protects the civilian's eyes and respiratory tract from the chemical agents encountered in the field. 

The principle of operation of the gas mask is based on air filtration. Air is drawn into the mask when a person inhales, the

mask is so constructed that this air must first pass through a canister containing filtration system. This comprises a mechanical filter to prevent the entrance of smoke or dust, and a filter of charcoal and soda lime to absorb and neutralize toxic and irritating gases and vapors. This air is purified by filtration. It is then drawn to the wearer's face and after being inhaled and exhaled is expelled from the mask. 

The gas mask gives full protection against lung irritants, sternutators and lacrimators in concentrations likely to be met in the field. It is not protection against carbon monoxide or ammonia gas and is not suitable for a fireman's mask. Antidim is furnished to assist in preventing fogging from moisture in the mask and from rain. The diaphragm mask is issued to those in charge whose duties require audible speech.

3. Protective clothing. There are two types of protective clothing. 

Impervious clothing and impregnated clothing. No design has been approved as standard yet. Made like cover-all type of suit to be worn over outer clothing. Elastic at ankles and wrist to keep out vesicant gases. Gloves of similar material to protect hands and wrists. A hood to protect neck and head. Gas mask to be

In excluding mustard and other vesicant vapors from the body such clothing also excludes air. It is hot, sticky and uncomfortable to wear. The lack of ventilation makes it impossible to be worn more than an hour or two at a time, without excessive fatigue and loss of general efficiency. Such suits are only for gas destroying personnel.

Impregnated clothing more suitable for general wear. This consists of ordinary cotton and woolen clothing impregnated with a solution of chemicals to destroy the vapor of vesicant agents. 

It does not protect against liquid agent. Ordinary clothing and

underclothing can be treated with the impregnite and made gas proof. If clothing is washed the impregnite is not destroyed until after third or fourth washing. Clothing so treated is more inflammable than ordinary clothing. Can be worn continuously without ill effects. 

All cracks and openings should be sealed to gasproof a room. Arrows show where to look for cracks and leaks. 

4. Improvised Gasproof Home Shelters (Nonventilated Type). A gas proof room in the home, to which the family together with the family pets can resort in case of a gas attack may be of service. 

However, it must be realized that the enemy is likely to use high explosives before the gas attack. The blast from the high explosives will probably shatter windows and otherwise interfere with the gasproofing measures adopted in the home. Nevertheless, materials may be ready at hand to seal such a room as effectively as circumstances permit although too much reliance cannot be placed on its being entirely gasproof. 

In the average home, the preferred location for such a shelter is the first floor or basement. Two exits should be provided. An air-lock doorway should lead into the shelter. 

A short passageway with the opening at each end covered by oil cloth or a blanket may serve as the air lock. Such an air lock prevents drafts of gas-laden air from being drawn into the enclosure as persons enter or leave the shelter. 

To render the room reasonably gas-tight all openings should be closed. Oil cloth, coated fabrics, or other nonporous materials may be used to seal all openings. Transparent cellulose tape is useful for this purpose. Dampened blankets may be tacked over the window frames. Chimney openings should be sealed and all fires in the room must be extinguished.

D. 

GAS ALARMS 

These items of equipment should be issued to all organizations at once to acquaint all citizens how to recognize these sounds over other signals that may be given for other alarms. They should be of a distinctive sound not easily confused with other sounds heard while a raid is on. Typical alarm devices are: Klaxon, Rattler, Horn, Triangles, Whistles, etc. There should be two classes of alarms, general and local. General, when attack is expected to cover a large area. A general alarm to be sent out by all normal methods of communication and directed to all localities that may be affected. Local alarms can be given with alarm horns.

E. 

DECONTAMINATION 

1. Men detailed for this work should wear masks and protective clothing. The entire body should be covered. They should be prepared to give First Aid measures in cases of necessity, should have necessary solvents and neutralizing agents close at hand. Men should be inspected before commencing operations to insure that they are properly protected. Facilities away from other personnel should be provided for undressing and bathing after completion of work. Men should be taught to assist each other in removing clothing used in decontaminating work without touching skin or contaminating other clothing. Facilities for disposal or cleaning of contaminated clothing should be provided. 

Limitations. Chemical agents may be destroyed or neutralized by other chemicals. No one chemical, however, is effective in all cases. It is not always practical to have a great variety or quantity of chemicals. Hence, decontaminating operations are necessarily limited. Whether they are practical at all will depend upon the character of chemical involved, extent of contamination, and importance of contaminated area or equipment. 

Marking contaminated area. When not practical to decontaminate area thoroughly, it should be marked with danger signs, showing agent involved, and the date of contamination or its discovery. 

It should be understood that when a shelling takes place at night or other times when temperatures are relatively low, little vapor may be given off, while later, in the warmth of the sun a high concentration may result. Areas treated during cool periods of the day should be inspected after the temperature has risen to determine whether further treatment is required. 2. 3. 4. 

When to decontaminate. Decontamination work should start immediately after shelling or as soon thereafter as possible. Small areas, buildings and equipment within certain limits can be decontaminated. Generally sentries should be posted to warn all unauthorized persons against entering gassed area.

Such covering is more effective if wet down with water. b. c. 

Water: Lewisite is readily decomposed by water, therefore a liberal wetting down of the contaminated area is sufficient to destroy this agent. Mustard is very slowly hydrolized by water. The action of cold water on mustard is so slow that it is practically negligible for decontaminating purposes. 

Hot water is more effective. If there is sufficient drainage mustard gas will be washed away by water. As it is heavier than water it will lie at bottom of pools and puddles remaining active for a long period of time, though water will retard the escape of mustard vapors. a.

Bleaching powder (chloride of lime): This material is a white powder, not very stable, readily giving up its chlorine when exposed to air or moisture. It should be kept in airtight containers and used as soon as possible after removal therefrom. Bleaching powder reacts quickly with mustard, forming a non-vesicant compound. In contact with liquid mustard it reacts violently not only causing flame but driving off high concentration of mustard vapor. If bleaching powder is mixed with sand or earth this violent reaction does not occur. The proportion should be one part bleaching powder to three parts of sand or earth. One pound of bleaching powder is required per square yard of gassed area. 

In decontaminating equipment, bleaching powder can frequently be more easily applied in liquid form with rags or swabs. When used as a liquid solution the proportion should be one part of bleaching powder to one part of water. For decontamination of skin of animals or human beings chloride of lime in proportion to one part of powder to two parts of water may be used. Protection of food and water. Food and water should be covered in air tight containers until required for use. Springs, waterholes, etc., should have air tight lids. Water contaminated by mustard should be avoided. Canned food and drink in hermetically sealed containers can easily be made safe for use after exposure to liquid or gaseous mustard by boiling the containers in sodium carbonate solution. Water contaminated by arsenical agents such as Lewisite and Adamsite, or by white phosphorous, cannot be purified and should be avoided. Public water supply systems are subject to contamination by the absorption of poisonous war gasses. 

Frequent water sampling should take place to detect any contamination. The use of chlorine and filtering, aids greatly in protecting water supplies. This is a job for the water department of all cities and towns and is not included in wardens' work. 

7. Protection of equipment. Steel and other metals can be kept in safe condition by covering with oil or grease. Mustard penetrates wood rather rapidly, being soaked up more quickly by soft wood than by hard. A wooden surface painted with ordinary paint will absorb mustard--sprinkle with dry chloride of lime. Ordinary concrete surfaces quickly absorb mustard. Wash floors with chloride of lime solution. After cleaning concrete wash over with sodium silicate (water glass). This forms a smooth nonporous surface coating which will prevent mustard working to the surface. Ordinary buildings will remain practically gas-proof as long as all doors and windows are kept tightly closed. Temporary measure only--as question of ventilation will arise by housing a large number of personnel in such buildings. 8. Grassland.

An area covered with high, dry grass or brush may be decontaminated by burning, provided this can be done without danger to nearby personnel. Heat will cause a heavy cloud of mustard vapor to be given off downwind during the burning. If burning is not feasible bleaching powder mixed with water may be sprayed on small areas. 

9. Clothing and fabric equipment a. Airing: Clothing contaminated by mustard vapor only can be decontaminated by hanging up the garments so that they will be exposed to the sun and wind. In warm, bright weather two days' airing will generally be sufficient. Do not hang clothes close together. In cool, cloudy weather this method should not be relied upon. b. 

Steaming: A more positive method of destroying mustard gas in clothing is to subject it to steam. If contaminated with mustard vapor only, clothing may be decontaminated by 2 hours steaming. If splashed with liquid mustard steaming should

10. 11. 12. be continued for 4 to 6 hours. The simplest steam disinfector is a large ash-can, with false bottom, which holds the clothing about a foot or two from the true bottom, 6 or 8 inches of water is poured into the bottom of the can, false bottom inserted, and garments piled in. Place over fire, the top being covered, but not so tightly that it will prevent escape of steam.

Shoes. Little can be done in this field to decontaminate shoes permeated with mustard. If only slightly splashed and before the mustard has soaked into leather, shoes may be neutralized by bleaching powder or bleaching powder mixed with water. Well-worn shoes absorb mustard much quicker than new leather.

Leather. Leather, unless specially treated, absorbs mustard almost instantly, should be treated with hot solution of bleaching powder and water immediately after being sprayed or splashed with this agent.

Buildings. If liquid agents have been carried into buildings by shell, fire, the building should be abandoned. If a building which has not been gas-proofed has been subjected to vapors from vesicant agents, the ceilings or walls should be sponged and washed with a mixture of bleaching powder and hot water and wooden floors thoroughly scrubbed with same material.

F. 

FIRST AID FOR GAS CASUALTIES 

General: First Aid for gas casualties must be strongly emphasized. 

There are certain simple rules all should know. 

Unit Commanders should be made responsible for First Aid training. 

Unit Commanders should determine which gas casualties will walk and which require stretchers.

Lacrimators: Personnel who are lacrimated do not require evacuation as casualties, only need to face the wind, allowing it to blow into their eyes. Do not rub the eyes. Loosen the clothes to get rid of entrapped gas. Bathe eyes in cold water or a weak solution of boric acid or sodium bicarbonate solution. 1. 

Irritant Smoke (sternutators). These agents, such as, DM are not lethal in field concentrations. May require evacuation. Remove patient from contaminated area. Keep away from heat, and remove outer clothing. Flush nose and throat with weak solution of sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) or ordinary salt. Breathing chlorine in low concentration tends to alleviate the irritation. 

This can be accomplished by breathing from a bottle containing bleaching powder (Chloride of Lime). Also, exposed surface of body is to be washed with soap and water. 

2. Lung Irritants. In order to reduce his oxygen requirements to the minimum--a lung irritant casualty should be made to lie down and not allowed to walk, even though he insists that he can.

GENERAL EFFECTS OF WEATHER AND TERRAIN ON CHEMICAL ATTACK 

1. Wind. The effective use of chemical agents is largely dependent upon favorable weather conditions. Chemical agents are most effective in light winds. Strong winds will rapidly dissipate them. Non-persistent chemical agents are comparatively ineffective in a wind over 12 miles per hour. Chemical agents of highly persistent type are not affected to the same extent by strong wind. 2. 

Temperature. On warm sunny days, when the temperature of the earth is higher than that of the air, ascending air currents carry gas upwards and disperse it. Warm weather increases the rate of evaporation thereby reducing the persistency of the agent. Cold weather accompanied by clouds increases persistency. Substances like mustard may freeze in the soil. When mustard freezes, it is much less effective. However, the evaporation is still sufficient, except in sub-freezing weather, to produce casualties if personnel are exposed for a considerable time. It must be kept in mind that solid particles of frozen mustard gas may be attached to clothing while passing through undergrowth and these particles will cause burns if they come in contact with the body. 

Fog and rain. Foggy and cloudy weather is favorable for use of gas. Rainfall tends to wash gas out of the air and in most cases slowly destroys liquid on the ground. 

4. Night. The most favorable conditions for use of gases, particularly those of the phosgene type, occur at night or early morning, because strong winds and ascending currents are then usually absent. 5. 

Ground. Surface: The most important effects of the ground on chemical agents in liquid form are as follows: a. Soft dry ground will absorb liquids and so reduce the danger from direct physical contact. It is difficult to detect vesicant gas, however, when absorbed in earth. b. c. 

On soft wet ground, gas may persist for considerable periods as free and unabsorbed liquid. When a shell or bombs burst on soft ground, a large proportion of the liquid content will be absorbed or buried in the crater, with resulting contamination of the earth and mud. When boots or shoes become contaminated with liquid mustard gas and are later worn in inclosed places, dangerous vapor concentrations may arise. 

On hard ground, the liquid contents of chemical projectiles are scattered over a relatively large area. Hard ground retards penetration of liquid agents, released in this manner,

6. consequently, the agents are exposed in greater degree to the influence of sun, wind, and rain, thus lessening their persistency, yet increasing the gas hazard.

Topographical features. All chemical agents successfully used in warfare are heavier than air. Unless set in motion by wind or air currents gas clouds tend to flow into gullies and valleys, leaving hilltops comparatively free. 

7. Vegetation and obstruction. Tall grass, bushes, trees, buildings, and similar obstructions retard the movement of the air and, in like manner the movement of vapors of chemical agents, thereby making them more persistent. Tall grass and undergrowth increase the danger from vesicants.

YOUR SAIETI 

As your work continually carries you into the streets, you are urged to develop the habit of thinking of safety. No safety device has 

The best means to safety yet been invented to take the place of the brain. is the understanding and observance of safety rules. 

Learn traffic regulations perfectly and obey them. 

Particularly should you obey the laws concerning stop streets, one way streets, traffic signals and the giving of hand signals when stopping or turning.

Be extremely cautious at blinker streets, blind corners, and at all intersections. Make a full stop before crossing boulevards or through streets.

Observe the general rule giving right of way to vehicles approaching intersections on your right. However, caution must be exercised whether the rule gives you right of way or not.

Your bicycle must be equipped with a suitable warning device, coaster brake and mud guards. If you ride a bicycle during a blackout, it must carry approved lighting device. 

Do not ride your bicycle on the sidewalk. It endangers pedestrians and yourself and is strictly against the law. 

Inspect your equipment daily. Keep it properly oiled and greased and see that all parts are in perfect working condition before starting your duties.

If the brake, pedals, crank hanger, chain, or other parts of the bicycle become worn or defective, have them fixed without delay. 

If you find a loose heel or sole on your shoe, have it fixed immediately.

If you ride a bicycle, keep both hands on the bars, head up, and eyes to the front.

Ride at a safe distance from trucks, buses, or other vehicles that make it difficult for the driver to see you.

Riding close to the curb is safest. Weaving in and out of traffic is dangerous. Be very careful in heavy traffic. If traffic is congested and stalled, dismount, and, if possible, walk your bicycle to a safe starting place.

When leaving your bicycle, so place it that it will not fall or obstruct the way and injure or endanger pedestrians. 

Make sure the way is clear before starting. Conditions may have changed since they were last observed.

When riding behind vehicles, keep your wheel under control so that you can stop as promptly as the driver ahead of you does. If possible keep at least 10 feet to the rear. 

Never attempt to pass a moving vehicle at an intersection. 

Coasting down grades is dangerous. Use your brake. 

Enter and leave elevators carefully.

Ride with extreme caution in rainy weather and keep away from trolley tracks.

Beware of dogs. An animal may appear friendly until you pass the point of safety and then become vicious. 

Always have a sharpened lead pencil with you. 

If you should lose a message, report the loss immediately.

If there are bulletin boards at your station, look at them frequently for new notices. 

Don't leave your post for personal affairs unless you secure permission to do so.
