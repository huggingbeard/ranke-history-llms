FOREWORD 

This story of his life left us by Uncle Joe is but a brief sketch of many years during which he endeared himself to all of us. He became in truth the patriarch of our family; his word was law to each child and youngster; his reprimands were just; his commendation eagerly sought; his never-failing humor, the merry twinkle of his eyes, his apt illustrations and amusing remarks made for a companionship ever to be remembered. 

As we grew older we learned to appreciate and follow his wise advice. In times of trouble and sorrow his calmness of spirit and a few sympathic words brought comfort and inspiration to go forward.

The affection with which he was held by his comrades of the Civil War, their appreciation of his steady courage, his devotion to duty, were evidenced by the long-continued friendships and the interchange of memories at the regimental reunions.

He enjoyed good literature. The heroes of Walter 

Scott were his heroes. The Lady of the Lake he knew by heart. His vivid recollection of the years of war experiences was supplemented by a study of the biographies of the great military leaders. He offered to serve in the Spanish War. When in England during 1914 at the outbreak of the World War he said to an English Tommy "I know what you are experiencing and I wish I were able to go with you. The Tommy's answer greatly amused him "Why, sir, were you in the Revolution?" 11 11 

His interest in current affairs never flagged. 

When over ninety years old he said: "Jim, I shall feel humiliated when I meet Lide and Crete* and have to admit I have never been in an airplane. A little later when a young friend, Louise King, who flew her own plane said, "Uncle Joe, will you take a flight with me?" he immediately accepted. As we flew about over Little Mountain *"Lide" was his wife Eliza, "Crete" his sister, Lucretia Rudolph, wife of Pres. James A. Garfield. -Ed.

EARLY LIFE AND CIVIL WAR REMINISCENCES OF CAPTAIN JOSEPH RUDOLPH 

A. D. 1841 on the old farm one mile north of 

Garrettsville my troubles began, and I surmise those of sundry other parties received a substantial increase. 

This date comes just at the turning point marked by the clearing of the original forests and the change from the log house to the somewhat primitive frame house and barn. 

One year later Father built a house in Garrettsville, to 1842 which place we moved on acount of the better school facilities for my sister Lucretia and brother John (ten and seven years of age). There we lived for three years, 1845 then returned to the farm. In Garrettsville I had the first experience within my recollection, not very creditable, but I mention it as being the only proof of precocity in my record.

It was of a Monday morning, Mother busy with the washing, when I got into trouble. She stripped and cleansed me and turned me loose in my pelt. Being much abashed I fled to the living room where my old grandfather was sitting in his armchair, and what I distinctly remember is that he covered me with his big bandana handkerchief and so relieved my embarrassment. I was in the house a few years ago, and if the chair had been there, could have placed it within a foot of where it stood at the time of the disaster and rescue.

We moved into the new house before all the floors were down, the plaster on, or the windows in. All was finished before school opened for the winter time, and we had that winter in the little cottage with twelve boarders.

There were not more than eight or ten houses in town and about one hundred and fifty students to be housed and fed. Most of the farmers within a mile or two took boarders, so all were provided for. Perhaps not just that, but at least stowed away. Board, including heat, lights (tallow candles), washing and mending for the boys--the girls did their own and cared for their rooms--was a dollar per week among the farmers, and one dollar and twenty-five cents in town. Several houses were soon built by the trustees for self-boarders, such students as could not afford the excessive board bills.

In 1853 Father sold the farm and the cottage and built a larger house east of the Center, and this was our home till 1881. All went quietly till 1861. 

In school part of the time, and to piece out a slender income, teaching school and working out. Meantime my sister, Lucretia, was married to J. A. Garfield [November 

11, 1858], then President [Principal] of the school, and my brother John, to Martha Lane [1856], they going West. 

When I was twelve I had my first great adventure.1853

The great Barnum brought his show to Ravenna. It was more moral at that time (no circus) than at some later dates, and Father consented to take me. When the day came, our big hired-girl put in a request to go along, which was granted. She took up so much of the seat in the big spring-wagon that I had to sit on a stool in front to drive. I was much excited, and we soon came to a signboard, upon which, I shouted, "Two and a half miles to Ravenna! hit the old mare a sharp cut, and was going

down the road at a three-minute clip when I heard a call from the rear. Looking around, I first noticed that the back of the democrat was empty. Then back about twenty rods I saw Father coming at his best pace, and about as much farther to the rear, the big hired-girl, standing in the middle of the road, white with dust and her coal-scuttle bonnet down over her face. Fortunately neither was hurt, and we were soon on our way again and had a bully day. At Ravenna, I saw for the 

1853 first time a railroad train. The blasted engine stopped just opposite me and gave a screech at which I nearly jumped out of my clothes. 

About this time I made my first big money. 

Worked seven days at haying for Old Squire Lidall. 

Turned grindstone from sun up till breakfast. Stirred hay and carried jug till dinner. Raked after and helped mow away till supper and after, till hay was all in. 

Was never prouder than when I received three dollars and fifty cents.

Our main interest was in the school. I might say that I did not prove an exceptionally brilliant scholar, for my sister Nell four years younger, was in the same classes with me and made a much better record. 

At the middle of the spring term of 1861 came the call to arms, and books were little thought of. We boys had been sleeping on the floor and taking long tramps before breakfast in anticipation, so some of us were ready. So far as I know, my cousin Jim6 and I were the only enlistments from Hiram township on the first call. Jim was three or four days ahead of me, going to 

Cleveland and joining the 23rd Ohio Infantry, a threeyear regiment, while I joined at Burton a three-month company, which was assigned to the 19th Ohio Infantry. 

Our camp was on the fair grounds at Painesville, and we were broken in very comfortably - meals furnished from a Painesville hotel, and often pieced out by our 

Chardon friends on picnic visits. The Seminary girls were also very kind. A blue shirt in those days was the only credential [necessary] for admittance to the best

On arriving at Cleveland, I found cousin Jim and several other friends who persuaded me to come over to the 23rd and be a regular three-year man. Captain 

Payne threatened to have me arrested if I made the attempt, but Captain McIlrath, who was twice his size and in a uniform of red cap and zouave pants, assured me that he would protect me, and so came desertion number one.

Our Camp Taylor was on the old fair grounds, with quarters in the cattle sheds. The following morning the first issue of regular army rations was made, soft bread, pork and coffee. The bread with no butter didn't seem quite like home or Painesville, but we got used to it, and beans and rice were soon added. In about a month we were ordered to Columbus, and the morning after our arrival the 23rd was the first regiment to go to Camp Chase. We found just a big field, the corn a foot high, and underneath, mud about the same depth. Wagon-loads of lumber came, and before dark we were all under cover, but no rations arrived. Then we had our first lesson in foraging, and chicken supper made the first, if not the last, variation from army rations. Our stay at Camp 

Chase was the farthest from eventful. I have often wondered that so little was done to prepare the troops for field service. We had dress parade occasionally. 

A little squad and company drill, but mostly were idle, varied by breaking guard at night, foraging and going to Columbus. On the 11th of June we were mustered into service for three years or during the war. The examina- 1861 tion was brief. Show your teeth and hands and every

man passed. On July 25th we were fully equipped and ordered to move. Took train at Columbus, stopped over night at Wheeling and next day on a train of cattle cars on the B & O Railroad made our entrance into Dixie. 

Soon after starting, it commenced to rain, and the cars having very little roof or siding, we were soon as black with smoke and cinders as the darkies we were going to free. However, we were next morning at 

Clarksburg. Made our first field camp and were glad to be in a tented field, not, however, for long. At two 

A.M. we were called out, ordered to load our guns, and in pitch darkness awaited a rebel attack till dawn, when we were marched out a short distance and loaded on a train of empty wagons and so made our first march south. 

Rations were short, but the road was lined with blackberry bushes in full fruit and of the best, so we did not suffer. Arrived at Weston before dark, very wet. 

Here we camped for several days and got in good shape. 

From Weston we made our first sure-enough march. Starting at noon of a very hot day, we had not then learned to go light, [so] the march was more disorderly than any I afterwards saw. We made only six miles, but the entire way was lined with stragglers and discarded knapsacks. 

Were right glad when the order came to camp, and the wagons soon brought up the knapsacks. Fortunately we had three farm wagons to a company (later reduced to one army wagon for a brigade) and next day our knapsacks were loaded on the wagons and we toted them no more that season. From now on till winter [there was]usually, only one half regiment and much of the time but one company in a command. I had a beautiful fall. The scenery fine and service easy. The only engagement worth mentioning was at Carnifax Ferry, where our regiment was united under General Rosecrans. 

The 23rd was held in reserve, so we suffered only noise. The Confederates retreated that night, and we followed them south to Big Birch Mountain. There our campaign for the season actually ended. There was much camp fever after the fall rains came on and many deaths. 

The hospital arrangements were nil and no medicines except quinine and capsicum. I had an attack of fever but was never unable to care for myself and soon recovered.

Cousin Jim's case was very severe and he was furloughed home. Our brigade went into winter quarters at Fayetteville. Our company was detailed on picket duty and stationed on a plantation two miles from town. The quarters were commodious and comfortable and we had a beautiful winter. This closed my service with the 23rd and brings us up to desertion number two.

When the 42nd regiment was organized in the fall of 1861, Company A was composed largely of Hiram students and boys of the town. I had quite a desire to be with them and applied for a transfer. Nothing came of it except a promise from Captain McIlrath that if I wished, I should go, transfer or no transfer, and about the first of March he took me aside and said, "Rudolph, you had my promise last fall that you should go to the 42nd if you wished. Now, if you will stay, I will make you a

Corporal, in which grade I had acted during the winter. "But my promise holds and, if you wish, go ahead. I said, "I will go." He gave me a penciled statement of my clothes etc., and armed with this I left on the following morning. 11 11 11 

The 42nd was then on the Big Sandy, not distant, but the route across country was not practical, so I said to the Captain, "I would like to go home for two weeks on the way." He said, "Oh, that is all right. 

Go and stay as long as you like. Next morning I tramped down to Gauley Bridge about ten miles. I had to cross the river to make a boat-landing. Just as I came to the bank a sergeant of a West Virginia regiment came over in a canoe with a picket guard. I asked him to take me over. He said I must show my pass. I sized him up and handed him my clothing account. He looked at it, first one side up then the other, seemed a little puzzled but finally allowed that he would take me along, adding that 

I wouldn't get any farther without a pass. 

That evening a boat came up, and I got aboard.

When about half way to Charlestown, a lieut. of the P.M. 

Department appeared and called for my pass. Being doubtful about offering him my clothing account, I said I had none. He seemed uncertain what to do. The night was pitch-dark, and the first stop was Charleston. After 1862

coming to me several times he concluded that the only thing to do was to land me there, which was just what I wished. Arrived at Charleston about midnight. Found a fine old darky at the hotel who brought me a lunch which I will never forget--a slice of soft bread with butter on it, and a generous section of apple pie, just like Mother's. I haven't had a better meal since. Next morning I boarded a Wheeling boat. No more said about passes, or for that matter, boat fares. I think the boat officers had all become discouraged about collecting fares from soldiers, and were glad when they landed without some of the boat property on their persons. Arrived at Ravenna the next day, and tramped up to Hiram Hill. 

Two weeks at home among family and friends; then off to the war again. Arriving at Coltersville heard that the 42nd had been ordered down the river, and would have waited till they arrived, but Adjt. Clapp stopped on his way to join the regiment and asked me to go up the river with

Nothing said about mustering-in till four months later when a paymaster came around. How my account was made out I know not, but I signed the roll and received pay for six months, two due me in the 23rd, and four in the 42nd, and no questions asked. So we took peaceful possession on July 4th. 

1862 After a few days the 42nd was moved over to the Kentucky side and went into our most delightful camp of the entire war. It was just at the foot of the range on a fine little mountain stream, in a beautiful grove, and here we spent the most delightful six weeks of our entire service. A half mile up the creek was a blackberry patch in which we could pick in an hour a pail of the most delicious berries, and from them our cook learned to make pies, which would have done honor to Delmonico's. 

This was, however, too good to last. General Kirby 

Smith with a large force of Confederates, to return our compliment, came up and soon had us surrounded. All supplies were cut off. First it was half rations, then one fourth, which we pieced out by foraging on the 

Tennessee as far as Tazewell (12 miles).

It was on one of these raids that I received my only wound--the only mark I have to show for distinguished service. It was this way. We had orders to take and hold for the day all citizens who came into our lines. Soon an old gentleman came in on a fine horse and with bulging saddle bags. While one of the Company 

A boys interested the old gent, a comrade examined the bag and realized a quart bottle of elegant mountain dew. 

On our return I noticed that these two rascals were frequently dropping out in the roadside bushes, and they being good friends of mine, and I being hungry and tired and not feeling first-rate, [I] took to falling out with them. So by the time we reached the crest of the Gap, I was stepping out "Mountainious" and feeling anxious to charge [General] Kirby Smith and all his minions. The road down the mountain was rough, and the off side precipitous, and incidentally my high-stepping right foot came down just on the brink. By good luck I landed head-on on a stone pile about ten feet below, which would have been a small matter except that my gun came down on my forefinger on a stone. The boys heard the clatter and fished me up. I noticed no damage until we were in camp, where I found that the finger joint was smashed. But I am not sorry, as I have ever since been able to show that I suffered for my country.

Soon after, our command moved for greater safety to the top of the range, about two miles south of the Gap. On the day that I came of age I was on picket duty about two miles from camp. We took several prisoners, and toward evening I was detailed to take them into Headquarters at the Gap. From there, had a delightful trip back to camp.

The necessity for foraging constantly increased. 

On one expedition we stayed over night at Tazewell.

Somehow old Colonel DeCourcy12 in command, got the idea that a large force of "Johnies" was just over a very steep, high ridge south of town. At daybreak he commenced shelling the woods, and toward noon the 42nd was ordered to charge up and over the ridge. We went at it valiantly, but about halfway up we encountered a most luxurious berry patch. The entire regiment halted, 12. Col. C. C. DeCourcy, see F. H. Mason, 42nd Ohio Infantry.

Another excursion was not so bloodless. Just west of Tazewell we came upon a body of Confederates much too large for us. I may remark that the 42nd, although it claimed to have been in more engagements than any other Ohio regiment, was fortunate in never being taken at disadvantage and never losing a large number of men at one stroke.

first few days we found corn fit to boil or roast and did very well. Then when the corn got too hard to roast, we changed our tin plates into graters by punching holes with our bayonets and made meal. As we had no salt the mush was rather flat, but filling. Fortunately we had plenty of coffee, every man having provided, by hook or crook, a good-sized bag before starting; and four or five quarts per day was quite encouraging. One day I remember we halted for an hour and from somewhere a little flour was issued. Fortunately we were on a ridge of flat stones. Soon had big fires started. Set the stones on edge before them, then wet up the flour, and slapped it on in big handfuls. I had a hunk of that bread when we came to the Ohio. Thought for a while that I might need it more next day, and then it got so hard that a dog couldn't have chewed it. Mush was the main dependence, but we couldn't carry that, and seldom stopped long enough to make it. When we did, the roar of ten thousand graters was equal to that of a good-sized battle. The main trouble was, the road being mountainous and heavily timbered, the Confederate company had blocked it with big trees, and we had to clear the way for the artillery and march night or day where it was clear. The loss of sleep I found more unpleasant than the lack of food or water.

On this march I could sleep only when it was dark, and as we marched most of each night, my sleep ration ran pretty short. One night near morning we went into camp near a big watermellon patch. As we were getting back well loaded, old Ed Rogers, with arms so full that he couldn't see where he was going, fell into a well. The water was not deep enough to drown him and we fished him out, with only the loss of the mellons. 

Our last stop was at Wurtland, and without orders. Then came sundry sleepy orders to fall in, but when we wakened next morning the entire command was just where we had stopped, totally petered out. The next twelve miles brought us to the Ohio at Greenup, tired, dirty, ragged, and lousy, but in extra good health. 

At Wheelersburg, where we went into camp, we came near meeting with disaster. The good-intentioned

Ohio people had learned of our approach, and the whole countryside came in with wagonloads of provisions,

everything we could think of, ham, chicken-pie, barrels of cider. The wagons stood side by side around the entire public square, and good housewives and pretty girls issued rations with liberal hands. No com. sergeants could compete with them. Our belts were lengthened hole by hole, and the wonder is that a soldier was left to tell the tale. However, so far as I know, there were no casualties, and a few days of pork and hard tack brought all to normal.

I must say, though, that we had lived on fresh meat and yams till the old pork and hard tack tasted right good when we got back to it. If we did little to put down the rebellion,

Our command was now broken up. ordered to Plaquemine on the Mississippi, which post we held for the winter. Had comfortable quarters, and a rather pleasant, easy time. I was detailed in charge of a wood squad and spent most of the time in the woods back of town, very agreeably.

About the first of March we were ordered to 

Baton Rouge, and Company A made provost guard. About 

April 1st we were ordered to join General Banks on Red

River. The 120th Ohio of our brigade preceded us, and on the way up the river was attacked from the shore, and about one half their number was killed or disabled. On arriving at the mouth of the river, we received news of 

Bank's disaster and went no further.

Meantime I had received word from Washington that I would be appointed Captain and A. Q. M., and Col 

Sheldon had kindly detailed me as assistant to our brigade quartermaster, that I might learn something of the details of the business. The boat on which we were quartered was one of a number which were placed side by side on the Atchafalaya Bayou, and a plank bridge placed across the bows provided a bridge for the Banks army which was now arriving. They were the sorriest looking lot of duffers, man and horse, which I had ever seen. 1864 1864 We now returned down the Mississippi to Marganza. 

While here the term of my enlistment expired, and I had June 11 leave to go to Baton Rouge for muster-out, but there learned that it is a lot easier to get into the U. S. 

Army than to get out, at least when a man has paid so little attention to red tape as I had done. Having failed, I returned to duty.

As I had not seen my folks for over two years I took the first boat for old north Ohio.

After two days I met up with Miggins, a 42nd boy and old schoolmate, and that made the balance of the trip right pleasant. His home was in Newburg, and when we arrived at Cleveland of a Saturday eve there was no conveyance to Newburg. We had no money to "hire a hack" so struck the road, as we had done so many times before, and arrived at the Miggins home about twelve, midnight. 

There being no Sunday train on the Mahoning Ry., I was obliged to accept the hospitality of the family till 

Monday. Needless to say it was most freely extended.

My leave was spent very pleasantly with my family and friends, and on its expiration I had orders to report to the Chief Comm. of Subsistence at New Orleans, rather to my disappointment, for I had hoped to join Sherman at 

Atlanta. I arrived at New Orleans early in September 

1864 and was assigned to duty as Post Commissary at Plaquemine. 

The force there was small, and I had a good chance to learn the details of the business. A fort was being constructed just below town by a colored regiment from Rhode Island. My quarters were in a large house some distance back of the business section of the town. A few nights after I was located I was wakened by a fusillade of fire arms very nearby. It lasted but a short time, and I learned next day that it was a frequent occurrence. A band of bushwhackers were amusing themselves by starting the alarm and seeing the darkies scurry to the fort. I took no further interest in the game. The winter passed quietly and pleasantly. I remember but few incidents. One was rather amusing.

On Xmas day a party of about twenty officers, including myself, were having a rather jolly time when it

was suggested that we take a ride. The men were mostly cavalry, so provided with horses, but one unfortunate was short a mount, so bestrode an old wagon mule. On our return we were dashing through town much after the style of western cowboys. All turned a corner successfully except the old mule which was several rods behind. 

On the porch of the corner house a half dozen ladies were enjoying their Xmas. The old mule was under such headway that he could or would not make the turn, but bolted onto the porch, his feet going through the rotten floor at each step. I never heard of any casualties among the ladies, but there was some scurrying and screeching.

About ten o'clock each Sunday the whole town was on the levee to witness a steamboat race between the 

Blackhawk and Grey Eagle of the Vicksburg trade. As they appeared, usually side by side, the excitement began, and bets were freely made as to which would first make the landing. All eyes were strained to see which had the lead as they came around the bend, and the victor was sure of an enthusiastic reception.

Later, my clerk and I went to board with a very delightful old German lady and her attractive daughter, and here of evenings I did have some real fun with cards. 

As the custom was, we always had a toddy on the table, a spoonful of which went to the Madam's head. The clerk, who played with her, was red headed and quick of temper and the fraulein, my partner, was the most persistent and skillful cheater I have yet met, so the game usually broke up with lightning flashes between Madam's black eyes and the clerk's red head, to the great amusement of fraulein and her partner.

While at Plaquemine we received news of General Lee's surrender, which caused great rejoicing among us of the U.S.Army and equal sorrow among the natives, many of whom had not yet given up all hope of success. Soon after, I was ordered to Ship Island, at which I was not greatly rejoiced, as it was my least agreeable post.

The force there was light at the time, it being a discontinued Confederate prison camp--just a spot of white sand; not a green thing in sight, and hotter than Tophet.

The officer whom I relieved was a drunken galoot, so the transfer could only be accomplished in his lucid moments.

Soon after I arrived, a gunboat stopped for supplies. Its orders were to cruise along the coast and watch for Ex-President Davis, who, it was suspected, might be taking a foreign tour. The Captain invited me to go with them, which I much desired to do, but could not leave my post on so uncertain an expedition. 

The old wooden war-vessel Constitution was lying at the Island. I supplied it and made quite an agreeable acquaintance with the officers, who had me to dinner and made a pleasant break in the monotony of the post. 

The post officers were not especially entertaining, so I was much pleased when in a month I was relieved and ordered to New Orleans.

The going, however, was not good, as only an occasional vessel stopped at the Island. After a week's waiting the Post Commander had occasion to go over, and invited me to go along in the Headquarters sail-boat. 

This I did, leaving my clerk to come on with the baggage at first opportunity. The boat was small, and had a rowing crew of darkies, eight or ten, with a number of passengers. The boat was pretty crowded, the sea rough, the sun hot, and with seasickness, the day was not all that could be desired. 

Arrived at Fort Pike at nine P.M., where we were well entertained by the officers, and at midnight resumed the journey through Lake Pontchartrain. Fighting mosquitoes the rest of the night, we arrived at Spanish Fort early in the morning and were soon in New Orleans. On arrival I found that I had been assigned to 

General Merrit's Command--a cavalry force at Shreveport.

But owing to my delay on Ship Island another officer had been assigned to him, and I was ordered to Galveston to await orders. 

I have always been thankful that I made the voyage safely, for we were on an old government tub, which had had no repairs during the war, and with a calm sea we were four days in making the trip. The boat was crowded with all kinds of people--returning Confederates,

refugees; and with only three families at Galveston the situation was little improved. Some gunboats had been there recently, not having heard of the surrender, and scared all the people out of town, so that the only quarters I could get were at an old sailors' boarding house. However, the citizens soon came home, and in two or three days I got a room in a nice house and had no more trouble.

In about two weeks my orders came to go to Indianola and establish a depot for the 4th Corps which was then on the way to Texas. General Granger kindly gave me passge on his government boat and we had a pleasant run of only one day, arriving at Indianola on July 4th.

The prospect here was not real good. The wharves were · burned, and the channel so shallow that all freight had to be lightered from the Gulf steamers to the landing. 

For storage I found three old cotton warehouses, with no doors, and much of the siding gone. No quartermaster had arrived, so I had to do my own transporting. From a colored brigade, then the only troops at the Post, I secured a detail of one hundred men, and as the supply steamers arrived, we rolled and toted the stores up to the warehouse a distance of about fifty rods. 

Here I had my first experience with pillaging. 

At night the darkies would roll barrels of sugar or meat down the side alleys to their friends and at the storehouses I had only colored guards who simply watched that no one interfered while the town niggers came to plunder.

In a few days the 4th Corps began to arrive. 

Under the circumstances it made pretty lively work, but 

I am rather proud to be able to say that not a wagon had to wait ten minutes when it came for rations. My object was to give the boys their grub and account for it by hook or crook later. I was fortunate in securing two very good extra clerks, one an old Confederate quartermaster man, and in about a week we had things running not so badly. 

About the first of August the chief commissary of the department came into my office at about three P.M. bringing an officer to relieve me, and ordered me to 1865

start for San Antonio next morning to establish the depot for that post. I was never more pleased, as that was just where I wished to go. I had three steamers unloading, so left my clerks to receive and turn over the stores, and early next morning took stage for S. A. 

The first forty miles to Victoria were over hogwallow prairie - a reckless driver, the stage, an oldfashioned, straight-bottomed affair. So we had to brace our feet and hold on to the braces for dear life. 

At Victoria we stopped over Sunday, and I was lame from the top of my head to the ends of my toes. From there we had an old Concord stage, smoother roads, and a comfortable trip.

I was the first Yankee to go from the coast to San Antonio after the war. As we came to the heights east of San Antonio from Ship Island and the not-muchmore-desirable Indianola, I felt that I had arrived in Paradise. The sun was just setting and the valley and city spread out before us in all their beauty. 

My chief desire in coming to San Antonio was to meet my old friend Judge Stribling, a Union man, originally from Alabama, and a refugee, whose acquaintance I had made in Plaquemine. So, the next morning, I learned that he had just arrived at home, and went in search of him. At his office I was told that he had just gone out on the street. Walking up the plaza I saw him coming. When we met he said not a word, caught my arm, wheeled me around, marched me back to his office, reached under a lounge, drew out a jug, and poured a glass of whiskey down my throat. Then he spoke, and I was in shape to listen.

He was a rough jewel, had a beautiful wife and three fine kids, of whom later. They made my stay in San

Antonio very pleasant. The Judge died soon after, but 

I have never forgotten him, and always considered him one of my very best friends.

My business in San Antonio was pleasant, but rather exacting, as I had the depot of the 4th Corps, the Post commisary, and had to lay in supplies for the winter. My clerks soon came on and in a few days we had all going smoothly. The old government buildings on the

river were commodious and convenient, with good office and lodging rooms. I secured board with another fine 

German lady from New York, and for the first few days arranged with her to sleep me. That plan came near making trouble, as her very pretty daughter was a violent Rebel. However, we later became very good friends. 

I do not flatter myself, but I had a fine gray ridinghorse and I suspect that the horse was the more successful wooer. Anyhow, as we were taking our accustomed ride, I let drop a remark which was taken as rather personal, when she gave the old gray a sharp cut and was quickly beyond my reach. Moral--never try to make love to a girl when she has the better horse. Further, I only remark that in our house I was the only Yankee among a dozen old Confederate Texans, and was never treated better.

Just before Xmas I was relieved, and retired from the service under provision of General Order number 108. My journey to Ohio was for the first two days by stage to Columbus, Texas; then two days by railroad to Houston and Galveston. The first night out we struck a dry norther, but with plenty of blankets and good supply for keeping up internal heat, well--we didn't freeze. The railroad from Columbus was in sad condition--no repairs for the past four years. The four cars were minus half the roofs, all the windows and much of the siding. At the Brazos river the track ran down a long decline, across a pontoon bridge, and up a corresponding rise on the further side. The passengers were notified that they could walk across if they chose, and by taking two cars at a time we arrived safely on the top of the hill to the east. The trip of one hundred miles was made in a little less than two days, and this was the only railroad in Texas at that time. At Galveston we found a comfortable Morgan Line steamer, and a two days voyage brought us to New Orleans.
