PREFACE 

This book grew out of a conviction that language expression is so completely a part of living that the nature of any individual's language is largely dependent upon the nature of that individual's life. Richness or poverty of experience, clear thinking or the lack of it, desirable or undesirable social attitudes and emotions are reflected in language whether we are conscious of it or not. To be sure, language expression has its special techniques; but its fundamental elements reach into life itself and cannot be separated from it. It therefore becomes the first duty of those who are interested in improving the quality of children's language teachers and textbook writers alike— to provide as far as possible that richness and variety of desirable first-hand experiences which are the basis of effective language expression.

Because language is closely bound to living, it is very difficult to consider the teaching of it as a thing apart -something which stands by itself. Almost every activity of a child's life demands an appropriate language expression. 

Growth in appropriateness and effectiveness of language can best be secured in connection with the activity itself. 

When language is separated from the situation which is its reason for being, it is likely to become lifeless and cold. 

It is the purpose of this book to stimulate, to enlarge, and to enrich those activities of children which involve language, thereby laying the foundation for interesting and effective expression, and at the same time to guide children in the use of the language forms which are needed in carrying forward their normal activities both in and out of school. iii

PREFACE The Teacher's Use of the Textbook. The several units in this book offer centers of interest out of which may develop activities typical of those in which children of this grade naturally engage. It is the thought of the authors that the teacher and pupils may at times find it profitable to substitute parallel interests for those suggested in this book-interests which may be more closely related to local needs. There have been developed in connection with each interest presented here, however, all of the language forms which pupils will usually need in carrying forward activities that may develop from a similar interest. 

The Child's Use of the Language Textbook. The most potent factor in learning to use language effectively is the child's own desire that others shall understand what he wishes to say. He uses language, not merely to be sociable, but primarily in order to accomplish purposes which are important to him. TODAY'S ENGLISH, by suggesting things which children really enjoy doing, brings about social situations in which effective language is needed. Conversation, discussion, storytelling, note-taking, outlining, record keeping, and the other aspects of language are taught, not as mere exercises, but as effective means of accomplishing the child's own purposes in real situations. The techniques and mechanics of writing are treated as tools for the improvement of expression rather than as ends in themselves. Adequate explanations and exercises are provided in the text, but they are to be used only by those pupils who need them, and only after the need for them has become apparent to these pupils. In short, TODAY'S ENGLISH is to be used as an aid to the individual pupil in carrying out effectively his own part in the program of work chosen by his class or group.

How the Teacher May Help the Child. The teacher should assist pupils (1) to discover and select interesting things to do which involve profitable language activities, (2) to set up acceptable standards of accomplishment, (3) to

organize their efforts, (4) to carry an enterprise through to a successful conclusion, and (5) to evaluate the results in terms of standards previously set up. At every step, her task is to stimulate, to guide, and to encourage. She must take care not to overinfluence the members of the group at any stage of an activity, nor must she do the work necessary to carry it forward; for that would rob the pupil of his opportunity to learn through the satisfaction that results from accomplishing his own purposes.

Provisions for Individual Differences. Regardless of how carefully pupils may be classified, those in any given school group will always differ widely in their experiences, abilities, standards, interests, and purposes. A teacher should discover these individual differences and should help each pupil to make his own contribution to the accomplishment of the group's purpose. Those who have need at a given time of exactly the same explanation or practice may be given it together; but growth in skill or knowledge will be the result of a pupil's own success in self-imposed and selfdirected effort.

Reviews and Tests. The most effective review of any language skill or knowledge comes from its correct use in vital situations in which it is required as one condition of success. The best guarantee of its mastery is the spontaneous use of the expression or form in the practical situations that require it. However, in response to a prevailing need, this book makes provision for concentrated effort upon specific skills and techniques by offering at the end of each unit generous review exercises. In this volume, these review exercises are of two types. The first section, "More 

Work on Language Skills," reviews and practices the new skills and techniques that have been taught within the unit. 

The second section, "Review Tests and Practice," offers tests and keyed practice exercises on the usage forms and the technicalities presented in the fifth year and the two preceding years.

1. TALKING ABOUT HOBBIES 

What do you do in your spare time? "A good many things," you may say. "I play with Charles, or Jane, who lives next door. I go fishing, or read, or build airplanes, or practice tricks of magic, or do crossword puzzles, or play the piano, or practice baseball, or make candy. It all depends on what the weather is, and how 

I feel, and what time of day it is." 

That may be quite true. But what do you enjoy doing most? What do you think of first? What do you enjoy so much that you have tried to find out more and more about it? Have you learned so much about different kinds of airplanes and about how to cut and carve and whittle that you have a collection of interesting airplane models? Have you practiced molding clay until you can make models of animals and people that delight you and your friends? Have you tried to become an expert in fishing, weaving baskets, or making candy? Have you gone about the work of collecting stamps, arrowheads, ferns, or seed pods until you have a collection that means something to you? 

When you have a very strong interest in one of your spare-time occupations, and have set out to know all about it and to become skillful in it, it is a hobby. 1

TODAY'S ENGLISH 

People have many different hobbies. Some enjoy collecting pieces of beautiful furniture made long ago. 

Some collect interesting pieces of old glass. Some give all of their spare time to gardening or to learning more about birds or the stars. Some like to build model boats or airplanes. Do you know people who have hobbies? 

Tell the class about them. 

What is your hobby? If you haven't thought of it as a hobby before this, what is your favorite spare-time pleasure? Tell your classmates about it. Perhaps you will find that two or three of you have the same hobby. 

In talking to each other about interesting hobbies, try to remember these helps in good conversation: 

1. Do not wait to be called upon. 

2. Speak when you have something interesting to say. 

3. Give everyone a chance to speak. 

4. Ask questions to encourage others to speak. 

5. Listen politely when someone else is speaking, and do not interrupt. 

6. If you interrupt by accident, say, "Please excuse me," or, "I'm sorry." 

Telling about Hobbies 

Here is what Lois said about her hobby: 

My grandmother has a patchwork quilt that is very old. 

One day she said, "Lois, why don't you make a patchwork quilt like this one?" I thought it would be too hard to make, but Grandmother helped me start it. It was the pine tree pattern. I found several books about patchwork quilts and other needlework of colonial times. Now I am very much interested in the life of that time. Some day I hope to have a large collection of patchwork quilts and to know the history of each pattern.

Telling Events in Order 

Did Lois tell her points in their right order? Did she tell you first how she became interested in patchwork quilts? What did she tell next?

Remember, when you tell about your hobby, to tell things in their right order. You may want to tell first how your hobby started, then what you have done, and then what you still plan to do.

Using Good Sentences 

In telling about your hobby, remember to speak in good, whole sentences. You will remember that sentences may do three things: (1) make a statement, (2) ask a question, (3) give a command or make a request.

Read these sentences: 

My hobby is making ship models. (a statement) 

Do you enjoy making models? (a question) 

Please tell me a good book about ships. (a request) 

Keep off the grass. (a command)

When you write, a capital shows the beginning of each sentence, and a period or question mark shows the end of each sentence. When you speak, you must make your voice show where each sentence ends.

This is what John said when his class was having a conversation about hobbies: 

I found a turtle near the brook on my uncle's farm and I put him in a pasteboard box and Uncle Russell told me he would die if he didn't have a better home.

When John got that far, one of the boys told John that he was running his sentences together with and's, so that it was hard to understand him. Then John started over again. This time he stopped at the end of each

Speaking Clearly 

You must speak clearly if you want others to enjoy your hobby story.

Some boys and girls run their words together. They say, "I usta listen to the radio," or "I wanna be an engineer," instead of:

I used to listen to the radio. 

I want to be an engineer. 

Ask a classmate who hears your hobby story to tell you whether you run your words together.

Using Words Correctly 

In telling about your hobby, watch the forms of the words you use. 

It is correct to say:

I drew a plan for each ship model first. 

I have drawn many pictures of airplane models. 

Drew never has a helper. You use the word drawn with have, had, or was; that is, you say was drawn, have drawn, had drawn. 

It is correct to say: 

I drove the nails an inch apart. 

I have driven the nail carefully. 

The word drove never has a helper. Use the word driven with a helper. It is correct to say was driven, has driven, or had driven.

2. FORMING HOBBY CLUBS 

Have you found that several of you enjoy the same hobby? What plans can you make for enjoying your hobbies together?

Starting a Club 

In one class the boys and girls who enjoyed the same hobby formed a little club. There was a stamp club, a science club, an art club, a hiking and woodcraft club, and a model-builders' club. The hiking and woodcraft club met on Saturdays and went on hikes with the father of one of the boys. The other clubs met after school in the schoolroom, and their teacher met with them. 

When these clubs worked together, the boys and girls learned a great deal more about their hobbies and also enjoyed them more. Would you like to form several

Planning Your Club Work 

Some classes make a hobby booklet. There is a chapter for each hobby club. Each boy and girl has one page in the book for telling about his hobby. Some boys and girls put photographs of the things they have made into the booklet.

Some clubs plan a hobby fair. They plan to have it near the close of the school year so that they may have several months' time to become skillful in their particular hobbies. Then they have some fine things to exhibit and are proud to invite their friends to the fair.

Think over these things that other hobby clubs have done. What would you like to do? Make your plans.

As you discuss what you want to do, remember these rules for good discussion: 

1. Listen closely, to understand what is being discussed. 

2. Stay with each question until it is settled. 

3. Speak only when you have something helpful to say. 

4. State your ideas and opinions politely. 

5. Speak clearly, but not too loudly. 

6. Do not interrupt. If you happen to interrupt someone, apologize and wait for your turn.

TODAY'S ENGLISH 3. PLANNING A HOBBY FAIR 

Have you decided to hold a hobby fair? 

If some of you have enjoyed giving puppet plays or acting plays of your own choosing, include a puppet show or a play in your hobby fair. Puppet shows and plays have been given at fairs for hundreds of years. 

If someone has become skilled in doing tricks of magic, ask him to give a magician's show.

A group interested in chorus singing or in playing in an orchestra might give musical numbers. 

Those who are interested in cooking might prepare and serve some refreshments.

Talk over the things you plan to do for your hobby fair. Remember to include not only handwork and collections of objects, but the things people can do before an audience as well.

Arranging Your Exhibit 

A good arrangement of the exhibits of handwork and collections makes each part of your exhibit tell its own story. How would you arrange a collection of dolls in costume to show the history of clothing fashions?

As you worked out your hobby fair, you probably worked together in groups. A group can do better work when it has a way for thinking and planning together.

2. ELECTING OFFICERS 

What officers do you want to have?

The officers usually elected are a president, a vicepresident, a secretary, and sometimes a treasurer.

The president acts as chairman of the meetings. 

With the help of his group and the teacher, he plans the work and appoints committees. 

The vice-president takes the place of the president when he is away.

The secretary keeps a record of the meetings. 

The treasurer takes care of any money the class may have, keeping a record of all money taken in or given out. 

Decide whether you want to have all of these officers in your organization.

AN ILLUSTRATED TALK IN A HOBBY-CLUB MEETING What Electors Have to Do 

When you have decided what officers you need to have, you should do some careful thinking before you elect those officers. If officers do not do their work well, it is usually because the people who elected them did not do their work well.

What is the work of the electors - the people who do the electing? Since each of you is an elector, you should be sure to know.

If an elector is to be helpful in electing the right officer, these are some of the things he must do before he votes: 

1. Know exactly what work this officer has to do. 

2. Think what kind of person can do this work well. 

3. Vote for that kind of person, even though he may not be his close friend.

After the officer has been elected, all the electors must help him in every possible way-even the electors who did not vote for him.

Handling a Motion 

Notice that each suggestion was made in the form of a motion and that each motion was seconded. The president always asks for discussion before he calls for a vote, except upon motions to adjourn and upon other motions where discussion is useless. There can be no discussion until a motion is made and seconded. 

If anyone wants to discuss a motion, he gets permission from the chairman, and then tells his points for or against the motion.

When the discussion is over, the president calls for a vote on the motion. The group may vote either by raising hands or by saying "Aye" and "No" (see pages 38-40).

3. The regular committees report. 

4. Any special committees report. 

5. If there is new business, it is taken up. If there is a program, it is given at this point. 

6. The meeting adjourns. 

There must always be a motion to adjourn the meeting unless the time is too short.

4. TAKING A FIELD TRIP 

Part of your work in learning to recognize birds will be done indoors-studying pictures and books and getting facts. But the most important work will be going out to study birds in their own haunts. Such a trip is called a field trip. 

Starting Well Prepared 

A good bird scout sets out on a field trip prepared in three ways: 

1. He carries in his mind pretty good pictures of the particular birds which he hopes to see. 

2. He carries in his pocket a bird guide, if he owns one. 

3. He carries in his pocket a notebook and a pencil.

If you start out prepared in these three ways, (1) you will have a goal for your trip, (2) you will have help if you see a bird you do not know, (3) you will be able to jot down the names of the birds you saw and when and where you saw them, as well as any questions that you want to take back to your group.

Observing Carefully 

You do not learn very much from just one look at a bird. It is only as you watch a bird patiently for as many minutes as you can that you learn to know it well enough to be really interested in it. Sometime, if you can keep quiet long enough, you will be rewarded by hearing unusual songs or calls or by seeing the bird do some interesting things.

The more facts you have about a strange bird, the easier it will be for you to find out his name when you get back to your group and to your books.

Bird Pools 

Besides houses, birds need water in the hot, dry days of the summer. You can make a bird pool by putting out a flat pan or bowl and filling it with water each day. 

The birds will use such a pool for bathing and drinking. 

To keep it out of reach of cats, the pool ought to be put up high on a stone post or metal pole. It should also be placed in a sheltered part of the grounds or yard, where people are not likely to pass or frighten the birds.

 1. A GREAT GIFT TO MAN 

Think about each piece of clothing you are wearing today, and of what it is made. How many different materials come to your mind? You will probably answer, "Six: silk, wool, cotton, linen, rayon, and leather." Would it surprise you, then, to learn that nine out of ten garments worn by all the people the world over are made of cotton cloth?

Probably the first clothing worn by the early tribes of men was made from the skins of animals. Some tribes learned to peel thin layers of bark from certain trees and to make them into crude garments. Learning in this way that plants might furnish them with tough fibers, men began in time to weave materials from tough grasses. Later they wove the fibers of the flax plant, which grew in some parts of the Old World. The day came when they learned to clip the wool or hair from the backs of their animals and to spin and weave it for use as clothing, and to spin silk cloth from fibers made by the silkworm.

But for a long time most people were unable to have good clothing. The flax plant was scarce. There were not enough sheep in the whole world in the early days to furnish clothing for all the people. The secret of 115

TODAY'S ENGLISH 

silk-making was locked up in China. Good clothing was worn only by kings and nobles and the very rich. 

At last it was learned that, in the country we call India, a new fiber plant had been discovered. The people of India had learned to gather the fibers from the plant, to spin them into fine thread, and then to weave the thread into fine cloth. It was the cloth that we know today as cotton cloth, although some of it was said to be ever so much finer than any cotton cloth that weaving machines make today.

But India was thousands of miles from Europe. It took camel trains and sailing vessels many, many weeks to carry bolts of the fine cotton cloth made in India to people in other parts of the world. For that reason cotton cloth was very costly, and in most countries very scarce. In time Egypt also learned to grow and weave cotton; but even then cotton cloth was too scarce and too expensive for most people to use. The climate of 

Europe was not suited to cotton-growing. 

Finally America was discovered. The people who came to the new continent found that great stretches of land in one section of our country were suited to cottonraising; and, from that time on, a part of the United 

States has been the greatest cotton-growing land in the world. A few of our states furnish over half of the raw cotton used in the world today, and the factories of our country make much of the cotton cloth. 

It wasn't only the climate that made this possible, though. It was the invention of machines that produced cotton thread and cotton cloth many times faster than the hand workers of the early days could produce it. 

Would you like to know more about this plant which produces most of your clothing? Perhaps it would be

of interest to you to read something about the cotton workers of the early days in faraway India - how they could weave such cobweb-like cloth, and how they learned to dye the threads and weave them into beautiful patterns.

Would you like to know how this plant is grown in our own country? Would you like to take an imaginary trip through the cotton-growing states to see how the cotton growers live? Would you like to take a book- and picture-trip to great cotton factories to see how cotton thread is spun and woven?

Poems are much like music. the regular beat of their lines. that reading a poem made you want to dance or trip lightly to the movement of its lines? Have you read other poems that made you feel like marching or running or trotting?

When we want to enjoy music, we do not think the tune quietly to ourselves. We sing it aloud, or we play it on a piano, a violin, or some other instrument. As we hear ourselves sing the tune or play it, we enjoy feeling the time that beats through it. We call that beating of time the rhythm of the music.

All poems were written to be said aloud. In the early days, before there were newspapers or books, the newstellers told their tales in verse. As the tales were told over and over again, people learned them from memory and said or sang them to their children. In this way there came to us the fine old poems which we call ballads. 

To this day we enjoy saying and singing the ballads.

 1. OUR WEALTH OF STORIES 

Is there anyone who does not love a good story?

Was there ever a time when people did not tell and enjoy stories?

Think of men in very early days. In your books about cave men, you have read how these early people drew pictures on the rock walls of their caves to tell about the wild animals they had met. In ancient Egypt the rulers had the stories of their victories over other tribes carved in pictures on the walls and columns of palaces and temples. Later on, the stories were carved in a strange kind of writing on tablets of stone. No matter how or where or when people have lived, they have found some way of putting down their favorite stories for men to read.

At the same time, people told their stories by word of mouth. Fathers and mothers told to their children the stories of great adventures of the tribes, and of their gods and goddesses. Great leaders or chiefs of the tribes gathered their warriors about them and told them tales of the bravery and wisdom of their ancestors. In many tribes there came to be one or two persons who told the stories better than the others, who could tell them in better language and could make them seem more real. 171

TODAY'S ENGLISH 

Such storytellers were the poets or ballad singers of their day. They went about from place to place, telling the great legends to groups of listeners wherever they could find them. Some of them, who sang these tales to the music of an instrument, were called troubadours. Some of the finest of the old legends, myths, folk tales, and songs that we enjoy today have been handed down to us by a long line of storytellers. 

In time, books came to be printed; and some of the books of today are your treasure houses of the great stories of the past. In these books of old legends you may find Norse myths, legends of the ancient Chinese and Japanese, Indian legends, the stories of King Arthur and of Robin Hood, and tales of all kinds that the storytellers in early Russia, Holland, France, Italy, and other countries have told through the ages. Sometimes the same tale was told in many languages. What a treasure house of stories these books of legends are! 

Other books tell tales of the days of not so long ago. 

They tell of the daring men in Europe who first set out on unknown seas to find our own country. They tell stories of the early pioneers in our country. They tell the tales of how men discovered new stars, worked out new inventions, or struggled to make possible some of the many comforts that all of us enjoy today. Interesting as the old legends are, it is also true that many of these modern tales of what man has brought to pass are even more interesting than the tales of ancient peoples. 

What kinds of stories do you like best? Name some of the most interesting tales you have read. See whether you can make a class list of twenty-five or thirty favorite stories. How many of them are myths or legends or fanciful tales? How many of them are true stories?

Who is the best storyteller that you know? Do you think that people are losing the art of storytelling? 

Since we have books, we depend so much on reading our stories that most of us take little time to tell or listen to stories. How would you like to form a storytellers' league? You might plan to get together regularly, either in school time or after school, and tell one another some of your favorite stories.

Telling Your Own Story 

Did your study of Arthur's story give you some points to remember as you tell your own story? What were they? Perhaps you will decide to remember these: 

1. Make your characters talk and act (instead of talking yourself about what they said and did). 

2. Use good describing words and good action words that will show exactly how things happened and how the characters worked, felt, and acted. 

3. Make your story begin and end with some speech or action of the characters (instead of with some explanation of your own).

4. HOLDING A STORY HOUR 

At the meetings of your storytellers' league, you will want to arrange things so that your audience will be comfortable and so that everyone can enjoy the stories. 

Do you want to plan, too, for a chairman who will announce the program and welcome any visitors?

Seating Your Audience 

Somehow a group of people listening to stories enjoy them more if they sit close together and close to the one who is telling the story. Some small groups of girls and boys arrange chairs so that the listeners and the storyteller may sit in a circle. Some large groups plan for the listeners to sit in a group on the floor, close to the storyteller who sits on a low stool or chair. 

When a younger class is invited, it is a good plan to have the visitors sit in a group on the floor, right at the

feet of the storyteller, while the group who are entertaining sit on the floor or on chairs behind the visitors. 

When the weather is warm enough, some boys and girls like to sit outdoors, under a favorite tree, to hold their storytelling hour. 

Make your own arrangements for seating. Your plans will depend upon the space you have and the number of people in your group. 

Conducting the Program 

Who will conduct your program? Has your league a president or a chairman? Is a different committee in charge of each program? Perhaps you will decide to let your president open and close each story hour, and let the chairman of the program committee announce each story on the program. 5. CHOOSING A STORY FOR ACTING 

Very likely you will decide to tell some of your stories by acting them. 999 Some of the finest stories to act are the ones that tell of real adventures. It is fun to act these stories, too, because they give you a chance to think out your own conversation and to plan interesting scenery and costumes. If you act a story like "The Bear Says 'North," the conversation is ready made, and you need only to memorize it. Often it is fun to act these simple stories. 

But it is still more fun to work out a whole play from a story yourself. 

Find some of the great adventure stories in your storybooks. Begin to read them, and then set a time when you can discuss the different stories and choose the one that you want to play first.

7. PLANNING SCENERY AND COSTUMES Acting without Scenery or Costumes 

Scenery is not necessary to a play. Many good plays are given without scenery. Many groups use a green screen or background curtain for a forest or an outdoor scene, a brown screen or curtain for an indoor scene, and a screen or curtain made to look like a brick wall for the background of a street scene. 

These will do very well; and, if you are going to give a number of plays, you may want to make plans for preparing curtains or screens of this type so that you may have them on hand for all plays. You can use screens or curtains when you are not using a regular stage. 

Costumes are not necessary for a good play, either.

The story and the acting are much more important than scenery or costumes. It is more fun to play a num-

ber of stories than to spend too much time on one story. 

You may enjoy giving a play in almost every story hour, with no costumes or scenery. Once or twice in the year, when you wish to invite guests, you might enjoy making costumes and scenery. But for the regular meetings of your storytellers' league, try giving a play just for the sake of giving a story in dialogue form.

On the other hand, it is fun to plan for scenery and costumes. There are boys and girls in your class who like to paint and draw, and to sew, and to use carpenter's tools. These boys and girls may wish to plan and make costumes and scenery.

VIII  1. EXCHANGING THOUGHTS 

Have you ever thought how easy it is for you to tell a thought or send a message to someone else and to get his thoughts or messages in return? We call this exchange of messages communication. 

How many things, in the course of a day, help you to communicate with others? 

In the morning you use the direct, spoken word to tell your mother that you would rather have orange juice than bananas for breakfast. A little later you step to the telephone and call up a friend to tell her that you will be ready for a walk by nine o'clock. Some unseen power makes a connection, and you give your message orally to your friend, although you do not see her. Before you go, you write a note to your cousin in another state and drop your note into a mailbox when you leave the house, and you are sure that your written message will reach your cousin. 

When you come home from your walk, you find that your mother wants to send a message to your father in a distant city. You go with her to a telegraph office, where she writes her message on paper. Someone ticks off the message over a wire to the distant city where your father happens to be. Perhaps by the middle of the afternoon there comes a reply from your father. 209

TODAY'S ENGLISH 

At noon, you turn on the radio, and out of the box comes the voice of someone far away who is telling you the news of the day. In the afternoon you go to a motion picture, and there again you get the voice of some unseen person coming from an unseen place. The strange thing about it is that the words you hear were spoken some time ago. 

All day long, you get messages without words, too. 

When you open your eyes, the hands of the clock tell you silently that it is time to get up. A little later, a bell tells you that it is time to go to school. As you walk down the street, a green light and a red light give you their silent messages, and, if you are wise, you heed them.

You reach the home of a schoolmate and press a little button beside the door. In some way the people inside get the message that you are outside and they come to let you in. Near a railroad track a clanging bell gives you a wordless message. In the schoolroom a thermometer tells you whether the temperature is too hot or too cold. You step on the scales, and a little metal needle tells you how much you weigh. 

Can you think of other ways in which you give and receive messages, either with or without words? Think of other places where you might be — at the seashore, on a boat, on a train. What other ways are there in which you either give or receive a communication?

SENDING SMOKE SIGNALS 

Among certain Indian tribes a steady column of smoke meant, "Stop." Four smoke columns in a row meant, "Come to council."

You make a puff by covering the fire, uncovering it for just a moment, then covering it again. You make a column by letting the smoke rise steadily. When the signaling is over, or you want a long pause, you rake the wet leaves off and let the fire burn with a flame.

All you need for a smoke signal is a small fire, some wet leaves and grass, and a wet animal skin or blanket. The wet leaves make the fire smoke, and the skin or blanket is used to cover the fire to show puffs and columns. 

Four large puffs of smoke mean, "Go on." One large puff, followed by many smaller puffs, means, "We are in danger from the enemy."
