PREFACE On June 19, 1968, the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 

1968 became law. The Act provided for increased federal aid to State and local law enforcement agencies through a comprehensive program of planning grants, action grants, and research, demonstration and educational aid designed to strengthen and improve the nation's crime control effectiveness. It superseded and absorbed programs- supported under the 

Law Enforcement Assistance Act of 1965. 

The new Act has made possible for the first time a wide-scale program of aid for States and local units of government. To qualify for aid, States must develop comprehensive law enforcement plans as defined in the Act. To facilitate such planning, the Act provides grants to 

State planning agencies whose primary function will be to develop, revise, and implement these State plans. During Fiscal Year 1969, the first year of program operation, all States developed comprehensive law enforcement plans and qualified for action grants to execute the programs set forth in such plans.

This 1970 edition of the Guide replaces the initial edition (November, 1969) as modified by SPA Directors Memo No. 10 which promulgated a simplified format for first year plans under the Act and an action grant application procedure (February 28, 1969). It combines previous issuances relating to 1970 planning and action grants released through the SPA Directors Memo series. Together with the LEAA Financial Guide (May, 1969) and Discretionary 

Grants Guide (January, 1970) it provides complete guidance on application, award and administration of planning and action grants during Fiscal Year 1970 Under Parts B and C of the Act.

SECTION I. THE PART B PROGRAM STATE PLANNING AGENCY GRANTS Authority for Planning Program. 

Title I of the Act establishes the new law enforcement assistance program. Its text is set forth in Appendix A. Part B of Title I authorizes "grants to the States for the establishment and operation of State law enforcement planning agencies" to prepare, develop, and revise comprehensive law enforcement plans. A total of $19 million was available for such grants during fiscal 1969 and $21 million has been appropriated for fiscal 1970. 

Administration of Federal Program 

The power to make grants to States is vested in the Law Enforcement Assistance Administration. It operates under the general authority of the 

Attorney General and is composed of an Administrator of Law Enforcement Assistance and two Associate Administrators who are appointed by the President by and with the advice and consent of the Senate. The Administration's Office of Law Enforcement Programs will administer Part B grants. 

Objective of Program 

The Act provides increased Federal assistance to help States and localities deal with crime and lawlessness. It affirms the principle that crime is essentially a local problem and that the Federal Government should support, but not supplant, local responsibilities for law enforcement. The State planning agency's role will be to develop and implement plans for orderly, realistic and effective improvement in existing law enforcement and crime control systems. It is grounded on the premise that comprehensive planning, based on 

State and local evaluation of law enforcement problems, is an indispensable requisite for significant progress in crime prevention and control, increased public safety and effective utilization of federal and local funds. 

Scope of Program Coverage 

Law enforcement as defined in the Act includes "all activities pertaining to crime prevention or reduction and enforcement of the criminal law, thus encompassing all aspects of law enforcement and criminal justice systems-police, courts, and corrections, as well as general programs for crime prevention and citizen action. It covers the prevention, detection and investigation of crime, the apprehension of offenders, the prosecution and defense of criminal cases, the trial, conviction and sentencing of defendants, and the correction and rehabilitation of convicted persons, including imprisonment, probation, parole, and community treatment. Where reference is hereafter made in the Guide to courts, this will include prosecution and defender systems as well. Similarly, reference to corrections will include institutional, probation, parole, and community treatment systems. 

SPA Guide 1 (January, 1970)

Jurisdictions Eligible for Grants 

States, the District of Columbia, the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico, and all other territories and possessions of the United States are eligible for grants to operate State planning agencies and develop comprehensive plans. 

Local units of government, or combinations of such units, may receive planning funds from States but may not apply for direct federal grants under Part B unless the State in which they are located failed to apply for a planning grant by December 19, 1969.

Standards of Eligibility 

The Act prescribes conditions which must be met to qualify and maintain eligibility for Part B planning and administration grants. An applicant 

State must: (1) have established and continue to maintain a State planning agency created or designated by the Governor and subject to his jurisdiction; (2) assure that its State planning agency will be representative of law enforcement agencies of the State and of the units of general local government within the State; (3) be willing and able to provide at least 10 percent of the expenses of establishing and operating the State planning agency; (4) agree to make at least 40 percent of all federal funds granted under Part B available to units of general local government or combinations of such units to insure local participation in formulating, revising, and updating the comprehensive State plan; (5) agree to provide the State planning agency with staff competencies and resources necessary to assure that the agency's statutory responsibilities will be carried out; and (6) agree to account for its federal grant funds and meet reasonable fiscal and administrative requirements. 

These standards of eligibility are explained in succeeding paragraphs of the Guide.

Amount of Grants 

The Act contains a formula which allocates the funds available for planning grants among the several States (Section 205). Applicant States may request funds up to the maximum allocation as needed during fiscal year 1970. 

The formula allocates $100,000 to each State and divides remaining planning funds among the States according to their relative populations. See Section 

SPA Guide 2 (January, 1970)

II, Page 37, for a schedule of State allocations and the population estimates used in determining the allocations. 

Types of Grants 

The Act requires a vigorous program of implementation during the initial years of operation. Accordingly, a two-stage procedure which provides early availability of funds for planning agency grants was established in fiscal 1969 and has been continued in fiscal 1970. The procedure currently provides for: (1) the immediate award of advance funds (in amounts of up to 25 percent of a State's allocation for Part B grants) to support continuing operation of the State planning agency; and (2) the subsequent award, following enactment of the current year appropriation, of the State's full planning agency grant for plan development, program implementation, subgrant award and administration, and all other authorized activities. 

Application for Grants

Application for planning agency funds may be made by States at any time during the 1970 fiscal year; however, applications should be submitted not later than December 31, 1969. Section II of this Guide sets forth the prescribed format for grant applications: 

(1) for advance funds a single page, combined "application award" form. 

(2) for the full planning agency grant - a single page face sheet to which informational appendices are attached relating to (a) a description of the State planning agency's staffing and supervisory board structure and plan for making available 40 percent of 1970 planning funds to local units of government and (b) budgetary needs.

State Planning Agencies - Functions 

Development of the comprehensive plans prescribed by the Act is to be achieved by the "State law enforcement planning agency. Planning grants under Part B are for the purpose of establishing and supporting such agencies. 

A State planning agency's responsibilities include: --preparation, development and revision of comprehensive plans based on an evaluation of law enforcement problems within the State; --definition, development and correlation of action projects and programs under such plans; 

SPA Guide 3 (January, 1970)

--establishment of priorities for law enforcement improvement in the State; --providing information to prospective aid recipients on the benefits of the program and procedures for grant application; --encouraging grant proposals from local units of government for law enforcement planning and improvement efforts; --encouraging project proposals from State law enforcement agencies; --evaluation of local applications for aid and awarding of funds to local units of government; --monitoring progress and auditing expenditures under grants to local units of government; --encouraging regional and metropolitan area planning efforts, action projects and cooperative arrangements; --coordination of the State's law enforcement plan with other federally-supported programs relating to or having an impact on law enforcement; --oversight and evaluation of the total State effort in plan implementation and law enforcement improvement; and --collecting statistics and other data relevant to law enforcement in the State as required by the Administration. 

State Planning Agencies - Structure 

A State planning agency may be created or designated as (i) a new unit of State government or (ii) a division or other component of an existing State crime commission, planning agency or other appropriate unit of State government. Details of organization and structure are matters of State discretion. 

However, the State planning agency must: (1) be a definable agency in the executive branch of State government charged with and empowered to carry out the responsibilities imposed by the Act; (2) have a supervisory board (i.e., a board of directors, commission, committee, council, etc.) which has responsibility for reviewing, approving and maintaining general oversight of the State plan and its implementation, of plan action priorities, of sub-grants or allocations to localities, and of other planning agency functions;

SPA Guide 4 (January, 1970)

(3) have an administrator and staff who devote full time to the agency's work.

While responsibilities for State plan development, implementation, and correlation must ultimately reside in the State planning agency, subject to the jurisdiction of the State chief executive, this does not preclude important roles by State law enforcement, correctional, judicial and prosecutive agencies in plan development relating to their respective areas of competence, nor by local units of government and their law enforcement agencies, nor utilization of staff of other State agencies to assist with State planning agency functions.

The Act contemplates that State planning agencies will be continuing bodies. Accordingly, State and local government should plan and provide for such continuity.

Planning Agency Standards 

Gubernatorial Designation and Jurisdiction 

The Act requires that State planning agencies be "created or designated by the chief executive of the State" and be "subject to his jurisdiction" (Section 203(a)). These requirements can be met whether the agency is established by legislative enactment, executive order, or a combination of the two. 

It is not inconsistent with gubernatorial creation or designation for the State legislature to prescribe the size, composition, or other characteristic of the agency provided the State governor's responsibility for establishing the agency and his jurisdiction over it are clear.

Planning Agency Standards 

Representative Character 

The Act stipulates that State planning agencies must be representative "of law enforcement agencies of the State and of the units of general local government within the State" (Section 203(a)). As has been indicated, State agencies must consist of (i) a supervisory board, and (ii) an administrator and staff. The supervisory board oversees the work of the agency and thus must possess the "representative" character required by the Act. The composition of such boards may vary from State to State; however, balanced representation is required and must include: (1) representation of State law enforcement agencies; (2) representation of units of general local government by elected policy-making or executive officials; (3) representation of law enforcement officials or administrators from local units of government; (4) representation of each major law enforcement function--police, corrections, and court systems --plus, where appropriate, representation identified with the Act's special emphasis areas, i.e., organized crime and riots and civil disorders; 

SPA Guide 5 (January, 1970)

(5) representation of juvenile delinquency as well as adult crime control competencies; (6) representation of community or citizen interests; and (7) representation that offers reasonable geographical and urbanrural balance and regard for the incidence of crime and the distribution and concentration of law enforcement services in the State. (8) representation, as between State law enforcement agencies on the one hand and local units of government and local law enforcement agencies on the other, that approximates proportionate representation of State and local interests. 

In determining conformity with the foregoing, it is possible for one board member to be representative of more than one element or interest. 

Because of the existing diversity of State governmental structures and of law enforcement conditions within the States, the representative character of a State planning agency and its staff will be evaluated by the Administration on a case-by-case basis in determining compliance with the statutory requirements.

Planning Agency Standards - Funds for Local Participation 

State planning agencies must make at least 40 percent of their federal planning grant monies available to units of general local government or combinations of such units to participate in formulation of the comprehensive State plan (Section 203 (c)). Because of such required local government participation, it is essential that planning agencies act promptly to implement a program for sub-grants, transfer of funds, etc., to local agencies. The method and plan for implementation of such aid to local units of government is a condition and standard for approval of any application for a State's full planning agency grant. Award of local subgrants should normally be completed within the 60-day period following award of the State's full Part B planning agency grant.

In complying with the "local availability" requirement, States should be guided by the following: (1) Availability. Funds should normally be made "available" to local units of government by direct grant or other type of fund allocation or transfer. The costs of State-provided planning services or State-conducted studies for local units may not be charged as funds made "available" to local units without specific approval of the State planning agency's supervisory board and of the units of local government to which the services will be made available. (2) Use. The purpose of any planning agency funds transferred to local units of government should be to (i) provide local components of the comprehensive State plan, or studies, recommendations, SPA Guide 6 (January, 1970)

analyses, and data to be used in formulating, revising, or expanding the State plan or (ii) where appropriate, establish and support continuing planning units or capabilities. (3) Priorities. Priorities in funding local planning should be given to the State's major urban and metropolitan areas, to other areas of high crime incidence and potential, and to efforts involving combinations of local units. (4) Multi-Unit Efforts. Planning efforts on a regional, metropolitan areas, or other "combined interest" basis are encouraged. Common or consistent planning regions with other federally-supported programs or with existing State planning districts, as contemplated by Bureau of the Budget Circular A-80 (January 31, 1963), should be considered, as well as utilization, where feasible, of the planning efforts of CDA's (community development agencies) under the Model Cities program conducted pursuant to the Demonstration 

Cities and Metropolitan Development Act of 1966. 

(5) Combinations of Units. Where funds are made available to combinations of units of general local government, the normal approval and review procedures which are considered on the basis of local law or practise to bind individual units to such joint efforts shall be followed. The Administration has not limited "combinations of local units" to formally constituted entities established by constitution or compact of participating local units. However, any "combinatiors" organized to request or receive Title I planning funds must have some pre-existing or new base of local consent. 

It is particularly important, where new regions have been organized by States or where pre-existing regions established for federal aid programs not directly related to crime control have been used as local grantees, that efforts be made to obtain and document acceptability by the local governments concerned. Arrangements which appear to raise questions may be referred to the Administration for review and assistance with respect to needed adjustments or documentation of local acceptability. State planning agencies should recognize that under the Act, regional combinations must be more than State imposed geographic units and need to enjoy a base of local unit acceptability and representation. (6) Local Funds Not Required. The Act provides that portions of the 

40 percent local share "not required" for local participation in State plan formulation may be expended for other State plan activities (Section 203(c)). No unused funds will be deemed "not required" unless the State planning agency can demonstrate that: (i) not later than 60 days following the award of the full State planning agency grant, a 40 percent local share plan was activated and made known to local units of government; (ii) local units were encouraged to participate and offered assistance to participate; and (iii) after a reasonable period of time (at least 60 days following such 60-day period) response from local units of government was insufficient to absorb or utilize the 40 percent local share. 

Where 40 percent local, share funds are originally offered only to regional aggregations or combinations of local units of government, and response from such aggregations or combinations is insufficient 

SPA Guide 7 (January, 1970)

to absorb or utilize the full 40 percent share, participation must be offered to individual units of government on some equitable basis before any balance of such share may be expended by State planning agencies for other State plan activities.

Planning Agency Standards Staff and Capabilities 

State planning agency programs and resources must provide reasonable assurance that the required agency functions can be properly executed. The following minimum standards are established for the State planning agency staff: (1) A full-time administrator. (2) A staff complement of adequate size (e.g., no less than three full-time professionals or equivalent) and competencies (e.g., police science, corrections, courts, planning and program development etc.) to accomplish required planning and plan implementation for each of the major law enforcement components, for administration of the State sub-grant program to local units of government, and for all other planning agency responsibilities. (3) The inclusion of the planning agency staff within the State's existing personnel system or some other adequate merit system, subject to Administration approval.

To assure that adequate funds are available to finance the level of planning agency staff capability necessary for the proper discharge of statutory responsibilities, not more than one third of a State's planning grant should be used for contracting with non-governmental agencies or organizations to provide planning services or assistance. A similar limitation applies to funds provided by the State as its matching contribution to the planning effort. In exceptional cases, States may request prior written approval of the Administration for a higher "contracted services" ceiling. 

States with sizeable planning grants will be expected to substantially exceed the staff levels and competencies appropriate for smaller States. 

Neither the foregoing enumeration nor the enumeration of "representative character" categories on pages 5 and 6 is meant to preclude the inclusion of relevant technical competencies (science and technology, education, community relations, systems analysis, etc.) within the planning agency's staff, advisory or policy review structures. The merit system requirement is not meant to preclude exemptions, if appropriate under State law, for the administrator of the planning agency and specified key aides.*

Planning Agency Standards - Cost Contribution by State 

Grants for comprehensive planning may not exceed 90 percent of the total cost of State planning agency operation (Section 204). Thus, States must agree to support at least 10 percent of planning agency costs, and their planning grant applications must reflect this commitment. State contributions may be based on cash allocations or specific fund appropriations for this purpose, or in-kind contributions of services, facilities or goods. In-kind contributions are subject to Administration regulations specifying, for example, that such contributions be based on a fair and current market valuation for the goods, services, or facilities involved and that allocations of staff time be valued at regular salaries and wages for the per sonnel involved. Any State which does require such local contributions must not impose type or valuation requirements which are more stringent than those required of the State in comprable program efforts. 

Planning Agency Standards - Fiscal and Administrative Requirements 

Applicants should be aware of reporting requirements, fiscal rules, and other grant conditions applicable to States receiving planning agency funds under the Act. Fiscal rules and general grant conditions are set forth in 

Section II of this Guide. These rules and conditions provide, in summary, for the establishment of adequate fund accounting systems, reporting requirements, fund disbursement procedures and compliance with policies generally applicable to administration of federal grants. The rules incorporate (i) the principles and standards for selected cost items set forth in Bureau of the 

Budget Circular No. A-87 entitled Principles for Determining Costs Applicable to Grants and Contracts with State and Local Governments (May 19, 1968) and (ii) the general financial requirements promulgated in the LEAA Financial 

Guide for Administration of Planning Action Grants (May, 1969). 

Comprehensive Plans 

The Act requires that plans developed by State planning agencies must cover all facets and elements of law enforcement activity, including police, court and correctional programs and systems as well as general crime prevention and control programs. Section IV of the Guide defines in further detail the essentials of a comprehensive State plan and the meaning of the comprehensive plan concept.

SECTION II. THE APPLICATION PROCESS--STATE PLANNING AGENCY GRANTS 1. GENERAL 

The Administration has utilized in fiscal years 1969 and 1970 an application format for State planning grants designed to permit early initiation of planning agency activities. Two types of applications are involved: 

Request for Advance Funds (Form LEAA-1) 

This is a single sheet combined application-award document pursuant to which the State governor, with insertion of the appropriate dollar amount and the names of those individuals responsible for program development and financial accounting with respect to the requested funds (permanent or interim basis), can promptly qualify for advance ("continuing resolution") monies to continue operation of the planning agency, meet prescribed standards, and otherwise implement the State plan and its programs pending submission and approval of a full planning grant application and determination of the State's total planning grant allocation for the new year.

Application for Full State Planning Agency Grant (Form LEAA-2) 

This application consists of a face sheet and two attachments: (1) A "planning agency data" section (current staff listing, current supervisory board listing, and fund availability plan for localities) and (2) A fiscal year 1970 budget for the State planning agency 

(Form LEAA-3). 

Completion and Submission of Applications 

The application forms, with instructions for all items, are set forth in the pages immediately following. Separate sets of application materials are available for preparation of official submissions. 

Two copies of completed "advance fund" applications (one executed) and six copies of full planning grant applications (two executed) should be forwarded to:

Law Enforcement Assistance Administration U. S. Department of Justice 

Washington, D. C. 20530

Attention: Office of Law Enforcement Programs or after September 15, 1969, to cognizant Regional Offices established within the Office of Law Enforcement Programs. 

SPA Guide 11 (January, 1970)

Deadlines were established at August 1, 1969 for "advance fund" applications and October 1, 1969 for full planning grant applications, in each case with provision for extension as appropriate to adjust for delay in approval of the fiscal year 1970 appropriation for Part B grants. 

Grant Conditions as Part of Award 

All awards of initial funds and full annual grants are subject to, and incorporate by reference, the grant conditions set forth on pages 27 to 30 of the Guide. These include general rules covering a variety of grant administration requirements and rules relating to fiscal administration of grants. The fiscal administration rules incorporate, in turn, the provisions of the LEAA Financial Guide (May, 1969) prescribing policies, procedures, and requirements on such fiscal matters as accounting systems and records, cost allowability, grantee matching contributions, award and payment of grant funds and financial reports. 

Amount of State Allocations 

The total appropriation approved by the Congress for planning grants in fiscal year 1969 is $21 million. A chart which indicates the amount of each State's allocation is set forth on page 31 of the Guide. This also indicates the State populations used in computing the allocations, which are derived from Bureau of the Census estimates of population as of July 1, 1968. Although applicants were instructed to refer to an allocation chart based on an amount equivalent to the first year $19 million appropriation to determine the "advance funds" for which they might apply (up to 25 percent of total allocation) and the maximum amount which could be awarded to support planning activities during fiscal year 1970, the chart on page 31 reflects the final approved Part B allocations.

A. Law Enforcement Needs, Problems and Priorities 

The summary statement prepared for this section of the comprehensive plan should give an overview of needs and problems as they exist throughout the state and in all aspects of law enforcement and criminal justice. 

Discussion of significant problems and needs should provide sufficient detail to support the general content and direction of the multi-year and annual action programs set forth in Sections C and D. Special statements of needs and problems in each of the state's largest cities and their metropolitan areas (i.e., SMSA's) should be incorporated in the discussion. 

This section should also include a general statement of priorities as appraised by the state planning agency and its supervisory board. Discussion would be at a broader level than the statement of goals and objectives for each functional category included in the multi-year plan (Section C), focusing on overall priorities and priority rankings as between the various functional categories and major groupings of program activity.

The identification of needs and problems should have the full participation of state planning agency staff, supervisory board members and such regional and local planning groups as are organized and operative throughout the state. This section will constitute a restatement and, as appropriate, an expansion of similar material presented in the state's initial action plan. 

Discussion of problems and needs should incorporate or refer, as much as possible, to relevant data and facts on crime incidence and state resources so that a concrete and quantitative portrayal of problems and needs may be shown.

B. Existing Law Enforcement Systems and Available Resources 

Describe the state's system of law enforcement and criminal justice administration including (a) organization, activities and resources of police, court and correctional agencies and (b) statistics re size, costs, workloads, manpower and facilities of each major component. 

The discussion should (i) indicate which functions are carried out by the state, which are the responsibility of counties or townships, and which are performed by cities and towns, (ii) describe those activities under the direct operational responsibility of the state, (iii) generally outline the powers of, relationships between, and current extent of coordination among, the various law enforcement agencies and between general government and the agencies of law enforcement, (iv) list those services which the state is currently providing to local law enforcement agencies and (v) describe the extent of cooperative arrangements which exist at multi-state, state, regional and local levels with respect to law enforcement services (specifically mentioning significant ones).

C. The Multi-Year Plan 

The Act requires that State plans describe the direction, scope and general types of improvements to be made in the future. This component should be an overall plan for law enforcement improvements during a period spanning July 1, 1969 through December 31, 1970 and the succeeding three (or more) calendar years.

This is a critical element of the state plan and will require a large share of planning time and resources. The four year period (current action period plus succeeding three years), or longer if desired by the SPA, should be the basis of plan projections. It will provide a common base for long term planning, budgeting, measurement of accomplishments, and assessment of national needs and progress within a time frame appropriate for multi-year planning purposes. 

Previous sections of the plan have described state and local law enforcement problems, identified needs and goals and outlined general priorities for accomplishing these goals. The general statement will provide the opportunity to set forth, as fully as possible, the current status and future direction of the state planning effort. It will serve to place in perspective and provide the overall rationale for the components of both the multi-year plan and the annual action program. 

A recommended way of organizing the general statement, as well as structuring the multi-year plan, is to seek to define the kind of law enforcement system deemed "ideal" yet attainable for the state and its localities in terms of manpower, training, equipment, facilities, workloads, operational standards, and services provided, even though achievement, under optimal conditions, will require several years (e.g., five to ten years). This definition should be as concrete as possible in terms of goals and capabilities to be pursued, e.g., specific police manpower levels desired; minimum training hours for new recruits; equipment goals for such items as automobiles and hand-held radios; number and kind of new juvenile detention or diagnostic facilities to be established; information system capabilities, terminals, and equipment levels, etc. With this "ideal" system concretely defined, the multi-year plan can be designed to show how much progress will be sought in each improvement area during each year of the "current plus three-year" planning period.

An Act 

To assist State and local governments in reducing the incidence of crime, to increase the effectiveness, fairness, and coordination of law enforcement and criminal justice systems at all levels of government, and for other purposes. 

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That this Act may be cited as the "Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968" 

TITLE I-LAW ENFORCEMENT ASSISTANCE DECLARATIONS AND PURPOSE 

Congress finds that the high incidence of crime in the United States threatens the peace, security, and general welfare of the Nation and its citizens. To prevent crime and to insure the greater safety of the people, law enforcement efforts must be better coordinated, intensified, and made more effective at all levels of government. 

Congress finds further that crime is essentially a local problem that must be dealt with by State and local governments if it is to be controlled effectively.

It is therefore the declared policy of the Congress to assist State and 82 STAT. 198 local governments in strengthening and improving law enforcement at every level by national assistance. It is the purpose of this title to (1) encourage States and units of general local government to prepare and adopt comprehensive plans based upon their evaluation of State and local problems of law enforcement; (2) authorize grants to States and units of local government in order to improve and strengthen law enforcement; and (3) encourage research and development directed toward the improvement of law enforcement and the development of new methods for the prevention and reduction of crime and the detection and apprehension of criminals.
