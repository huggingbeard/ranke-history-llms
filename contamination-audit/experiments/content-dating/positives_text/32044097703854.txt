Annual Report of the Governor of American Samoa H. Rex Lee, Governor 

​Review of the Year 

Political development continues to play an important role in American Samoa. With the approval and promulgation of the first Constitution of American Samoa on April 27, 1960, the territory awaited with anticipation the opening of the Seventh Legislature of American Samoa on March 13, 1961, the first held under the new Constitution. 

The opening session attended by traditional leaders and Government officials and the tone of the whole session complemented the remarks of the Secretary of the Interior, The Honorable Stewart L. Udall, that the Legislature's record will greatly influence the rate and extent of the future development of self-government and self-determination. 

The first bill signed by the Governor on April 3, 1961, under the new Constitution, created a sports commission. Subsequent acts revised the appellate court system, provided for regulations of professional surveyors, repealed the Liquor Control Act, provided for the possibility of the sale of the bank, prescribed penalties for the littering of public highways, and provided for payment of compensation to legislative members.

The appointment of H. Rex Lee as Governor of American Samoa was made on May 6, 1961. A veteran career specialist on American 

Indians and dependent peoples, Governor Lee was inaugurated on May 24, 1961, in a public ceremony on the malae in Fagatogo, American Samoa. Announcement was also made of the appointment of 

Air Force Major Eric J. Scanlan on July 1, 1961, as the Government Secretary.

After the inauguration of Governor Lee, it was immediately decided to start a complete rehabilitation and development program on the islands to correct the lagging economic and social development and a $5 million supplemental fund was requested to accomplish this program. 1

After the end of the fiscal year (July 1) congressional approval of a supplemental appropriation of $42 million was granted. This approval contains about $3.5 million for construction to replace dilapidated public structures and to build new facilities. Education construction ranks high among the items included in the appropriation and will provide space in school for every child who should be in school, correcting the present system in which all qualified graduates of the junior high school system are not able to enroll in the high school system.

Other construction included in the supplemental appropriations are road improvement, repairs to Government facilities, such as docks, improvements to the water system and other utilities, and reconstruction of certain medical facilities. 

Economic development by private capital is not being overlooked and it is intended that hotels to accommodate the tourist industry in American Samoa will be constructed in the near future. Plans are also being developed for possible location of a coconut processing plant in the territory.

Among the major assignments facing the new administration is the preparation for the South Pacific Conference in American Samoa in July 1962. Since this is the first time the conference, which is scheduled every 3 years, will be held on American soil, international interest will be focused on the policies and programs in American Samoa. Realizing the conference will be a major challenge and an unparalleled opportunity, the Government began early to consider its requirements. A South Pacific Conference Planning Committee, consisting of Samoans and local Government employees, was appointed to coordinate the needs of the conference for necessary landscaping, catering and housekeeping, public relations, and entertainment arrangements.

Construction of adequate housing for the expected 200 delegates was the first major project to be instituted. Work was begun in 

February on the construction of two new buildings at Utulei which will be completed early in 1962. Construction is in accordance with the Government's 6-year plan for developing school facilities and at the termination of the conference, the buildings will be used as a part of the high school system. Airport construction continued to receive emphasis during the fiscal year. Insufficient equipment and breakdowns plagued the construction of the proposed 9,000-foot runway but work in general was accelerated. The objective is now a minimum operating airport facility by June 1962 with final completion scheduled for early 1963.

A special Senate Study Group arrived in American Samoa in December 1960 to conduct a study and investigation of conditions in the territory to determine economic improvement and provide a greater amount of self-government. Emphasis on the responsibility of the United States in the South Pacific and in the forthcoming conference was further evidenced by the Study Group in proposing a more active role in the development of Samoa in the American tradition without a sacrifice of the native culture. 

As a means of training Samoans in the art of long-line fishing, the local tuna canning company purchased a fishing vessel in Japan. 

The acquisition of this vessel will add to the skills of the men of the territory, provide additional employment opportunities, and help raise the standard of living in Samoa. 

A change in the Social Security Act as authorized by Public Law 86-77 extended coverage to American Samoa effective January 1, 1961. All employees of the Government and its instrumentalities, exclusive of those persons contributing to the Federal Retirement System, were included in the program on a compulsory basis. For the first time applicants in the southern hemisphere were issued Social Security cards and Government workers were provided with an important extension of benefits.

Geography General Information 

American Samoa is an insular possession of the United States administered by the Department of the Interior. It comprises the eastern islands of the Samoan group which are located along the 14° of latitude south of the Equator at about the 170 meridian west longitude. These islands are approximately 2,300 miles southwest of

Hawaii and 1,600 miles northeast of the northern tip of New Zealand, in a direct line. Western Samoa (77 miles westward) is a trust territory administered by New Zealand pursuant to a trusteeship agreement with the United Nations. 

There are seven islands in the American Samoa group: Tutuila, where the capital, Pago Pago, and central administrative offices are located; Aunu'u; the Manu'a group, which includes Ta'u, Olosega, and Ofu; Swains, and Rose, both of which are coral atolls, the latter uninhabited (total area 76 square miles). 

The people of American Samoa are American nationals and represent one of the few remaining societies of Polynesians with a rela- 632842-62- 2

tively small group of persons of mixed blood. The population of the territory has increased from 5,697 in 1900, when the jurisdiction of this Government was taken over by the United States Navy, to 20,051 according to the 1960 census. 

The main islands are of volcanic origin and are now mostly a series of ridges rising abruptly from the sea. Tutuila is of irregular shape, approximately 18 miles long and 6 miles across, at the widest point, and contains 42 square miles of land. A mountain range extends almost the whole length of the island which is nearly bisected by Pago Pago Bay, one of the finest and most beautiful harbors in the South Pacific. 

The climate of the islands is tropical but pleasant. From May to 

November moderate southeast trade winds blow; during the other months the winds are variable. The heaviest rainfall occurs from 

December to March and the average precipitation per year for the past 40 years has been approximately 200 inches. The yearly temperatures range from 70° to 90° F., and the humidity is about 80 percent. 

Rainfall and temperature statistics are found in Appendix V. 

The soil of the islands is of two kinds-clay and sandy loam. In the valleys the soil is alluvial and quite fertile for growing tropical fruits and taro. Due to the heavy rainfall the soil on hillsides is thin with little subsoil. Certain elements necessary to temperate zone plant life were apparently burned out at the time of the formation of these islands. In certain localities one kind of tree or vegetable will grow well and only a short distance away the same tree or vegetable will not thrive. Lack of growth in certain localities is due to mineral deficiencies in the soil. The natural resources of the territory are meager.

History 

American interest in the islands of Samoa commenced with the report made by the United States exploring expedition, which visited the islands under the leadership of Lt. John Wilkes in 1839. It was not until some 30 years later, however, that a formal relationship was entered into with the people of Samoa by a representative of the United States. Primarily as a result of commercial interest in obtaining harbor facilities and rights for a coaling station on the shores of 

Pago Pago Harbor, the U.S.S. Narragansett visited Tutuila in 1872 

and Comdr. Richard Meade entered into an agreement entitled "Commercial Regulations, etc." with High Chief Mauga, the high chief of Pago Pago. While this treaty was never ratified by the U.S.

+ 

Senate, it served effectively to prevent foreign influence from asserting any strong claim to the harbor. 

In January of 1878, a further treaty of friendship and commerce was negotiated with the leaders of the villages adjacent to Pago Pago and this treaty was ratified later in the same year. It was proclaimed jointly by the United States and "the Government of American Samoan Islands." This treaty remained in force for more than 20 years until it was superseded.

As a result of international rivalry between Great Britain and Germany and because of warfare between various factions of the Samoan population, the United States, Germany, and Great Britain entered into a general act on June 14, 1889, for the purpose of providing for "the security of life, property, and trade of the citizens and subjects of the respective governments who were residing in or having commercial relations with the islands of Samoa." This act also had as its aim the desire "to avoid all occasions of dissensions between their respective governments and the people of Samoa" while at the same time "promoting as far as possible the peaceful and orderly civilization of the people."

Under this tripartite agreement a form of government for the islands was brought into being; however, after a trial of some 10 years, it proved to be ineffective and destructive of the ends for which it was created and was superseded by the convention of 1899. This treaty between the United States, Germany, and Great Britain had the aim of "advancing amicably the questions which have arisen between the three powers in respect to the Samoan group of islands" and provided that it would supersede and annul all previous treaties between the powers relating to Samoa. 

A major provision of the convention was the renunciation by Great Britain and Germany of any claims to the islands of the Samoa group east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich. Reciprocally, the United

States renounced in favor of Germany all rights and claims in respect to the islands of Upolu and Savaii and all other islands west of 171° longitude west of Greenwich. This treaty was proclaimed, after ratification on February 16, 1900, and on February 19 of the same year,

President McKinley directed the Secretary of the Navy to "take such steps as might be necessary to establish the authority of the United States" in the island of Tutuila and other islands of the Samoan group east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich.

The Secretary of the Navy established a naval station at Pago Pago. 

In April 1900, deeds of cession were negotiated with the leading chiefs

+  

and orators of Tutuila and Aunu'u, and on April 17 the U.S. flag was raised for the first time over these islands. On June 14, 1904, High Chief Tuimanu'a, the King of Manu'a, and the chiefs of Manu'a agreed to cede their lands to the United States. 

The islands remained under naval administration with Pago Pago as an active naval base from 1900 to June 30, 1951. During this time considerable progress was made in the establishment of public works and medical and educational facilities. Little, however, was done to disturb the traditional village life of the people. 

Under the terms of the deeds of cession, the United States agreed that the chiefs of the villages would be permitted to retain their individual control over their separate villages, provided that their control was in accordance with the laws of the United States pertaining to Samoa and provided that such control was not obstructive to the peace of the people and the advancement of civilization. The United 

States also agreed to respect and protect the individual rights of the people, especially in respect to their lands and other property. As a result of this commitment, no large tracts of Samoan communally owned land have been alienated during the 61 years during which the United States has had administrative responsibility for these islands. 

Although the deeds of cession were not formally accepted by the Congress until February 20, 1929, the fundamental personal rights of the inhabitants of Samoa were protected by the Constitution and the people of American Samoa were given the status of American nationals. On June 29, 1951, the President of the United States by means of Executive Order 10264 transferred the administration of American Samoa from the Secretary of the Navy to the Secretary of the Interior, effective July 1, 1951.

People 

Ethnic composition.-The inhabitants of American Samoa are 

Polynesians and are closely related to other people of the central and eastern Pacific. They form a part of the family which includes the 

Hawaiians, the Tahitians, the Tongans, and the Maoris of New Zealand. They are a friendly and generous people and their culture is rich in ceremony and mythology. The Samoan language is closely related to other eastern Polynesian tongues. The best evidence available to date indicates the ancestors of the present Samoans arrived in the islands from a homeland in southeastern Asia between A.D. 400 and 600. Although the Samoans have had increasingly close contact

with European institutions, they remain today an outstanding center of Polynesian culture. After more than 125 years of contact with western influences, the fabric of the Samoan social organization remains intact and continues to be the dominant influence in the lives of the Samoan people. Samoa's basic economic and political unit is the extended family composed of related kin tracing their origins bilaterally back through the generations to mythological ancestors. 

Within their extended group, which is administered by a matai or chief, a collective family economy prevails. The matai is responsible for control of family lands and property and represents the family in the political affairs of village, county, and district councils. He is responsible for their welfare and is trustee of family lands and property.

Since the middle of the 19th century, the people of Samoa have been converts to Christianity and have been active in sending missionaries to other islands of the southwest Pacific. They have, however, at the same time retained those customs and traditional ceremonies which have remained meaningful within the context of the contemporary island environment. To a large extent they have also retained the warmth and the grace of their ancestors and have preserved unspoiled much of the charm of the Polynesians. 

Government 

American Samoa is an insular possession of the United States administered by the Department of the Interior by Executive order signed by the President of the United States, effective July 1, 1951.

Executive Branch 

The executive branch consists of the Governor, Secretary, and departmental and office heads. As the chief executive, the Governor is appointed by, and exercises his authority under the direction of, the 

Secretary of the Interior. The Secretary of American Samoa assists the Governor in the administration of the territory and serves as Lieutenant Governor. He is also appointed by the Secretary of the 

Interior and represents the Governor during the latter's absence from the territory.

The executive branch department and office heads are appointed by the Governor and are responsible to him. The following departments and offices comprise the Governor's staff: Agriculture, Audit, Budget

and Finance, Communication, Education, Information and Adult

Education, Legal Affairs and Public Safety (Attorney General), Local Government, Medical Services, Personnel, Port Administration, and Public Works. 

Legislative Branch 

Article II of the Constitution of American Samoa provides for the establishment of the Legislative Branch of the territory. The Legislature of American Samoa consists of the Senate and House of Representatives. Each of the 14 political counties of American Samoa elects a matai (chief) by Samoan custom to the Senate to serve for 4 years. One additional senator, who serves for 2 years, is elected on rotation from four counties in the Western District. With the present Seventh Legislature under the Constitution, the first additional senator was elected in and from Lealataua (Fofo) County during the 1960 general election; his successor shall be elected in and from the 

Leasina (Aitulagi) County; his successor shall be elected in and from the Tualauta County; his successor shall be elected in and from the 

Tualatai County, and thereafter in rotation from such four counties in the order named.

The 17 members of the House of Representatives are elected by popular vote at the polls from the 16 representative districts. There is one delegate to the House of Representatives from Swains Island which the adult permanent residents elect at an open meeting. This delegate has all the privileges of a member of the House except the right to vote.

The 17 Representatives and the delegate from Swains Island each hold office for 2 years. In the present Seventh Legislature, all the members of both the Senate and the House of Representatives, with the exception of the delegate from Swains Island, are matais. Business of the two houses is carried out in the Samoan language and later translated into English. 

Constitution.-The first Constitution of American Samoa was ratified and approved on April 27, 1960, by the Secretary of the Interior.

It was signed by 68 duly appointed delegates to the Constitutional Convention and the Governor, the Chairman of the Convention. The 

Constitution became effective on October 17, 1960, as the basic law of the territory. Under the Constitution, the Legislature assumed increased authority in enacting laws for American Samoa. The promulgation of this law marks the beginning of a new era in the political development of the territory.

Judicial Branch 

The Constitution of American Samoa, which became effective on 

October 17, 1960, provides: "The Judicial Branch of the Government of American Samoa shall be independent of the Executive and Legislative Branches."

The judicial power is vested in the High Court and five district courts. All courts are courts of record. The High Court has a Trial 

Division, a Probate Division, and an Appellate Division. The court system is under the general administration of the Chief Justice of American Samoa who is appointed by the Secretary of the Interior. 

During the fiscal year ending June 30, 1961, a total of 357 persons were tried on criminal charges. Of these, 311 were convicted and 46 acquitted. Of those convicted, 21 were imprisoned, 270 paid fines, 15 performed light labor for the public benefit under the direction of the pulenu'us (village mayors) of their respective villages, and 5 had their fines suspended.

During the year the courts disposed of 209 civil cases. 

Criminal cases are disposed of with as much promptness as the circumstances permit, proper consideration having first been given to the defendant's rights guaranteed to him by the Constitution of the territory.

All felony cases are prosecuted by the Attorney General. Under a court rule if a defendant pleads guilty to a charge of felony, the

✦ 

Government is nevertheless required to prove a prima facie case to avoid the possibility, remote though it may be, that the guilty plea may have been made by mistake. A defendant is always furnished in advance with a copy of the charge in his own language. By the 

Constitution his failure to take the witness stand in his own defense cannot be commented upon or taken against him. 

In the case of a misdemeanor, the court must be fully satisfied that the defendant understands the charge, and if he pleads guilty, that the plea is made voluntarily. Should it appear to the court after a plea of guilty that there may be a reasonable doubt as to the defendant's guilt, he may withdraw his plea of guilty and substitute a notguilty plea to avoid any possible miscarriage of justice. 

The Government provides a public defender and an assistant public defender who are available for the assistance of those charged with crime.

The High Court has construed the due process clause in the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States to be applicable in American Samoa. The fundamental human rights of the inhabitants of American Samoa are protected by the Constitution of the United States as well as by the Constitution of American Samoa.

The Samoan Affairs Office continues to have as its primary function. the administration of the local government organization and acts as a

link between the local Samoan officials and the various departments of the territorial administration. The major emphasis of the work of this office has been the development, upon a base consisting of the traditional policy, of a self-sustaining and self-reliant system of local government, competent to deal with village, county, and district interests and concerns of both traditional and modern nature. The office also acts as an instrument for the coordination and dissemination of information from departments of the territorial government to the local political organization, and to assist in effecting departmental programs and providing useful assistance to the villages in solving their problems such as agriculture, water supply, sanitation, public health facilities, schools, and roads. The office is responsible for census taking in the territory and for conducting general and special elections. Presently it is staffed by an acting director, administrative assistant, and two typists.

A Samoan talking chief, who is well versed in traditional matters, acts as liaison officer in the handling of district affairs. The appointment of a Secretary of Samoan Affairs is provided for in section 4 of Article IV of the Constitution. He will be chosen by the Governor from among the leading chiefs and shall hold office during the pleas ure of the Governor. The Secretary of Samoan Affairs shall be the head of the Department of Local Government and be responsible for the proper administration of the district, county, and village affairs as provided by law and shall supervise all ceremonial functions as may be provided by law.

The administrative head of each of the three political districts within the territory is presently a Samoan district governor. He is selected by the district council of chiefs and appointed by the Governor for a 4-year term. The district governor has a district administrative assistant and a policeman on his staff. Within each district there are a number of counties and each of these 14 counties has a county chief who is also selected by his county council and appointed by the Governor for a 4-year term. Within each village of the territory, a village Government representative is selected by the council and appointed by the district governor. His term of office may vary in length, being subject to the continuing approval of his village council, his county chief, and the district governor. All local officials are responsible to their respective district, county, and village councils. 

Under the provisions of the Code of American Samoa these councils are empowered to recommend laws which they consider to be desirable or necessary and to enact regulations of a strictly local nature in

✦ respect to the cleanliness of their villages, the making and clearing of roads, the planting of their lands and other local matters. 

A local government council composed of 3 district governors and 14 county chiefs was created during the fiscal year 1957. This council is gradually assuming the role of a policymaking body with the local government organization. It appoints its own officers who form an executive committee. This body maintains constant liaison with the 

Governor and the Secretary of the Territory and acts as the coordinating body for all matters pertaining to local government. The council had its origin in the regular monthly meetings conducted by the Governor with the district governors and county chiefs. As it became apparent that these officials were able to assume a more important role in determining the direction of local government, it became desirable to establish a more formal organization. The council meets in the administration building and its officers are provided office space in order that they may be readily available for consultation with the Governor and other territorial officials. 

Structure of Island Administration 

For purposes of administration the territory is divided into 3 districts, 14 counties, and 52 villages. The islands of Tutuila and 

Aunu'u are divided into two districts, known as the Eastern District. and the Western District. The three islands forming the Manu'a group constitute the District of Manu'a. Swains Island, privately owned, is not included in any of these districts; for purposes of administration of governmental services, it is directly under the Governor of American Samoa. By an executive order in 1954 a village council, with mayor and policeman, was set up and a Government. agent appointed. Appendix VI shows the division of the districts into counties and villages.

Status of Samoans.-Persons residing in American Samoa on April 

17, 1900, and persons born in American Samoa after that date are nationals of the United States of America and owe allegiance to, and are entitled to the protection of, the United States. Nationals have free access to the United States and become citizens after having met the necessary requirements of the Immigration and Nationality Act. 

Qualifications of voters.—All residents of 5 years' standing, over age of 20 years, not having been convicted of a felonious crime for which no pardon has been issued and not owing allegiance to any foreign power, who have resided for 1 year within the county in which the

they intend to vote, are eligible to vote in elections in American Samoa. 

All persons who have these qualifications, and who are at least 25 years of age and are either American nationals or American citizens, are eligible to run for and to hold office in the House of Representatives. 

At the village level the heads of families each year select one village chief to be the village Government representative and the county and district councils make recommendations to the Governor with regard to the appointment of county chiefs and district governors whenever vacancies occur in these offices or whenever elections are held. 

Section 7 of Article II of the Constitution (Appendix IV) provides that "Every person of the age of 20 years or upwards, belonging to either of the following classes, who shall have lived in American Samoa for a total of at least 2 years and have been a bona fide resident of the county or division where he offers to vote for at least 1 year next preceding the election and who meets such registration requirements as may be prescribed by law shall be deemed a qualified elector at such election: (1) U.S. nationals; (2) other persons of at least one-half Samoan blood who owe no allegiance to any foreign sovereign state. No person under guardianship, non compos mentis, or insane shall be qualified to vote at any election; nor shall any person who has been convicted of a felony be qualified to vote at any election unless he has had his civil rights previously restored to him or unless he has maintained good behavior for 7 years following the date of his conviction or his release from prison, whichever is the later."

Social Conditions 

The population of American Samoa is predominantly Samoan. 

There is a minimum of conflict of cultures, and no problems are created from diversity of races. It is a well-established policy of this 

Government to foster understanding and respect for Samoan customs, culture, and traditions. The Government endeavors to conduct its affairs in upholding the laws and policies of the territory without interfering with the Samoan way of life. 

Human Rights 

The Constitution of American Samoa contains a bill of rights. 

The fundamental personal rights of the inhabitants of American Samoa are also protected by the Constitution of the United States. 

There has been no need for specific antidiscrimination legislation in 

American Samoa.

Status of Women 

The women of Samoa today enjoy privileges equal to those of the men. In some cases they have held matai titles, and have represented their villages as members of the House of Representatives. 

Labor and Employment Conditions 

The personnel office is responsible for recruiting Government employees and for processing documents pertaining to their employment. It is the policy of this Government to hire employees, other than Samoans, only for those positions requiring skills and administrative abilities not available in the territory and to train local personnel to fill these positions as rapidly as possible. 

At the end of fiscal year 1961 there were 1,419 permanent employees on the rolls of the Government. In addition there are 145 persons employed in the local government of villages and counties. There are also 49 Federal and contract employees from the United States and other sources outside of American Samoa. Approximately 21 percent of the total personnel is employed in the Public Works Department, 23 percent in the Department of Education and 17 percent in the Department of Medical Services. A 40-hour week is standard and straight time is paid for overtime.

As a result of the policy of employing Samoans or part-Samoans in key jobs when their abilities merit, the following positions are now held by experienced local personnel: Director of Port Administration, Manager of Experimental Farm, Supervisor of Agricultural Ex tension Agents, Assistant Director of Budget and Finance, Assistant to Public Defender, Accounting and Disbursing Officer, Assistant Di rector of Education, Chief of Adult Education and Public Informa tion, Medical Supply Officer, Supply Officer, Customs Officer, two

Occupational Organization 

There are no employers' or employees' organizations. Labor or employment disputes are settled by discussion and arbitration.

The Government of American Samoa has appointed employment policy officers and assistants and is presently issuing local regulations in conformity with the Non-Discriminatory Employment Policy Program established by Executive Order 10590 and the Equal Employment Opportunity Program directed by Executive Order 10925.

Although there is no evidence of discrimination practices in this Government the Classification and Pay Plan is in the process of revision in order that there may be no doubt as to the policy of equal opportunity for all qualified persons. A new Merit System Law and Single 

Pay Plan are presently under advisement and will be effected in the near future. The present Merit System Law covers the employment of career Government employees, but does not include contract or local Government appointive employees. This law is based on the Federal plan and is revised as conditions warrant. Candidates for employment are interviewed, examined when practicable, classified, and placed on employment registers from which positions are filled. Positions are subject to desk or field audits and reclassification as changes occur. New positions established totaled 369. Eleven stateside employees were recruited to replace personnel who had completed their employment agreements or for additional positions. During the fiscal year 430 Samoans were promoted to a higher grade. Five Samoans were removed for disciplinary reasons. Under the present Merit System Law, employees have the right to appeal to the Personnel Advisory Board for any personnel action they feel is unjust. This Board conducts hearings and submits a report of its findings and recommendations to the Governor for decision.

Unemployment is not a substantial problem at the present time because persons who are not otherwise gainfully employed are needed on plantations. The labor shortage on plantations is partly caused by the continual emigration of a sizable number of young adults to the United States for the purpose of employment, education, or travel and partly by the fact that increasing numbers of persons so employed prefer to work for wages.

There is no seasonal unemployment. The local fish cannery employs additional help when heavy cannery schedules warrant. Employees average more than 31 hours work per week.

Training Off-island training programs are maintained on a continuing basis as part of the Government's policy for further education of the Samoans in order that they may assume full responsibility for the operation of their Government.

A local high school teacher was sponsored for summer post-graduate work at the University of Southern California during the fiscal year and four students attend the Avele Agricultural College (high school) in Apia, Western Samoa. Each year candidates are also selected to attend the Central Medical School, Suva, Fiji. At the present time there are seven students enrolled in various courses of general medicine and allied science. 

In cooperation with the Department of Education, a program is followed whereby high school commercial students receive on-the-job office practice training in the various offices of the Government 2 hours daily, 5 days a week. Students are marked by their supervisors. 

Should their work be unsatisfactory they are transferred to other offices until their work becomes effective and their experience worth while.

Students are selected annually by the Department of Education for scholarships in colleges in the United States under the auspices of the Frederic Duclos Barstow Foundation and other stateside educational institutions.

All graduates of the High School of American Samoa and Vocational High School are interviewed and examined by the personnel office prior to graduation, with the view to proper job placement or to arrange for further education and training in an appropriate field of study.

The Department of Public Works Surveyor conducts evening classes in the high school 2 nights weekly for students and adults interested in mathematics, surveying, and related subjects. Field classes are held on Saturdays.

There are 71 students in the School for Nurses in the Samoan Hospital with plans for a program expansion to include 100 students. 

The course is presently a 3-year one and graduates are licensed practical nurses recognized in the United States. It is anticipated that this training will be improved professionally within the next 5 years

The Chief Medical Technologist holds regularly scheduled classes for 13 students in the Samoan Hospital in clinical laboratory work and related sciences.

There are presently 17 adults on board the Atu E, a fishing vessel provided by contract from the local fish cannery to teach Samoans the art of long-line fishing. They are paid on a share basis while at sea and $1 per day and room and board when working in port. 

Heavy equipment operators and mechanics are all locally trained both on the airport project and in the Public Works Department. An additional heavy equipment mechanic is scheduled for employment in order to intensify this training and supervision. 

Agricultural extension agents are trained by the Director of Agriculture and his assistants in improved farming methods which they in turn demonstrate on the plantations throughout the islands. 

Town and Rural Planning and Housing 

There is no substantial housing problem in the villages of Samoa, except in the crowded bay area. When a matai determines the necessity for a new fale (house), it is built by Samoan custom and handled within the family group. Samoan fales are of graceful architecture and require much experience in their construction. 

In October 1956 the Director of Local Government was assigned as development officer to work in conjunction with the Planning Commission in preparing, developing, and maintaining a comprehensive and coordinated plan for territorial development. This commission acts as a coordinating agency for the clearing of new building permits and for decisions in respect to the leasing of Government land and facilities, and serves as a clearinghouse for interdepartmental programs. It makes recommendations as to drainage, sewage, water supply, housing, zoning regulations, public and private buildings, commerce and industry, electricity supply, wharves and docks, harbor and airport facilities, roads, use of Government property, and all other proper elements of territorial planning. 

The Commission is composed of the Secretary of American Samoa as chairman, Attorney General, Director of Port Administration,

Director of Budget and Finance, Director of Education, Director of Public Works, Director of Agriculture, Public Health Officer, Sur veyor, and two members at large from the public.

Social Security and Welfare 

Social Security coverage, as approved by Public Law 87-778, extended Social Security coverage to American Samoa for the first time. effective January 1, 1961. Under the Old Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance Act, American Samoans will be eligible for benefits by July 1962.

At the present time, aged and infirm Samoans are cared for by their families. However, due to social and economic changes, a system to provide more security for Government retirees appears desirable. A limited Retirement Plan is under study in the Social Security Administration for employees of the Government of American Samoa who are unable to continue work until they reach the retirement age under the Social Security Act. Employees who have given many years of service would be provided for under this plan until they are eligible for Social Security payments. It is doubtful whether this Government or the employees, in the present stage of economic development, can afford to pay for greater retirement benefits than offered by the 

Social Security Act. 

A Workmen's Compensation Law has not been enacted to date; however, preliminary measures have been taken in this direction. Sufficient funds have been budgeted for fiscal year 1962 to cover Government employees and a United States insurance company has tendered a policy similar to the Federal Employees Compensation Act of 1916, as amended. A law is presently being prepared for presentation to the Legislature during its next session for consideration of this matter.

Free medical, dental, and hospital care is furnished the Samoans as a Government service. The only cost for hospital care is 25 cents per day for subsistence. When a Government employee is injured while on duty, he is assured of free medical treatment since hospital subsistence charges are waived by administrative action. If he is obliged to take sick leave over and above that to which he is normally entitled. consideration is given to waiving the sick leave. If death should occur in the line of duty, funeral expenses are paid (an important item of custom and prestige in American Samoa) and at times a nominal monetary gift is presented to the family to tide it over. At times it has been possible to compensate for the loss of one wage earner by the employment of another member of the family by the Government. These types of actions, in the Samoan mind, usually measure the appreciation of the Government for the work and record of the employee, and have served as a form of employment compensation.

Group health insurance has been investigated but has not been instituted as yet.

There is a welfare society comprised of local women who work closely with a unit of the Red Cross in Samoa. This group collects voluntary contributions through donations and benefits and performs social services in the hospital and leprosarium and grants loans for emergency need.

Juvenile delinquency is not considered a serious problem in Samoa. 

A qualified social worker is employed in the schools in a child guidance program and in counseling in homes when it is considered advisable. Prostitution is of little consequence; it is unorganized and sporadic in nature. However, it is recognized that the increasing use of the monetary system as a medium of exchange, gradual industrialization, economic development and tourism will rapidly bring these social problems into focus.

Public Health and Sanitation 

The Department of Medical Services consists of the Hospital Division, a School of Nursing, Dental Division, and the Public Health and Sanitation Division. Services are provided for the territorial Samoan population, stateside personnel, missionaries and teachers, tourists, and a Japanese fishing fleet of approximately 1,200 men. The Director of the Hospital also acts as Public Health Officer, allowing for the coordination of the public health programs with the medical care program.

An unknown number of the population (estimated at 10 to 20 percent) are of Western Samoan nationality. This group is, however, constantly changing, and it is believed that a portion of the constantly decreasing American Samoan population by emigration to the United States is replaced by migration of Western Samoans to this island. 

This imposes a varying load on medical programs as well as invalidating reliable statistical analysis of morbidity and mortality. 

The main hospital of 154 beds and 20 bassinets is located in the bay area. Auxiliary dispensaries are located at either end of the island and staffed by Public Health nurses. Approximately 75 percent of the population resides in these readily accessible areas. 

The remaining 25 percent of the population can be cared for only by highly diversified programs with disproportionate costs in time, money, and effort due to difficulties of transportation. This includes the 10 villages of Tutuila not served by roads, the island of Aunu'u and the more distant islands of Manu'a and Swains.

Medical Care Program.-Increased attention was directed to the development of public health programs during fiscal year 1961. The greatest single need is for increased health education measures and it is felt that greater progress of more lasting nature can be achieved at the personal and village level through involvement of more Samoan medical and dental personnel in public health activities. 

To facilitate this program emphasis was given at the beginning of the second half of the fiscal year to the need for enlarging areas of responsibility for Samoan personnel qualified and willing to progress, especially the native Samoan doctors, dentists, nurses, and laboratory personnel. While such a transfer of responsibility will not result in rapid changes, it is believed that this is the primary means of dispelling the apathy afflicting the majority of technical workers. It is essential to the success of such a policy that there be adequate contract personnel in these divisions who act in a supervisory and teaching capacity, rather than directive, and that provisions be made for furnishing key Samoan personnel with wider experience in the technical fields by means of a planned program of off-island training and fellowships and an on-the-job training program. 

It is also recognized that there has been little incentive from the standpoint of professional recognition or financial return for technical advancement or improvement. For these reasons, provisions have been made in the budget for fiscal 1963 to provide new positions for this purpose, with corresponding increases in grade.

During the months of November and December 1960, simultaneously with Western Samoa, an outbreak of poliomyelitis occurred leaving a residual of 11 cases with varying degrees of paralysis. Fol-

lowing closely, in January and February 1961, an epidemic of influenza-like disease occurred. Epidemiologic assistance was requested from the United States Public Health Service and a team of epidemiologists visited Samoa from the Communicable Disease Center of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. Attempts at isolation of a viral agent were unsuccessful, however studies of sera were obtained. In addition, a cross section of the population is being studied by means of sera collected. Studies completed to date confirm the poliomyelitis as being due to polio virus, type I. No identification of the causative agent of the subsequent epidemic has been possible. Further study of the sera obtained will be of value in the planning of future programs. Of particular importance now is the need for immunization of the population against poliomyelitis.

Obstetrical Service.-During the calendar year 1960 there were 858 live births of which 54 percent (464) were delivered in the hospital. 

The number of hospital deliveries has increased steadily over the past several years and can be expected to increase even more as transportation systems and education improve. 

Review of the statistical reporting for previous years showed that the Uniform International Classification has not been followed in criteria set for stillbirths and neonatal deaths. This prevents reliable comparison of statistics, especially in respect to neonatal mortality rates since many neonatal deaths, both in and out of the hospital, have previously been recorded as stillbirths (including full-term infants as well as prematures which was mentioned in the Annual Report for 1960). Even with the more definitive recording in calendar year 1960, the neonatal death rate showed a decrease of 24.5 percent (from 18.5 deaths per 1,000 live births to 13.9 per 1,000).

More important is the attention focused on the causes of neonatal deaths. Four neonatal deaths (33.3 percent) are classified as due to birth injury or complications of labor or delivery. Of seven stillbirths (late foetal deaths) recorded for the calendar year, two were recorded as having died during labor. Thus seven (36 percent) of the combined neonatal deaths and late foetal deaths are considered as preventable deaths. It is felt that there is a need for a qualified obstetrician-gynecologist to head this service for teaching purposes and the development of an effective maternal health program throughout the islands.

Medical Service. The Chief of Medicine, in addition to this service, serves as advisor to the Resident Chief of Leprosy. Since January 1961, all examinations for overseas travel and immigration were conducted by this service. Assisting the Chief of the Service is a 

Samoan medical practitioner as Resident Chief of Medicine. 

During the fiscal year there were 1,005 admissions to the medical service.

Pulmonary infection, including 84 cases of pneumonia, accounted for 12.2 percent of the total admissions.

Eighty-nine admissions (8.9 percent) were because of intestinal parasites, notably amebiasis and hookworm, further emphasizing the clinical laboratory findings indicative of an overwhelming incidence of parasitism. Clinical findings in the Manu'a survey showed the incidence of clinical anemia among adults to be as high as 10 percent. 

This is in all likelihood due to combined parasitism and deficiency disease. Significant numbers of children have been admitted from this area for hookworm disease and anemia.

Of the first 100 stool examinations done on the 300 teachers receiving annual physical examinations by this department, 93 percent were found to harbor 1 or more intestinal parasites. Twenty-three percent were found to have hookworm. Eight percent harbored E.

Histolytica. 

These findings indicate the great need for mass treatment of the population combined with an effective health education program in hygiene and environmental sanitation, and effective measures to furnish sanitary latrine facilities and their use throughout the islands. 

Seventy-six admissions (7.6 percent) were for diseases of the cardiovascular system. During the calendar year 1960, diseases of the heart were the leading cause of death (16), and diseases of the vascular system accounted for five additional deaths. 

A statistical survey of clinical filariasis was begun during the last half of the fiscal year. At least a year will be required for the collection and evaluation of data. When these data are combined with those of the sanitation division on mosquito control and microfilarial indices the effectiveness of the latter program can more adequately be evaluated.

Prevalence of Dental Disease.—No accurate statistics are available at present. Generally the Samoan people have good teeth; however, dental disease is becoming a bigger problem each year. Most young adults have a full complement of teeth evenly erupted, straight in alignment with no crowding. Malocclusion is rare. Dental caries,

although rare among adults, is increasing among children and young adults. There appears to be a definite relationship of change from the Samoan diet to a diet including store bought foods with an increasing rate of decay. The Samoan diet is largely carbohydrate (taro, banana, breadfruit), fruit, and fish. Some sugar cane is chewed as a treat. This diet is being supplemented more and more each year with commercial foods which include canned meats, bread, rice, and sweet food and beverages. The Samoan family with the least amount of money or living the farthest from a grocery store will often have the best teeth.

Periodontal disease continues to be the biggest dental problem for middle age and older people. This disease is responsible for the loss 그 ​of more teeth than all other causes. The primary cause of this disease in American Samoa is dental calculus which is found on teeth of children and adults. Few Samoans get their teeth cleaned so that dental calculus is a constant source of irritation to the gingiva. This, combined with poor oral hygiene, makes periodontal disease almost universal among the Samoan people. 

1 Problems Facing Dental Services.-1. There is a lack of trained personnel in the service. 

2. Insufficient dental health education of the people is apparent by their lack of concern for their teeth. Poor oral hygiene is common and few Samoans seek regular examination or prophylaxis. 

3. Cost of dental treatment for the Government or the people will be a future problem unless adequate preventive programs are begun. now to keep dental caries in check and to reduce the number of teeth lost due to periodontal disease. Preventing dental disease may cost considerable money but it is cheaper and more desirable in the long run than treating an increasing amount of dental disease.

Measures To Prevent Future Dental Problems.-The most satisfac tory solution to the dental disease problem is to take measures to prevent dental disease from occurring. Prevention of dental caries can be accomplished by fluoridating communal water systems and Dental Services is studying the desirability and feasibility of fluoridating the water system in the Pago Pago harbor area. Another way of preventing tooth decay is by topical fluoride treatment of the teeth once a year. This treatment must be given by a dentist or dental hygienist. 

Dental Services is planning to train Samoan nurses to be dental hygienists. A few hygienists would provide sufficient personnel to make topical fluoride treatments a successful preventive measure.

Prevention of periodontal disease can be accomplished by regula prophylaxis of the teeth. A program of regular prophylaxis of the teeth for all Samoans will require additional dentists or dental hy gienists. Again, plans to train dental hygienists would accomplish this. Needed in a successful program to prevent periodontal disease is patient cooperation. Samoans must be taught the importance of good dental hygiene. They must also learn the importance of regular dental examination and prophylaxis in preventing dental disease. 

Dental Services is expanding the dental health education program and is presently giving lectures in villages and schools. Educational movies are planned. The Department of Education has agreed to teach dental health in the schools featuring supervised toothbrushing each day. Dental Services will prepare short courses in dental hygiene and dental health for the Samoan teachers.

General Sanitation.-Improvement, though of moderate degree, has occurred during the fiscal year, particularly in the outlying village areas. Filariasis Control Program-Mosquito Control.--Substantial prog ress is being achieved in the control of the mosquito vector of filariasis. In 2 years of extensive control, by sanitation and the elimination of breeding sites, the density of Aedes polynesiensis mosquitoes has been reduced by 53.5 percent in the Western District of 

Tutuila. The second round of mosquito surveys on Tutuila should be completed for all of Tutuila by the end of calendar year 1961. 

The reduction in mosquito density will reduce the potential of infection for filariasis. By this time measurable data to correlate will have been obtained by the Medical Department as to incidence of acute filarial disease.

Initial mosquito surveys were completed in the Manu'a District in 1960-61, and sanitarians began routine village control inspections in April 1961. With the exception of Swains Island, the filariasis control program is now operating in all of American Samoa. 

Mosquito Control Progress Report, November 1958 to May 1961 

in the Western District on Tutuila.—This district is composed of 21 villages (13 shore and 8 inland) on the south shore in the area west of Tafuna and extending to the western end of the main road. These villages have a combined population of 5,489 persons (about one-third the total population of Tutuila) according to the census of April 1960. 

Having determined that Aedes polynesiansis is the principal, if not the only, vector of filaria in Samoa, efforts have been directed at the 632842-62-7

control of this species by the elimination of breeding sites. Instruction of the village people on mosquito breeding habits and their subsequent cooperation in the removal of breeding sites such as empty coconut shells, discarded tin cans, automobile tires, etc., within a radius of 120 yards of their houses has been the only means of control aside from frequent inspection.

Prior to the sanitary inspection program, a mosquito survey was made of the district in the period November 1958-May 1959. Mosquito collections were taken on humans. A 10-minute sampling period was used at each station. All stations were sampled between the hours of 6 and 9 a.m., the period of optimum morning activity of Aedes polynesiansis. The mosquito density (average rate of capture per minute) was calculated to be 0.325 for the 439 stations surveyed in early 1959. A resurvey made in late 1959 showed a density of 0.1175, a reduction of 46.2 percent. As the sanitation program had only been in operation for a few months, it is quite possible that seasonal variation had an effect on this initial reduction. However, a third survey undertaken November 1960 to May 1961, under similar weather conditions to those of early 1959, revealed a new density of 0.151. This is a 53.5 percent reduction from the results of the original survey.

Reduction in mosquito densities varied from village to village as did public cooperation with the program. The extreme variations were Amanave with the greatest change in density, an 88-percent reduction and Pava'ia'i with the least change, 15.4 percent. It should be noted also that villages with lower reductions, such as Pava'ia'i had low densities in the first survey. In early 1959, Pava'ia’i had a density of 0.114, already 84 percent lower than the 0.722 density found at Amanave. The fact remains, however, that Amanave was able to reduce its mosquitoes to a density of only 0.087, a figure 11 percent lower than the 0.98 density now found at Pava’ia’i.

Crime Prevention, Legal Affairs, and Public Safety 

The Attorney General is the chief legal officer of the Government and is the Director of the Department of Legal Affairs and Public Safety. He serves as legal counsel for the Executive Branch of the 

Government, represents the Government as prosecutor in all criminal cases involving felonies and in most cases involving misdemeanors, and acts as the Government's attorney in all actions in law and equity to which it is a party. 

The office of the Attorney General drafts proclamations, executive orders, general memorandums, and all other legal documents or forms required by the Government. Pursuant to the Constitution of American Samoa, legislative authority is now vested in the Legislature of American Samoa, and that body now is responsible for the passage of new laws and amendment of existing law, thus performing a function

which heretofore has been vested in the Attorney General insofar as drafting of laws and amendments was concerned. The office of the 

Attorney General, however, still prepares the Governor's legislative program for submission to the Legislature. A second function, formerly vested in the Attorney General, which was affected by the Constitution, is that of legal advisor to the Legislature. The Constitution provides for the appointment of a Legislative Counsel. While the position has not been filled, an attorney on loan from the Department of the Interior acted in that capacity during the first session of the Seventh Legislature.

Legal Aid and Counseling. The office of the Attorney General also furnishes free legal aid to the general public, and in that capacity renders free legal advice, drafting service, and conciliating service to the people of American Samoa. Most legal aid matters are handled by a locally trained Samoan who holds the position of Legal Assistant to the Attorney General and Chief Investigator. Those matters which require the attention of a formally trained legal officer are referred to the Attorney General. Legal aid of this nature is necessitated by the absence of any attorneys in private practice in American Samoa. 

Immigration and Consular Functions.-The Governor of American 

Samoa has consular powers and the implementation of such powers is a function of the office of the Attorney General. Under the supervision of the Attorney General, the Immigration Branch, headed by a locally trained Samoan with many years experience, is responsible for the investigation of applicants for documentation and preparation of papers in connection with such consular powers.

Comparison with the previous year's figures indicates neither an appreciable increase nor decrease in the work of the branch.

Educational Conditions 

Objectives and Policies

The basic approach to education in American Samoa is to provide training which will enable the people to serve more effectively within their social, economic, professional, and political structure. At the same time, education must provide suitable background for those who will find it possible to take advantage of opportunities for higher education in the United States or other countries. Not only must the mastery of the English language be taught, but also mastery of basic skills related to science, medicine, law, business, and the arts, and other varied fields. Therefore, it becomes the responsibility of the 

Government as the Samoans are influenced more and more by contact with Western civilization, to conserve the best of Samoan culture, and at the same time to guide them in developing social changes which are to their advantage, and to bring them to a better understanding of what is best for their well-being, health, and general improvement. 

It also becomes necessary to help them preserve and develop their economic resources, to enable them to cope with the problems of an increasing population and increasing demands which accompany modern society, and to become a government as self-supporting as possible.

As English is the international language and the official language of the Government, it is essential that the children become literate in that language while maintaining the bilingual nature of their society. 

In line with these objectives, it is the policy of the Department of Education to maintain a program adapted thereto in the following fields: (a) Curriculum.—(1) Instruction in both the Samoan and English languages in the elementary schools; (2) the fundamental processes of reading, writing, and arithmetic; (3) science and health; (4) social studies with emphasis on Samoan culture, geography, government, and environment; (5) music and art; (6) vocational preparation; and (7) avocational pursuits.

(b) School organization.-Village elementary schools (grades 1 to 

6); junior high schools (grades 7 to 9); the senior high school (grades 

10 to 12); the vocational high school (grades 10 to 12), the two latter schools for a select group of junior high school graduates; and the teacher training college (grades 13 and 14) for high school graduates. (c) Compulsory education. This is a long-established and popular policy of compulsory education between the ages of 7 through 15. (d) Buildings and equipment.—An expanding school population makes it essential to keep up in this respect. The general policy is a cooperating enterprise between the local communities and the Government, but as a school becomes more advanced in character, the Government is responsible for additional support. In the elementary school, the villages provide the school buildings and teachers' living quarters; the Government provides teachers, equipment, and supplies. 

In the junior high schools, high school, vocational high school, dependents' school, demonstration school, and teacher training college, the Government has sole responsibility. (e) Scholarships.-The Government desires to provide for and promote scholarships and special training for qualified Samoans in needed fields. (f) Standards.—It is the policy to upgrade the entire school system as rapidly as possible, with emphasis being placed on the elementary level with the improvement of the teaching staff, and on the junior high school level where the schools will send a better-trained group of young people into the high school and community life. Special attention is being given to the teacher training college and related demonstration school. This is essential in order to provide facilities whereby teachers can be better trained to do their job in upgrading the school system.

Educational Administration The Duties and Responsibilities of the Director of Education.— 

The function of the Office of the Director is that of general administration of the entire school system in direct regard to objectives and long-range plans set forth by the Governor of American Samoa. It is the function of this office to supervise and coordinate activities of the school system in relation to curricula, organization, building facilities, training programs, scholarships, budget submissions, accreditation, certification, etc. The administrative responsibilities include supervision over the Division of Elementary Education and the Divi sion of Secondary Education.

It is responsible for coordinating the

✦ activities of private schools in order to insure inculcation of basic educational objectives and policies. It is responsible for educational public relations on the village and district level. 

The Division of Elementary Education is responsible for the administration of grades 1 through 9, and includes village schools, junior high schools, the dependents' school, and the demonstration school. It is also responsible for the annual Teachers' Institute and the supervisory staff. The division is also responsible for the selection of elementary textbooks, the management of elementary education budgets, and the establishment of curriculums set forth and ap proved by the Director of Education. 

The Division of Secondary Education is responsible for the admin istration of grades 10 through 14, and includes the high school, vocational high school, and teacher training college, and, indirectly, the nursing school. It is responsible for the selection of secondary textbooks, the management of secondary education budgets, the supervision of entrance examinations, the establishment of rules and regulations covering training, and the application of curriculums set forth and approved by the Director of Education. It is also responsible for an adult educational testing program. 

Local authorities have a voice in matters related to education in the local community through an advisory Board of Education, consisting of seven members, including the Director of Education, three members appointed by the Governor, and three members who are elected to represent the three districts of American Samoa. Village chiefs are responsible for providing and maintaining elementary schools and teachers' houses and for providing land for small plantations for the elementary school teachers. Structure of the Education System 

A description of the school levels with regard to curriculum and language of instruction is as follows:

Preprimary Schools. None. It is planned to begin a preparatory class, pre-first grade, for 6-year-old students at the start of the 196263 school year.

Primary Schools. The elementary grades extend from grade 1 through grade 6. These grades are administered and taught by locallyeducated Samoans, with the exception of one teacher with a B.A. degree. The average age at entrance is 7 years. A program of instruction in both the Samoan and English languages is maintained as an aid to instruction where the students do not understand the English. 

The percentage of Samoan-English used during the 1960-61 school year in grades 1-3 was 60 percent English and 40 percent Samoan; in grades 4-6 it was 75 percent English and 25 percent Samoan (with less Samoan in grades 5 and 6).

It is to be noted that Samoan is not taught as a language in the public schools, but used only to help students in their transition from the Samoan into the English language. A majority of the public school students study the Samoan language at pastors' schools before beginning grade 1 in the public schools. They usually continue this 

Samoan language study privately at the pastors' schools for several years after starting public school. The junior high school division. consists of grades 7 through 9. The administration and instruction in the junior high schools are carried out by locally-educated Samoans with the exception of one teaching principal with a B.A. degree. The average age at entrance to the junior high school is 13-14 years. The 

English-Samoan language ratio is 95 percent English and 5 percent Samoan, with less Samoan in grade 9. The present elementary and junior high school curriculum consists of the usual school subjects used for those levels of education such as science and health, social studies, arithmetic, language arts (including English language, 632842-62—6

spelling, reading in English and Samoan, handwriting), music, art, and, in addition, courses in Samoan culture, tradition, history, environment, government, and the values of the Samoan way of life. In grades 4 through 8 one Samoan reader or journal is used each year; this is for Samoan reading or literature and not for language instruction. These readers were added during the 1960-61 school year to meet just criticisms that the children in the middle and upper grades were not able to read Samoan correctly. Instruction in Samoan culture in grade 8 is in both English and Samoan; instruction in Samoan government in grade 9 is in the English language. 

Instruction is based principally on standard elementary textbooks published in the United States; all students have textbooks as a result of an inexpensive book rental program. The program was extended to private schools during the past school year and was used by four of these schools. In the first grade the oral-aural approach to English is used; this is carried out particularly in oral English and reading readiness. Both of these subjects were added to the first grade curriculum for the first time during the 1960-61 school year. Oral English was used daily throughout the school year; reading readiness was used every day the first 6 weeks of the school year. Reading readiness will be expanded to a 14 weeks' program during the 1961-62 school year. In addition to the regular English readers used in the elementary grades, special English language books for students who use English as a second language are also used in the school system. 

Information regarding the United Nations found in the textbooks is used as the basis for instruction on the subject. The Feleti Memorial 

Demonstration School has a special program for United Nations Day annually. Magazine and radio information are also used. 

One elementary school known as the Dependents' School is maintained for English-speaking students, originally established for children of contract employees. The curriculum and standards are comparable to stateside schools and the school is staffed by certified stateside teachers. 

Secondary Schools. The present high school is composed of grades 

10 through 12. During the year the 14th anniversary of the founding of the High School of American Samoa was observed on October 21, 1960. Admission is presently based upon examination; however, it is planned to provide a high school education for all ninth-grade graduates beginning with the 1962-63 school year. One-hundred and thirty-five new students were admitted for the 1960-61 school year. 

The language of instruction is English. The instructors are university

✦ graduates. For the 1961-62 school year all instructors will also have teaching certificates. The subjects taught meet the requirements of 

U.S. certification agencies. The average age of students on entry is 

16 years; at graduation 19 years. The instructional materials used are standard high school textbooks and equipment from the United States. The Preliminary Scholastic Aptitude Test was given to all high school seniors for the first time. Eight high school seniors were tested for the NROTC program. Classes in geography, public health, and English conversation were added to the curriculum during the year. The high school offers courses to both Vocational High School and Nursing School students so that eventually both groups are graduated from the high school.

The Vocational High School is operated to provide special training in crafts and the use of trade tools. Boys, who are selected ninth-grade graduates, are trained to do cabinet work, carpentry, machine work, electricity, boat repair and boat building, logging, sawmilling, plumbing, welding, etc., with the ultimate objective to do work typical of local conditions. Thirty-five new students are admitted yearly. 

Long-range planning calls for the Vocational High School and the high school to be consolidated into one school. 

The Feleti Memorial Teacher Training College began its 2-year course for the first time with the opening of the 1960-61 school year. 

Two additional stateside instructors were added to the staff. Students admitted must be high school graduates. The function of the college is to train teachers in the basic skills of instruction especially as related to teaching in the elementary schools. Graduates from this college are in great demand and are assigned immediately upon graduation to teach in the elementary and junior high schools. A pilot program of oral-aural English was conducted for 10 weeks of the school year with the trainees in the teacher training college. This was under the direction of a linguist from Cornell University, who came to American Samoa for a 15-month study of the tone and accent of the Samoan language and also of the Samoan gesture system. For the first time, trainees were employed at a regular school teacher's salary upon admission to the teacher training college. All trainees sign agreements that they will remain in American Samoa and teach for at least 2 years upon the completion of their training college course. During the past school year one college instructor spent half-days in village schools assisting teachers with their instructional program. 

In a further effort to improve the instructional program in the village schools, five experienced teachers were kept on a salary basis and

were brought in from the field on local scholarships in order to attend the 2-year teacher training course. A model school, the Feleti memorial Demonstration School, is operated in connection with the training college for observation lessons and practice teaching assignments. 

A Teachers' Institute is held yearly to keep the Samoan education personnel abreast of new techniques and methods. Approximately 

5 weeks is devoted to this activity attended by all Samoan teachers and principals. In addition, a large class of candidates is accepted every year to receive basic training so that they may serve as substitutes and fill vacancies during the school year. A number of private school teachers also attend the Institute. Following the pattern established during the 1959 Teachers' Institute, guest instructors were invited to participate in the 1960 Institute. These instructors were from New Zealand, Fiji, Cook Islands, Hawaii, Texas, and Maryland, and were sponsored by the South Pacific Commission, the Barstow 

Foundation, and the Department of Classroom Teachers (NEA).

Summary-Education 

While this section of the annual report evidences progress during the year, it does not reflect the great and vital need for a tremendous change in the educational programs in American Samoa. Present elementary school buildings are in the main dilapidated and inadequate structurally and do not provide sufficient space for the present children, and certainly make no provision for a growing population. 

The present high school facilities permit only about 25 percent of junior high school graduates to continue their education through high school. The vast majority of the elementary school teachers only have the equivalent of a sixth-grade education. In order to provide proper facilities in adequate number to permit each child to go to school, it is necessary that a major construction program be initiated, and in order to give each student attending school the benefit of good teaching it will be necessary to hire a large number of qualified teachers. 

Current plans have been submitted for approval by the Congress which will result in a tremendous change in the school facilities available to Samoan children and in the quality of teaching provided them. 

It is a certainty that next year's report on the educational program in American Samoa will reflect a terrific acceleration in the development of a full and much needed educational program in this area. 

Natural Resources Economic Conditions 

The primary natural resource of American Samoa is the land and the agricultural crops produced from it. The agriculture of the area is basically on a subsistence level, with copra the major export. There

are no known mineral resources. Although there was an ample supply of wood in the forest areas for native-built house construction, there has never been an attempt to process timber commercially for local use. 

Agriculture 

Through the Department of Agriculture the Government of American Samoa has the policy of providing assistance, guidance, and encouragement to its people in improving and increasing plantings of existing subsistence crops and to introduce additional practical supplemental subsistence crops. The objective is to enable the people to become more self-supporting and to help self-government by delegating responsibility through the political authority of the traditional chiefs, and to develop through long- and short-range programs the potential production of an agricultural economy. 

The department is headed by a Director and by Assistant Directors for Field and Administration. The entomologist's position, while presently vacant, is expected to be filled by July 1962, with new positions of plant pathologist and two new agronomists. Research continued during the fiscal year in research for injurious plant pests and in the breeding of parasites and predators in the laboratory for release in badly infested areas for pest control. Twenty-five persons are employed in the field while two staff members manage the office. 

Particular emphasis during the fiscal year was placed on increasing soil fertility, development of small farms with the object of establishing an adequate and well-balanced diet, development and expansion of existing cash crops, development of mixed farming to incorporate both subsistence and cash crops and promote adult education in proper production management, eradication and control of insect. pests and plant diseases the immediate objectives being bunchytop disease of bananas and the rhinoceros beetle, and rat control.

Experimental Farms.-The Government operates three experi mental and demonstration farms consisting of 248 acres at three locations: Tafuna, Taputimu, and Mapusaga. A program for soil improvement of these farms has been carried out under the direction of one of the Assistant Directors and in a period of approximately 2 years the program has established excellent pastures and good agricultural land. The most noticeable improvement is in the original basaltic clay (40 acres) area at Taputimu. At the Taputimu Farm the amount of land developed for pastures during the fiscal year was increased from 10 to 25 acres. This increase will allow a rotation

grazing system to be carried out while worn-out land can be allowed to lie fallow.

The Mapusaga Farm is used primarily for livestock production. 

It is the present center for the beef herd program. The amount of pasture land developed during the year was increased from 15 to 25 acres. Approximately 30 acres of worn-out native pastures require redevelopment and planting with introduced grasses and legumes. 

An additional 30 acres are still uncleared. Elsewhere on the farm are sections set aside for plant introduction, experimentation, demonstration, and distribution. Trial plantings of forest trees were made at Mapusaga beginning in August 1960 and included the following: 

teak, ifilele, tanagave, cedarella, mahogany, and poumuli. Work at the Tafuna Farm was concentrated in the improvement of the cattle herd and in chicken and egg production.

Control of Animal Pests and Plants.-In order to carry out a plant pest and disease program, extension agents are being trained in proper practices. The animal disease and parasite program is actively underway. A concerted effort was made throughout the year to bring the bunchytop virus disease of bananas under control and this was quite successful, although much work yet remains in this field. 

The bunchytop law, enacted in 1959, is being brought into force through extension personnel to ensure continuity of control. Spraying and dusting for banana scab moth is being intensified. 

Spraying operations to control hawk moth and army worm, serious taro pests, are being carried out, and a new fruit piercing moth (possibly imported 2 years ago) is being attacked with insecticides and vegetative controls. These three economic pests breed extensively on erythrina, and, although a good legume, this tree is being removed from some areas and replaced with leguminous ground cover such as pueraria (tropical kudzu). 

Oryctes (rhinoceros beetle) control has continued throughout the year using sawdust heaps for trapping. Some experimental work was carried out using black (ultra-violet) light and BHC treatment in the crowns of coconut trees. No further parasites and/or predators on Oryctes were introduced but work underway by the South Pacific Commission promises new introductions from Madagascar during 1962.

Crops, Livestock, and Animal Products.-The principal crops are copra, taro, banana, yam, breadfruit, and cacao. Local subsistence crops are grown in individually cleared and cultivated gardens.

Sweet potatoes are not readily acceptable as a food in the territory but new improved varieties are now under development on the Government farms which will prove more palatable to Samoan taste. Taro and bananas supply the bulk of the foods eaten although there is an increasing consumption of vegetables, such as beans, tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, eggplant, lettuce, and cabbage. Two newly formed farmers' cooperatives are providing a good means of introducing new crops.

Summary-Economics 

It is apparent that continued growth of American Samoa's economic development must keep pace with accelerated health and educational programs being initiated. Surveys have been made and plans have been developed which, when implemented, should greatly

increase the tourist industry, increase the productivity of the land and bring in additional industries to the territory. Broadening and strengthening the economic base of American Samoa is absolutely vital to the successful development of the potentials of the area. 

Industry 

Power Production.-The installed capacity of the two powerplants located in Utulei and in Anua (SRU) is 2,518 kilowatts. Of this total, only 1,680 kilowatts can be utilized due to obsolescence of the equipment. The Anua powerplant bears the load of operation with the 

Utulei plant assisting. Peak load demand is 1,510 kilowatts. 

In October 1960, a major breakdown of one of the 450 kv.-a. transformers at the local cannery substation occurred and a set of transformers totalling 500 kv.-a. was air freighted from Honolulu to 

American Samoa. The cannery was back in full operation within a week.

Power was curtailed in March 1961, for a period of 10 hours in the Pago Pago, Anua, and Eastern areas so that major repairs could be accomplished on the switchboard at SRU Power Plant. In May to 

June 1961, all seven units of both plants were overhauled at a cost of $12,000 for parts. Repairs and maintenance to the transmission and distribution system were accomplished as far as funds permitted. 

With the completion of the jet runway, the proposed Federal Aviation Agency housing, construction of a new hotel and the South Pacific Conference which will be held in American Samoa in July 1962, the load demand will be such that for the first increment the Government is planning to install together with substation and building two 1,000kw. diesel generating units. Plans, specifications, engineering service, and installation of equipment will be done by an experienced contractor. This will also include a power survey of the island recommending future additions to the generating plant and complete and detailed plans and specifications for the construction of a 12-kv. transmission system to serve as the basis for future force account construction.

Kilowatt hours produced during fiscal year 1961 total 7,220,400, as compared to 6,850,500, an increase of 369,900 kilowatt hours. Metered consumers total 1,011, an increase of 30 consumers over the previous year.

Public Finance 

The financial management of the territory is divided into two main parts: (1) Federal activities-composed of the Office of the Governor, the Judiciary, and the Legislature. (2) Territorial activities-composed of the operation and maintenance of the territory, which consists of eight executive departments, several staff functions, and the construction program. 

Federal activities enumerated above are financed by direct Federal appropriations on funds drawn on the Treasury of the United States. 

All territorial activities, including construction, are financed by the collection of local revenues which are supplemented by a Congressional grant-in-aid. The grants are payable to the Treasurer of 

American Samoa and subsequently commingled with local revenue and made available for disbursement by the Treasury of American 

Samoa. 

The Department of Budget and Finance has been delegated the responsibility for the collection of all revenues, the disbursements of all Federal and territorial funds, the fiscal and financial accounting, the complete budget process, the managing of all business-type activities, including the supply operations, land transportation, print shop, the hotel and related services.

The Department's planning theme for the fiscal year 1961 was based on the premise of a relatively normal workload situation and called for continuation of efforts to achieve the best level of internal administration and service to the public and other Government departments and offices. 

Effective January 1, 1961, the Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability

Insurance Program was extended to American Samoa. The Governor, by authority contained in Treasury Department Order No. 150-55, has delegated to the department the functions of the Internal Revenue Service, pertaining to the act, in the administration, collection, and enforcement of the taxes imposed by chapters 2 and 21 of the Internal 

Revenue Code of 1954. 

During the fiscal year, the department prepared a supplementary budget amounting to $565,000 covering construction and allied cost relative to the South Pacific Conference scheduled to be held in American Samoa in July 1962. Of this total, $100,000 was financed by local revenue which had been carried forward from the previous fiscal year's operations.

Before the territorial Government can become financially selfsufficient, it must first make permanent improvements in public utilities, including power, water, telephone, and roads. Sanitation and public health programs must be accelerated and broadened. These improvements are necessary in order to adequately develop the agricultural economy, improve the standards of living, and at the same time make American Samoa attractive to such industry, and tourism that will be compatible to the island and the people. 

The existing tax pattern is inadequate and must be revised to meet an expected expanding economy. The broadest base of all taxes is the import duty which brings in the most revenue. This tax is levied on all merchandise entering the territory except merchandise which may be classified as perishable or religious. There are certain exemptions but for practical purposes the average ad valorem equals approximately 17 percent. The next largest source of revenue is the 2 percent gross business tax assessed on total sales made in the territory. There is no income tax in the territory; however, U.S. citizens who are employees of the Federal Government or the territorial Government are subject to Federal income tax.

A personal tax is levied on all males of 18 years or over, except for citizens of the United States who enjoy a 5-year exemption, and American Samoans who are attending educational institutions. The present rate of assessment is $10.

Congressional grants amounted to $2,486,800 or a total income available for territorial activities of $3,555,215. Included in the grant-inaid were funds for the jet airport amounting to $750,000 and the South Pacific Construction Program of $465,000. The Government incurred total General Fund obligations for the fiscal period of $2,059,474 and $1,213,957 for construction projects. 

The average staff of the department consisted of 87 Samoan employees, 2 stateside persons, and 1 European.

There are five operating divisions within this department in addition to the Budget Office. The Budget Office has the responsibility to obtain preliminary budget estimates, evaluate them, interrogate requestors, and in concert with the director accept or reject justifications. 

The Revenue Division receives all payments and maintains the roll of taxable persons, the gross receipts returns by the business activities, and the records of all receivables including rents, utilities, etc. 

The Accounting Division maintains the accounts for all Government transactions both Federal and territorial. The payroll section is part of this division and all records for pay purposes are retained there. 

The General Supply Division handles the processing of all requisitions for the Government for general stock items. It also provides supply items to the general public in the instances wherein local merchants do not have the commodity available. The value of general supply inventory at the commencement of the year was $637,674.37 and closed at $725,357.58.

The Medical Supply Division maintains a medical inventory for the hospital and for the general public. It is sole source of pharmaceutical supplies in the territory. It also stocks equipment peculiar to the needs of the hospital. At the outset of the year the medical inventory was determined to be $150,288.05 and $149,243.62 at the close.

The Management Services Division maintains the property accounts of the Government and operates the quasi-commercial activities of the Government. This activity controls the assignment of real and personal Government property, assuring responsibility for its care and proper utilization. The broad scope of property includes buildings, heavy and light automotive equipment, office equipment, as well as furniture and fixtures.

The quasi-commercial activities include the operation of American Samoa's sole hotel, the print shop, and the management of an extended bus service.

Audit Office.-The Audit Office is under the Executive Branch of 

Government. As a staff officer, the Auditor receives supervision and direction from the Governor and/or Secretary. The Audit function is organized to conduct audits of departments and offices and such Government instrumentalities as the Copra Fund and the Bank of American Samoa. It also conducts special investigations of any suspected illegal financial manipulations.

Findings of seven comprehensive audits of departments and offices, and three special investigation reports were submitted to proper authorities for final action.

The staff consists of one contract auditor from the United States, two assistants, and one trainee.

Banking and Credit. The Bank of American Samoa was established by the Government of American Samoa in 1914 to provide a depository for the people of American Samoa and to carry on general banking business.

The affairs of the bank are managed and its corporated powers exercised by a Board of Directors which consists of the Governor, as the chairman, Secretary, Attorney General, Treasurer, Cashier of the bank, and three residents of American Samoa appointed by the 

Governor. 

The fiscal year ending June 30, 1961, was a record year for the bank. 

Deposits, loans, and earnings reached an all-time high and a net profit of $69,000 was earned. Profits were up 23 percent over the $56,000 reported in 1960. A continuing high demand for loans and a general increase in income from bank services made this profit possible. 

Deposits total $2,560,000 at an increase of $260,000 over deposits on June 30, 1960. A continued increase in business activity and a substantial increase in Government spending in American Samoa has made this gain possible.

A total of 1,630 loans totaling $415,000 were granted during the past year. This figure reflects a substantial increase over both the number and dollar amount of loans granted during the previous year.

Several villages have cooperated with the bank during the past year by accelerating their loan payments on village, school, and utility loans. The villages of Leone, Aua, Futiga, Alao, and Faleniu paid. their loans in full. Other villages also made substantial payments on their loans. This resulted in a reduction of over $60,000 in low

return, interest-bearing loans. These funds have been directed toward investments showing a greater return and sounder security. 

At the suggestion of the management, a change in the Banking Code of American Samoa resulted in the appointment of two local merchants to the bank's board of directors. The addition of these gentlemen, with their business judgment and ability, provides an additional asset to the bank program. 

Construction and Maintenance 

The Department of Public Works carried out its activities during fiscal year 1961, primarily in operation, maintenance, and repairs. to all Government of American Samoa facilities. It also rendered assistance in the field of special projects and contractual services to other departments of the Government as well as to private concerns on the island of Tutuila and the Manu'a Group. 

The total operating budget of the department at the close of the fiscal year was $524,570, an increase of $19,995 from fiscal year 1960. 

Of this increase, $12,000 is included to cover the cost of emergency overhauling seven diesel power generators and the balance for the improvement of the Marine Railway and Yard, and additional personnel.

Marine Railway and Yard.—The Marine Railway and Yard Division of the Public Works Department is operated by 1 supervisor with the services of 17 regular employees and 10 contractual services employees. This division is solely responsible for the hauling out of 

Government, private, and commercial vessels, including the Japanese fishing fleets, under contract to the local canning company. for

All classes of repair work were undertaken throughout the year various types of vessels, including those of Port Administration. 

This repair work includes replacement of planks and sundry woodwork; welding of cracked engine parts, ammonia and water pipes; machining of tailshafts, stern bearings, other engine parts; repairing of refrigeration machinery; repairing electrical motors and wiring; cleaning, scraping and painting ships' hulls; and straightening propellers and repairs of small craft engines.

The total number of vessels hauled during fiscal year 1961 was 71 with a gross revenue of $75,412.17. This total was covered by 206 job orders issued and completed during the fiscal year. The operating budget of this division at the close of the fiscal year was $30,577. This expenditure covered only the routine operations and maintenance, equipment, and improvement to the Marine Railway and Yard. A

new machine shop building is being completed, and moving of equip ment from the old machine shop to the new shop will be effected during fiscal year 1962. 

Engineering and Surveying.-Twenty triangulation stations were set, occupied, and observed with regular first order procedures during the fiscal year. This includes first order measurement of the 5,000foot base line at Tafuna. Computation and adjustment is now in progress.

Three extensive preliminary studies were made in the fields of civil engineering and surveying to determine the requirements for the territory with reference to roads, sewage disposal, trash and garbage disposal, land reclamation, housing, equipment, personnel, land administration, and mapping.

An average of 6 hours of classroom work and 3 hours field exercise per week was spent in technical and professional level training in a course attended by 25 students, including the entire surveying section staff and others. Classes and field exercises were held on 2 evenings a week and on weekends with the section head instructing. 

Legislation to provide for the registration of professional surveyors was passed by the Fono as House Bill No. 5 entitled "An act to create a Board of Registration for Professional Surveys and to provide for the regulation of Professional Surveyors." The bill was ratified by the Governor on May 17, 1961, and the first registration examination is anticipated in September of 1962.

The investigation, design, and supervision of construction of 3,900 feet of main highway was accomplished as well as field investigation of an additional 1½ miles of main road. Plans for 43 private buildings were examined and approved in line with the Planning Commission policy.

Roads. Maintenance of the present 57 miles of main and secondary roads demanded an unusual amount of work due to the exceptionally heavy rainfall. An estimated 3,700 cubic yards of coral fill material was dredged, hauled, and placed on the roads throughout the island. 

In addition, a total of 1,500 cubic yards of coral fill was placed on the 3,900 feet of main road between Satala and Atu'u and graded and compacted as a subbase for paving. A total of 700 linear feet of penetration treatment paving was applied at Atu’u. 

As a village project under the direction of the District Governor of the Eastern District, rough excavation was completed on 31½ miles of road between the villages of Fagaitua and Masefau and extending to Masausi and Sailele. Work was accomplished on a volunteer basis

with the Government providing hand tools and occasionally automotive equipment. Expected completion of the 6-mile road is November 

1961 and before the close of the fiscal year, new incentive was evidenced by other villages in starting construction of roads on a similar basis. 

Annual Airport Construction Project Report 

Despite equipment breakdown and delays encountered in the procurement of spare parts for equipment, activities at the project continued as rapidly as possible under the 3-year construction plan for minimum daylight jet operations in July 1962. A total of $2,350,000 was scheduled for allotment by the Department of the Interior in July 1961 for completion of the jet strip. 

In preparation for the paving work connected with the project, at the end of the fiscal year 6,000 cubic yards of paving rock was crushed and stockpiled for future use. During the period the original estimate of 1,200,000 cubic yards for coral fill necessary to complete the strip was revised to a total of 878,000 cubic yards. At the end of the year 

712,000 cubic yards of this total was in place with a balance of 165,000 scheduled for completion in latter October 1961. In addition, 30,000 cubic yards of class B rock was drilled and hauled to complete the roadway inside the sea walls. Three concrete culverts running under the runway were also completed. 

One additional stateside person assigned to the project as construction superintendent increased the stateside employees to four. 

Total number of local employees as of June 30 was 270.

Final Summary 

While it should be evident to the reader that progress in individual programs was made during the fiscal year 1961, it should also be evident to the reader that there is a great need for bringing health, education, economic and social activities to a much higher level than that which has been in effect during the past number of years. The true needs of this territory have been submitted by this Administration to the Congress of the United States. That body has been most understanding and sympathetic. This Administration will be given more realistic appropriations with which it can and will bring about the betterment of conditions so badly needed in all phases of life in 

American Samoa.
