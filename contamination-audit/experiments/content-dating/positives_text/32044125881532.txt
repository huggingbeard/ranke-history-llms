ECONOMICAL PRACTICES IN CAPITAL OUTLAYS When Report of the 

Virginia Advisory Legislative Council 

Richmond, Virginia

December 1, 1953 

To: Honorable John S. Battle, Governor of Virginia and

The General Assembly of Virginia 

The General Assembly at its regular session in 1952, being concerned about the extent of present and anticipated capital outlay expenditures by the State and its political subdivisions, and feeling it desirable that this program should be examined to see whether these governmental units are deriving the greatest possible benefit for their citizens from the expenditures being made, directed the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council to make a study of these matters. The resolution directing the study was as follows: 

HOUSE JOINT RESOLUTION NO. 102 

Directing the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council to report upon methods to obtain the greatest return upon certain capital outlay expenditures.

Whereas, the State, counties, cities and towns are engaged in a tremendous program of capital outlay for many diverse types of buildings, structures and facilities; and 

Whereas, State grants and loans are involved in many of these projects and it is highly important to each taxpayer that the fullest possible return be received for expenditures made therein; now, therefore, be it 

Resolved by the House of Delegates, the Senate concurring, that the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council be, and hereby is, directed to make a study and report upon the methods that should be employed for the most economical expenditures of appropriations for capital outlay expenditures of the State, counties, cities and towns involving State grants or loans. 

All agencies of the State shall assist the Council in its study. The 

Council shall especially consider the cost of preparation of plans and specifications for structures and other facilities, the cost of supervision, materials and methods employed in construction and any other matter in relation to the foregoing or in connection therewith that the Council deems appropriate. The Council shall complete its study and make its report to the Governor and General Assembly not later than September one, nineteen hundred fifty-three.

The Council assigned this study to Mosby G. Perrow, Jr., of Lynchburg, member of the Senate and member of the Council, to act as Chairman of a Committee to make the preliminary investigation and study and report to it. The Committee considered all of the material, including information as to practices in other states and, in this State, practices employed by both the State and the political subdivisions in the matter of capital outlay expenditures. It held a public hearing at which interested persons were offered an opportunity to discuss this situation with the Committee and offer their views. It conferred with representatives of the State Departments and agencies most concerned. When the Committee had determined the facts, and reached its conclusions it submitted its report to the Council. 

The Council has reviewed the report of the Committee and submits the following findings and recommendations:

A. The Problem of Capital Outlay Expenditures. 

During the past four years there has been expended or authorized for expenditure for public school capital outlays State funds in the total sum of $75,000,000. At the present time State and local expenditures for this purpose are running at the rate of approximately $50,000,000 a year. This contrasts sharply with similar expenditures in past years. For the 20 year period ending June 30, 1944, there was expended from local funds throughout the State of Virginia for school building construction the sum of about $50,000,000, or an average of $2,500,000 annually. 

With the population of the State steadily increasing, with continued demands for equalization of facilities as between the races, and with a Statewide need of replacing obsolescent equipment and carrying out a program of consolidation estimated, in 1950, to cost a total of nearly $400,000,000, it will be seen that the magnitude of the capital outlay program for public schools is likely to remain at a figure comparable to the present one.

In addition many millions of dollars have been expended for capital outlays at the several institutions of higher learning throughout the State, at the tuberculosis sanatoria, and at the mental institutions. It has been reported that budget requests for nearly $100,000,000 in capital outlay expenditures for the next biennium have been made by the institutions and agencies of the State, and these requests probably do not cover fully the long range plans for capital improvements. 

Local school construction was historically in Virginia a purely local affair. The State's program for the maintenance of the public free school system was directed primarily towards assisting the localities in instructional costs, transportation, education in special fields, etc. However, when the needs for local school buildings became more than the localities could supply, a program of State aid was initiated which has resulted in the appropriation of State funds in aid of local school construction in the amount referred to above.

The localities have had almost a completely free hand insofar as the size, type and details of construction of school buildings are concerned. The authority of the State is limited to establishing, and requiring the localities to meet, certain minimum standards relating to health, safety, and 2

suitability for instructional purposes. As a consequence, the local school boards have been supreme in most matters connected with public school construction. They have the authority to select the architects, advertise for bids, award the contract and superintend the construction. In most cases, the division superintendent of schools has been the representative of the local school board in attending to the details of these operations. 

Capital outlay expenditures for buildings at the State institutions also have been largely decentralized. The process has been that the General 

Assembly, upon application made by the respective institutions in their budget requests, has allocated a certain sum of money for the construction of a particular building. The institution then has proceeded to select the architect, advertise for bids, award the contract, and have the building constructed. Of recent years advice from, with a measure of control by, the Division of Institutional Engineering of the Division of the Budget have been available in connection with capital outlay expenditures by the several institutions. However, basically, the responsibility has in most cases rested upon a building committee chosen locally by the governing authorities of the institution and entrusted with all the detail of carrying out the building program. 

B. Defects of present system.

The superimposition of the State aid program upon the historical basis of local public school construction in Virginia has resulted in a situation where the State has been furnishing a great deal of the money in many cases and yet has had little to say about how it should be expended. Certain advisory services by the State Department of Education are offered, but the State has had little to do with the actual details of planning and constructing the buildings. To secure funds a county need only to show that it has a well-planned building construction program, that the same is necessary, and that the plans and specifications for the particular project are suitable and adequate. Once the funds for construction are made available, the supervision is entirely local.

Thus the responsibility for the expenditure of these funds has rested on the shoulders of the several county school boards and the division superintendents of schools. A division superintendent, being the full time executive officer of the local board, has in most instances borne the brunt of this responsibility. The division superintendents and the members of the county school boards are chosen for their offices because of their qualifications as educators and as administrators of a well-rounded school program and not as builders. Even if these individuals possess the necessary experience and technical knowledge to plan and supervise building construction, the performance of these functions must of necessity lessen their effectiveness in the field of educational duties, the primary responsibility which they were chosen to carry out.

It is inevitable under these circumstances that the roles of the architects and contractors have been disproportionately large in the school building construction program. Being persons experienced in their fields and dealing in many cases with those who are not qualified by experience or do not have sufficient time to devote to this work, the architect or the builder as the case may be has frequently been the controlling factor in the overall school construction program.

An instance has been brought to the attention of the Council in which the architect on a particular project has also been the builder to whom the contract was awarded. This practice is contrary to the policy and code of ethics of the American Institute of Architects, but there is nothing in the present system under which local buildings are constructed to prevent this from reoccurring. 

A school board may act as its own contractor, and there is no requirement that competitive bidding be utilized in the awarding of contracts nor that, if bids are submitted, the lowest. bid must be accepted. 

While the situation is somewhat improved from the point of view of the State institutions and agencies there may still be an undue amount of responsibility on local building committees who may or may not be qualified for their function. At some institutions there are well qualified personnel who are charged by the institution with the execution of a building program. 

At others, the building committee may be composed of persons who are not trained for this purpose. Some assistance is given by the Division of 

Institutional Engineering in the case of building projects at State institutions and, according to information received, the person who directly supervises construction, called the "clerk of the works", is in many instances selected upon recommendation of that Division. 

C. Recommendations.

It is recommended that all capital outlay expenditures in the nature of building construction to which the State contributes (either in whole or in part) through grants-in-aid or loans, be made under the general supervision as to construction of the Division of Institutional Engineering. The State 

Board of Education should retain its authority to fix and enforce standards relating to safety, health and educational requirements. 

The local school authorities and other agencies expending State grantsin-aid, loans or appropriations for costs, in whole or in part, of construction should be required to consult with the Division of Institutional Engineering upon the contract with the architect or engineer selected by the local school board or agency. The local school boards and the authorities of such agencies should also be required to consult with the Division of Institutional Engineering concerning plans, materials, costs and terms of the contract for construction. Before State funds are allocated for local school construction or authorized to be expended by any such agency for construction purposes the local school board or other such agency should certify to the Superintendent of Public Instruction or the Director of the Budget, as the case may be, that it has consulted with the Division and has reviewed all data presented to it by the Division. 

All contracts for such construction should be required to be let only after competitive bids. No building contract should be awarded to the person, firm or corporation who has been engaged as architect or engineer for the same project.

The Division should be charged with the responsibility of providing construction inspection services adequate to insure that the interests of the State and locality are safeguarded and that the terms of the contract and the plans and specifications are adhered to.

The Division should also compile complete cost data on every construction project in which State funds are employed, such cost data to include unit costs, comparative material costs and comparative labor costs. In addition there to the Division should be charged with the responsibility of maintaining data on the various types of material and their relative advantages in construction and use. This information should be made available by periodic reports to every division superintendent of schools, and other agencies expending State grants-in-aid or loans for construction. 

As to local expenditures for public school or other purposes where the State does not make any contribution (either in whole or in part) by way of grants-in-aid or loans it is recommended that the services of the Division be available only on a voluntary basis according to the desire of the locality.

D. Discussion of Recommendations. 

The Council feels that with the present system of supervision of capital outlay expenditures made both by the several State institutions and agencies and, with State funds in most instances, by local school boards, it is impossible for the State to receive full value from the large sum which it is contributing to these purposes. Building costs have risen steadily throughout the last few years and the trend has been generally upward during most of the 20th Century. If the physical plant which is necessary for our public school system and for the proper functioning of our several State institutions and agencies is to be completed it is essential that all possible economies be effected. Moreover, if the State contributes toward the cost of State or local capital outlay expenditures it is only reasonable that the State should be permitted some voice as to how its contribution shall be expended.

It is the belief of the Council that the program outlined above will provide for the exercise of State control where, and only where, it is needed. There is no desire on the part of the Council to interfere with local freedom in the selection of styles of architecture, materials, site or any of the aspects of building construction which should be decided upon by those who will use the buildings. However, it is felt that where savings can effectuated by the use of new materials, by making available to one locality the benefit of experience in others, and by supervision of the contractor in the execution of the building project, these savings should be brought about and the locality as well as the State will receive the benefit thereof.

In conclusion it is again pointed out that the building program to which the State is contributing is now a business of sizeable proportions. The architects and contractors who receive the benefit of the sums appropriated are experienced in this highly technical field. It is felt that the source which

provides the funds from which these costs are paid should be represented by persons of comparable training and experience so that these parties can deal with each other on an equal footing.
