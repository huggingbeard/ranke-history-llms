I-NO REFEREE IN THE STREET 

I'm surprised I lived to finish this book. 

This isn't the story of a dying man in the medical sense. 

There is nothing the matter with me physically except for a broken nose, an old gunshot wound in the hip and a scar here and there. I don't have any incurable disease. 

I'm young, so to speak, in years-only thirty-nine. But 

I feel I've lived for a century. Why am I surprised that I'm still around? Why am I worried? It's a fair question and deserves an honest answer. 

My occupation is going to kill me. 

I'm a "muscleman."

I play knuckles across faces for a living. And when work is coming my way, the pay is good. The mob boys pay me for action. I don't use a gun or a knife. Sometimes 

I use a sap, but most of the deals call for two fists and a fast foot.

Of course, if you saw me in the street or in a gin mill, you wouldn't believe the bartender if he said: "Don't bother that guy. He's rough and don't care who he hits as long as he hits first."

The reason you wouldn't believe the beer-slinger is that he'd be pointing to a little guy-a five-foot-eight shrimp type that probably jockeys a taxi around the streets. You wouldn't know that I pack one hundred and seventy pounds. No flesh. No pot.

The bartender just tells you because he knows I can 1

fight in the ring or on the street. And I like the alley fights better. There's no referee in the street. You win or you don't, and rules-hell, who ever heard of rules in the gutter? 

I hire out for the fast dollar. I've got to win, because there's no loser's share-no return bouts in Madison Square Garden. The mob boys pay on delivery. No excuses.

If I don't do the job and take the guy good, then I don't work. I'd better blow the town and look for something else. And there's nothing else I can do. I'm like a guy on "hop." He needs the nose candy to live. I need to fight to live. It's as simple as that. 

You paid your dough to read this book. Maybe right now you think this is just a lot of talk. Maybe you're thinking, Who is this tough guy whose name is not wellknown like Frank Costello and Lucky Luciano? Scan this police record and remember this: I led the Jersey 

State Prison riots a few years ago. Any cop or con will tell you that only the toughest guys lead prison riots. 

They're the boss men of the murderers, stick-up men, muggers and perverts, right down or up the line to musclemen.

Let's roll back the years to when I got started in the rackets. I was born in Trenton, New Jersey. My dad was very much on the side of the law. He was a magistrate in Mercer County. He started as a justice of the peace, but he was good with his fists. He could have whipped my ass any time. He used to play semi-pro baseball, and wanted me to become a lawyer. What a laugh that is today! Me a lawyer! A con who has to hire a mouthpiece to get himself out of a jam! Mom and Dad-why the hell talk about them? God love them, even if they disowned me. And I've got three legit brothers who don't know where I am and probably care less. I don't blame them.

I can't complain about the way I was brought up. We

just didn't see eye to eye on what was right. They got disgusted with what I did for pleasure and with my brushes with the law.

At seventeen I took my first arrest, and it didn't scare me a damn. I had become important for the first time. 

It wasn't too bad that first time in the precinct. I didn't have a racket name, and everybody seemed to be on my side. Lectures poured over me from the cops and my dad. 

All I had done was batter a couple of guys. The police asked me, "Johnny, boy, why did you do this? What's the matter with you? You got a nice family and you're smart." 

My answer didn't sit too good with anybody. I just told the truth. "I did it because I enjoyed it." 

That was twenty-two years ago, and my answer hasn't changed much. I still like to belt a punk around. It's my trade, and I get paid better than the going wage for a muscleman. At least, I did until this year. Now I'm on the lam again from the mob boys, who don't like the names I'm going to name, and telling who did what to whom.

I was making a pretty fair name around Trenton as a hard guy who liked to mix it for kicks, and the racket boys like to sign up comers in their stable who can handle dirty jobs for them. I used to fight fair and square, but then I learned a lesson that I never forgot. It paid off. 

One night I was sitting at the bar in a honky-tonk joint on Furman Street, minding my own business. I had a cute broad with me, and I was making a play to impress her. It was a tough place, and usually there were a dozen fights every night. A three-piece colored band was belting out a fast beat, when a little shrimp asked my doll for a dance. I told the guy, politely, to take a powder. He didn't seem to understand, and asked the doll again.

I stood up and walked over to the little punk. Hell, I'd 9.

fought amateur fights and could take this jerk! Man, I learned the facts of life in a hurry!

I started a jab to set him up. That's just about all I remember. He kicked me in the shin. I bent over and got shoe leather in the groin. Then something in the face. The gutter was my bed that night, and the broad with me never spoke to me again. I'd learned a lesson the hard way. 

Then, I thought it was unfair. Now I know it's the only way. When you kid, you kid. When you fight, you fight to win. No rules.

I met the bastard a week later. It was no contest. I kicked him. He's married, but he can't have kids; and his face looks like a truck ran over it.

From then on I was making a pretty fair name for myself around Trenton. One day in a poolroom a fifthrate punk walks over to me. "Johnny, boy," he says, tough-like, “I got a message for you." "Take a blow, Jack. I ain't bothering with messages." "Listen me out, Johnny D. You got a rep. Daylight 

Kelly wants you. He pays good for what you got to sell." 

That was enough for me. I needed dough bad. The family weren't kicking through, and running errands and bouncing guys in a poolroom didn't pay enough.

This sounded good to me. Anybody around the Tenderloin district in Trenton knew the name Daylight 

Kelly. The head man in the pinball and juke box rackets.

The bossman who got his name because he never got up in the winter until five in the afternoon and later in summer. He hated the sun, and operated at night.

That was the start of my life in the rackets, and it sounded good to me. This was to be my first real job; but 

I knew enough about the racket boys to play my hand hard. I looked young for eighteen, but I was betting my chips on my rep as a guy with fast fists and guts. I grabbed a shine on the way over, for class, and spent the rest of 10

my change on a pack of butts. The suit I wore didn't look too bad and didn't look too good, but I wasn't trying for a model job! I couldn't change because I didn't have another suit anyway. I wasn't worried about looking good. 

My rep wasn't built on fancy clothes. 

I walked into a tavern on Ferry Street. The cops used to have a police car there every night to save time running to the fights. Nobody paid any attention to me, and 

I walked to the bar. I ordered a short beer and waited. I saw his stooges look me over. There was Pappy, Bucky, Frank and a crumb called Brownie. I took a sip, and then 

Daylight motioned for me to come over. I finished the beer and went to his table. The other punks just listened as Daylight spoke. "Sit down, kid. I hear you can take care of yourself. I can use a kid like you. You want to work?" he says quietly.

I knew what to do, so I sat back and nodded and let him do the talking. I had to make him think he needed me more than I needed him, and I was right. "Okay, kid. You're on the payroll. Starting now."

He reached in his pocket and handed me five yards. "You get two and a half yards a week. The rest is a present. You start now. It's week's pay plus, kid."

I started to say okay, when this guy Frank stood up and laughed. "Why, you little two-bit jerk! What the hell can you do? 

I can take you with my left hand sitting down," he says to me.

No one else said a word. They just looked, and Daylight folded his hands.

I took a quick look at this six-foot slob and walked to the bar. I took a drink away from a broad and a beer bottle from a guy. Both of them break easy on a table, and I use them as boxing gloves. Frank took a look at me, and he wasn't happy. 11

I knew this was no time to stop, and I had to make the play good. I walked over to him and kicked his shin. He bent, and I connected to the groin with the other foot and shoved the glass in his face. No one said a word, and the fight was over. Daylight kind of smiled, and Ponzi, a fast man with a shiv, walked over to me. "Johnny D.," he says quietly. "You're in, but don't fight with the boys."

I took his advice and sat down again. This was going to be easy.

I asked Daylight just what he wanted me to do, and the orders were simple. "Johnny D., I want my pinball machines in a lot more places. I want them in fast, and I don't want no for an answer. You see that the owners of these joints get on our side. If you get in trouble don't say anything. Just wait for me. I hear when any of my boys gets picked up. I take care of the lawyers and anything else. Don't mention my name, and don't worry. You start tonight."

I started to work for Daylight an hour later, but not before we ate. I remember that meal like it was yesterday. 

I had veal scallopini and hot peppers. The rest had wine, but I only had a short beer. I never drink anything but beer when I work, and just short ones. When I'm playing and out with a broad I'll cabaret around and drink whisky, but not too much. You never know who's going to be outside the honky-tonk when you leave. 

That night I started out on my first job. Daylight gave me twenty-three places to visit, and I went to every one of them. There was trouble in some, and it was a cinch in others. I learned quick and it paid off.

The trips became routine. A candy-store owner that didn't like the idea of changing machines or putting them in, got glass broken and a belting. A saloon got different treatment. First I took the bouncer and bounced him. Then, with one of my associates, I broke twenty or 12

thirty bottles of whisky and turned the beer taps on. And the owner got his, too. No favorites. One trip usually convinced them that we meant business and that cop-hollering was a waste of time. 

I'd walk into a gin mill with two of my boys. They were all brawn and no brains. Strong backs are all they need as long as they know how to take orders when they're told. My creeps would stand at the bar and I'd go sit at a table and order a beer. After I got a drink I'd walk over to the pinball machine and start playing.

After one or two shots, I'd tilt the machine and call the owner over. "Mac," I'd tell him, "your machine is faulty. Who the hell is giving you these cheap machines? You ought to make a change. You're not getting a fair shake."

The owner usually had a pretty fair idea why I was complaining and knew there would be trouble if he didn't listen. "Listen, mister, I got no complaints. Here's a nickel and play it on the house. These machines are okay,” he'd say. 

While he talked I'd put on my leather gloves so I wouldn't hurt my hands. That was the signal for my boys to walk over, and they'd grab the guy. No rough stuff. 

They just held him and I smashed the glass. "See how easy the glass breaks?" I'd say. “Your customers might get hurt, and you wouldn't want that, Mac." 

Then I'd turn the machines over and let them crash. If the guy made a move, my creeps would work him a little to convince him this was a business trip. This was usually enough to convince him to switch his trade. "Listen, fellow, I don't want no trouble. But how about the guy who's already selling me?" he'd say.

I'd tell him that he had no worries-that I worked for a nice guy called Daylight Kelly, and part of our service was to guarantee no further trouble from anyone. The 1 13

owner would agree to take from us, and I'd tell him that a truck would be around the next morning with a couple of machines and a new juke box. I'd drop around just to see there was no trouble in getting them installed. We'd throw the old machines and the juke box out in the street and tell the owner to call up the other salesman and tell him about the change. 

One saloon keeper, Milt, didn't like the idea, and when I finished breaking the glass on his machines he told me to go to hell. He thought he was tough and had connections. He had no guts, because after I belted him three times he had a change of mind. He took the order, but overnight he started to play it cute. We got back in the morning with our truck and started to unload, when a county dick walked in, and a big smile came over Milt's face. Even the bartender started to play hero and told my boys to blow. They stood still waiting for me to make my play.

I turned to Milt and the dick and laughed right in their faces. "So you think you're pretty smart, Milt, and called in this punk? You got yourself some ideas since we talked. 

You're still taking our machines," I said.

Then the county dick threw in his two-bits' worth of advice. I heard him out polite-like. "Listen, smart boy," he started. "I'm off duty, but Milt is my friend. You just take your machines and get the hell out of here. If you don't, then you and I are going to take a walk to the station house."

If he thought that worried me, he was as wrong as a guy can get. I told my boys to stand quiet and do nothing. I had my instructions for something like this. I went to the telephone booth and called Daylight. The call woke him up, and he asked for the telephone number of the booth. I stepped out and told Milt and the dick to hold off for a couple of minutes. They said to hell with that and 14

to get my boys moving out of the joint. I told them we weren't moving. The dick began to act tougher, but the telephone rang and Milt answered it.

He came back and told the dick that the precinct was on the wire. The dick seemed surprised. He wasn't on the telephone more than a minute, and he was shook up when he came out. He walked over to Milt and me. "Milt, I'm sorry. I got to go,” he said.

The punk didn't say anything more. He just blew the joint, and Milt looked like he'd been slugged. I knew what he'd been told. Daylight had protection, and no plainclothesman was going to walk in and interfere with our business—not when the payroll took care of such things.

I was sore at this unnecessary interruption, so we worked Milt and the hero bartender over for a few minutes. We never had any more trouble with them. 

I felt elated with this job. I had a feeling of power. 

Now I was a big man and through with penny-ante poolrooms. I could break jaws, kick faces and have dough. I had protection from the racket boys, and life was great. 

Once or twice the owner of a store would cry like a baby and scream when he got hit. But Daylight wanted results and I was paid to see that he got them. I liked the pay, and the hell with the squawks. 

I reported to Daylight every night, and he was pleased with the action I was giving him. Now and then we'd have to buy a little protection. There was a dick that I bought cheap. A punk detective called J M- He got one-fifty a month, and now he has a seventy-fivethousand-dollar home in New Jersey. He must have smartened up after I left. 

My record with Daylight for fourteen months was pretty good. In fact, I'm pretty damn proud of it. When I started he had 2,100 places, and I built it up to 3,600 before I left. And now, when I look back, I know what a 15

jerk I was to work for him at that price. He took 70 per cent from every place, and the average take a week was at least $65. That was dough. And I was making it for him.

But I thought I was living good. I stayed with my family and didn't pay room or board until they kicked me out. 

My family got to hate me. They despised me. I always had a broad, and the dames thought I was great. I lived it up good, but I wanted to do better and make more dough. 

Trenton was getting too small.

The last time I saw Dad, I nearly knocked his teeth out. 

I came home, and he was sore and slapped a butt out of my mouth. I busted him in the nose, and my brothers broke up the fight. It was time to leave Trenton, and I was waiting for the word.

II-PERSONAL BODYGUARD 

The next day I moved out. It was time to leave home and Trenton, but I wanted time to think and make plans. 

I took a room for seven bucks a day at the Jersey Hotel on Broad Street. That was big money for those days, but 

I was sort of a celebrity and had to live in a classy joint. 

There was nothing to do one evening, so I walked down to the local Democratic Club and ordered a beer. Tony, the bartender and bouncer, brought me a Seagram's and a chaser. I burned. "Tony, boy, I ordered a beer. Change it." "You take what I want you to have and pay for it."

He turned to leave, and I kicked the glass onto the tile floor. Tony turned around and came back. I waited. "John, I watched you grow up. I figured sooner or later 16

we'd clash. You owe fifty-five cents for the drink and twenty cents for the glass. Pay."

Tony carried about 185 pounds, and it was muscle. 

There were two beer glasses on the table. I kicked them off and told him to put them on the bill. I didn't wait to get his answer- -I kicked him in the groin. He doubled up, and I drop-kicked him in the left side of the face. He went down like a ton of bricks, and I walked all over him from head to toes. You gotta give punks a lesson, or you lose standing.

The owner walked over to me. Fats Moran wasn't looking for trouble. He got real polite. "What's the trouble, Johnny? Did you get the wrong order?" "Yeah. I want a glass of beer. Okay by you?" 

Fats brought me the beer and told me three guys from

Philly wanted to see me. I looked down the room and saw them sitting at a table. "Fats, if they want to see me, tell them to come over. 

I'll be here."

A few minutes later they came over to the table and sat down. They didn't say who they are and I didn't care. 

I listened. "Johnny, you ever hear of Frankie Meyer in Philly?" one of them said. “We hear that you're going to Philly. 

How'd you like to work for Frankie?”

I knew that I wanted to work for Meyer-he was the 

Number Two racket man there. But why tell these punks? "Sure, I'm going to Philly, but I'm going legit. Tell 

Meyer to drop dead." 

They left, and I finished my beer and left to see Daylight. Now I knew I was wanted in Philly, but I had to act like I didn't care. Daylight was kind of surprised to see me, and I gave him the news quick. "Daylight, I'm leaving Trenton. I'm hurting my mother by staying here and got to get out of town." 17

"If it's more dough, Johnny, you can have it here. 

Things are going good. But if you want to go, then do what you want. Do you want a knockdown to Frankie Meyer?"

I told Daylight that I didn't want to ask for a job there, but would play it my way. We shook hands and I left. 

Philadelphia was a new place to me, but I heard of the right place to go and make a little noise and then wait. I got a room and then went to the 33 Club and sat down at the bar and got a beer. I purposely elbowed the glass, and it broke on the floor. I told the bartender to give me another and put down a dime. He asked me for twenty cents more for the glass. "Take the dime and shut up, Mac. I'm thirsty."

The bouncer heard me and walked over. Like a damn fool he moved to my left and said, "Fella, be nice like the man says."

I gave him the usual routine and walked like an animal on him. The owner came over and smiled. "Where do you work, kid?" "Right here," I answered. "What do you pay?”.

When he said forty-five bucks a week I laughed right in his face. I told him the price was sixty the first week, and from then on one hundred.

At the end of the first week I got a yard plus food and drinks and a two-room apartment right over the joint. 

This was chicken feed and I knew it; but I was waiting for Meyer to make a move. He had to come to me. 

One night three weeks later, a noisy guy came into the place. He had a cauliflower ear and a punched-in nose, and looked like an ex-pug. He started to give the bartender trouble, and I went into my act. Drunks and fresh kids you don't hurt much; but this guy looked like trouble. I got to his side. "Finish your drink, bud, and scram," I told him nicely. 18

He looked at me as if I was nuts. "Do you know who I am?" he says. "Are you Jesus?" I asked, and drank his drink. The sucker made a move. His left hand was slower than my foot, and his face bent to the ground and caught my knee on the upswing. I took him like a sack of potatoes and propped him up in the alley outside. I got a bucket of water and threw it on him and told him to get going while he could.

The owner didn't say anything, but I knew something was going to happen. It did the next night about nine o'clock. There weren't many people in the place, when four guys came in. One guy stayed near the door, two stood at the bar and the fourth sat at a table. One of the guys at the bar walked over to me. 39 "Frankie Meyer wants to see you. He's at the table." "Okay, I'll go over. But you guys stay where you are.

I sat down at the table, and Meyer didn't waste any time getting to the point. I listened and kept an eye on the others. "You worked a guy pretty hard here last night," he said. "You know who it was? That was my kid brother. 

He's an ex-pug. A little stupid, but he's my brother. And don't play tough with me. I got three guys here and each has a piece. They'll take you fast.” 

I knew they had rods with them. You can spot the bulge easy. "Mr. Meyer, you're the customer. As long as you behave, you got no worries. Talk if you want. It's your breath." "Take it easy, smart boy. I'm calling the signals. You want to work for me? It's two-fifty a week and expenses."

We agreed on the terms and I told him there were two things I wouldn't do and that was stealing or murder.

Frankie agreed. He told me that I'd have an apartment on 19

South Fourth Street, where he lived, and to meet him the next morning at a joint near there.

I got there and there were three other guys with him. 

One of them spoke to me. I told him to get lost. "Buster, I'm not talking to creeps. I came to see Frankie." 

Frankie motioned me to come over and sit down. "Ready to work, Johnny?" he asked as he reached into his pocket for his wallet. "Sure, but I want three bills plus," I said, waiting for him to cut a fifty. 

Meyer never was a cheapskate. He threw five yards on the table. "Kid, you may not live long with your mouth. The first time you get out of line, you're through. We play for keeps. The extra deuce is for clothes. Get some new ones." 

For the next three months I was Frankie Meyer's personal bodyguard. I was in the big leagues. I always had plenty of cash in my pockets. Hell, Frankie picked up the tab every place we went. And if I had a broad he'd slip me a hundred and lend me his car for a date. I lived with the guy for three months almost night and day until I got promoted.

And our days and nights were plenty busy keeping the rackets under control. My day started about ten o'clock. 

Frankie didn't get up till twelve. Every morning I put on sweat clothes and ran two miles in Fairmount Park to keep in shape. Then I'd stop in Cambria's gym and go a few rounds with anyone handy. I was in good shape. 

I'd get back to the apartment close to twelve and sit down with Frankie for breakfast. He ate light, and the maid nearly wore herself out serving me four or five eggs, sausages, dry cereal and hot cakes and a quart of milk. 

Frankie nibbled on juice and toast, a little coffee and then a scotch.

After a quick shower we'd get dressed and Frankie 20

would stuff the envelopes with cash. There were usually about twenty-five of them. Pay-off money is always in small bills and put in envelopes. Frankie also had about five or six grand in his pocket for emergency money.

When the money was put away we'd go down to his light 

Cadillac convertible and put the envelopes in the glove compartment. This was our public relations money. 

Our first stop was always at the police station down the street. I never went into the station house. It was Frankie's own job to take care of the captain. Anyway, station houses bore me. They're too dreary. I'd go across the street and get a glass of milk while the cops were being paid. I didn't have bull fever then. Just contempt.

From there we'd drive down to South Philly and make stops to pay plainclothesmen, meet our boys in charge of districts and pickup men for the bookies. We'd get envelopes too, but never counted them. If there was a shortage we'd get to that later and take care of the shortchangers. 

They knew and we knew it. 

Lunch was the worst part of the routine. Every lousy day we'd hit a delicatessen, because Frankie loved Jewish food. It was the same one, and we got there at two o'clock on the dot. He liked boiled food, because he thought now and then that he had an ulcer. I always ordered what they didn't have and they sent out for it. We were top customers.

We would stay there for just about two hours, and the small overlords, aldermen and racket lieutenants would come in to pay court. Frankie would get reports, make suggestions and get news. I hated that joint with its lousy food, but Frankie was the boss. At four o'clock, when most of the races were off, we'd leave. 

Then we'd start on a good-will tour of all the main bars. This was public relations, and Frankie would buy drinks for the house in a lot of places. By the time this 21

tour was over it would be close to seven-thirty, and we'd get back to the apartment and change clothes. 

The night tour of duty was much better. No cheap stuff. 

We got to the better places. Lew M- 's was the meeting place, but this was class. No cheap hoods and lousy food. We had glitter dolls and the money was fast. 

Of course, it was business too. The big boys met there, and the out-of-town big shots would always drop in. You don't miss seeing the right people when you're a racket man from out of town. There's protocol in this business too.

Important policy matters would be discussed before the dolls joined us. Plans would be made to take care of certain cops that wanted to boost their take. Or politics would come up. I listened close, because I wanted to learn and get ahead. 

Frankie would sit and listen and then make a quick decision. "Okay,” he'd say quickly. "So we got to get a new councilman. Send the word out and get somebody who'll play ball. Do it."

And when you listen close you get to learn things that aren't spelled out. Soon I learned that forty per cent of the take in Philly from the rackets-and that included just about everything from bookies and prostitutes to pinballs and narcotics-went to the national syndicate headquarters. That headquarters changed from Chicago to New 

York or Detroit.

Plans were made there every night to meet every important problem. The right people knew who I was, and 

I had money and bank accounts. We'd stay there until eleven, and the rest of the night was on the town for pleasure. We'd cabaret it up with plenty of dolls. But I wasn't allowed to drink.

Hell, I was Frankie's personal bodyguard and could only have two beers. I had to be ready in case a sucker 22

tried to take Frank. The broads thought it was funny I didn't drink. I let them get loaded. It was easier. Frank was a big tipper and that meant an extra seventy-five bucks a week for me. It was simple. He'd always tip the waiter himself. No silver. Just bills. But he'd give me a ten spot to get the hats. I'd keep nine and give the chick a single. I always wished he'd let me take care of the waiter.

Then back to the apartment and he'd count the money for the day. He never let me do it; but I got good, eyes, and there was at least sixty to seventy grand a day. The smallest I ever saw was twenty-two thousand. A special messenger would take the dough to headquarters. Frankie was the Number Two man in Philly. Mickey S

was Number One. I hadn't met him yet. I did later.

This was great for three months. The pay was good and money was great! I got bonuses and began to ride high.

My reputation was growing and Frankie liked me. I thought I had life made good.

III-PROMOTION

V-KEEP THE HABIT GOING 

Night life is good, and broads are great, but in the rackets you gotta work like an honest guy does. The hours are different but there is work to be done. The mob had everything knocked up in Philly, and dope was making a fast buck for everyone. If people have the habit, you might as well keep it going for them. If you don't, then someone else will. I've never taken a shot. I know what it means to see a guy or a broad on the hop. I know what happens. And a hop will do anything to get what he needs. And, brother, he needs it like nothing you ever knew or will know, unless you've got the habit too. 

I've watched the mainliners-the jerks that have to use a needle to take it in the arm-plead for just enough to get the monkey off their back. They'll kill, steal, rape-do anything for a shot. On the way back to the pier we got stopped by the Harbor Patrol. They didn't do a thing except to ask a few silly questions, and I slipped the captain half a yard to buy a drink. He took it, like they all do. I told Frankie I gave him two yards, and he told me, with a laugh, to "cut out robbing the mob."

I waited at another pier for the fishing boat to come in early that morning. They had their share of fish and the hop package was given to one of my boys. He took it to a waiting car, and it was on its way to South Philly to be cut up.

I went along to watch the whole operation, and it ran like clockwork. Within an hour the package was cut and twenty-five runners had picked up their haul. The joint used for the distribution was a Jewish delicatessen. The backroom was the distribution center. The runners came in, ordered a sandwich and left with the stuff.

The runners took their stuff, and I later saw to it they were met by our musclemen and shook down. Why miss a bet? They couldn't squawk. The runners had to pay the other musclemen later. This was in sealed envelopes. 

If the law moved in there were standing orders. They were simple: If you're caught, you take the rap. 

Sure, we supplied them with dough and a mouthpiece 41

and arranged for a fix. But there was to be no talking. If something went wrong, that was too bad.

They all knew better than to talk. They knew the rule perfectly. It was simple, too. Squealing to the law meant quick death even if they were in jail. That wasn't my department. There was a separate section to take care of killing. But I made it clear to the boys what would happen. They knew I meant it.

Sometimes I hired a hoppy for a muscle job, but it was usually a light job. Not a tough one, but an easy goingover with just an arm or a nose busted. They'd do it cheap for a shot. To make them feel better we'd give them a free half-shot and then promise them three free shots and a double sawbuck. For twenty bucks we'd get action.

There wasn't much need to worry about them squealing. When a hoppy is under the influence his memory isn't too good. Anyway, it saved a little dough for the racket. 

And why waste good men on cheap stuff? There was plenty to keep them busy. 

The hop trade was moving good and there were plenty of customers to take care of with heroin. The system was good and I didn't run into any trouble. Our muscle boys knew their way around, and routine stuff was a cinch for them.

VI-BOOTLEG FIGHTER 

Being top muscleman for the mob almost became an administrative job with a few exceptions when I did a job myself. I kept going to the gym every day to keep in shape, but I got the urge to get in the ring for kicks. I let 42

the word drop in the right places that I was available for a bout. I wanted to fight for the dough. I had plenty of that and more. I did it because I love to fight. I like it better than being with a broad. I have a fetish about fighting. There's nothing like it in the world. The only trouble, I thought, was in a ring you've got to fight clean. Maybe 

I'd forget if the going got rough.

At that time I weighed in between one hundred and fifty-five and sixty pounds, and no fat. I was ready any time for action. There are plenty of chances to be a bootleg fighter. That's a pug who fights under someone else's name. A kid doesn't show up, and you step in and fight under his name. I fought fifteen or sixteen fights under another guy's name. I built a rep for a lot of guys and made some extra sugar betting on myself. 

I was sitting in my office when I got a call from Mickey

Salerno. He's still around controlling most of the managers and knows the score from every angle. "Johnny, I hear you want a little action. I got a boy who's supposed to go tonight in Pittsburgh. He's my boy, but he can't make it. You go under his name and the take is a G note.' 

"Okay with me, Mickey. But you can drop dead if you want a dive. I fight under my terms and I'll kick the hell out of whoever I fight. You can drop dead if you don't like it."

Mickey didn't put up any argument. He was building this boy and had arranged for him to get a semi-windup fight. I finished up my morning work and hopped in my car and drove to Pittsburgh. Mickey had a guy meet me at the hotel room. I made the conversation short. "Where's the bills, buddy? I don't go unless I got them right now. And see my car is ready at the arena right after I get through. I'm heading back to Philly tonight."

The character gave me the grand and got out quick. I 43

took a nap and got up in time to go to a bookie. I bet five bills on myself at eight to five and told the creep to wire my winnings to me in Philly right after the fight. 

I was fighting a shine, and there never was a shine I couldn't take. They got heads like billiard balls, but they don't like it in the guts. All I had to do was to lift my belts a foot or so higher than usual and catch him right in the gut.

I got a kick out of being in a dressing room again. I like the smell. The talk is good. I borrowed a pair of tights and got my hands taped. The gloves felt like pillows compared to my usual rough leather gloves that cut a guy to ribbons. Mickey had given me his kid's license. The only close thing was we weighed about the same. 

I almost forgot to stand up in the ring when the announcer called his name. My second jabbed me in the ribs and said, "That's you." I stood up. I was nervous until he hit me and then I felt like I was walking on air. The more they hit the better I like it. It's a tonic to me. The first round went slow. I felt him out and knew I could call the shot when I wanted.

The second round went pretty much like the first and I let him win it. The second in my corner seemed a bit worried and told me to get moving. I laughed and told him I had enough exercise, and the next round was the end. He didn't seem to believe me. He was wrong.

At the bell I walked out for action and belted the shine in the gut. His hands came down and his chin was wide open. I didn't waste time and let him have it good. He never knew what hit him and I didn't wait for the ref to finish counting. It was fun.

I had another grand in my pocket, and by the time I got home the bookie had sent some more dough for me. 

It was a real pleasant evening. I decided to do this more often. I liked to combine pleasure and easy dough. 

Most of the fights were fixed except the main events. 44

The mob boys that controlled the fight game liked to get their money down on sure things and didn't want to take any chances of losing easy dough. The fighters won and lost in the correct rounds so that the boys could get the best odds and take the suckers. I knew when a fight was set up for a kill. I used to make extra dough. Why not? 

It's smarter to take easy dough than let it go.

The afternoon of my second fight as a bootleg pug made me nearly miss it. Business comes before pleasure, and one of my musclemen had got himself in a jam. They're all back and no brains, and one of the creeps got himself arrested. He'd belted a guy for kicks and the victim had called the law. We had a set routine for these interruptions. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred the victim didn't talk and never testified. Victims usually were close to the rackets and knew better. 

Once you work over those guys they're afraid of violence. They know the law coming in just delays their beating for a short time. The muscleman that was bagged by some jerk cop who didn't know better knew what he had to do. Just keep quiet and the mouthpiece would spring him on bail quick. The precinct captain would get a visit and, depending on how serious it was, would get a fast hundred or so. If the rap was tough, then five yards would be handed over. You could buy them cheap, but you had to be careful not to spoil them with too much dough. We didn't want operating expenses to get too high. 

You got to run your business smart.

Somehow or other witnesses never seemed to follow up on their complaints. They got a message and believed the word that it was better to forget. The muscleman who got in trouble for extracurricular work like this case also got a message. He was told the next time it happened another department would meet him. The word was short and sweet: "You'll be turned over to the gun."

The gun was another part of the racket mob. I never 45

spoke to the gun. I didn't want to have anything to do with that department. I didn't want to get close to murder. I ruled my boys with a stiff hand. I liked that better. 

I was set to fight that night and I was sore about one of my boys getting in trouble for no good reason. I was still seeing red when I got a call from Frankie George.

He's a no-good bum and still is, but between him and 

Mickey they controlled the fight racket. "Johnny. About that fight tonight," he purred. “You got the instructions. I want that my boy wins.' 99 

My answer came fast and to the point. I was boiling. 

Imagine that jerk thinking he could tell the top muscleman in Philly what to do in a fight! Who the hell did he think I was? I had a rep, and didn't intend to let anyone tell me how to fight and what to do. Suppose I lost? The guys under me might think I wasn't so tough. Why give them ideas? You can lose command easy if anyone thinks you're soft. "George, get lost," I shouted. "I'm going to hand your boy's brains to you tonight. I can lick any spade any time and don't forget it. You just tell that shine to take care of himself as best he can. This is for real tonight.” 

I guess George knew I meant what I said, because he talked real sweet. "Johnny D., don't get mad. I was just thinking of you. 

My boy can take you anyway. I just didn't want you to get hurt. He'll whip your ass off, so I was making it easy." "Just tell your boy to be in the ring and say a prayer for him. Hang up. I got more important things to do.'

I knew George was sore and I didn't give a damn. He was trying to bring this kid along, and he was a good boy. 

But I wasn't taking any dives for any cheap managers. 

What the hell, this was extracurricular for me.

As soon as he hung up I called one of my select eight to my office and told him to take two grand and bet it 1 46

against George's boy. I told him to bet it that his boy wouldn't be around for the sixth..

Some of the boys in that league don't like to get crossed. I don't want trouble with them." "Don't get no worries. They think their boy can take me. I think they're wrong. Bet a grand on me, Frankie. 

You know I don't take dives." “Okay, Johnny, but why do this? You got enough to take care of without fighting." "Boss, I just love it. Don't worry. Your boy Johnny will be back from Chester tonight without getting hurt."

I got one of the boys to drive me to Chester, and we got there just in time to go to the arena. I didn't bother with no hotel. If George or anybody was sore I wanted the car ready to take me back to my own territory with no delay.

I got my hands taped out and the gloves put on. I had my boy sit with me in the corner just to be sure nothing went wrong outside the ring. I spoke to the ref when we were getting instructions. I told him I wanted him to open up the other boy's gloves and take a look. I didn't want to get hit by plaster of Paris or tin foil in his mitts. There wasn't anything wrong, and I figured that George must be pretty sure of his lad. This might be some real action and 

I was sore enough to hope it would be rough.

I walked back to my corner and looked around the ring side. There was George and a few of his pals. He looked smug and happy. I swore to myself that his grin wouldn't be on his kisser for too long. The shine in the other corner looked hard. He was slick and trained down 47

good. Not an ounce of fat any place except in his head.

He didn't know what was going to happen and probably figured that this was going to be another easy go and his record would be better. He didn't know that build-ups don't always go right along the main track.

The bell rang. My gut was tight and my knees felt wobbly. Maybe I couldn't take this boy, and then I'd be in trouble. Meyer would lose respect and so would a lot of others. What a sucker I was to risk my job just because 

I liked to fight!

Nothing happened for a minute or so and then he stuck a left in my face and a jolt to my ribs. It didn't feel good and I clinched. He spoke fast to me. "How you like that, white boy? You gonna get plenty more from here on." 

I didn't say a word. My gut hurt and this shine was telling me what was going to happen. He felt strong in the clinch and his hands worked my belly over good. 

Those late hours and broads hadn't been so good for me. 

The ref broke us apart.

The round ended and I rested for a minute. I didn't like the way things were going. I couldn't get started. 

I sneaked a look at George and his pals. They were smiling and happy. The bell stopped me thinking about them and put me back to do the job I had to do. The shine was strong and fast. He jabbed me hard in the nose and the blood came. It felt good and I relaxed. The crowd roared. They thought the spade had me going. I guess George and his boys were laughing. They were making a mistake. The boy against me moved in to take advantage. I wiped my nose and the blood glistened on my glove. I was about to make a transfusion.

I hooked with my left and it splattered his ribs. It hurt and his guard dropped just enough. The blood on my glove made contact with his jaw and he didn't like it. I went for his belly. Just high enough to be legal. I felt like 48

an animal. He may have hit back but I don't remember it. 

I hardly heard the bell. I was getting my kicks. This time

I sat down on the stool and felt fine. It was going to be my fight from here on. I was going to work this boy. Not because I didn't like him. He was just flesh. I wanted 

George to feel sick. The shine was listening to his seconds. 

They didn't look too happy. They knew this was no dive and that trouble was there tonight.

The third round was what I liked. The boy was hurt and cut. I stopped hitting him in the face because I didn't want the ref to stop it on account of a lousy cut. I worked him in the belly until my arms were getting tired. 

He wasn't hurting me any more. He couldn't have knocked out a baby by that time. I was living and liking it. The next round I decided to end this little bout. I didn't want to get too tired. Hell, I might want a broad later. Why disappoint them? 

Halfway through the fourth I went to work. Two or three fast lefts and a combination, and the shine was listening to the birdies. It was over and I felt like a million bucks. George left when the shine hit the deck. I thought he might come to the dressing room for a chat, but he failed me. I missed a.laugh and got dressed.

It didn't take too long to get back to Philly, and I went to Lew's to start a little cabareting. Frankie was there, but he wasn't sore. We sat down and got one of the boys to call for some broads. Why stay in shape forever? 

Before they got there Frankie spoke to me quietly and he was serious. "Johnny, you got to smarten up a little. Tonight was okay, and there'll be no hard feelings. I spoke to George and told him that you're not the kind of a guy that takes dives. He didn't know, and they lost some dough. But they're not too sore now. But stop bucking those boys. 

We're all in the same league. There's no use fighting each other." 49

I took the advice Frankie gave. He was smart and why should I louse things up? From then on my bootleg fights were just for kicks and I made sure that the mob boys didn't give a damn who won. I got my pleasure extracurricular and nobody was sore. It kept me in pretty good shape for my career work and the boys under me got more respectful. They knew for sure that I could dish it when and if it became necessary. I didn't bother to tell my wife. She didn't have to know how much I loved to belt. 

She might get scared and the kids might get the wrong idea about their pop.

VII-PIMPS, PROSTITUTES AND POLITICIANS 

The prostitute control in Philly was almost all Lucky Luciano. He got the big part of the take. It was seventyfive per cent of the racket and we kept the rest. The take was close to ten grand a week. And he was welcome to what he got. I hated that part of the racket. I hated it. I had two little daughters and I always thought some son of a bitch might get them some day.

Lucky had to spend some dough there. He had to take care of the lawyers. We did the muscle. He controlled prostitution throughout the East. Like in Baltimore, he had Paul. He answered to Lucky. They all did.

The Philly mob didn't want much of whores and pimp money. Not that they didn't like the dough, but the police in Philly were hot on vice. That seemed to be the one thing they did try to stop, and that's why the price for broads was high. Sure, there were the cheap cathouses where a john could get a piece cheap and pick up a dose. 50

Lucky wasn't interested in these quick-throw places. Little pimps could operate for their two-bit sluts. Lucky was only interested in a mark who could pay big dough for a broad. If a mark couldn't pay our price, he wasn't worth the effort to have him taken care of. That kind of cheap money puts rackets in the red.

The cab drivers were our pimps. They knew where to take the customer or get him a broad. They got paid and got protection from us. Our cut was right down the line. 

We got it back from the hotel room and the dame. 

The mark is put in touch with a cabbie and he takes the sucker to a middle-class hotel. He gets his twenty and the john doesn't have to register. But the room costs another twenty, and if booze is wanted the fee goes high. 

The hotel is allowed to keep half and a muscleman picks up the difference.

At least we gave the john a good-looking head. Nothing cheap about the looks of the broads. The guy could get whatever he wanted and anything went. The mark was paying for what he wanted and service was our middle name. There was one fat three-hundred-pound slob who came regular from New York. He wanted the special class treatment and he got it. A Cadillac with chauffeur met him at the train. The car would take him to a deluxe apartment fixed with all anybody could want. The broads would come in evening gowns and there would be four of them. It was like a Farouk harem. A big dinner, wine and music played by a three-piece orchestra. After the dinner he gave his instructions. He paid fifteen hundred for his big weekend, and the stupid son of a bitch never knew the broads came down from New York on the coach while he came first-class. But I guess he was contented, because his wife couldn't find out where he was. 

The closest I ever did get to this lousy part of the racket-except to direct the muscle work when a cabbie got out of line, or a fix was needed-was a time when a 51

broad I knew that gave it to me free started to make extra dough on her back. She didn't need to because I could have taken care of her bills. But we never talked money; we just did what we liked for kicks. The word got to me that her pimp took her dough and blew to Baltimore. He belted her and took off. I didn't like it and wanted some action. She was a good lay and deserved some protection.

I got one of my select eight to drive the car and we got to Baltimore quick. I called Paul and asked if he knew where this lousy pimp was hanging his hat. He said he'd call me back and I sipped a beer and waited. Five minutes later I got a call back and had the address. Racket boys have a better intelligence system than the police. 

You get your answer quick when you're on top. I was riding high, and Paul knew it was better to give me the straight play than no answer.

I told my boy to stay in the car while I went up. This was my personal deal, and I like to work a pimp over. He was lying in bed when I walked in and hit him. I took three hundred bucks from him, and a ring. The broad thought, at one time, that she was in love with this creep and had given him a ring. He cried when I kicked him in the groin. The expedition was worth it. The doll was very warm when I gave the ring back to her, and I had gotten a little extra exercise during the afternoon. It was fun. I loved it.

I put the politicians in about the same class as the pimps. They took their share of the loot, except they were a lot more greedy and thought they were important enough to get their take of the loot faster. Almost all of them were on the payroll in one way or another. 

Christmas time was envelope time for the elected officials. It would take hours to stuff the letters with the sweet money that sooner or later took care of the fixes. 

Fixes were always oral contracts, but they might just as 52

well have been written with the best mouthpiece in town going over the tiny print. Contracts in the rackets are paid off or else.

It was a nice setup. Some of the councilmen worked, so they said, for the poor people. They gave them the handouts and helped on personal problems. They must have had tin ears to listen to all the sob stories. 

As I said, Christmas was a busy envelope time. I stayed in the office late in the afternoon on Christmas

Eve. I had got the presents for the kids, but the office job came first. The letters were thick and juicy. I called one of the boys and we started out on the tour. What a helluva lot of politicians there were. One character always got five yards. He took the envelope like it smelled. And he was right. It stank plenty high but he was able to stand it. 

A good number of the officials were ready for their 

Yule letter. And as for the precincts-we almost ran out of gas taking care of those joints. The desk sergeant's hands were hot and very itchy when I'd get in his place. 

He didn't smile but I could see he was happy that the boys had remembered. How the hell could they forget?

All small bills-the proper size that couldn't be traced. 

There were not many places that couldn't be taken care of so that if a racket boy got taken by mistake some palm wasn't ready to be crossed. The county prison looked tough from the outside, but your treatment inside depended on your connections. If you were a punk with no support, then I guess you obeyed every screw's order like a good little soldier. Or else you got a night stick rapped around your head. But if you were with us, you got hotel treatment. What the hell, you should. The racket boys were paying high prices so you could live decent for your short stay.

It was pretty easy to buy a guard. Any joker that's getting a lousy thirty-seven and a half bucks a week is an easy target for fresh folding money. He's got kids and 53

likes to eat. Or else he likes something that his miserable salary won't buy in a million years.

The guards had their own little loan-shark rackets which kept them in better whisky. There were two crap games there that went on regular. The big and little game, and the screws cut in on this heavy. We never gave much of a damn what their percentage was, because the more we knew the easier it was to get them to do our work. 

A guy would go broke bucking the dice and he'd go to a guard for dough. A watch was the usual trade. One guard had three hundred watches at one time. He pawned them and got his dough easy. The guests didn't complain. They'd steal another watch when they got out.

It wasn't theirs to begin with. 

A muscleman that got stuck in there for a short time didn't worry too much. He knew that he'd be taken care of by us in a very short time. He wouldn't take a rough answer from a guard for anything. 

If a screw got feeling like he owned the joint, our boy had the right answer. He wouldn't shout and make noise. 

He would just wiggle his little finger and say, “Listen, screw, do you want your lemon beat in? You stink for my dough. Don't get red-assed and swing that stick. You got a family. Get off my back and stay off." 

The screw would know better than argue, and the muscleman wouldn't humiliate him. He would say it soft so that the punks wouldn't get ideas that the county jail was a picnic grounds. We didn't give a damn for the others. It was just that our boys had to be taken care of in the right style. 

Sometimes you have to spend a night in a cheap hotel, but if you do you get the best. That was the way it was in jail in Philly. A mistake had been made, but it was corrected, and just one or two nights were shot. And it impressed the other punks there and made them want to be part of our association. Most of them we wouldn't have, 54

for they weren't pros. The amateurs didn't have the right connections and were no good to us. We only wanted guys that knew the score. And we got the best. Hell, a rap now and then made the cops look like they were doing a fair-to-middling job. It didn't bother us. 

Between the pimps, prostitutes and politicians and all the pay-offs, we had to have a terrific bookkeeping system. 

Of course, the books were moved all the time, but it kept a big staff working on them.

We paid big sugar to keep these books in top-flight shape. The syndicate wanted to know just what their share was and we didn't plan at any time to hold out on them. They were like regular stockholders and we knew better than to hold out. They would know if we did, and why kill something that was good for all?

I guess the books were kept as well as any bank in the country. We had CPA's taking down everything. They were legit guys, but we made certain they didn't talk. 

They didn't mind the job for they got paid extra dough for their figure-adding. They were smart enough not to ask questions. We had our code that we gave them so that they couldn't identify who was who. They added the figures, made out the sheets and left. And when we found out enough about them we'd get one to work regular for us. Everyone has a weakness, and when we'd get enough on one to wreck his family life, he'd work straight for us and then no more worries.

The CPA's were a great help with the loan-shark boys. 

That was all figures plus muscle. I knew one sucker that borrowed two hundred bucks because he needed the dough in a rush. He began to miss a few payments. By the time he got through paying off the loan, he had settled nine hundred and fifty-five bucks on the shark. 

That was the way it went with loan sharks. I kept seeing that the muscle was supplied when strength was needed. And it was. A worker who can't get a bank loan. 55

A guy who works on the docks and can't get a loan easy -who has to take the rough rates that multiply the money owed very fast. And they like to see a guy miss a payment. That makes the take better and the interest gets higher.

Six bucks for five soon begins to add, and a missed payment means more interest. 

We got a lot of calls to take care of delinquent payments. My musclemen didn't mind this kind of work. 

They knew that they had to take some jerk who was slow and work him just enough to convince him, and anyone else, that paying is the best way out. 

We cut back the loan sharks, because they knew they couldn't operate unless they got our sanction. It was easy loot for us, and my strong-backs thrived on making guys pay their tab.

If you want to take advice from a guy that knows, don't go to a shark for dough. If they don't take care of you, then the muscle boys will. Their bookkeeping is good, and they don't miss on what's owed them.

VIII-SOHO 

Frankie called me in one day and said that he was going to take a personal trip. "Johnny D., while I'm gone, why don't you take a vacation? You've been working hard and ought to take it easy for a while," he said.

I told Frankie that I'd like to go along with him, but he said he didn't need anyone, so I decided to relax for a month and have some fun. I had dough and it might be a 56

pleasant change. I thought I might even take a trip some place.

The third day after he left I ran into some racket men I knew in a cabaret. They were from out of town and came over to my table. They started talking business right away. "John, we got the word you're free for a month. We're going on a little trip. How would you like to go to England with us?" "Is it official?" I asked, feeling that a trip without extra dough doesn't interest me. "Just come along with us and we'll see you get extra pay plus expenses. We might need some muscle. You'll get two bills a week. There's a guarantee of two weeks." 

I said it was okay with me and I'd be ready in a few days, and for them to let me know when they needed me. "John," one of them said. "It's not in a few days. We're going tonight. We've made arrangements for a plane.

There's a guy who makes a flight regular and he'll take us with no questions asked."

They explained to me that the plane flew over regularly and was legit. "I got no passport," I told them, and they laughed. "Let us worry about that, Johnny. Just bring a small picture of yourself with you tonight at the airport. The rest will be taken care of then."

The idea sounded fine with me and I left for home to get some clothes. I had an old fight picture that would do the trick. I didn't bother to tell my wife where I was going. I just told her I'd be away for a short time and not to worry. I gave her a few bills for groceries and left. 

I don't know much about planes except this was a big one. The boys met me at the airport, and in a few minutes we were on our way.

It wasn't a bad flight, but they didn't bother to tell me the reason for the flight. I knew when you're hired for 57

muscle you don't ask questions. They drank highballs on the way over, but I didn't touch anything. They kept me out of the conversation. I didn't care. They were footing the bill.

I don't know where we landed. We just got off the plane and the pilot took off in a hurry. There was a car to meet us. The field we landed on was a great big pasture. We climbed a fence and got in the car. I had my passport, but there wasn't anyone to ask for it. The car drove us to London and we checked into a hotel. 

They went about their business and told me I might be needed later. I went into a pub. The place looked funny to me. There was a globe light at each end of the bar. I had to tell them I wanted a cold beer. I had a warm one first and I almost threw up. I still don't know how those 

Limeys can like warm beer.

We stayed right in the Soho district, and I found out that English musclemen are as tough as they come. In fact, an American muscleman would get taken by them the first time unless he knew what to expect. I didn't know that until I saw one of them in action.

We were there for only six or seven days. I got a big kick out of hearing the king's English spoke. One night the four of us were standing at a bar when three guys came in. Two of them were wearing homburgs, and one looked like a movie version of a cockney thug. 

He was what you might call my opposite number. I wasn't even introduced to the two others. They just went to a table and started talking. 

One of the Englishmen said to my side, "Leave your man with ours," and we were practically dismissed.

The two of us sat at the bar. I was drinking beer out of a pewter mug and he was drinking hot buttered rum. We got friendly, but we both knew that we were to watch out for our teams. 58

"My boss is a big man in the Soho district. You got to be a big strong-arm to work for him," he said.

I did a slow burn and told him that he would have to go to school to be in my league.

He didn't like the tone of my remarks and told me so. "I'm under orders not to fight,” he said. "Look out, creep. Forget your orders," I said, hoping for a little action.

Then one of the fellows in homburg hats tapped him on the shoulder. "Georgie boy. Forget it. And keep your mind on business." 

Every night, for four nights, we met at the same place and sat at the bar while they talked. After the meeting was over I suggested that we get some broads, and

There was plenty of booze put in the suites to cater to any special taste that any of them wanted. Nothing was spared to make their stay lavish. Broads were contacted and told to be available at all times. They were the best in the city and could charge from a hundred a night up. 

We could have them work for free, but we gave them money anyway.

Every guest that came to Philly with his crew was met by a representative of the hosting mob at the train. The delegate stayed with this assignment throughout the meeting and picked up the tab at every place. Two or three cars would meet each train, and take the boys to their hotels.

Among those who attended this meeting were such top racket men as Joe Adonis, Frank Erickson, Lucky 

Luciano, Caropi the Enforcer, Bugsy Siegel, "Rebel" 

Natone, and Tommy "the Bull."

Lucky Luciano had with him one of the toughest musclemen that I ever seen, a guy called Harpy. I guess he got that name from his mick kisser. Lucky was very quiet and didn't say much.

The first couple of nights were purely social. The boys were established in different hotels, but each group was told where the others were and what names to ask for in case they wanted to get together. 78

We had already taken the necessary steps to visit some of the police precinct captains and drop an envelope with them, so that they wouldn't get curious about what they might hear.

After we met them at the train and took them to their hotels, the first thing they all seemed to want to do was take a shower. Getting clean seemed a fetish with them. 

Barbers would come up to their rooms and give them the works. And manicurists were on tap. All of them liked these assignments because the tips were extra good. The visitors wanted to show the Philly boys they had class. 

All of them liked sweet-smelling soap except Joe 

Adonis. He settled on plain Ivory soap.

I guess they tried to keep clean because they had been dirty kids and when they grew up they were still in dirty business.

Our boys were with them to make sure they didn't get close to picking up any bill. I had a roll of bills in my pocket to pay the cabaret bills and any girls they wanted. 

And they didn't pass these lovelies up. They were the best and knew they had to do a more than satisfactory job.

It was the same when we went out one time to Los 

Angeles and Bugsy Siegel hosted. He had a lavish setup and there were plenty of movie starlets cutting out for the boys.

No one left these social parties early. We'd go from one cabaret to another and the bills ran high. The tips were big. I had to mingle with the musclemen. Joe Adonis' boy was lousy with his fists, but he was great with a gun. 

There were no guns allowed to be brought to Philly. 

That was a standard rule for all the meets in any city. 

Our musclemen frisked the strong-arm boys, and as an act of courtesy each visitor had the right to frisk a local to see if he was carrying a piece. No one wanted to have any trouble during a business meeting. The newspapers 79

would have had a field day if somebody got shot and they learned who really was in town staying at the swanky hotels. 

Frank Erickson was the quietest guy at the meeting. Joe

Adonis wanted to be sure that his voice was heard at all times. He made his presence known.

Adonis and I got along fine. He was the top guy in 

Murder Inc. in New York. He is a real man. He had a fetish about keeping his family away from the racket mob. 

Very few of the boys ever went to his home at Fort Lee, New Jersey. I went there several times. When I'd get set to go he'd slip me five or six yards to buy a couple of suits. What a guy with a buck! Joe thought nothing of giving a cabbie a twenty-dollar tip for a ninety-cent ride.

I knew he always carried a big roll with him, and I asked him one time why, so many times, he didn't have his muscleman with him. "John, I sometimes hope a punk tries to take me," he said. "I'll convince him of the error of his ways. I would personally cripple him for life." "And, Joe, suppose I was the guy?” I asked. "Well, John, I've thought of that," he said. "I'd have to kill you." 

And I knew very well that Joe A. meant that seriously.

He wasn't the type to chicken out and crack up before any committee like some of the other guys did.

There was only one gal here for the meeting. That gal was Virginia Hwho gave Joe A. his last name.

It really is Doto, but one time in Las Vegas she saw him come in a cabaret and said: "Man, who is that Adonis?" and that stuck with him. 

I guess he liked it, and her.

Virginia was no gun moll who followed a racket man round to keep him happy in bed. In the first place, out- 80

of-town broads were barred at these meetings. We provided new talent.

But this broad had brains. She was one of the top syndicate, and her specialty was prostitution. She was as tough as any racket man in the country. She could swear with the best, and she invented a few choice expressions of her own. These can't be printed.

I've seen her in action, and she took no back talk from anyone. Virginia didn't slap anyone like the average broad does. She'd just haul off and belt like a man. 

She never sang to the law-ever. And the proof of it is that she was well taken care of in later years. She was smart, and the other top boys respected her opinions. 

After the social evenings were over the racket boys would settle down for their business sessions. These lasted two days. But they weren't eight-hour meetings. The guys wouldn't stand for any long work day like an office worker. The meetings were just for four hours, and then it would be over.

I wasn't allowed to sit in on these meetings, but I pretty much knew what was going on. I could hear from the outside while I watched that no one interfered or disturbed them.

Some of them would take notes while others would memorize what was going on. Territories would be discussed and adjustments made. The top boys didn't like any guy in a certain area branching out into new sections unless he had gotten the approval at a regular meeting like this. 

Or maybe some guy was getting out of line. Instructions would be sent to him to get back in line or else lose out. And they also wanted to be sure that the take from the rackets was going to the top and not sliced or cut down.

The special business meetings were usually held in one of the local's home, for protection purposes. With so 81

many top men meeting, the idea was to keep it as secret as possible. They all would sit around in easy chairs, and there was plenty of booze or snacks for all. 

After the final session the Philly hosts would throw a final big party. And, man, they pulled out all the stops. 

There was nothing missing, and it wouldn't break up until early in the morning. I would get pretty tired around twothirty, but I had to stay until all the guests went to their suites. I didn't drink, especially at a meet like this, because as top muscleman I had to see that nothing went wrong.

When the meeting was over the guests would leave in the same way that they came. Our boys would check them out of the hotel, pay their bills and escort them to the train or airport. They charged everything, including new suits. We didn't care, because they extended us the same courtesies when we came to their town. 

This wasn't the biggest business meeting that was held, but it did have the top racket men who controlled the crime syndicates throughout the country. 

I was at one once in Atlantic City where there were about three hundred and fifty attending. This one lasted for a week, and for doing practically nothing I got twentyseven hundred bucks in tips from the out-of-towners. 

Hell, I wouldn't mind that kind of work steady. 

And just because the meetings I was at were held many years ago, don't think for a second the pattern has diedout or even changed much. It hasn't, and I personally think it will continue for a long time. Why? There isn't the slightest evidence to show that the crime syndicate boys have had to cut down their operations. 

And some of the names are still around, and in their trade they are big.

There was, for example, a secret meeting held in a plush New York hotel in December 1956. This was an extra special meeting to decide Frank Costello's posi- 82

tion in the national syndicate. There weren't lavish parties for this meet. This was a quick one. There was no telephoning or writing letters to invite the racket men. 

Telephoning is too dangerous with wire tapping going on. Today, as for this meeting, there is one man who goes from city to city personally to give the details to those asked to attend. For the December meeting he flew to Detroit, Los Angeles, New Orleans, Kansas City and a few other cities to invite the boys.

There were no guns at this meet, and the instructions were to operate in the greatest secrecy. The boys were to arrive at the hotel at a scheduled time and go directly to the twenty-third floor. A man who gives his trade as furrier lives there, but you'll never find him at his office. He hasn't got a real, legit job.

The meet lasted just four hours, and there were separate rooms for small groups to discuss their particular problems. But at the conclusion of the meeting a sumptuous buffet was served and booze flowed. Of course, there were no broads asked to attend because none of the boys wanted reports to leak out. Costello was in jail at the time on a federal income tax rap. 

Even though some of them got a little loaded during the social part of the meeting, they didn't forget secrecy.They left in pairs and went back to their respective territories. 

I'm sure at this writing that a lot more business meetings have been held. They have their regional problems like any big legit business, but their franchises are given out and controlled differently. Guns and muscles are their proxies. I don't think the years have made many changes, except maybe that experience has made them a little smarter. 83

XII-LITTLE AND BIG BETS 

A lot of people are under the impression the guy they bet with on the corner is a bookie. He is not. The guy that takes your deuce on that sure-shot in the third race is just a runner. In Trenton, for example, you make the bet with the runner on horses, numbers or a fight. That city, when I was operating there, was divided into thirty different sections. From the section you bet, all the money is collected and goes to the real bookmakers. 

If there is a hit, the guy that picked up the money and slips comes back to the runner and you get paid off. The bookmaker keeps 30 per cent of the take. Half of the state of New Jersey-from New Brunswick south-paid off to the Philly mob. The rest of the state paid off to 

New York through collectors in Newark.

Every two days a collector would leave Philly and make a tour of the territory and take back 70 per cent of the total. They had it worked out pretty accurately, and could tell if a section was below average. If a section was short, the guy in charge would get a shellacking. It didn't happen very often, because the word was around not to be light when the Philly collector came.

I did the collecting for a while, and found it interesting at first. I sort of liked driving to Mapleshade, New Brunswick and Atlantic City. There were extra expenses. But 

I got sick of it. It was too routine. 

The take from Trenton averaged between eighteen and nineteen thousand every two days. This was considerably less than Camden, where the total hit close to forty thousand. One of the reasons for the low take in Trenton 84

was that there were a lot of outlaw books in that city. 

An outlaw book was one not controlled by the syndicate. 

That book would have ten or fifteen steady players and they were small bettors in the three- and five-dollar class.

The syndicate would let them operate up to a time when they'd get ambitious and enlarge. As soon as they got too big, the boys would move in and demand the proper cut. 

Most of the outlaws would fold, since they were afraid they couldn't make the quota.

There were some pretty big dice and card games held in Roebling and Bordentown with a guy called Martinelli running them under Philly supervision. Cars would pick up the good customers at no charge. They were nice cars, not big and black like the movies. They were ordinary colors so as not to attract attention, and the plates used were legitimate. For a reason I never found out, there was a ten-dollar per person charge one way to Bordentown. 

One time there was a jerk called Brennan who was stationed in the Army at Fort Dix. He stuck up the game in Bordentown and got twenty grand. And then he took the boys at Roebling. They let him walk out of each place without a fight. They didn't want any trouble and newspaper headlines. But Brennan made a big mistake. He went to Philly for a weekend, and he was tailed. He shacked up with some broad, and when he came out somebody machine-gunned him. The two gambling spots were closed down, and still are, because this made a big stink. 

Mapleshade was raided by the attorney general in 1948, and it made headlines. Two guys were thrown to the wolves and took the rap for the rest. They were Wop 

Mingora and Frank DiLuca. Wop was the smart guy and

DiLuca was the muscle. He was a crude, vulgar, common slob. He's a sworn enemy of mine. They both got two to three years and went to the honor farm at Leesburg.

The farm got the nickname. "The Bookmakers' Country 

85

Club," because the joint was lousy with them. They were supposed to work, but they usually managed to buy their way out of such problems. 

For taking the fall for the rest of the boys, Wop got five hundred a week and a bonus of twenty-five grand when he got out. DiLuca was not as big a man in importance, so he was cut to two yards a week and ten G's at the finish. For a while, he didn't know that Wop was getting a bigger reward. He found out somehow and went to Wop. "I don't like the way I'm being treated, Wop,” he said. "I want a cut as big as you. If I don't, then I'll sing." 

That's where the DiLuca was wrong. You don't threaten the mob and get away with it. DiLuca had a visit a few days later from three of the toughest guys in Philly. They talked to him in the visitors' room. I don't know what they said to him, but when he came back he was white as a sheet. He drank a pint of whiskey to settle himself down.

He got hold of Wop and pleaded with him. "Help me, Wop. Can you square it for me? I don't want no trouble," he said.

Wop took him at his word and told the Philly boys that DiLuca had learned a lesson and would behave. A year after he got out Wop was picked up with policy slips on him and fined ten grand. DiLuca paid it in ten one-thou-¨ sand-dollar bills and drove off in his Caddy. I guess he was damn glad that Wop had put in a word for him. 

One of the "class" gambling joints was the Palace in Newark. The trade came mostly from New York. Limousines in the vicinity of Central Park West would pick up the players and take them over the George Washington Bridge.

The Palace had everything from dice to roulette, and they were ready and able to handle big bets. They didn't like the penny-ante player. A guy would have to have a couple of G's in his poke before they were interested. The boys wanted players who liked fast action. A watcher 86

never was allowed to come back again, even though the friend who brought him bet heavy.

I saw Frank Costello try and buck the dice table one night and lose a quick twenty grand.

The Palace was opened up only three times a week. 

Man, was the money spent when it was open! Customers got all the food and drinks on the house. And it was the best food around. The chef, Felix, was paid four hundred and fifty bucks a week. He was terrific. He used to get tips as high as a hundred bucks when someone especially liked the dinner. I guess he averaged close to two grand a week. He came to work in a big Caddy. 

Dinner was served around nine o'clock and the games got underway about eleven. You could get anything you wanted in the way of food. If your girl suddenly decided she wanted baked Alaska, she got it. 

One time two guys came up from New Orleans for a night's play. They had credentials and were dressed nicely, They were à couple of professional gamblers and worked as a team. One of them got caught rubbing the dice, and said he was rubbing them for luck. The house man grabbed the cubes and looked them over. They had been exchanged for a loaded pair. It was all done very quietly from then on. The house man gave a signal and four or five guys were next to them quick. None of the customers knew what was happening as they escorted the New Orleans pair out of the place. They took a nice ride to the Jersey Meadows and beat them up within an inch of their lives. They were told if they were seen close to the place again it would be permanent for them. They never came back. 

No smart guy tried to take the boys at the Palace. 

This was too profitable a joint to let anyone move in. One thing I'll say about the syndicate boys. They were honest.

The way they felt was, for every sucker that wins there are fifty chumps who'll lose. The house percentage was 87

good enough for them. They took in as much as a hundred and fifty thousand dollars profit a night.

One mark, a society guy, started to buck the dice pretty good one night. He won sixty-five grand, and there were a lot of customers waiting to see if he got paid off. There. wasn't a question. To make sure that he wouldn't have any trouble, he was assigned to bodyguards that escorted him right to his doorstep in New York. They would have killed in a minute if anyone had tried to take him. The place was afraid the word might have gotten out that he'd won a lot of loot and the Palace's rep would be hurt. 

When the place wasn't open there never was much money left. Just enough to pay the bills. Tony Cowuna, a fifth-rate hood from Newark, got ideas to take the place one weekend when it was closed. There was about ten grand in the joint. He got caught by the law and served time. The boss gave instructions to take him on sight when he got out.

No smart lad was going to try and take the place when it was open. There were at least one hundred strong-arms ready to stop any moves. And there were plenty of offduty cops doing odd jobs or chauffeuring cars. Police protection averaged around eight or nine grand a month. 

The only time you might say the joint was run a little crooked was when city and county officials came in to play. Somehow they were very lucky at cards. I know many a dealer in blackjack would throw in a good hand just to let the special guests win. The house man sure could play badly when a politician was being given a little present. These guests were told not to press their luck too far, This was considered good public relations. 

The overhead to run the joint was big and everything was run first-class, but the take made everybody happy. 

The customers tossed their dough around and the syndicate was glad they came.

Each section that had gambling had its own treasury, 88

and if another section needed money in a rush they would take care of that area. They all kept the best books in the world. But unlike a legit business, they moved them around from place to place. I transferred the books to 

Philly at least three times a month. They were dynamite. 

I was often tempted to steal them and start a nice little shakedown. But I knew that within forty-eight hours Mr. 

Matthews would be getting flowers at a nice parlor. I didn't want that kind of action.

When I was collecting or moving the books I always made it a point to look and act as if I was a small-timer. 

I did it successfully. The cops didn't think I was important enough to give them trouble.

Today there is very little high-class gambling like the Palace. The boys have found catering to that kind of action is too obvious. But the big-time gambling has moved along even bigger than before. Policy games in 

New York run into big money. Read your local papers closely and see how many times there are raids by police on policy banks. And read the official figures given out. 

In one place up in East Harlem the police estimated the section did an eighteen-million-dollar-a-year play. New 

York is cut up into territories, but the money still goes to the syndicate.

Only the small fry seem to get caught. The big boys don't hang around the street corner like the runners and watchouts do. They sit back at their own headquarters and wait for the money to come in. If it is below the average, they know where to send their musclemen. One big policy guy, Mike Carlone spends most of his time directing the play from his luxury home in Miami. But he knows what is going on every single day and can tell almost down to the penny what the receipts should be. 

The racket boys have got an interest in almost everything where a bet can be made Most of the sports interest 89

them-especially basketball. That's the easiest game for putting in a fix, except for fights.

The betting on basketball is heavy, and it's not too hard to reach a player. I don't mean the pros. I mean the college boy who finds a grand very easy to take. A thousand bucks looks good to him when he wants to get extra dough. He doesn't have to lose the game; he just has to make sure that his team wins by enough points. As long as his team makes it close the gamblers can win because they'll bet the team against him so their score plus a certain number of points wins. 

With baseball, the boys can't make a fix with the players, so every now and then they do it more subtly. 

They study the records very close, and when an important betting game is up they'll hire a broad to go out with a pitcher. If she can get him tired, and he isn't up to snuff, then they got an edge on the bettors. A little house percentage never hurts.

The fight game is where they exercise strict control. 

The big guys know who is going to win eight out of ten fights. The other two they lay off the heavy bets and take a chance. They can't control championship fights, except for a few. But a fighter has to play ball with them to get a shot at the title.

Almost every fighter who gets to the top has to get a manager who sits in solid with the boys. And to sit in solid, a manager had better know Frankie George and 

Mickey Salerno in Philly. 

If a young fighter looks like he's got promise, they take him on a scheduled buildup. The kid will get plenty of fights, and may not even know they're fixed. When he's got enough experience, then a spoiler is brought against him. A spoiler is a pretty tough guy who knows all the tricks. He's a test. If the kid gets by him, then he'll move fast. If he doesn't, then his fight career is over as far as the racket boys are concerned. 90

Take a fighter like Archie Moore. There was a guy who tried for years to get a shot at the title. For ten years he was the real top light heavy, but he didn't play ball. Finally, he got too good, and they couldn't stop him from getting a chance. There are few exceptions like Moore.

I guess the reason the boys can't get to fix the horse races is that the owners can't be reached. I don't mean that every now and then it isn't done, but it's very rare. 

I used to beat the bookies once by a radio system. There was a guy at the track with a transmitter, and he'd give me the result. I'd telephone a bookie fast and place a bet after I had the winner. This had to be quick, and I made a few bucks. But a book gets suspicious when you're too close to the deadline too often. 

I wouldn't bet on the fights myself unless I had the right word. Why guess which way the mob wants to have a winner? I'll still go into a gambling joint and bet my hand against the house, because they keep these honest. They don't have to be crooked. The percentage is too good for the boys in charge. The suckers don't seem to think so, but I do. I ought to know. I've been with them long enough.

XIII-FIFTY GRAND MISSING 

Frankie Meyer was sore when he called me to a meeting. I didn't know what it was about but the way he talked made me get to his home quick. He had a set of books on the table when we got there. There was just myself and another guy. Frankie did the talking and didn't waste time with small talk. 91

"Johnny, we got a holdout in Erie. Chubby Malone is behind fifty grand for the last three months. The books have been gone over good and he's short. I'm sick and tired of his excuses. You go up there tonight and do a good job. I mean good. That cheap bastard has held out for the last time. You know what to do. And don't hang around. Do it fast." 

Frankie didn't bother to show me the books. I didn't care what they showed. We had a holdout for fifty G's and that was enough. Before we left, Frankie told me that a car would meet our plane outside Erie, and for my group to be at Media airport late that night. "Johnny D.," he said as I left, "make sure he never holds out again. I don't like it.”

I laughed and told him not to worry anymore-that a proper job would be done on Chubby. This was going to be a personal job and the idea gave me a kick. I hadn't worked on a guy for quite a while, and this was going to be fun. I went to my office and called in three of my select eight. I gave them instructions and ordered them not to tell anyone where they were going or what they were going to do. They were to stay apart for the rest of the day and meet exactly at nine o'clock in front of 

Harry's. All three were to carry pieces in case the action got rough.

I went down to the gym and took a light workout and a turkish bath. I felt great, and had a little trouble keeping from getting too excited. A good going-over always made me feel good, and this was going to be a good one. 

A sedan with phony plates picked me up around eightthirty and I met the boys right on schedule. We drove for over an hour to make sure there was no tail on the car. 

Before we headed for the airport I called Frankie for any last-minute instructions. There weren't any, and we drove to the airport.

The plane we had was meant for three passengers, so 92

one of the boys had to lie on the floor, and he had the worst ride. He could barely move by the time we got to 

Erie. The pilot was a legit guy who never asked questions. 

He liked the extra dough and was smart enough not to pay attention or repeat conversation. 

It was cold as hell out that night, and we almost froze to death in the plane. You couldn't sweat if you wanted to. It wasn't the first time the pilot had made a night trip to Erie, and he knew how to get to our special landing place. One of the racket boys there had a dairy farm where he cooked mash. It was about twelve miles out of town, and he was able to make plenty of illegal booze there. Just as we got there he lit some special flares, and our pilot set the plane down as neat as you could want. 

Before we could get back to the farm buildings, the flares were out. The guy that ran the still had a car ready for us, and a driver. The pilot was told to wait and be ready for a quick departure.

The driver knew exactly where to take us, and we got to the night club in no time at all. This was a classy joint which stayed open until seven in the morning. Each customer, because it was an after-hours club, had to pay an extra five bucks. And everything else that they got there cost them plenty. There were three floors in the joint, including the basement, with high-class entertainment. 

The doorman seemed to be leery when we got there so we took him in with us. I didn't want him to give a signal and let the word get to Chubby that he was having visitors. I wanted the odds strictly on my side. Why not? The owner of the joint spotted me from the bar and came right over. I spoke to him quick, "Butch, you just keep very quiet and don't say nothing.

You're clean, but we got a little business to talk to Chubby about. Get him and tell him to come here."

Butch didn't say yes or no, but just took the order and went to the back of the place. He came back with Chubby, 93

who didn't look too happy. We walked him right out of the place and into the car. One of the boys took the doorman with us. He looked fresh and needed a bouncing. 

About seven and a half miles out of Erie we came to a dirt road and stopped the car. We took the doorman and gave him a fast belting. Just enough to let him know it would be better not to talk. He lay in the dirt as we drove off. 

Chubby wasn't saying a word. He looked like he was going to throw up. We didn't ask him any questions. We drove for another half-hour until we got to a real lonely place. The driver kept the motor idling and we got out. 

Chubby started to plead. "Listen, Johnny. You got to listen. I didn't mean to hold out for long. I was going to make it up. I just got short. Please, let me alone. I swear it won't happen again. 

Don't take me!"

He was almost screaming so I hit him hard in the kisser and he blubbered. "Johnny, I got fifty-two hundred bucks in my pocket. I guarantee to get the rest up in a week. Take this, but don't work me," he yelped.

I took the dough from his pocket and told him it would help pay for our trip. I nodded to the boys and they spread-eagled him over the hood. I worked him over from head to toe until my arms wore out. We threw him in the ditch and gave him an extra kick for good luck. Chubby was hurt bad, and he wasn't going to be short a nickel from then on. He knew the routine. A beating first, and a trial period. Then the gun would be sent up and no more Chubby.

The dairy farm was on the other side of town so it took us a little while to get back there. We had time to take a couple of drinks and relax before the pilot was ready.

He couldn't warm up the plane until we were ready to go, because we didn't want too much noise. We didn't drink 94

the home-made booze. That was for customers, and why get cut stuff? I split the dough I had taken with my boys but managed to keep the biggest part for myself. They didn't get sore. The top man always gets the biggest cut. 

It was found money and Frankie wouldn't care. 

I thought that everything had gone great. Chubby had had a class beating, and from now on he'd see to it that his bookmaking dough came back to us with no delays. 

And the word would go around to the other cities that it wasn't a good idea to be short with the sugar. 

I was never so wrong in my life. I was right only about 

Chubby and the other cities. I forgot about the doorman.

He must have been a new son of a bitch that was stupid enough to talk.

It was just my luck that a truck driver spotted him. lying beside the road and picked him up. The driver, not knowing better, went right to a police station, and before the word could get to the precinct the doorman spilled his miserable guts. Somehow, and I never did find out, he knew the head gut that pulled this caper. He knew me from some place and talked. I heard he was found in the river a few days later, but it was too late. 

The plane trip back wasn't bad. It was a little misty and still cold as hell, but we all felt good. It had been a good job, and I couldn't wait to get Frankie on the phone and give him the report. The plane didn't have much in the way of instruments; but our pilot got us in okay, and we headed back to town for a good dinner and some broads. 

As soon as we got to one of the all-night joints and ordered some steaks, I called Frankie. I started to tell him how we carried out the job to the letter. I never got to tell him. He spoke quick and fast. "Johnny, you were made. Something went wrong. The doorman at Butch's joint told the cops. You're hot and you got to blow this town fast." 

I felt like some one had kicked me in the groin, and the 95

sweat began to trickle down my face. All of a sudden I felt tired. "Frankie, don't worry. It will blow over. I'll go back."

He cut me off. Frankie was sore and he was the boss. "You're not going any place. I got the word that you're hot. This is going to take time to square and I don't want you around here. We'll take care of you. This won't last too long."

Frankie told me that I was the only one made and the other boys were safe to stay around, but they were to keep quiet and not tell anybody where they had been. He hung up after telling me that dough would be sent to me regular.

boy would call me for an appointment. Then a deal would be made and I'd be back as good as when I was in Philly. 

I knew better than to call Pete. In this business you wait for the call. You don't pester, because it makes you look small, and a muscleman can't afford to look small. 

Sylvia didn't seem to mind that I apparently wasn't doing anything. She was a very trustworthy broad. I put my money in a drawer in her bedroom, and I'd notice from time to time that she'd slip a few bills in it. I guess I got about twenty-five hundred from her. And she was paying for the groceries when we went out. 

One morning in bed she spoke seriously to me. "John, I've got a funny feeling that it's dangerous for you around here. Let's go some place. I'll foot the bill."

For a moment I thought she was politely telling me that I was through and that she had found another bedmate. 

I got sore. "Listen, Sylvia," I said. "You don't have to beat around the bush. If you want me to pack my bags and go, just say the word." 

"Johnny, darling," she said, giving me a big kiss, “I want you. Just call it a woman's intuition. I think something is going to happen."

I told her she was crazy and got up and went out. I don't known why, but I began to feel edgy. I hopped a cab and went to a gin mill near Twenty-eighth and Second Avenue. It was a cheap little bar where a lot of longshoremen hung out. The bookies and loan sharks were there, of course, to service their needs.

Most of the bookies were small punks, but at times a guy like Harry Gross would come in for a few minutes. 

He was pretty big, and at one time he had had a lot of cops and big police brass on his payroll. But he got caught like they all do, and did a bit.

I walked in and sat near the end of the bar a few seats away from a bookie. The boys called him "Clothes," be- 114

cause he liked to dress sharp and loud. He especially liked a white tie and a dark blue shirt and a stickpin that had a real mother of pearl like a walnut. I was sipping my beer and he started a conversation. His bodyguard was at the other end of the bar. Clothes had a strong-arm, because he carried plenty of dough with him and was known to pay off quick if you had a hit. "Well, well, the big wheel has decided to pay us a visit! 

And to what do we owe this honor?" he laughs. "Look, I'm in no mood for your crap. Drink your drink and I'll take care of mine," I answered fast.

Clothes looked around and spoke to the bartender. "Isn't the big man getting touchy? I think if the big man can't take it, he ought to blow. He thinks he's too good for us."

The other customers stopped talking, for they smelled a fight coming up. I didn't disappoint them. I had Clothes on his ass so quick he didn't know what happened. I gave him an extra kick in the ribs and felt better. His bodyguard saw what happened and ran over. I didn't know if he carried a piece so I grabbed a beer bottle and bounced if off his temple. He staggered and I hit him two times more. I kicked him in the shins. I was so hot I wanted to kill. I never felt so vicious before. I grabbed a bar stool and was about to clobber him when a couple of guys stopped me. They didn't want to see murder. 

Clothes was crumpled up and moaning. His minorleague muscleman was very quietly asleep. I went back to the bar and ordered two beers. I drank one and walked to Clothes and poured it over his nice white tie and left. 

Even the fight didn't make me feel better and I took a cab up to a West Forty-second Street, and hit a bar. I was there about a half hour when a guy sits down next to me and talks to me out of the side of his mouth. "John, I want to see you." 115

I told him that I was there and we didn't have to go any place. "Don't get sore, John. Certain guys are asking for you." "What certain guys?" I asked. Maybe this was the word from Pete. "The certain guys are the law. I got the wire they indicted you in Jersey and the feds are after you."

I slipped him a deuce and left. I knew this punk would sell anyone out for a dollar. I took a cab to Nesserole

Street. Halfway there I told the driver to stop. I called 

Sylvia and told her to pack a couple of bags for me. She had the bags packed by the time I got there. 

Sylvia was upset when she saw me. I was getting bull fever again. "Johnny, don't leave by the regular ways. I'll drive you any place you want.”

We got in her car and drove up to the George Washington Bridge and made a stop near Hoboken. I called the 

Trenton police and asked them if they were looking for Johnny Matthews. They were, and I hung up. In Jersey I felt even more ill at ease. We got to Elizabeth, and I made another call. This time it was to the FBI agent at the post office in Newark. I said I had a tip on Johnny Matthews and did they want him? The answer was yes and I asked the charge. "Matthews jumped the state line to avoid lawful prosecution."

I hung up and ran back to the car. I was hot, and I didn't like it one bit. For some reason I wanted to go to 

Trenton. I thought maybe they wouldn't be looking for me there and maybe I could put in a fix. Sylvia talked me out of it. We drove to Philly by every back road there was. She stayed with me for three days and then went back to New York. I didn't even bother to call any of the boys. I told Sylvia that I'd be back with her, but that I wouldn't write or telephone. 116

I went to Richmond, Virginia, by train and took a cab to a motel setup about ten miles outside the city. It was a pretty nice place with a good bar. I had two rooms and stayed there for about ten days. I left in a hurry when I got into a fight with a rebel in the bar. This guy got smart and I busted him in the nose and he beat it. I had tipped the waiters good, so when I went to get my bags there was a knock on the door. One of the waiters I had taken care of big spoke to me. "You know that guy you belted went to town to get the law. I figure you as a guy that doesn't want to talk to the law. I got a car here. I'll drive you where you want to go." 

I took his offer and he drove me to Martinsburg, West

Virginia. I gave him half a yard and he left. I went to a bar to make plans. I wasn't in the joint for fifteen minutes when a guy who used to work for me in Philly spotted me. He came over to my table. He was a right guy. "What the hell are you doing here, Johnny?" he asked. "You're a hot potato."

I was shook up and couldn't believe him. How the hell could I be hot? I asked him. "Johnny, I dunno, but you are. The word seems to be that you're in this territory. What the hell, I was surprised to see you. Look, you better move. You did me a lot of favors once and I know what it's like to be hot, so I'll drive you any place you want."

He took me to Newcastle, Delaware, and I checked in a joint there for four days. I thought this was okay and no one would bother me. I was wrong again. An oldtime strong-arm guy called Zabap gave me a warning. "Juan, I wouldn't stay this close to Jersey. You're wanted bad and the word will get around soon that you're here. You better move." 99 I thanked Zabap and took a bus to New York. I slipped the driver a fin and had him drop me off before we got to the regular stop. I scooted across the street and hopped 117

a cab and went to the Bronx. There was a rooming house up there that I knew was safe. I stayed there for over two months and saw a lot of guys on the lam in the area. We didn't mix for we all knew we were hot. Finally I got bored with the place and went to Brooklyn. There was a bar there close to Nesserole Street and I knew the owner. 

I put my bags behind the bar and got him to go over to Sylvia's place. He told her where I was and to bring her car. She did and I got in. She had a sister who lived with her husband in the Greenpoint section. We stayed there for a few days and then we went back to her apartment.

We had to stay indoors all day and then at night we'd go out some place. Early one evening when she came back from a store she told me that she thought she saw some detectives near the place and that I'd better leave. "Johnny, don't go by the front door. Go up on the roof and to the end of the building. Then you can get down in the back and no one will see you," she said.

I thought she was nuts and went down to the front. I looked out the glass doors and saw two plainclothesmen standing across the street. I didn't waste any time and went out the back way and down an alley. I was lucky, for there was a cruising cab and I got it without having to wait. I went to Eighth Avenue and Fortieth Street and changed cabs and went to Hoboken. I decided that night to go to Hamilton Township, which is close to Trenton and to find out myself what was what. In the morning I went to New Brunswick and called a pal of mine in

Trenton. 

He had a little house on the outskirts and when I told him I needed a place to hole up in he gave me the word to come right down. It was evening by the time I got there. I told him that I'd have to stay there a few days. I gave him enough dough to keep him happy and not ask too many questions. I thought it was a pretty safe area, 118

but some lousy cops have too damn good memories. One night a few blocks away from his place a cop spotted me and told me to stop. I ran like a bastard. He fired two shots over my head and the third at me. That shot skimmed my hip. It bled like a son of a bitch but I got away. I was lucky that the slug didn't stick in me. I knew now that the heat would be on heavy and that I would have to move quick.

I left where I was staying and went to a gin mill on Broad Street. I felt very edgy. I saw a guy who was pretty loaded come in with a girl. From where I sat I could see they had a Buick. An old one, but it ran. I walked out and stole the car and headed for New York. Then I changed my mind, because I was afraid that the gal might find out and the cops would throw a roadblock.

I drove to Trenton and parked the car a few blocks away from a saloon right across from City Hall. I went in and the owner, Mike, saw me. "You're crazy to come in here, Johnny. The heat is on you good," he warned me.

I took his advice and bought a beer. I stood close to the door for there wasn't any back way out of this joint. 

I saw two police cars pull up in front. There was a detective across the street. I moved in back of the juke box and waited. One of the detectives was just about my size and wearing a hat almost like mine. They opened the door and walked in. I slid down from behind the juke box and walked out straight to a car. I guess the cop thought 

I was one of the dicks, because they didn't say a word. 

The keys were in it and I drove off. 

I got about a block away when they woke up. I jazzed the car and they started chasing. The sirens sounded loud as hell, but it was no contest. I knew how to drive in 

Trenton and got away. I drove to Hightstown and ditched the car and took a cab back to Trenton. I went to where 119

I had left the Buick and it was still there. Luck was going my way.

There wasn't any choice but to use it and hope that an alarm wasn't out for it. A police car drove by me while I was parked and didn't pay any attention. I followed the car for a couple of blocks and then turned for the River 

Road. 

I hoped the cops would soon get word on the car I left in Hightstown, because that would throw them off. They'd think I was holed up there.

I was getting tired, but I knew that no matter what, I had to get back to New York that night. I could lose the 

Buick in the city, and myself too.

It seemed a helluva long trip but I finally got over the George Washington Bridge. It felt good to be back, even though I didn't know exactly where I was going to stay. 

I didn't care, because I had fooled the cops again.

I headed for the Lower East Side of Manhattan and made a mistake. I'll never know how long I could have fooled the bulls. I might still be on the lam if I hadn't been tired and gone down a one-way street. It was close to daybreak and there wasn't any traffic. I got almost to the end of the block when a police car blocked the way. I was caught and I knew it. I grabbed my money and stuck all but forty bucks in my shoe. Cops have a way of frisking you and taking the loose money.

I just sat and waited and I didn't have to wait long. The cops had guns in their hands and I didn't want to be a target. 

I listened while they talked. "Let's see your license, buddy. Have you got one?"

I shook my head and they made me step out and lean across the hood of the car. One kept his gun in my back and the other went over me quick. I'm glad I had stashed my dough in my shoes. There wasn't much left of the forty when they got through. 120

They handcuffed me and took me to the station house and I spent the night there. I was sore, but there wasn't a damn thing I could do about it. The next morning I went to Centre Street and through the police headquarters routine of being fingerprinted and mugged. I was still sore at my mistake during the line-up.

It wasn't long before the FBI agent in Newark showed up and told me that I was going back to Trenton. My lawyer told me there wasn't anything I could do. I was on my way back to my home town for an official reception. 

My dad would have been real proud of me. I was a big man coming back to his old home town. 

XVII-ON TRIAL 

The FBI agent kept his word and I was put in prison instead of the city jail. The Trenton cops didn't like this because they wanted information. If I was in their hands there might be some talking, or so they thought. They know a human can stand only so much and then he acts like a canary.

There were two officers on duty the night I was locked in the prison, Joe R and M.B. Joe is a career cop who takes it serious. M. is simply a sneak-take man who can be bought cheap.

M. signed me in, and I declared I had three thousand dollars on me. I took eight hundred and sixty and put it in my shoe.

M. listened quietly when I spoke to him. "You know, M.," I said, "I may need some favors.”

He knew the score and went along with me. I knew I was going to have a con's meal, and I didn't want it. This 121

was one of the worst-feeding jails in Jersey, New York or 

Pennsylvania. I slipped him a deuce. (You buy him in little pieces.) I told him I wanted a good meal and I got it. 

He arranged for me to have a guard's meal. I had three slices of roast beef, six eggs, home fried potatoes, a large glass of milk and coffee. That's not bad for a lousy two bucks.

M. also got me brand new blankets and I was assigned to Two West, which is where they put those who haven't been tried. I was assigned a job as head runner, which meant that at six-thirty in the morning to nine-thirty at night my cell door was open. Every time a meal was served they rang a bell. Then I'd go to the dumbwaiter and take the food off it. Another prisoner and myself would set the table up. Then the prisoners would come out and eat, and when they were finished, go back in their cells. Then we'd clean up the food. After that, they could come out again. The tables were a folding type and about a foot wide. 

I never swept or mopped once. I had a flunky take care of that for cigarettes. Why do it yourself when you can get it done cheap?

After two days a county dick, Denny, came over to see me. He stood outside the cell door. It was an expected visit, except I didn't know who they'd send. "John," he said. "This is a routine visit. Do you want to sign special sessions?" 

In case you don't know, that's the trade word for pleading guilty and throwing yourself on the mercy of the court. I was bitter and angry. I spit right in his face and laughed. "Go back to your chief, Denny,” I said. "I'm not copping a plea for any rotten bastard."

I sat down, because I didn't want to continue the conversation. Denny was as sore as I've ever seen a coppy. 

His face turned bright red. He almost shouted at me. 122

"Matthews, I wish you were our prisoner. I'd beat your brains in," he said, and left. I was glad I wasn't his boy, because he meant every word.

I went for a hearing, and my bail was set at fifteen thousand dollars. I called a bondsman to arrange to meet bail. It costs six bucks a hundred. I went back to my cell to wait for the bail to be met. It wasn't. The bondsman told me that every time he got it the amount was hiked. 

No one wanted to let me out, and he couldn't lay off the money with other bondsmen. I was stuck. 

I asked for permission to make my one telephone call and they let me have it. I called Frankie Meyer. He didn't seem too concerned. "Johnny, you needed a spanking. Maybe this will teach you a lesson. Just take it easy. The most you'll get is six months and maybe a fine. We'll take care of the fine; but if you got to stay inside there's nothing we can do." "Frankie, I don't like this," I answered. "I'll beat your brains out when I'm through here." 

Frankie tried to calm me down and told me not to blow my top. He told me that he'd see to it that I got money each week and that my family would be given money. I felt a little better after knowing the boys hadn't completely forgotten me.

When I got back to my cell, I started to make the necessary arrangements to collect the money. There was a certain hack I paid fifty bucks a week to go to Philly to get the envelope. I didn't mind paying a guard that much, because I wanted privileges. It cost me seventy-five dollars a week not to starve and for other favors. There were plenty of guards who took money. We called them take-hats.

In the cells where they kept woman prisoners there were arrangements to get a broad. You could get there for about fifteen bucks. There was five each for the guard and 123

the matron, and the same for the broad. What the hell, that isn't bad for a piece when you're locked up.

There was one guard who got himself suspended at least five times that I know. He was another we could buy pretty cheap. A yard a month kept him happy, and he used to get us booze. He'd go outside the prison and get us a fifth of scotch. The price was ten bucks, but we figured it was worth it instead of having to drink the stuff made inside.

After about a week I was taken for a pleading. It was held before a judge who I knew was a close friend of my dad's. Incidentally, my father never even got in touch with me while I was there.

The judge made a long windy speech to me that lasted about twenty minutes. "You come from an old, respected family in Trenton. 

Your father is a personal friend of mine," he started. I don't remember it all but you can get the drift. I sneered as he talked and hoped he wouldn't spend too much time trying to make me feel sorry for myself. 

When he got through, he asked me a question. "Did my talk have any effect on you? Do you have any comment?"

I looked him right in the face and told him that, first, I had a question and then a comment. He told me to ask my question. "Can you turn me loose?" I asked.

He told me it was possible but illegal, and he certainly wasn't going to do so. I knew he was going to say that. "Here's my comment, your honor. Drop dead, you creep!"

I could see the red rise in his face. He seemed barely able to control himself. "I'll overlook your remark because you're feeling bitter. 

You can plead guilty now or stand trial," he said. 124

I asked him if I could have another question and comment and he said that I could. "Is it correct that there will be twelve men on the jury?” "Yes, that is correct."

My comment got him even madder than he had been. "Then I'll take a chance that someone on the jury will believe my part of the story. We'll scuffle for it. I won't take any chance on one person deciding what's going to happen to me."

The judge rapped his gavel on the bench and shouted to the guard. "Take this man away immediately."

I turned to Dave, the guard, and told him what he was not going to do. "Dave, if you lay a hand on me, I'll bust you right in the face here," I said. 

Dave knew me because I was a local boy, and he said he wasn't going to touch me. I walked beside him out of the court and back to my cell.

The newspaper men covering the hearing ran out of the place to telephone their papers. I was Public Enemy 

Number One in that section, and this was news for them. 

Maybe, now, they're trying to cut me down to size, but I was hot then.

The indictment came down pretty fast after that hearing, but it was about five months before my trial started. 

In the meantime I lived as well as you can in stir. I had my privileges and paid for them right along.

I tried to make some arrangements for a deal to be made, but nobody on the outside was helping me. The mob had let me down good, and no fix was coming along. It wouldn't have been too tough for the boys to have reached the cab drivers. I know money talks; and while they couldn't have pulled back on their story the whole way, it could have been softened. But nothing was done to help me. 125

I always felt that my own lawyer was shanghaiing me. I heard the dicks had talked to him. They probably told him, "Give us John, and when you get a real big case we'll do you a favor."

I could feel the cards were stacked good against me. 

The trial lasted five days, and each day I could see my chances to beat the rap were getting worse and worse. 

On the fifth day I decided to hell with my lawyer, and took things in my own hands.

I stood up and interrupted the assistant district attorney, who was nailing me a little bit more, with most co-operative witnesses. I knew what to say, because I had learned some things from my dad about what went on in a courtroom. I even got polite for a change. My lawyer was surprised and tried to get me to sit down. "John, what are you doing?" he asked. "This is no time to get the Court sore at you. Sit down and let me handle it.". 

I looked down at him and sneered. "You miserable creep. I know what you're doing, and

I'm sick of what's going on. My defense is just about as lousy as it could be. You're through as far as I'm concerned."

The judge rapped with his gavel and demanded order in the court. I could see the reporters taking notes as fast as they could and the photographers getting set for action. 

I started to talk. "If it pleases the Court, your honor, I want to plead.

I'm not going to plead non vult. In English, what I'm going to say means the same thing. I'm copping out. I plead guilty."

The newsmen rushed out of the joint. I could see the prosecutor smiling. My lawyer just started to pack his papers. I didn't give a tinker's damn for the whole lot of them. I figured I had a better chance copping out than getting sunk deeper. 126

The judge deferred the sentence for a week and I was taken back to my cell. I hoped I'd made the right decision.

The week seemed to pass fairly slowly. I wasn't feeling too happy, especially when I got word that my father had written the judge who was going to sentence me. When I . heard what he had written I stopped betting on what I'd get and tried to lay off the ones I had made. My figuring that copping out was going to do me good seemed wrong now. 

The note he wrote went like this:

If my son is guilty as he has so indicated in his pleading, and you give him mercy because of me, I'll consider it a personal affront. If he's guilty, he should have known better.

That was all he wrote, and it didn't come as any surprise to me. If my mother had still been alive I knew she would have done something for me. She had died of cancer before I was arrested. I loved my mother, and she understood me despite my faults. I'm sure she would have come to see me even though my father would have told her not to come. I was a complete disgrace to him. 

He died from a stomach operation while I was in the can. The prison authorities didn't even let me know for three months after it happened. That is against regulations, but I couldn't have cared less. I wouldn't have gone to his funeral, because I hated him. On second thought, maybe I would have gone. And the reason I would have gone is that I would have been in handcuffs and his good old friends would have been shocked. It would have taken the play away from a dignified ending. Yes, I would have liked that part very much. 

No friend of mine was in court the day of the sentencing. I didn't consider my lawyer a friend, and no one from the mob was there to watch me get it. My wife wasn't there, and I didn't want her to be there anyway. I 127

never told the warden I was married. I just put down single, because I didn't want her and the kids to be bothered by the law. They were in Philly, and what my little girls didn't know about their dad was better. 

The courtroom was jammed when the calendar call of cases was read.

I was just being sentenced on the counts for beating up one cabby. The other was to come later. There wasn't going to be any chance of the time running concurrently. 

It was quick and to the point. The words hit me hard inside, but I didn't move a muscle. No one was going to be able to write in any paper that I got pale when the sentence was given. I sneered as the judge talked. "John D. Matthews, having pleaded guilty to the charges, you are remanded to the New Jersey State Prison for a term of not less than three years and not more than five."

I said nothing, and was taken away. I told my lawyer to forget filing an appeal. I was going to serve my time.

I didn't think, then, that it was going to be eight lousy years before I was going to be able to walk out a free man. There was plenty ahead for me. But I was determined right then and there that no one was going to push me around. I knew they'd keep me in. But I wasn't going to play patsy for the cops or anybody. I knew how to organize men. And I knew how money and muscle make a helluva combination.

The courtroom emptied. The other cases weren't worth space in the papers. John D. Matthews had gotten his sentence and was through. At least they thought so. But 

I wasn't. 128

XVIII-CELEBRITY CONS 

When I use the words "celebrity cons," I mean one thing: I'm talking about racket men that have made their mark in their field. They are the boys that newspapers and magazines have devoted a lot of space to, telling about what they did. They are the mobsters that get headlines. 

They are the racket men that Senator Estes Kefauver used as a campaign platform.

The top men in the rackets did their dirty work years ago. They worked their way through the ranks until they took control. Then they got others to do the muscle work, and they stayed behind the scenes. Every now and then they take a fall and wind up for a bit in jail. Most of the time it's for income tax evasion. The boys in blue don't seem to get them for the real stuff. Why? Well, money talks big, and they have their share of fall guys to take the raps.

Now, any con who has a good-sized poke can buy his privileges in stir. But the big boys, from the start, are treated with kid gloves. Not at the trial, but after they get their number-then they do all right. And don't think for one second that they lose power when they're sent away. The only time that they lose out is because they were through when they got picked up. If not, they run the rackets straight from their cells. I know that, because 

I was with some pretty big guys-racket men that the average reader has heard of more than once.

Joe Adonis was one of the real top boys in the can with me. If he wasn't the top man in the national syndicate, he was very close to it. Right now, he's back in Italy, 129

having taken a voluntary deportation. There's no reason to think that Joe A. still isn't pulling the strings and directing operations. He was big. 

I was in the Big House in Trenton when Joseph Doto signed into the hotel. That's Adonis' real name, but the newspapers rarely use anything but Adonis.

He got the red-carpet treatment right from the start. He had money and bought privileges starting the first day. 

Instead of spending fifteen days in quarantine like everyone else, Joe A. was in general population on the first day. Instead of having his head shaved bald like every con does, he didn't get close to a barber. Maybe they didn't think he had lice. Their excuse is probably that he was waiting for an appeal on his trial and might be set loose.

Joe A. got a cushy assignment immediately. He was put in the storeroom as a clerk. He didn't do any work at all. He had a flunky do his job and just pretended to work. The work started at eight, with time off for lunch, and then ended at five. It was soft for him. 

He never had to go to the mess hall to eat. His money began to talk loud and clear. The butcher was taken care of, and an electrician made him a hot plate fast. His cell was never searched like the others. He ate in his cell all the time, and never had to go to the yard and mingle with the rabble. His cell door was open. And he had a mattress and a bed brought from the outside. The guard watching him couldn't see anything going on!

Doto had his own flunky in the can with him. This boy, did everything for him. He had a "lob," which is a prison term for flunky, sweep his cell and do any necessary dirty work.

I've heard wardens say in articles that the so-called big shots never caused any trouble and were model prisoners. The reason they don't cause any trouble is that 130

they don't want any of their privileges taken away from them.

The rules are that you can't spend any more than twenty-five dollars a month for extra supplies at the prison store. Joe A. took care of that bookkeeping problem in a very simple way. He picked twenty cons that weren't getting anything from anybody. These guys didn't get letters or money until Joe A. hit the place. Then it started. 

Joe A. had his boys send them fifty dollars a month. Out of the fifty they could save twenty-five. Fifteen of what was left was used for what Adonis wanted, and they could spend their ten anyway they wanted. It was simple and the guys liked it. Before he came they had nothing. 

Joe's flunky would tell them the night before what to order from the store. Cons aren't supposed to have more than four cartons of butts in their cell in a week. Joe always had between twenty-five and thirty. He used them as tips for other cons to pull his work. No one looked in his section to see if anything was wrong He locked with me for a year and a half; and when you're close to a guy you learn certain things. Doto never lost control of his rackets when he was inside. He gave directions to his visitors. It wasn't as pleasant as being outside, but the loot was still coming his way. 

Another big shot with me in the can was Frank Erickson, the gambler. He was different about buying privileges. He never once missed going to the mess hall, and never went over his twenty-five a month limit at the store room. He practically saluted the guards when they spoke to him. Frank wanted to get out on parole as fast as he could, and he was set to be a good little boy. 

But while he put up a nice front, acting like a real model prisoner, he was still giving the orders to the outside. His visitors reported to him regularly on what was going on in his gambling empire. He told me one time: 131

"John, everything is going as I left it. I got guys I can trust."

He didn't give me details, but the reason he talked and let me know he was still in charge was simple. Every guy. inside has got to talk to some one sooner or later. It can't be kept bottled up all the time. Everyone gets a confidant. 

When Frank got sprung, he did something that surprised a lot of guys. He sent two hundred bucks to about a hundred and fifty cons. They didn't expect it, and it was a big thing. He still is big outside and never lost control. 

Artie Sposato was another big-time guy with us in 

Trenton. He was an arrogant, name-dropping wheel. He was a top guy in bookmaking in Jersey and the East. He never stopped letting everyone know how important he was, and had five or six guards running errands for him. 

All the time he was there he smoked dollar cigars-and you have to pay someone to get those for you! I never thought much of him, and he knew it and didn't cross me once. I would have belted him quick if he had.

In the State Prison at Rahway there were some other pretty well-known racket boys. I got to know Charlie 

Workman pretty well. In case you've forgotten, he's the lad that took Dutch Schultz in Newark one night and gunned him. The Dutchman, whose real name was Arthur

Flegenheimer, ran the rackets in New York. In his day he was second to none, and had the top politicians like Jimmy Hines in his hip pocket. The Dutchman was a real big man and took orders from no one. But he had his share of enemies, and Workman stopped him cold one night. The Dutchman was laid out on a slab after Charlie shot him. I got to know Charlie pretty well in Rahway. I can say right now there's going to be trouble when he gets his parole. 

Charlie seldom confides in anyone and is behaving. But just wait for headlines after he's sprung. He works, or did 132

when I was there, in the school and library. But he's a very bitter man.

I had a pretty fair fight with one of the top boys called 

Tony P one day. I did fifteen days in the lockup for busting him, but it was worth it. He was a name-dropper and made a mistake with me. He's out now and operating like a millionaire.

It happened very simple-like. I'd told him plenty of times never to call me a bastard, even in fun. I felt it was a reflection on my mother, and I loved her. We were arguing about a ball game and he said, "John, you're a stupid bastard."

I did a quick burn and told him to apologize in ten seconds or take the consequences. I didn't like his answer. "Stop thinking you're a prison gangster," he said. “You don't bother me none. 99 I just walked over and busted him in the mouth and splashed a few teeth on the floor. He quit. 

But he had a buddy on his payroll called Frank who used to fight. He was an ex-pug and ex-punk. He tried to play hero for his boss, and lunged at me. I sat him on his ass. I say this without conceit but as a matter of fact. I beat the hell out of him. Nothing would have happened if he hadn't squealed. Tony didn't; he just said he fell on the concrete. But the other jerk squealed. 

There were other pretty well-known guys like Johnny 

N- who ran a book joint in Hackensack with sixty phones.

The state attorney-general personally directed the raid on that place. He had a buddy called Junie, who was a topflight stoolie. He had to be put in protective custody to save his skull from being caved in. He used to send for the county dicks and spill info. It didn't do him any good. 

When I had about fifteen months to go I was transferred to the "ho," or prison, at Leesburg. And if you don't think privileges can be bought, you've never heard how 133

a certain ex-judge lived there. He was the judge that took a fall for having embezzled 650,000 dollars. He had it pretty soft at Leesburg. His assignment was inmate nurse, and he had his own shower and a choice of six beds. He also had his own radio and a small television set.' 

The rules were different at Leesburg, and we had a lot more freedom. You only had to wear prison pants, and could wear other types of shirts. The ex-judge always wore twenty-five and thirty-dollar shirts and hand-tooled shoes. His doll used to bring him clean shirts all the time. 

And when it was cold he wore fur-lined coats. 

The rules said that we were to get only ten pounds of food sent from the outside each month, but he managed to get at least fifty pounds. I had been twice at the farm and this was the last trip there. I knew the ropes pretty good. Everyone was supposed to do some sort of farm work, but I didn't. If you didn't, they were supposed to send you back. But they didn't want me back in Rahway. 

They felt I was an organizer.

The judge is out, now, and married to his doll. She waited for him. Well, he had enough dough to keep going good. There were rumors he was in Newark while he was in jail. There was a night sergeant who took care of such matters for the inmates who paid the right tariff. 

From what I hear today, he reports to the parole officer when he feels like it. He told me that he'd sue me if I ever mentioned how he lived in Leesburg. I'm waiting. 

If you don't think there were good pay-offs to guards, just take a look at the cars they have. See what they get for salaries and how they live. Add up what they spend on the surface, and you'll be convinced. 

Another inmate was Tommy Y-, who was head of a truck union in Newark. He was so big the union saw to it that he got two hundred and fifty bucks a week. He was a tough strong-arm man, and he offered me a job when I got out. I spit in his face. I didn't want anything to do with 134

him. I just didn't like him. He's back in charge now, and got twenty-five grand as a bonus when he got out.

Buck A-, who is a leader of the colored crime syndicate in the country, was there with us. He always has two or three thousand bucks on him at all times. He bought what he wanted. He got out in ten months on parole; but they found out he had left two months early and they sent him back.

On visits it was easy to get money. There was no supervision, and guys with the right connections had their wallets full.

Frank DiLuca, who was picked up in a raid on a gambling joint in Mapleshade, New Jersey, got twenty-five thousand dollars for taking the rap for the mob, and two hundred a week came to him for expenses.

If you have the money, or if it's coming from the outside racket boys, then your life in prison can be made a lot easier. You can practically get what you want. Just don't lose your mob power before you take a fall. If you do, then you've got to depend on your own resources. I hope they are better than mine were.

XIX-PLANNING A RIOT 

Eight years after I stepped through the front door of the New Jersey State Prison, I came out with one big idea. I never wanted to be put back in again. I lived a lifetime in the can. I thought before I went through the eight years that I knew something about what prisons were like. I knew very little. But I do know now and want no part of them.

I got ideas on how I was going to be treated right 135

after I got in. I wasn't going to be pushed around like some punk. I planned to make the right move as soon as I saw what the setup was like.

I was told to go into a little room, and was locked in there with an officer. I stripped naked as a jaybird and was told to "forget your clothes" from now on. I was gone over from head to toe. He was looking for dope. 

They don't miss any part of you. They even run a fine tooth-comb through your hair-not too find lice, but narcotics. There wasn't any part of me that didn't get searched.

The guard then gave me a pair of bedroom slippers which were too big. No one ever gets the right size. Then 

I was given a pair of pajamas with the word FISH stenciled on them. It gives somebody a laugh, and stands for "another guy has been hooked." 

All the cigarettes you have are taken away, and they tell you it's because they might contain dope. The center runner and the stoolies get free butts.

The population inside calls what I was doing "playing shortstop," because the office is between Third and Second Streets. After I got through playing shortstop I was moved to another room. They don't take any chances. 

They keep locking doors all the time so you can't make a move. 

The "white hats"-the boss guards-don't miss any tricks in trying to break your spirit. I had to stand for more than an hour and a half underneath a clock, and I had to keep looking at it. It's to let you know that you're pulling time.

At lunchtime I was still standing there. That's when the inmate population gets a chance to look you over. The homosexuals or "wolves" get a chance to look over the new material. They're looking for young, fresh-faced kids. 

You're asked if you have any enemies in the joint, because if you do they'll put you in Wing Six, which is 136

protective custody. The rules are that you can't buy anything from the prison store for a while, and this gives the wolves a break. They make arrangements to send butts and candy to the fresh-faced kids. The freshfaced kids think it's nice, until one night they learn the facts of life. The wolves don't spend their dough to be nice. They want their pound of flesh.

And if the kid says no, he'll find the cards stacked against him. The wolf will cut him up and claim the kid started a fight, and the new one gets six months in the lockup. And he's known as a stoolie. Everybody in prison hates stoolies, including the guards. They're afraid a stoolie will tell on their rackets. 

I didn't mind standing under the clock. I had a beard, a battered nose from fighting, and no man was ever interested in me sexually. I was sore at the world and had a chip on my shoulder. I knew some of the guys and got supplies sent to me, but not for the same reason.

After the clock bit, I went into what they call quarantine. I was officially registered in the hotel. I was given my clothing and supplies. You're not supposed to be able to buy anything, but there is a racket there. For the right price, things are done for you; but if you don't have it, then you're dirt. 

The storekeeper doesn't tell you that you are only allowed to spend twenty-five dollars a month in the storeroom, so if you spend your dough too fast, then you're broke and don't get the right treatment. 

I was asked if I wanted to have a cell on the "flats."

Those are cells on the bottom tier, and the best. And your cell door will be left opened-provided you pay.

I thought it was a pretty cheap, vicious way to start, but I had dough and started buying the privileges. 

The prison radio plays from eight in the morning until two the next day, and you can buy your earphones for four dollars and a quarter. If you're transferred, then 137

you get one-and-a-quarter back, but the repairs aren't covered by this. And they always cost more than they should. I told them I wasn't going to pay for any earphones because they were paid for by the County Freeloaders. I was sent to see the warden. He didn't like my attitude. "Matthews, you're disrupting the system," he said.

I told him I wasn't going to pay for them, and I never did.

He told me that I was starting off on a bad foot, and sent me back to quarantine. The storekeeper there tried to sell me some junk jewelry made by the cons. It looks glittery and he tries to sell it for about ten bucks, though it's only worth a buck. The guards know what's going on, but they don't mind getting their rake-off. It's part of the system. The guard there got his coffee made for him and gave him cake. 

It gave me a laugh when they gave you a chance to buy raw eggs and coffee. It's an inducement to break the rules, because you're not supposed to have hot plates in your cell.

In two days I had gorilla-ed my way to the flats, although you're supposed to stay in quarantine for fifteen days. I was now with the population. 

It was a jungle. The place was broken up in little cliques of five to ten cons. I decided that I was going to form the biggest clique of them all. It didn't take too long until I had between thirty-five and forty in my group. It was the roughest of them all.

I had to be careful in starting my clique, for the other cons don't like to see a new guy get the power. I wanted top muscle to start off so I decided to enlist the Polack. 

He was just a little over six feet and weighed about two hundred and thirty pounds. All man. He was as strong a guy as I've ever met. The Polack could make a fivehundred-pound military press or splinter a one-and-a- 138

half-inch plank without any trouble. He had a bad habit of getting drunk every two months on what we called "prison shake-up." It's made of raisins and fruit and yeast stolen from the prison bakery. The population stored it in two-gallon jars. Man, is it strong! An average drinker who can take four good shots without blinking an eye will be drunk as a skunk on one shot.

When the Polack would get loaded he'd try and rush the center section. The orders were to shoot to kill if a con rushed. He'd stand by the side and shout and challenge the guards. It took quite a few of them to take him. 

He had done twelve flat years for atrocious assault and attempt to kill.

When he got out, it lasted for only three months and he was back. The Polack was raised in the penal system of New Jersey. He did bits in all the places from reformatory right up to the Big House. He'll never stay out for long.

The Polack had no brains, but he liked to write. So I wrote his letters for him. He was like a little kid, and I babied him around. I made him my right-hand man, and he followed me like a little puppy. Once I told him one of the bunch that I wanted in my clique refused. They knew he worshiped me and would do just what I wanted, and no one wanted to tangle with him. I had the Polack assigned to my cell. I kept him out of trouble, and when he went to challenge the guards I'd grab him by his ear and lead him away.

In the yard each day we'd have a chance to talk. You keep moving around and get a chance to discuss what you want. In the mess hall you can sit where you want and that gave me a chance to circulate and build my organization. We had movies twice a week, and that was another place to make plans. 

I was simply Number 27229 for four years, and then when I got another four it was changed to 29666. Those 139

were my numbers, but I was more than just a number in prison. I was the top man because I had the top clique. 

The guards knew my position after I had been there only three weeks. They'd call me when they felt a situation was building up, and I headed the "grievance committee."

There are a lot of advantages in belonging to a strong clique. If one of my boys had to go to the hospital, he knew that he would be properly taken care of and not left to himself. They had inmate nurses, and they were a bunch of creeps. They'd do nothing but lay around all day with their fags. They wouldn't do anything but what the book said. When our boys were there, cigarettes would be provided and the best food. 

We controlled everything in the prison. No one did anything that didn't meet with our approval. And by "our" approval, I mean mine.

There were a lot of things that we felt weren't being properly done by the prison authorities. Our list of grievances was the basis for the riots that were to come. 

Complaint Number One was that the food was lousy. It wasn't properly cooked, and there wasn't enough of it. In addition, we didn't eat between five-thirty at night and seven the next morning. It was too long to wait without food. 

Our second complaint was the way the parole system worked. We thought that a con, when he went to a hearing, was supposed to be given a chance to sell himself. 

Instead, all you got was a lousy three minutes. A con could barely give his name and number and look at the parole board. A member would ask you to give a reason why you should be let out on parole. You'd start to talk, and they'd look at the clock and say that's all. 

Then you have to wait two or three weeks for an answer, and only two out of ten make it. If you don't make it, then you have to wait another year. The inmates felt 140

this was no incentive to be good and that they didn't have a chance.

The only inmates that seemed to have a better deal to get out were the guys there for sex offenses with little kids. The attitude seemed to be that they should be pitied instead of punished. We hated those bastards. I had kids; and despite what I've done to break the law and beat guys up, I loved them. I think any father would think a hell of a lot before he let a sex offender out in a hurry. I wonder how quick they'd get out if a parole official ever had his youngster violated.

Our third beef was the rotten plumbing in the prison. 

Oh, they had certain wings to show the public and inspectors; but all the rest smelt like a sewer. 

The fourth and last major complaint was guard brutality. They had some damn rough skull-busters who must have been born with a club in their hand. There was one guard with the intelligence of an ape. He was a big, ugly, illiterate creep who beat the daylight out of a con. I'd have loved to tangle with him, but he had too much support.

When a con did get a belting around, he'd be put in a special cell and the prison doc would examine him. The examination was thorough. The doc, who was drunk most of the time, would stick his head close to the cell door and say, "How are you? You'll be better soon."

Then he'd go back to his office and write down that Number So-and-so was improving from the fall he had had.

And as long as I'm talking about the great medical setup, I might mention again the inmate nurses. Those fags had good connections on the outside with a druggist who would supply them with narcotics. Just enough stuff to keep a guy high during the day, and a pheno so he could sleep. A con could get his share for about fifty 141

bucks a month. Of course, he wouldn't get too much; but it was better than nothing.

Those were the main reasons that were creating tension in the prison. I wasn't the only con on edge and sore about the conditions. Almost all of them were, except the fags. (The fags were having a better time in stir than on the outside; they had new material from time to time.) 

And it wasn't conditions that started when I got there. 

The old-timers told me things hadn't changed for years. 

It was always promises and more promises, and no action taken.

I decided that some sort of action had to be taken. But it had to be very carefully planned. There were lots of ideas as to what would be the best way to convince the officials that something had to be done to correct the system.

At night we would discuss the matter. And during the day when we met in the yard I'd hear suggestions. We tried to keep our plans as quiet as possible so that some lousy creep of a stoolie wouldn't get to a guard and shoot off his mouth for special favors.

In my cell one night we came to a final decision. The 

Polack stood guard so that no one could listen in. By that time my cell door was open, and we could move around fairly free. In fact, I then had a hot plate and had coffee and eggs whenever I felt like it. 1 The guards were taken care of so they didn't bother me. 

It was easy as hell to tell when a con was cooking. The smell itself was enough.

We went over the major plans. One was for a large breakout, but I felt that would miss. My plan was for a demonstration—a show of power. If we didn't make this show of power and take over the prison, the guards would realize we were weak. We didn't have to convince just the guards and officials. They weren't paying any attention 142

to our grievances. We had to let the public know and force action that way.

The decision was made, and every day for three months I went over the plans. The details and various assignments began to fall in line. Our target was to take over three wings and hold out.

Every day, when I was in the yard, I would talk to the heads of the other cliques. This had to be a general riot or we would miss our goal.

And then, when I thought that everything was set and it was just a matter of the time to put the plan into operation, some bastard stoolie tipped the guards. This sneaking son of a bitch had spoiled our plan. I think a lot of guys would have killed him, if they had known which one it was. We never did find out. I hope he rots in prison. 

Or better yet, I'd like to meet him for five minutes alone.

We didn't get any warning. The guards grabbed eighteen or nineteen of us and shipped us to Rahway State 

Prison. 

xx-A RIOT STARTS 

The guards didn't take any chances with us on the trip to Rahway. We were put in vans and double-handcuffed so we couldn't make move. There were police cars in front and back, plus State Troopers. I was disgusted. All the work and planning had blown up. We got to Rahway in couple of hours and were put under extra close supervision the minute we got there. 

Very soon after I got there with most of my clique, including the Polack, there was an announcement made over the prison loud-speaker system by the director. The 143

word had been given me that the cons here were restless and had plans. His little talk confirmed it. "Reports have come to my attention," he said, "that some of the men here are planning a demonstration. I advise you against starting any trouble. All of you will be watched by guns." 99 I felt better right away. I was going to be in on anything that started. We heard that the cons back in Trenton had rioted, but it didn't last more than forty-eight hours. The police and officials made them submit. They lost out in a hurry.

The population in Rahway had been making riot plans for weeks, and they were well prepared. They had seven or eight police positives for their main weapons. Those guns you have to get from the law. I heard it cost them fifteen hundred bucks to get them.

The food-supply department had been well taken care of, and the boys weren't going to be starved out in a hurry. Besides stealing from the prison stores, they had kept all food packages sent to them from the outside. We could have opened up a food store with what we had. 

We had three sides of beef, two eight-hundred-pound pigs, two hundred provolones, one hundred and fifty Italian hams and five hundred pepperones. We were short on bread, though, with only one hundred and fifty loaves.

In addition, there were twelve gallon jars of prison whisky. Later, I made sure that this was very carefully rationed out. I didn't want the mob to get drunk. It was going to be tough enough to keep them under control without dynamite booze.

The time had come close to the start of the riot. There was a guard who stood close to a riot bell. The plan was to get hostages first and then go into action. Some of the cons started making noise, and guards with flashlights started to come into our wing. We waited until they had 144

gotten in and then rushed. We took them in a hurry, and the outside guard rang the riot bell. 

It was under way, and it didn't end for five more days. 

Each day was a nightmare.

We took the clubs away from the guards. One of them tried to talk us out of the riot. "You can't win. Give up right now,” he said.

The population laughed in his face. 

I gave their clubs to men I trusted, and we took the guards to the center and put them in six beds. In the downstairs cells they were doing almost the same thing. 

We had, after others joined us, about two hundred and thirty-six men. I started giving orders. The first thing I did was to order every inmate to get a bucket and fill it with water. "Do this fast," I directed. "The water supply will be cut off very soon."

We had two hundred and fifty buckets of water and twenty garbage drums filled quickly. I was right. The water was shut off a few minutes after we got our supply. 

The men wanted to blow off steam and I let them. They went berserk and broke all the furniture and window panes. They ripped out the toilets.

We barricaded the wing entrances with beds and furniture. We drilled holes in the floor so we could look down and see what was happening.

The tables that weren't smashed were put on top of the radiators at one end to make a counter for the food. We arranged to have two meals a day. It was run like a cafeteria. 

Some of the men made kites out of paper and we sent messages out so the public would know what was going on. We even had a fifteen-piece prison band, and they played a lot for us during the five days. The mob outside could hear the band playing. 

I was afraid that some of the guys would want to kill 145

the hostages, so I arranged three shifts of fifteen men each to protect them. But even with this protection I had trouble.

There was a big Russian who went berserk. He tore the chains that held the radiator, and started to rush the guards. I told the Polack to take him. The Polack did the job perfectly. He smashed eight ribs and busted his jaw. 

The Russian didn't give us any more trouble. An inmate nurse set his jaw for him. I didn't care how good a job he did.

The riot had started at eleven-thirty at night, and by twelve we were in command of Three Wing. I found that 

I was staying up eighteen hours a day. A con called 

Champ took over when I slept and the Polack guarded me.

I made so many speeches during the time of the riot that I felt like an orator. We'd make plans, and I said that 

I would accept full responsibility for the riot. I got a vote of confidence without any trouble.

The guys were on edge, and I had to watch all the time to stop trouble. A lifer called Frank tried to start a subversive group. He wanted to have us try a mass escape. 

I put a stop to that quick. I called a meeting. "Don't listen to any screwballs that want to make a break. There are too many guns outside, and we'll get killed," I said.

I pointed to Frank and told him to forget his plan. 

When he looked like he wouldn't I walked over to him. 

Two of my guys held him and I smashed him in the face.

I blacked his eyes and kicked his ribs in. He didn't give me any more trouble.

The prison whisky was kept under guard, and I had it rationed out in small shots. I couldn't afford drunks. A con called Crow somehow got more than his share and got loaded. He grabbed a sledge hammer and tried to batter down a wall to get out. He was pounding on the wall 146

when the Polack took him. He slept off his drunk with a busted head.

There were about thirty fags with us, and they hoped the riot would never end. For the first time they had no supervision, and they didn't care who watched them or what they did.

I carried a gun for the first time in my life. I never used it. The trusted members of my clique had the others.

One of the guards got sick. He had some sort of stomach trouble. I had him released, and saw his wife and children outside from a window. When he got out she shouted, "God bless you."

I told her to save her prayers for her husband because he'd need them. 

Two stoolies were caught throwing notes out the window. They were telling the officers what the situation was inside. We held a kangaroo court. We had to do something quick and fast. If you show weakness, you're dead.

It was a very fast court, and there wasn't any high court to appeal the sentence. Each of the bastards got thirty lashes. But it didn't do much good in the long run. 

They squealed on us later.

One of the creeps talked plenty when it was over. I wasn't surprised three months later when someone slit his throat when he was sleeping. He almost died. Lousy stoolies like this punk usually get taken care of good. The con who worked him should have used an ice pick and stuck him in the heart. That would have been quick, and maybe no one would have known what happened when he was found.

I managed to keep them in line. This was a time when a leader had to show strength, and I did.

When a con would get out of line I'd personally see he was stopped. One guy got a rasp-file knife and tried to take me. I smashed him across the eyes and worked his 147

face over good. An inmate nurse put ten stitches in his face with white hose from a sock. The scars probably still show.

The guys knew I was carrying a piece, but I didn't like to have it on me. No muscleman likes to have a gun to make people think he's got power. I like my fists and feet to do my talking.

We kept up running negotiations with the authorities outside. Time after time we were told to give up our hostages. I did the talking for our side.

A deputy represented the authorities. We talked through the door. “Matthews," he said, "you've got to give the guards up. 

I demand that you turn the officers free."

He must have thought I was the prize sucker of all time. I gave him a short answer. "Do you think you're dealing with idiots? They are our only insurance. Come up with a better idea."

I told him that we had no intention of dealing with just the prison authorities. "It won't do us any good to deal with you people," I said. "We intend to bring this whole matter to outside authorities. We want impartial people to hear our grievances. We are going to bring this to your political opponents."

We had regular meetings three times a day. Plans would be discussed and regular votes taken on what we would do. I was amazed by the rabble, They were worried about reprisals. In fact, from the second night on, a lot of them got worried. I'd keep telling my boys to be sure and keep things in control and smash anyone who got out of line. Everyone was supposed to pool what they had, and every so often a guy would hold out. We dealt physically with those creeps, and quite a few guys got their lumps.

The guys would start card and dice games and play on 148

credit. That was good, because it kept them quiet. We had sheets covering the windows so the guards couldn't see where the hostages were held.

The heat was turned off and it got pretty cold on the concrete floor, so we burned things to keep warm and cook. At one time there were five or six small bonfires going. Some of the guys would sing around the fires.

There was no curfew, but I had regular shifts of cons at the windows and doors.

Keeping some of the punks from working over the guards was a continual problem. I had one helluva fight with a guy who was trying to organize a bunch to cripple one of the hacks. He got some creeps pretty well stirred up, so I spoke to him. He was really spoiling for trouble. 

I didn't want to show any weakness and let the Polack take him. This was my job. I just told the Polack to watch and see that no one else joined.

We had a knockdown fight that lasted about forty-five minutes. We fell over chairs and tables and rolled on the floor. No one broke it up. I guess they got a kick out of watching us. I thought it would never end, because this was one tough baby. He didn't know what the word quit meant, and I knew he'd take over if I lost. I finally got a good shot at him in the groin, and he folded like a tent. 

If he had gotten up, I'd have been through. That was my 

Sunday punch. I stayed in command.

I knew that we had to get the public in on the matter or we would be just as bad off as if we hadn't started this. In fact, we'd be worse off. 

Drew Pearson, the columnist, flew up from Washington, but the warden wouldn't have anything to do with him. 

On the fifth day we again submitted our list of grievances, which were the same as I explained earlier. We were told that Governor Driscoll would set up an impartial committee to study the entire problem. When we heard that, we held a vote and decided to give up. I hoped 149

that everything would come out okay for us, but I wasn't too sure.

The agreement was that the hostages would be let out first. Eight out of the nine were crying, and several of them said they would never work as guards again. But they got over that feeling quick, and were just as lousy as ever.

Then we surrendered. It was an orderly surrender. 

When you lose, you start taking orders all over. I was among the last to leave. We came out in pairs, and were stripped naked and searched. 

We had made our mark on the prison. One wing was completely demolished. There was at least three hundred thousand dollars worth of damage, despite what any of them say.

They took us straight to the drill hall and there wasn't any trouble. They had promised us a good meal and they kept their word. We had hot roast beef, mashed potatoes, string beans and carrots. But the prison cake was lousy, as usual, and the coffee stank.

Then we were put back in cells and there were no clothes. It was miserable.

Twenty-four hours later we were called in for the first prison hearings. One by one we went before the officials. 

The hearings lasted only a few minutes. I remember it well. 

Q. What's your name and number? 

A. Matthews. No. 29666.

This was the prison hearing, but I still hoped that things would be better when the impartial committee met. 

The inmate population insisted that I meet with the committee, representing them. And I did. I had been thirty days in solitary when the meeting began. 

A guard came to take me to the meeting. "All right, Matthews, you're to go before the committee. We want you to get cleaned up first." 

I wasn't going to let them make me look nice and clean. "The hell with that noise. I'm going as I am," I said.

XXI-WHERE DO I GO? 

I thought October 11, 1956 was going to be the greatest day in my life. I was going to step out in the world again. I was going to throw away a prison uniform. I was going to be my own boss and not listen to any damn guards tell me what the hell I had to do and when I was to do it.

I swore by all that is holy that I never was going to be put back in the can. I was determined to quit the rackets once and for all.

I wanted that day and I want this right now. I want peace of mind.

I want to be able to talk and not look back over my shoulder. I don't want to get bull fever again. 153

It wasn't what I expected when I got out. I got a jolt as the doors opened to let me free. The lousy guard said, "Good-bye, John. See you soon." And he laughed. 

Eight years in the can boiled over. I spit right in his face. I didn't care if they pulled me back for another ten days. I hated that punk. "Listen, you cheap bum. You're never going to see me again," I shouted. He took it.

I had made plans. Yes, I did have a solid plan. I was going to get a legit job and hook up with someone and write an exposé of the bastards-I mean the racket boys --who never came to help me when I was in trouble. 

I'm sorry for what I did, but I'm not sorry for the big money I made. I'd do it again. I never killed anyone. I just did a workmanlike muscle job for dough. 

I know damn well that I could have made money legit. 

I didn't know it when I got started. I could have been a bigger success doing honest things honestly. I led kids when I was younger, and I led a lot of real tough guys when I was older. I took orders from guys making a lot of money. Not dumb bastards-smart, crooked racket men who knew almost every angle. 

I do feel I'm responsible in part for the death of my wife and two children. If I hadn't been in the can they wouldn't have driven to see me. And they wouldn't have been killed in a car accident on the way home. That's how my wife and youngest died.

Very few people knew I was married. I wanted my wife and children to live a life apart from what I was doing. 

And I failed them. I admit that. I didn't even go to their funeral, because I would have had to go in handcuffs. I didn't want the other people to see that. Oh, sure, the top boys in Philly sent flowers and picked up the tab for the funeral. But they owed me that, to say the least.

And just a few weeks before I was to get out, my oldest 154

got run over by a drunken son of a bitch. All three were wonderful. 

I didn't have a damn thing when I got out. No one to go home to and see. I felt very much alone when I left the can.

I went back to Philly and tried to get a legit job. 

Every place I went I lasted a few weeks and then I got booted out. The law told the boss who I was. That was enough for anyone. And I know the racket boys were in back of it. 

How do I know? I saw them and they wanted me back.

Frankie Meyer talked to me in his soft, miserable manner. "John, we're glad you're back," he said. "We admit we made a mistake. We thought you'd take a fall for only six months and maybe a fine. Your old job is back, and you'll get five yards a week. And a five grand bonus." 

I almost laughed in his face. Now, he was going to help!

I hadn't forgotten eight lousy years away from good living. "You can take that job and stuff it down your lousy throat. I've had it, Frankie. I'm going to write and tell what I know," I said. 

I don't think he believed me. I'm pretty damn sure

Meyer thought I had got religion or had lost my touch.

I knew right then and there that Philly was no longer good for me, but I wanted to try my luck. Eight weeks later I knew for sure that this town was too hot for me. 

The racket boys weren't going to let me earn an honest living. I had to go back or blow. So I left. 

I figured in New York I could get a fresh start. Maybe someone would stand still long enough to listen to what 155

Somehow the word spread fast that I was in the city.
