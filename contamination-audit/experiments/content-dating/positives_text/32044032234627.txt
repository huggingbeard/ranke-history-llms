ON PUBLIC LANDS ARE FREE LANDS AVAILABLE FROM UNCLE SAM? 

No. 

There are NO FREE LANDS available from the Federal Government. No matter how a person obtains public land, it will cost money. Sometimes lands are sold to the highest bidder. They are never sold for less than their fair market value. 

At other times it is necessary to build a house, live on the land, and undertake expensive farm development, in order to obtain title. 

Sometimes public lands are not expensive, but they may often cost hundreds of dollars. Successful farm homesteading, for example, may require a capital investment of $15,000 to $50,000.

HOW CAN LANDS BE OBTAINED? 

Certain Government-owned lands can be obtained by following the laws and regulations. The specific way to obtain lands depends on the purpose for which the land will be used and sometimes on its location. 

Land may be obtained for farms, homes, and towns, for vacation cabins and business sites, and for parks and other public purposes. Before land may be acquired for one of these purposes it must be open and available under the law. It must also be examined by the Bureau of Land Management and determined to be of primary value for that purpose. 

WHAT LANDS MAY BE AVAILABLE FROM THE GOVERNMENT? 

The lands available are usually from the vacant public domain. These lands, called vacant public lands, are administered by the Bureau of Land Management in the U. S. Department of the Interior.

Vacant public lands are Federally-owned lands which have not been set aside for a special purpose. National Forests, National Parks, National Wildlife Refuges and military reser vations are NOT vacant public lands and it is not usually possible to make application for lands in any of these areas. 1

MAY I BUY SO-CALLED TAX LANDS FROM THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT? 

No. Lands which are usually called tax lands are those which have been taken over by State or local governments for nonpayment of local taxes. How you would obtain these lands would depend on the laws of the State. 

WHERE ARE THE VACANT PUBLIC LANDS? 

Most vacant public lands are west of the Rocky Mountains and in Alaska. There is very little public land that could be acquired outside the western States and virtually none in the East. What was probably the last sale of vacant public lands in the eastern States was held early in 1959. Twenty-three states contain no vacant public land at all and 11 others have almost none.

WHAT ARE THE BASIC STEPS FOR OBTAINING PUBLIC _ANDS? 

Find the land. The responsibility for finding suitable vacant public land is yours. There are no general maps or lists of available lands. Lands change status almost daily. 

See the land and examine it in person. Don't buy or apply for lands you have not seen. The Government usually requires it, and, besides, it is good business sense. 

Check the official land records in the BLM Land Office. 

Make sure the lands are open and available for the specific purpose you have in mind.

Know the law and regulations. 

Even if someone else is handling the details for you, find out for yourself what he is doing and why. Don't pay anyone to do anything until you know the official requirements. 

If you hire someone to locate the land for you, know exactly what services you are paying for. Do not pay for services you do not get or can not use!

File a complete and proper application in the BLM Land Office, if the land is open and available and you are sure you have done everything required by the law and regulations. You will also have to pay certain fees at the time you file your application. 

Wait for BLM to act on your application. BLM will process your application and examine the land and classify it. If BLM classifies the land in favor of your application, your application will be "allowed." Then and only then may you use or occupy the land. In some cases it will then be necessary for you to make certain developments before you may obtain ownership. In other cases you must simply pay the necessary money to receive title.

Expect to wait at least a few months, but often up to two years and longer. The adjudication of applications is a complex job that often requires months of detailed work.

WHAT IS MEANT BY "CLASSIFICATION” OF LANDS? 

Except under the settlement laws in Alaska, land must be classified before any action may be taken on an application concerning it. Land classification is a necessary part of managing the public domain. Active forestry and range management programs are conducted on these lands by the Bureau of Land Management. So that these programs may continue on an orderly basis and in order to develop the lands to their highest potential, the Bureau has been authorized by law to classify lands for particular uses before any disposition is made of them. 

BLM land classifiers must first decide whether the land should be retained in Federal ownership for public purposes. 

If not, they must then decide which law would provide the best kind of development for that particular tract of land. 

Classification favorable to the applicant may be followed by "allowance" of the application, provided it complies with the law in all respects. Allowance is necessary before an applicant may use the land, but it is not the same thing as transfer of title. Title is usually transferred by a "patent" after all requirements of the law have been met. 

HOW DO I FILE AN APPLICATION? 

Applications must be filed in one of the Bureau of Land Management Land Offices listed at the end of this pamphlet. 

Forms may be obtained from a land office for most types of application.

The applicant must comply with the regulations in Title 43 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Copies are located in many public libraries and may be purchased from the Superintendent of 

Documents, Government Printing Office, Washington 25, D.C., for $6.25 plus 70 cents for the annual supplement. Reprints of certain regulations are available from the Bureau of Land Management.

HOW SHOULD THE LAND BE IDENTIFIED ON THE APPLI- CATION? 

You must provide a legal description of the land. If the land is covered by a public land survey, you must find out the legal subdivision, section, township, and range. This information is available from BLM and from many State and county maps.

If the land has not been surveyed, you may identify it by describing its boundaries in relation to natural landmarks and also by giving course and distance to a surveyed public land corner.

In Alaska, where very little land has been surveyed, you may also relate the land to an official triangulation station. 

Remember--land must be surveyed before title may be obtained. 

This may sometimes mean an extra delay. 

HOW CAN I FIND THE LAND I NEED? 

The best place to start is with the land. Most people already know the area in which they are interested. When they have narrowed their search a few specific sites, they may find who owns the land by consulting the county tax rolls. A legal description of Federal lands in these specific areas may be obtained from this source. The status of individual tracts of land may be obtained from the Land Offices by furnishing exact legal descriptions of the land. When a person is interested in numerous tracts, he should go personally to the Land Office to examine the records.

Many people have been able to obtain public lands by following announcements of sales or openings of lands to application which are posted in the Land Offices and often published in the Federal Register.

Copies of the Federal Register are available in most libraries or may be purchased by subscription from the Superintendent of Documents, U. S. Government Printing Office, Washington 25, D.C., for $15 a year or $1.50 per month. The

Federal Register is published five times a week--Tuesday through Saturday.

ARE THERE ANY GENERAL MAPS OR LISTS OF "AVAIL- ABLE" VACANT PUBLIC LANDS? 

No not in the usual sense of the word "available."

Each Land Office maintains detailed records and maps of all public lands within its jurisdiction. These records show the present status of the land--whether it is still owned by the Government and who (if anyone) is using it and for what purpose. 

These records cover all lands, some of which might be available for a specific use or purpose.

Current maps for distribution are not made because lands in some areas change in status quite rapidly. The job of locating suitable available land is yours.

MAY I HIRE SOMEONE TO FIND LAND FOR ME? 

Yes. Land locators and filing services have offices in many western cities. For a fee they will examine the status of lands on the public land records, inspect the characteristics of 2 public lands on the ground, fill out application blanks, and prepare supplementary information that may be required with the application. 1

However, be careful. None of the land locator services is licensed or regulated by the Federal Government. Some unscrupulous operators have led some people to believe that hiring them will result in obtaining Federal land, even though most of the applications they file will be rejected. Before you hire a land locator, you should be sure that you are dealing with a reliable person and that you understand fully the extent of the services which he will provide.

In the real estate business, realtors do not usually earn their fee until the final sale has been completed. Until that time, a person only makes a deposit of "earnest" money to show his good faith. If the realtor fails to complete the sale, he will return the money deposited. People doing business with land locators or filing services would do well to obtain similar agreements. A person then would not lose his money if his application is later denied. Such arrangements, of course, have to be worked out directly with the locator or filing service. 

The Government does not require or encourage the use of land locator services. All applications received by the Bureau of Land Management are considered on their own merits. No one would receive any kind of special treatment or consideration simply because he had filed his application through a land locator. All BLM information, data, records, maps, and other materials which are available to land locators and filing services are also available to anyone who is interested in filing an application. Land locators receive no special or "inside" information that is not available to anyone else.

MAY I OBTAIN AN ISLAND FROM THE FEDERAL GOVER MENT? 

Nearly all of the Federally-owned islands passed in State or private ownership during the last century. The island which remain are often masses of rock, inaccessible, unsurveye subject to periodic flooding, or otherwise unavailable. 

If an individual knows of an island which he believes be vacant public land, he may find its status by taking or sen ing the complete legal description of the island to the Bureau Land Management's Land Office for that area. Should the islan be vacant public land, he could then apply for it under an a propriate land law. However, most islands are sold as recreati areas to State and local public agencies.

CAN I GET LAND FOR A FARM? 

Probably not. Nearly all the land which could be farme economically has already passed into private ownership. Mos of the remaining land outside Alaska is too dry or otherwis unsuitable for cultivation.

Some arid lands can be reclaimed, although at very hig cost.

The Government may classify up to 320 acres as a far unit if an adequate water supply and a means of irrigating th whole farm can be proven. An individual may later buy this lan for $1.25 an acre after he has developed a permanent irrigatio system for all of the farm and has brought one-eighth of it unde cultivation. Costs of such development run as high as $40,00 or more.

The Bureau of Reclamation from time to time makes available public lands for farm units that have a supply of irrigation water already developed. In order to qualify for a reclamation farm, an applicant must have at least two years Farming experience, minimum capital assets of from $4,500 to $8,500 and good character and health. 

IS THERE FARM LAND IN ALASKA? 

Yes, there is. However, homesteading in Alaska is expensive. 

A citizen over 21 may "homestead" up to 160 acres of vacant public lands in Alaska. After paying a small fee, he may eam title to the land by building a home, living on the land for seven months in each of the first three years, and by clearing and cultivating at least one-eighth of the tract. 

Costs of clearing the land, building roads, and purchasing supplies and equipment are very high. To develop a farm that will sustain a family in Alaska may require a capital investment of $20,000 to $50,000. Development loans close to this amount are not available at the present time. 

Farming in Alaska also presents other difficulties. A short growing season and long, cold winters limit the crops which may be grown. Rugged, undeveloped country makes access to cultivable lands difficult. Isolation is often a serious social handicap.

MAY MY COMMUNITY BUY PUBLIC LANDS FOR A PARK? 

Yes. The Government may sell up to 640 acres of vacant public land to States, local governments, nonprofit organizations and associations for recreational and other public purposes. 

Areas also may be leased on a long-term basis. There is no acreage limitation for such leases. The Government usually gives preference to public agencies that wish to purchase or lease public land for recreation or public purposes.

MAY I GRAZE CATTLE AND SHEEP ON VACANT PUBLIC LANDS? 

Vacant public lands are used by ranchers under leases annual licenses, and long-term permits. The amount of forage that may be used under these agreements depends upon the grazing capacity of the land. An applicant must also hold ranch property that depends on Federal range to support a year-round livestock operation.

Only ranchers who own ranch property may qualify for use of these lands for grazing. They pay fees for this privilege. 

WHAT ABOUT SUB-SURFACE MINERALS? 

Congress intended for people to acquire minerals under the mineral laws only. Land laws are not to be used to get valuable minerals. Any known deposits on land acquired fo a home, summer cottage, or farm are reserved to the 

United States.

By the same token, the staking of mining claims is not a way to obtain land for a home or summer cabin. The purpose is to develop valuable minerals. Anyone who uses a mining claim for non-mining purposes is violating the law. 

Whether or not you can secure rights to any minerals that might be in the land depends on the particular law under which you obtain the land.

MAY | PROSPECT FOR MINERALS AND STAKE A CLAIM? 

Yes. To encourage mineral development, the Government allows people to prospect on vacant public lands and to remove minerals without advance permission. If you make a valuable discovery of minerals, you may stake a claim to the immediate area.

To stake a claim, you must mark the area on the ground and file the necessary papers with the county in which you find the minerals. This will give you the exclusive right to mine the minerals. Later, you may obtain title to the land if you prove the value of the discovery, comply with other requirements, and pay the necessary fees.

WHAT ARE MINERAL LEASES? 

Coal, oil, gas, sodium, potassium, phosphate, and--in Louisiana and New Mexico--sulphur may be extracted only under leasing agreements with the Bureau of Land Management. 

To prospect for coal, sodium, potassium, and sulphur, you must first obtain a prospecting permit. Any discovery made would give the finder a preference right in obtaining a lease. 10

Oil and gas prospecting is done by obtaining a noncomtitive lease on lands which have no known oil and gas tential. Where there are known mineral values, leases will be ued to the highest competitive bidder.
