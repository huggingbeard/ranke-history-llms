HOMESTEADING Past and Present 

In the early days of our country's history most of the western United States was unexplored and undeveloped wilderness. 

Most of the people lived along the eastern seaboard. There were only a few small cowns in the West and not very many people ived there.

California. This discovery brought many new people to the West who wanted to hare in the new wealth that had been ound.

The Public Domain 

When all these lands became part of the United States, they became the property of all the people. The Federal Government owned the lands and was responsible for taking care of them. The name given to these Federal lands is public domain. 

At its greatest size, the public domain included almost all of the lands north and west of the Mississippi and Ohio Rivers (except Texas) plus Alabama, Mississippi, Florida, and Alaska. This vast land area covered about 1,800,000,000 acres. That is almost equal to the total area of the present United States not counting Alaska. 

So that you can better understand where these lands are and how much land was involved, a map is included which shows all of these additions to the public domain. 

There is no public domain land in Texas because that State kept its public land when it became a State.

The Homestead Law

In other countries it had not usually been possible for people to own their own land. In 1862 the Congress passed a law which made it possible for a large number of people to do this. The Land Is Settled 

Since 1862 people have earned the right to own more than 1,400,000 homesteads on over 247 million acres that had been public domain. This is equal to more than 10 times the size of the State of Indiana. If all of the homestead lands were laid side by side, they would cover a broad strip of land more than 125 miles wide all the way from 

Boston, Mass., to San Francisco, Calif. It is a very interesting fact that about twothirds of all homesteading was done in the first 30 years of the 20th century—not in the very early days before 1900.

The exciting stories of the early pioneers who settled west of the Mississippi have been told and retold many times. The early settlers had to work hard to get their land and often endured great hardships and sacrifices.

Most of the early settlers were interested in finding homestead land in order to build farms. Much of the Federal land open to homesteading was good farmland and, of course, people settled there first. And this was just what the Government wanted them to do to build good farms and make good homes for their families.

Today's Public Domain 

Many people have asked the question, "Well, if the public domain land is no longer much good for homesteading, what is it being used for?" There are many ways in which the public lands and their natural resources are being developed for the good of the whole country. 

Part of the public lands have been set

aside for the enjoyment and use of all the people. These include the national forests, national parks, and national monuments (such as the Homestead National Monu ment in Nebraska). Other lands are used by the Army, Navy, Marines, and Air Force for camps and bases and as places to train soldiers and test new military equipment

These lands are called reservations or with drawals, and they are not usually open to homesteading.

The National Land Reserve 

In 1934 the "unreserved and unappro priated public domain" was closed to in discriminate settlement and use by the' 

Taylor Grazing Act. That year, and the next, the President issued Executive Order which closed the lands to most types of pri vate acquisition. That does not mean the lands are not used. They are still open to the staking of mining claims, and for public hunting, fishing, camping, and other recrea tion. The lands may be covered with valu able timber. They may have grass on them for livestock and wildlife to eat. Or the may contain valuable minerals—oil, gold lead, or uranium.

The public lands are today regarded as a "national land reserve." People cannot just go out and move onto the lands as they did in pioneer days. Except for settlement homesteads in Alaska, a homesteader must get permission to use the land before he can begin to farm it.

Alaska State Lands 

When Alaska became a State on January 3, 1959, it received from Congress a land grant of over 103 million acres of Federal lands. These lands will help Alaska pay for its new State government and State programs. As the State selects the lands it. wants, farmers may also be able to get land from the State government. The State has 

25 years to select all of the lands it was granted.

Other Alaska Programs 

As a result of the selections of public lands which Alaska will make, the Bureau of Land Management will have a big job of surveying in Alaska. Before the Government transfers land to other people it must lay out the boundaries of the land on the ground.

These boundaries are the familiar checkerboard pattern of townships and sections. 

Another big job the Bureau has in Alaska is protecting the millions of acres of forests from fire. These forests are often far from 18

any roads and towns. To help meet the problems of putting fires out before they get too big, the Bureau uses trained fire fighters who jump from airplanes with parachutes. 

These men are called smokejumpers.

Getting Public Lands Today 

Nowadays homesteading is not the only way a person can become the owner of public lands. In 1938 the Congress passed a law which made it possible for anyone to obtain a small plot of land (usually 2 to 5 acres) on which to build a cabin for vacationing or a house in which to live permanently. It could also be used for a store or other business. Under this law it is not de necessary for a man to farm the land in Jorder to become the owner of it. The Gov- 

Sernment, however, does not give these lands away free. Sometimes they are not expensive, but often they are sold for many hundreds of dollars. 

So you can see that while homesteading is still going on, it does not mean the same thing today that it did nearly a century ago. 

The days are nearly gone when plenty of good land was available to almost anybody who wanted to farm it. Except for a cer- @tain amount of "pioneering" in Alaska, homesteading is dying out.
