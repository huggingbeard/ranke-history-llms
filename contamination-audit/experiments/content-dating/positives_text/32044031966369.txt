THE BILL OF RIGHTS 

(In the Amendments to the Constitution) Here are given the principal guarantees of the Bill of Rights with brief comment showing what the Supreme Court has done in interpreting them or limiting their application. Only the essential holdings of the Court are mentioned here.

Article I. Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof;

Congress may not interfere with religious beliefs, but it may prohibit religious practices deemed harmful to society, such as polygamy among the Mormons. or abridging the freedom of speech,

Congress may punish utterances if there is a "clear and present danger" that they will result in action harmful to the country.

Peaceful picketing (as a form of free speech) is protected both against criminal penalties and injunction. or of the press,

Neither Congress nor the states may censor publications in advance except for obscenity. Printed matter may be punished like utterances. Congress may deny mailing privileges to printed matter deemed harmful. 

Leaflet distribution on the streets may not be banned or taxed. or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, No one may be punished for participating in a peaceful assembly even when called by an organization charged with violation of law.

Ordinances prohibiting all street meetings are void. and to petition the government for a redress of grievances,

No issue has arisen under this clause. (While the First Amendment is a restraint on the Federal Government, the Supreme Court has held that all the rights there specified are also protected against invasion by the states under the due process clause of Amendment XIV.)

Article IV. The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated,

This restraint affects only federal officers, and covers "unreasonable" searches. Evidence obtained in illegal searches cannot be used in a criminal trial. Tapping telephone wires to get evidence is not an illegal "search." 

But evidence obtained by wire-tapping made illegal by statute cannot be used in a federal court. and no warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, "} Warrants must show the commission of a crime and the nature of the property to be seized. 

Article V. No person shall be held to answer to a capital, or otherwise infamous crime, unless on a presentment or indictment of a grand jury • • This is a restraint only on federal courts, covering crimes punishable by a penitentiary sentence or hard labor. nor shall any person be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life and limb; 

A person once tried and acquitted cannot again be

tried for the same crime, but may be tried for different crimes arising out of the same act. nor shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself, This prohibits the government from forcing a defendant, or even a witness, to incriminate himself, or to produce his documents. nor be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law; . . .

This provision insures a fair trial. 

Article VI. In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial by an impartial jury . . .

This restraint on federal courts does not prevent Congress from permitting the trial of minor offenses in the District of Columbia without juries. and to have the assistance of counsel for his defense. 

The denial of the right to counsel, in a federal court vitiates a conviction. In a state court counsel may be denied if defendant be not prejudiced. But the right to counsel can be waived at the trial or by a voluntary plea of guilty.

Article VIII. Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishment inflicted..

This amendment applies only to the courts of the United States and not to the courts of the various states. 

There have been very few decisions dealing with the subject of excessive bail. The general rule is that the capacity of the prisoner to give bail is a determining element in fixing it, although the enormity of the offense can also be taken into consideration. 

The provision with regard to cruel and unusual punishments was intended to prevent punishment such as

burning at the stake, and other medieval forms of torture. Attempts to invoke this provision against the use of electrocution failed. There is no indication in the books of what would be considered an excessive fine. 

Article XIII. Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime, whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction. 

This restraint operates on Congress, on the states and on individuals. It outlaws forced labor contracts, except as to seamen.

Article XIV. 

No state shall make or enforce any law which shall abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens of the United States; 

This protection of citizens against discriminatory action by state authority affects only rights of national, not state, citizenship-such as the right to travel. Attempts by Congress to protect Negroes from discrimination by private individuals were declared beyond the scope of this amendment. But Congress may prevent such discrimination in interstate commerce. 

The right to discuss national issues in public places is protected by this provision. nor shall any state deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law;

This provision to insure fair trials (as in the Fifth 

Amendment), has been widened to void laws violating fundamental rights,-freedom of religion, speech, press or assembly, and freedom to teach. 

State action outlawing leaflet distribution, peaceful picketing or peaceful solicitation for religious purposes is a denial of due process.

The Court at one time voided laws outlawing "yellow dog" contracts but later upheld laws prohibiting discrimination against unions. 1

nor deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. 

This restraint on the states does not require absolute equality. If racial segregation laws give equal accommodations they are valid. 

Article XV. The right of citizens of the United 

States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any state on account of race, color or previous condition of servitude.

This restraint on the states does not prevent property or educational qualifications for voting and its intent is thus commonly frustrated. Attempts by Congress to punish for interference with voting have been declared unconstitutional, unless limited to national elections. (The Nineteenth Amendment in effect added the word "sex" to the Fifteenth.)

The following provisions of the original Constitution affect civil rights.

Article I, Section 9. .... The privilege of the writ of habeas corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it.

This is a prohibition only on Congress, not on the states. 

No bill of attainder or ex post facto law shall be passed.

A bill of attainder is a law which punishes a person without a trial, such as prohibiting persons who had engaged in rebellion from carrying on their professions. 

The prohibition against ex post facto laws (making criminal an act not criminal when committed) does not affect aliens in deportation proceedings, since deportation is a civil action. (Both these prohibitions are, in Article I, Section 10, made binding on the states.)

The Supreme Court and Civil Liberties 

'HE debates in 1937 on the President's proposal to of the least likely sources, loud praise of the Court as protector of civil and religious liberties. And from other sources which often appeal to the Supreme Court to safeguard civil liberties, came the demand that the Court be deprived of all power to challenge the constitutionality of laws. The more extreme of these groups believed that the Court had accomplished little substantial protection of civil liberties against legislative encroachment. It is important therefore that the actual record of the Court be examined.

The Supreme Court has been confronted chiefly with two problems: What rights are included within the protection of the federal constitution? To what extent, if at all, can rights so included be impaired? Sometimes these questions have arisen in considering the constitutionality of legislation, both state and federal, sometimes in reviewing acts of administrative, executive and judicial officials. Wide differences of opinion have often arisen within the Court itself in the solution of these problems. 

It is impossible to cover briefly the entire field, and what is presented here must be taken as illustrative. It should be kept in mind, however, that not all civil rights are protected by the Constitution. Some of the rights protected against invasion by the Federal Government are not protected against invasion by the states, and vice versa. 

In Time of War or Civil Disturbance 

URING the Civil War, the military authorities with the approval of President Lincoln, persistently ignored civil rights. They also ignored court writs, and, as Chief Justice Taney noted, the courts were able to do nothing more than protest. However after the war, the

Supreme Court in Ex Parte Milligan held that trials of civilians conducted by military commissions in areas not actually involved in warfare were void. The Court in that case said: "Those great and good men foresaw that troublous times would arise, when rulers and people would become restive under restraint, and seek by sharp and decisive measures to accomplish ends deemed just and proper; and that the principles of constitutional liberty would be in peril, unless established by irrepealable law. 

The history of the world has taught them that what was done in the past might be attempted in the future. The Constitution of the 

United States is a law for rulers and people, equally in war and in peace, and covers with the shield of its protection all classes of men, at all times, and under all circumstances. No doctrine, involving more pernicious consequences, was ever invented by the wit of man than that any of its provisions can be suspended during any of the great exigencies of government." 

The decision aroused a storm of protest on the part of those who had been most vociferous supporters of the war, but it has been hailed by later generations as a landmark in civil liberties. It has had, however, little actual influence on the law; and the spirit which animated it was ignored by the Court during the excitement of the First World War in cases we shall discuss under the head ings of Freedom of Speech and of the Press. 

The Milligan case also laid down the rule that the 

President alone might not suspend the writ of habeas corpus. Since the Civil War, the writ has not been suspended in the United States, but during the Second World War the military authorities suspended the writ. in 

Hawaii immediately after Pearl Harbor.

Early in 1943 the military permitted the Hawaiian civil courts to function, but continued the ban on the issuance of writs of habeas corpus. On application by interned persons Judge Metzger issued the writ and ordered their release. The commanding general ignored the writ and was adjudicated in contempt. The impasse was solved by the removal of the petitioners to the main- 10

land and their release there, by the withdrawal of the ban on the issuance of writs of habeas corpus and by the President's pardoning the general on the contempt conviction.

Later conflict between the civilian courts and the military in Hawaii occurred because of the insistence of the military that criminal offenses should be tried in military courts. Judge Metzger ruled that this could not be done and ordered several convicted persons released. This time the military obeyed the order, but appealed. The Circuit 

Court of Appeals in San Francisco upheld the military; the cases are now before the Supreme Court. 

The most important question arising out of the Second World War was presented by the evacuation from the entire West Coast of all persons of Japanese ancestry, citizens as well as aliens, and their subsequent detention in camps. This was done without either the declaration of martial law or the suspension of the writ of habeas corpus. Violation of the military orders issued to accomplish this operation was made a criminal offense. 

In the Hirabayashi case the Supreme Court upheld the right of the military to exercise control over civilians and to discriminate against citizens of Japanese ancestry to the extent of imposing a curfew on persons of Japanese ancestry. Chief Justice Stone said that the power to wage war was "the power to wage war successfully," that such power extended to everything which substantially affects the war and its progress. The Court concluded that the military had a right to determine that a curfew "was a protective measure necessary to meet the threat of sabotage and espionage." The Chief Justice justified the singling out of the Japanese because "residents having ethnic affiliations with an invading enemy may be a greater source of danger than those of different ancestry." Justice Murphy expressed the view that that case went "to the very brink of constitutional power."

The Court applied these principles also in sustaining a conviction for violation of an evacuation order. This 11

time Justices Roberts, Murphy, and Jackson dissented.

However, in the Endo case, the Court unanimously refused to sanction the detention of a citizen of Japanese ancestry who was conceded to be loyal. The extent to which such citizens may be detained when their loyalty is in question and the procedure by which that loyalty should be tested have not as yet received judicial clarification.

Evacuation orders directed by the military to individual citizens of German origin on the East Coast fared differently. Two federal judges held them void because the government had adduced no evidence to show imminent danger. That issue never reached any higher court. 

Ordinarily federal courts will not pass on the propriety of action taken by the states in industrial disputes or other civil disturbances. Thus the Supreme Court held that no constitutional right had been invaded when certain labor leaders were detained by the military after a declaration of martial law because disorders were anticipated in connection with the labor dispute. 

In 1932, in Sterling v. Constantin, the Supreme Court reviewed a state of martial law declared by the Governor of Texas under which he tried to enforce a certain method of oil production. This case laid down the important rule that where the declaration of martial law was arbitrary Federal Courts would review the action taken by the state. 

In Aid of the Negro?

FTER the Civil War Congress attempted to give meaning to the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, all then just adopted. It sought to protect Negroes from discrimination by their former masters, and especially to protect them from the tyranny of the Ku Klux Klan. Congress enacted laws which forbade discrimination against Negroes in places of public entertainment, such as inns and mail coaches, and which made criminal any interference with their basic rights. 12

But largely to no avail, for the Supreme Court persistently interfered. Hiding behind verbalisms and turning its face from the realities of the post-war South, the majority of the Court denied power to Congress to enact most of these laws. Convictions of persons who beat up and terrorized the freedmen were reversed in Cruikshank v. United States. And convictions of those who persisted in discriminatory practices were set aside in the Civil 

Rights cases. 

Two legal questions were involved. Could Congress punish action by private individuals as well as by state officials? Could Congress take under its protection fundamental rights, such as the right to vote or freely to assemble? Both questions were answered in the negative, the first because the 14th and 15th amendments imposed prohibitions on the "states," not on the people; the second because the rights Congress sought to protect were considered attributes of state citizenship, not of national citizenship—and therefore not protected by the "privileges and immunities" clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. In spite of a vigorous dissent in the Civil Rights cases by Justice Harlan the Court has steadily adhered to the views which were then expressed by the majority. 

The effect of these decisions has been to prevent Congress from punishing wrongs done to Negroes by private individuals. The expectation of the Court that the rights of persons aggrieved "may presumably be vindicated by resort to the laws of the state for redress" has not been fulfilled. But the Court has upheld Congressional efforts to prevent discrimination when limited to interstate commerce.

And the Court, early in 1945, upheld federal prosecution of state officials who had conspired to bring false charges against Negroes and who had used violence on them while in jail-in one instance resulting in death. 

However, the Supreme Court reversed the conviction of the state officials because the jury had not been sufficiently instructed that the defendants could not be found guilty 13

unless they knew that rights under the Federal Constitution were being infringed. It remains to be seen whether juries will convict under such instructions. IN "Equal Protection”

N spite of the equal protection clause, the Court has permitted discrimination against Negroes on the principle that segregation is permissible where equal facilities are afforded both races. Thus state laws compelling separate railroad accommodations for Negroes and whites have been repeatedly approved. And while there is no 

Supreme Court decision on separate schools, many state courts even in the North have upheld this kind of segregation. The Supreme Court also approved laws fixing a more severe punishment for adultery between the races than for adultery between persons of the same race,on the theory that since the guilty person of each race was punished alike no violation of the constitutional command of equality had occurred.

These decisions may readily serve as grounds for approval of other sorts of racial discrimination. Indeed the 

Court has upheld state laws prohibiting alien ownership of land. And many courts have upheld laws forbidding marriage between Negroes and Whites. The United

States Supreme Court has interfered only when no pretense of equality was preserved in the law, as in housing segregation, or where the law was administered in a discriminatory manner.

But in the recent case of Missouri ex rel Gaines v. 

Canada, the Court compelled the University of Missouri to admit a Negro as a law student, or set up a separate Negro law school and held it to be a denial of equal protection to require an applicant to go out of the state for such education, even at the state's expense. 

In connection with jury service, however, the Court has upheld the right to equality persistently, although with little result in actual practice. At first the southern 14

states by law forbade Negroes from serving on juries. 

Such laws were promptly held void. But Negroes were nevertheless kept off juries by discriminatory practices, which at first the states made no attempt to deny. While in such situations the Court reversed convictions of Negroes, later it refused to interfere when the state denied discrimination and where no testimony was offered to establish the discrimination. The Court made it very plain that the Constitution did not require that any Negro actually sit on the jury. It required only that persons be not excluded from service because of their race.

The first substantial improvement came from the Scottsboro case. There the state denied that there had been any discrimination. When the defense showed that many qualified Negroes were available for jury service and that in a generation none had been called, the jury commissioners testified that no discrimination had been intended. But the Supreme Court refused to accept this testimony and unanimously concluded that discrimination had been practiced. As the result of this decision 

Negroes are now added to the jury rolls and occasionally even sit, at least on grand juries. 

In Smith v. Texas the Court unanimously reversed a conviction for rape because of improper exclusion of Negroes from the grand jury which indicted him-even though there were some Negroes on the list. Mr. Justice 

Black pointed out that chance alone could not have brought about the listing of so few Negroes, nor their being so placed on the list that they would practically never be called for service. He said: "If there has been discrimination, whether accomplished ingeniously or ingenuously, the conviction cannot stand." But recently a majority of the Court found no improper discrimination in the determination of a jury commissioner that there should be one Negro on every grand jury, and no more. 

It should also be noted that even this limited interference by the Court with discrimination against Negroes or other minorities has taken place only when the dis- 15

crimination resulted from state law or official action. 

When discrimination resulted from private action Congress has not been permitted to interfere, and the Court will not.

Thus the Court has repeatedly refused to pass on the validity of covenants against the sale of land to Negroes. 

And on the day on which it handed down the Scottsboro decision, the Court in Grovey v. Townsend approved the denial by the Democratic party in Texas of Negro participation in its primaries,-which in effect denied Negroes effective participation in elections. For in Texas winning the Democratic primary is equivalent to winning the election.

Fortunately, the Court several years later overruled the Grovey decision and recognized that a state's regulation of primary elections made these part of the state electoral machinery in which discrimination based on race could not be tolerated. Mr. Justice Reed said: "The United States is a constitutional democracy. Its organic law grants to all citizens a right to participate in the choice of elected officials without restriction by any state because of race. 

This grant to the people of the opportunity for choice is not to be nullified by a state through casting its electoral process in a form which permits a private organization to practice racial discrimination in the election. Constitutional rights would be of little value if they could be thus indirectly denied. "The privilege of membership in a party may be, as this Court said in Grovey v. Townsend, 295 U.S. 45, no concern of a state.

But when, as here, that privilege is also the essential qualification for voting in a primary, to select nominees for a general election, the state makes the action of the party the action of the state." 

The decision aroused a storm of denunciation in the south, participated in by members of Congress, governors and others who proclaimed that "white supremacy" must be preserved. They threatened that the decision would be disregarded or circumvented. South Carolina promptly repealed all of its laws relating to primary elections. On the other hand, the Attorney General 16

has been urged to proceed criminally against election officials who continue to deny primary ballots to Negroes. 

It remains to be seen whether the decision of the Supreme Court will become effective and whether the Court will confine the decision to states where the election laws regulate the primaries or extend it to all instances in which the primary determines the final election. Indeed, the language of Justice Reed which we have quoted, lends itself to the interpretation that since the franchise is an essential attribute of democracy no private discrimination can be permitted which results in the denial of the right of franchise to anyone on account of race. So construed, the decision would apply to all primary elections, regardless of the nature of the election machinery or the dominance of a single party. 

Slavery or Peonage

URSUANT to the Thirteenth Amendment which pro hibits slavery or involuntary servitude, Congress enacted legislation punishing anyone who violated this prohibition, directly or indirectly. This legislation was invoked to punish various devices for enforcing labor contracts. A person fined for a petty offense would agree to work out the fine for someone who came forward to pay it. Sometimes an agreement to work for a given period was made in consideration of an advance payment. State laws punished as criminal any violation of such contracts.

The Supreme Court of the United States has declared such devices void under the Thirteenth Amendment.

It has upheld the punishment of persons assisting in the enforcement of such contracts and has also reversed convictions in state courts for breaking such contracts. An exception has however been made in the case of seamen's contracts because compulsion has from earliest history accompanied employment at sea-an exception fortunately abandoned by Congress. 17

THE Freedom of Speech 

HE most important issues in modern civil liberties grew out of the First World War. Then for the first time the Court was called upon to consider the meaning of freedom of speech and of the press. The Alien and 

Sedition laws of 1798 never reached the Court. During the Civil War repression had been accomplished by executive act, not Congressional legislation. As we have seen, the only case which reached the Supreme Court as the result of the Civil War related to the powers of military tribunals, not to questions of free speech. 

In 1917 Congress passed the Espionage Act which punished any one who made a statement calculated to impede recruiting or to produce disaffection among the troops. Many persons were prosecuted under this law for utterances supposed to have that effect. Both judges and juries reacted to war hysteria; in consequence there were many convictions with excessive sentences.

The Supreme Court took the position that while expression of opinion must be entirely free from prior censorship, the government might punish utterances where there was a "clear and present danger" that advocacy of action would ripen into action itself. The decisions on this principle were unanimous. Justice Holmes said in his opinion in the leading case of Schenck v. United 

States: 

"The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent. It is a question of proximity and degree. When a nation is at war many things that might be said in time of peace are such a hindrance to its effort that their utterance will not be endured so long as men fight, and that no court could regard them as protected by any constitutional right." 

Presumably Justice Holmes believed that individuals were afforded substantial protection from unjust prose- 18

cution by the formula which he created. Actually it proved to be of little practical value during periods of hysteria. This fact was soon illustrated in cases in which the majority of the Court upheld convictions from which Justices Holmes and Brandeis dissented because they believed the rule was improperly applied. The most shocking of these was the case of Abrams and others, in which sentences of twenty years were imposed for the issuance of pamphlets condemning American intervention against the new Soviet Government of Russia. Mr. 

Justice Holmes in his dissenting opinion said: "Congress certainly cannot forbid all effort to change the mind of the country. Now nobody can suppose that the surreptitious publishing of a silly leaflet by an unknown man, without more, would present any immediate danger that its opinions would hinder the success of the government arms or have any appreciable tendency to do so. * * * * "Persecution for the expression of opinions seems to me perfectly logical. If you have no doubt of your premises or your power and want a certain result with all your heart you naturally express your wishes in law and sweep away all opposition. "To allow opposition by speech seems to indicate that you think the speech impotent, as when a man says that he has squared the circle, or that you do not care wholeheartedly for the result, or that you doubt either your power or your premises. "But when men have realized that time has upset many fighting faiths, they may come to believe even more than they believe the very foundations of their own conduct that the ultimate good desired is better reached by free trade in ideas,—that the best test of truth is the power of the thought to get itself accepted in the competition of the market; and that truth is the only ground upon which their wishes safely can be carried out. That, at any rate, is the theory of our Constitution. "It is an experiment, as all life is an experiment. Every year, if not every day, we have to wager our salvation upon some prophecy based upon imperfect knowledge. While that experiment is part of our system I think that we should be eternally vigilant against attempts to check the expression of opinions that we loathe and 19

believe to be fraught with death, unless they so imminently threaten immediate interference with the lawful and pressing purposes of the law that an immediate check is required to save the country."

The Court also upheld the validity of a Minnesota war-time law barring the teaching of pacifism (Gilbert v. Minnesota). That case presented a new issue; was freedom of speech guaranteed against state action? Those who contended that it was, argued that this freedom is so fundamental as to be within "due process of law" as guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment. In a long line of decisions the Supreme Court had expanded the meaning of the due process clause from its original conception of procedure alone, to embrace various liberties, particularly the so-called "liberty of contract." But until 

1920 it had regularly decided that the guarantees of personal liberties, contained in the Bill of Rights were not binding on the states as part of "due process." Such had been its decisions in relation to prosecution by indictment, freedom from compulsory self-incrimination, freedom from unreasonable searches and seizures, indeed even as to trial by jury.

On freedom of speech doubt had been expressed in earlier cases. Even in this Minnesota case both the majority and minority passed over the issue without decision. But Mr. Justice Brandeis stated in his dissent that he had difficulty in believing "that the liberty guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment includes only liberty to acquire and enjoy property."

The question so left in doubt was finally resolved by the Court in the Gitlow case (1925). It is now definitely settled that the Fourteenth Amendment protects all fundamental rights, including specifically freedom of speech and of the press. But in spite of that view, the 

Court went further in upholding restrictive state laws in the field of opinion than it did in the Espionage Act cases. 

After the First World War the increase in radicalism resulted in the passage of many state sedition and crimi- 20

nal syndicalism laws which prohibited the advocacy by speech or writing of the overthrow of the government or any industrial change by force and violence. The hysteria of the period resulted in many convictions for the expression of radical opinion. Especially were the 

I.W.W. and the Left Wing Socialists under attack.

The first of these cases, that of Gitlow v. New York (1925), arose under an old law enacted in New York in 1901 after the assassination of President McKinley. 

The majority of the Court reached the conclusion that the Left Wing Manifesto, which Gitlow had written, advocated illegal action. The state therefore was entitled to punish him. The opinion ignored the "clear and present danger" rule of the Espionage Act cases. Mr. Justice 

Sanford held that the state was not required to wait until the utterances led to actual disturbances or "imminent and immediate danger" of the state's destruction. Following the general rule that a statute should be presumed to be valid, the majority concluded that the criminal anarchy law was not arbitrary or unreasonable as an exercise of the police power. Justices Holmes and Brandeis, dissenting, expressed the opinion that no present danger to the state could exist in view of the very small number of persons who shared Gitlow's views, and the indefiniteness of the time when action might result. 

A few years later (1927) the Supreme Court reaffirmed these doctrines with certain additions in the Whitney case from California. Anita Whitney was charged with having taken an active part in organizing the Communist Labor Party in California. Because that Party was found to have advocated violent revolution the conviction was affirmed. The opinion of the Court even intimated that mere membership in such a Party might constitutionally be punished. In many states convictions have rested on that fact alone. But the Supreme Court of the 

United States has not yet been required to decide that narrow issue. 

In the Whitney case Justices Holmes and Brandeis 21

concurred on technical grounds, but Justice Brandeis in an eloquent opinion took pains to point out that a defendant should have the right to submit to the jury the question whether in fact there was a "clear and present danger" that harm would actually result to the state from his acts.

In one case, Fiske v. Kansas, the Court reversed the conviction of an I.W.W. organizer under a criminal syndicalism law because no evidence had been introduced to show that the I.W.W. actually advocated violent change. In the Stromberg case, part of California's law prohibiting any display of the red flag was held void because it punished such display as "hostility" to government and might therefore punish hostility manifested in perfectly lawful ways.

The constitutional protection afforded free speech received further clarification in the Herndon case (1937).

Herndon, a young Negro Communist organizer working in Georgia, was convicted under an old statute punishing "insurrection," unused until the depression. The only charge against Herndon was that he had distributed literature which advocated the violent overthrow of the government. Herndon's attorneys challenged the conviction on the ground that the literature he distributed was entirely innocuous. They argued that the evidence was insufficient to justify conviction under the "clear and present danger" rule.

The Supreme Court, after first refusing to interfere on technical grounds, reversed the conviction of Herndon because the Georgia statute as construed and applied by the Georgia courts was an unconstitutional interference with freedom of speech and freedom of assemblage, and also because it laid down an insufficient standard of guilt. Mr. Justice Roberts for the majority, came to the conclusion that no insurrectionary matter had been distributed or advocated. The conservative minority held that one pamphlet dealing with the Negro question, containing language of "revolution," left no doubt that the 22

use of force was intended. For, said Justice Van Devanter, "all know that such measures could not be effected otherwise." The majority pointed out that even if it might be argued that the aims there set forth were unlawful there was no proof that Herndon had approved of them. 

The opinion of the majority is important because it refers to the "clear and present" danger test with approval, and overrules the contention that the "dangerous tendency" of speech may be a basis of guilt. Any abridgement of free speech, says Justice Roberts, "must find its justification in a reasonable apprehension of danger to organized government. The judgment of the legislature is not unfettered. The limitation upon individual liberty must have appropriate relation to the safety of the state."

Considering the contention that the statute was too vague, Justice Roberts said: "The statute, as construed and applied, amounts merely to a dragnet which may enmesh anyone who agitates for a change of government if a jury can be persuaded that he ought to have foreseen his words would have some effect in the future conduct of others."

Recently the witnesses of Jehovah have been the most vociferous supplicants to the Court to vindicate their Constitutional rights (Cases involving these witnesses which specifically relate to religious freedom we will consider later in the section on that subject). In two cases involving these witnesses the Supreme Court marked out the line between utterances that can and those that cannot be punished. In the Cantwell case the Court reversed a Connecticut conviction for disorderly conduct based on the playing of a phonograph record which attacked the Catholic Church; in the Chaplinsky case it affirmed a New Hampshire conviction for the same offense based on a statement calling a policeman a fascist and a racketeer. The difference was that in the latter case the statement was made to the person maligned and was likely to cause reprisals, whereas in the former case the circumstances did not justify an inference that a breach of the peace might result. 23

During the Second World War the Supreme Court has made a far better showing than it did twenty-five years before. The Court unanimously struck down a 

Mississippi law which punished the distribution of literature "calculated to encourage disloyalty" in cases involving witnesses of Jehovah who expressed anti-war opinions. Justice Roberts noted that there was no proof that defendants had an "evil or sinister purpose" or that their actions threatened "any clear and present danger" to the government. And, by a five-to-four vote, the Court reversed the conviction of one Hartzel under the Espionage Act for distributing pro-Nazi, anti-English and anti- Roosevelt material. The majority could find nothing in the pamphlets or the method of their distribution to justify a finding that Hartzel intended to interfere with the armed forces. Justice Murphy said: "We are not unmindful of the fact that the United States is now engaged in a total war for national survival and that total war of the modern variety cannot be won by a doubtful, disunited nation in which any appreciable sector is disloyal. For that reason our enemies have developed psychological warfare to a high degree in an effort to cause unrest and disloyalty. Much of this type takes the form of insidious propaganda in the manner and tenor displayed by petitioner's three pamphlets. Crude appeals to overthrow the government or to discard our arms in open mutiny are seldom made. 

Emphasis is laid, rather, on such matters as the futility of our war aims, the vices of our allies and the inadequacy of our leadership. 

But the mere fact that such ideas are enunciated by a citizen is not enough by itself to warrant a finding of a criminal intent to violate §3 of the Espionage Act. Unless there is sufficient evidence from which a jury could infer beyond a reasonable doubt that he intended to bring about the specific consequences prohibited by the Act, an American citizen has the right to discuss these matters either by temperate reasoning or by immoderate and vicious invective without running afoul of the Espionage Act of 1917." 

But the Court three times refused to review the conviction of a group of Trotzkyites under the 1940 so-called Alien Registration Act on charges that, in peace time, 24

they had conspired to overthrow the government by force and to cause disaffection in the armed forces. In this case the trial court had refused to charge the clear and present danger rule and the Circuit Court had affirmed on the ground that since Congress had determined that opinions of the kind expressed by the defendants were harmful to the country the clear and present danger test was inapplicable.

Questions have arisen concerning views attributable to individuals because of their membership in various organizations. In the Shaw case the Oklahoma Court of 

Criminal Appeal reversed the convictions of several

Communists for lack of sufficient proof of what the Communist Party stood for. The Court ruled also that the defendants were entitled to have the jury determine whether the principles of that party were such as to present a "real and imminent danger" of violence, sabotage or other unlawful acts.

In the Strecker, Schneiderman and Bridges cases the Supreme Court avoided a determination of the principles of the Communist Party. In the Strecker case the Court held only that the law as it then stood did not permit deportation of persons not members of the proscribed organization at the time the deportation proceedings were instituted. The law was then amended to include former members, and it is under the law that Bridges was ordered deported.

In the Schneiderman case the Court held that a naturalization certificate could not be cancelled because the applicant for citizenship had been a member of the Communist Party at the time of his application, by imputing to him the view, accepted by the Department of Justice and the Trial Court, that the Communist Party advocated the overthrow of the government by force. The majority of the Court held that citizenship could not be revoked without proof that illegal doctrines had actually been advocated by the applicant. The Chief Justice and 

Justices Roberts and Frankfurter dissented. 25

The principle of the Schneiderman case was soon applied to the case of a German, Baumgartner, who became naturalized in 1932. The evidence against him consisted of pro-Hitler statements made before and after naturalization. Justice Frankfurter rejected the former as not indicating any mental reservation at the time of naturalization in favor of the Weimar Republic. He found the later statements insufficient to form the basis of any relation back.

In the Bridges case, the Court found it unnecessary to discuss the various Constitutional points raised on his behalf, because the majority concluded that the departmental hearing had not been a fair one. Justice Murphy alone discussed the basic issues. He expressly stated that 

Congress in the exercise of the power of deportation was limited by the Bill of Rights, and that the attempted deportation of Bridges violated the Constitution because Bridges had done no more than "exercise his personal right to free speech and association." He believed that deportation, even for membership in an organization which overthrows the Government by force cannot be permitted unless there is a showing that there was a "clear and present danger" that the activities of the organization might produce the forbidden result. Mr. Justice Murphy said: "Deportation, with all its grave consequences, should not be sanctioned on such weak and unconvincing proof of a real and imminent threat to our national security. Congress has ample power to protect the United States from internal revolution and anarchy without abandoning the ideals of freedom and tolerance. 

We as a nation lose part of our greatness whenever we deport or punish those who merely exercise their freedoms in an unpopular though innocuous manner. The strength of this nation is weakened more by those who suppress the freedom of others than by those who are allowed freely to think and act as their consciences dictate. "Our concern in this case does not halt with the fate of Harry Bridges, an alien whose constitutional rights have been grossly 26

violated. The significance of this case is far reaching. The liberties of the 3,500,000 other aliens in this nation are also at stake. Many of these aliens, like many of our forebears, were driven from their original homelands by bigoted authorities who denied the existence of freedom and tolerance. It would be a dismal prospect for them to discover that their freedom in the United States is dependent upon their conformity to the popular notions of the moment. But they need not make that discovery. 

The Bill of Rights belongs to them as well as to all citizens. It protects them as long as they reside within the boundaries of our land. It protects them in the exercise of the great individual rights necessary to a sound political and economic democracy. Neither injunction, fine, imprisonment nor deportation can be utilized to restrict or prevent the exercise of intellectual freedom. Only by zealously guarding the rights of the most humble, the most unorthodox and the most despised among us can freedom flourish and endure in our land."

Some further rather special aspects of free speech remain to be noted. In 1897 in the case of Davis v. 

Massachusetts, before the modern conception of due process had been worked out, the Supreme Court held that since a city might entirely forbid the use of public property for speech-making, it could require a permit before allowing public addresses in a park. No direct issue of free speech was involved, merely a question of the use of public property.

But that case was held not applicable in Hague v. 

C. I. O. (1939). The majority of the Court, while not expressly overruling the earlier decision, concluded that public streets and parks are held in the public interest and that an appropriate public use is "for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens and discussing public questions."

In the Virginia Electric and Power Company case (1941) the Supreme Court laid down the rule applicable to employer discussion of union matters. The Labor 

Board had decided that it was an unfair labor practice for the employer to post on its bulletin board an analysis 27

Court set this order aside. The Supreme Court reversed and remanded the case to the Labor Board because the Court was not sure whether the Board's criticism of the employer's action was based on the notice alone or had taken into consideration other circumstances, such as discharges which the Board had found to be discriminatory. Justice 

Murphy said: side "The employer in this case is as free now as ever to take any it may choose on this controversial issue. But certainly conduct, though evidenced in part by speech, may amount in connection with other circumstances to coercion within the meaning of the Act."

In a later case the Supreme Court, by refusing to review a decision of the Second Circuit has indicated that non-coercive speech unaccompanied by any wrongful acts cannot be the basis for a Labor Board order.

What if they were sold, or a contribution requested?

The Court ruled expressly, however, that a municipality might not entirely prohibit house to house solicitation. The Court, however, again by a five to four vote, ruled that a state can prohibit sales of leaflets on the streets by children. On the other hand, even free distribution may be entirely prohibited on the public streets if the material is commercial. But a leaflet that is an invitation to buy a religious tract cannot be considered commercial. 

Newspaper publishers have often claimed that they were immune from regulatory laws on the ground that any governmental interference with their methods constitutes an infringement of liberty of the press. Their claims have generally been rejected. 

Thus the majority of the Court could find no violation of "freedom of the press" in the provision of the National Labor Relations Act barring the dismissal of an employee for organizational activities when applied to the Associated Press. Mr. Justice Sutherland, dissenting for himself and Justices Van Devanter, McReynolds and Butler, believed that "freedom of the press" had been interfered with because the employee was an editorial writer and his union connection might conflict with the newspaper's policy. While assuming that bias might constitute a proper ground for discharging an employee, Mr. Justice Roberts for the majority, ruled that no such issue had been raised. He pointed out that nothing in the law prevents a publisher from discharging an employee who fails to comply with a paper's policies. 

And the Court could find no threat to freedom of the press in an anti-trust suit against the Associated Press because of its restrictive rules with regard to the admission of new members. 30

A majority of the Court concluded that conviction under the Espionage Act was not necessary to the revocation of the mailing privilege, and that the paper's general opposition to the war justified the action of the Postmaster. Justice Holmes and Brandeis dissented.

In Near v. Minnesota (1931) the Court held void a state law which permitted a Court to enjoin the publication of a newspaper because past issues had been consistently scandalous. The Supreme Court intimated, however, that the statute was valid insofar as it permitted censorship of obscene matter. 

During the Second World War, the Postmaster Gen eral interfered with Father Coughlin's Social Justice and with the Trotskyite Militant. The first of these suspended publication; the mailing privileges of the second were restored after it modified its anti-war propaganda. Thus, the courts were not called upon to deal with Post Office censorship on account of the war. 

Recent court tests of other Post Office action, however, resulted in important restrictions on the Post Office's power of censorship. Consumers' Union successfully dedefeated the right of the Post Office altogether to ban from the mails a pamphlet dealing with birth control material. The court held that the statute forbidding the dissemination of birth control material could not be construed to cover distribution limited as it was in that case to persons who certified that they had been advised by their physicians to use contraceptives. The court also held in another case that no pamphlet could be barred from the mails unless the Post Office first gave the distributor a hearing. Finally, in the Esquire case (1945), it was held that the Postmaster General could not deprive a magazine of the second-class mailing privilege merely 32

because the Postmaster thought that the contents of the magazine were not sufficiently elevating. 

These decisions were all rendered by the United States 

Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia. Of special interest is Judge Thurman Arnold's statement in the 

Esquire case: "The second-class mailing rate is conceived by the Post Office to be an award for resisting the temptation to publish material which offends persons of refinement.

But mail service is not a special privilege. It is a highway over which all business must travel. The rates charged on this highway must not discriminate between competing businesses of the same kind. If the Interstate Commerce Commission were delegated the power to give lower rates to such manufacturers as in its judgment were contributing to the public good the exercise of that power would be clearly unconstitutional. Such a situation would involve freedom of competitive enterprise. The case before us involves freedom of speech as well. 

Little more need be said to decide this case. Nevertheless, since we hope that this is the last time that a government agency will attempt to compel the acceptance of its literary or moral standards relating to material admittedly not obscene, the voluminous record may serve as a useful reminder of the kind of mental confusion which always accompanies such censorship." 

This case is now before the Supreme Court. 

Aside from these free press cases the issue of censorship has come before the Supreme Court only in relation to motion pictures. Right of Assembly FTER the enactment of the Fourteenth Amendment the Court in the Cruikshank case (1876) held unconstitutional an attempt by Congress to punish private citizens for depriving Negroes of the right to assemble freely. It declared that nothing in the Fourteenth Amendment justified Congressional action against individuals. It held that the right to assemble freely, although mentioned in the First Amendment, was inherent not in national but in state citizenship, except where the assembly was for the purpose of petitioning Congress. 

In deJonge v. Oregon (1937) the Court placed the right of assembly within the concept of due process, thus protecting it against state interference. In that case a conviction under the Oregon criminal syndicalism law for assisting at a meeting was set aside because it rested on the fact that the meeting had been called under the auspices of the Communist Party. The state court had ruled that since there was sufficient evidence that the Communist Party advocated the overthrow of the government by violence, any person assisting in the conduct of the meeting was guilty. 

This ruling Chief Justice Hughes declared to be violative of fundamental liberties, saying: "These rights may be abused by using speech or press or assembly in order to incite to violence and crime. The people through their legislatures may protect themselves against that abuse. But the legislative intervention can find constitutional justification only by dealing with the abuse. The rights themselves must not be curtailed. "The greater the importance of safeguarding the community from incitements to the overthrow of our institutions by force and violence, the more imperative is the need to preserve inviolate the constitutional rights of free speech, free press and free assembly in order to maintain the opportunity for free political discussion, to the end that government may be responsive to the will of the people and that changes, if desired, may be obtained by 34

peaceful means. Therein lies the security of the Republic, the very foundation of constitutional government. "It follows from these considerations that, consistently with the Federal Constitution, peaceable assembly for lawful discussion cannot be made a crime. The holding of meetings for peaceable political action cannot be proscribed. Those who assist in the conduct of such meetings cannot be branded as criminals on that score. The question, if the rights of free speech and peaceable assembly are to be preserved, is not as to the auspices under which the meeting is held but as to its purpose; not as to the relations of the speakers, but whether their utterances transcend the bounds of the freedom of speech which the Constitution protects." 

The deJonge decision proved of value in insuring to Communists the right to hold meetings free from police interference. The argument that a meeting should be forbidden because the persons calling it are violators of law has been met with the conclusive answer that if they violate the law they should be arrested, but that the meeting cannot be prevented. 

The Hague case definitely upholds the right of individuals to hold meetings in the streets and even in the parks although that issue was not clearly presented there since the only "park" involved was really a public square. Moreover, the Court recognized the right to "regulate" the use of streets and parks "in the interest of all."

Chief Justice Waite said: "Laws are made for the government of actions, and while they cannot interfere with mere religious beliefs and opinions, they may with practices."

The refusal of witnesses of Jehovah to permit their children to salute the flag at school ceremonies occasioned numerous tests of this principle. In some cases the chil- 35

dren were expelled from schools; in others proceedings were taken against the parents. At first, the Supreme Court refused to review any of these cases. Then, in Minersville School District v. Mr. Justice Stone, in solitary dissent, conceded that the guaranty of religious freedom was not absolute, but believed that since methods other than compulsion could be found to teach patriotism and loyalty the state could not justify compulsion in a case such as this.

Voting 

HILE the Constitution expressly gives Congress the power to regulate the manner of holding elections for members of Congress, nothing in the original Constitution authorizes Congress to determine the qualifications of voters. As has been stated, the Cruikshank case (1876) decided that the right to vote was not a privilege of national citizenship. The Supreme Court has therefore restricted Congress to supervising national elections. It has upheld legislation which protects voters from intimidation and which assures that elections be honestly conducted only in elections for members of Congress or for President. The Court has voided legislation enacted to regulate all elections.

The Court has approved the denial of the suffrage to polygamists. The constitutional amendments protect the right to vote only against discrimination based on previous servitude or race, color or sex. Even then, dis- 40

crimination by private individuals or political parties free from state regulation is outside constitutional protection. As it now stands, the Constitution does not prohibit either property or educational qualifications. 

Thus the educational tests commonly given Negroes in the South are an effective and legal barrier to voting. 

Although property qualifications are today of little importance, many states, especially in the South, disfranchise poor people by imposing poll-taxes, often requiring that all back poll-taxes be paid before the right to vote is allowed. And such a requirement has recently been upheld by the Supreme Court. But in Lane v. Wilson the 

Court held void an Oklahoma law which restricted the franchise to those. who had voted in 1914 or had registered between May 10, 1916 and June 30, 1916.

As we have already noted the Court has now protected the right of Negroes to vote in primaries, at least where these are regulated by the state. 

The Right to Travel 

HE right to move freely from one state to another which the Supreme Court has declared to be derived from national citizenship, and therefore protected by the "privileges and immunities" clauses of the original Constitution and of the Fourteenth Amendment. But the Court has restricted the usefulness of its pronouncements by ruling that the protection of the original Constitution does not extend to action against a person in his own state, and that the protection of the Fourteenth Amendment affects only action by the state, not by individuals. 

The Court therefore in the Wheeler case (1920) held that Congress could not punish persons who forcibly transported a citizen out of his home state and threatened him with injury if he returned. It is an open question whether Congress could punish if the person threatened was a visitor to the state from which he was transported. 

In Hague v. C.I.O., (1939) the Supreme Court upheld 41

the decision of the lower Courts on the "deportation" phase of the case without any discussion. 

The privileges and immunities clause was relied on in a challenge of a California law which punished anyone who brought an indigent person into the state. The Supreme Court was unanimous in its condemnation of the statute, but a majority of the Court based its decision on the commerce clause. Justices Black, Douglas, Murphy and Jackson thought that the right to move freely from state to state was a privilege of national citizenship which could not be restricted because of the economic status of the citizen. The value of the majority decision, however, is that it benefits aliens as well as citizens. 

Aliens and Citizenship

ALIENS, while protected like citizens by the due process clause against arbitrary action in their property rights, are largely at the mercy of Congress in the vital matter of deportations. Though entitled to a hearing, they are not entitled to a review of the facts found by the administrative official.

They do not have the benefit of the prohibition in the Constitution against the enactment of ex post facto laws. 

On the theory that deportation is not punishment, the Supreme Court has permitted Congress to change the grounds for deportation regardless of the time when the acts were committed. Although an alien may have escaped deportation because the old law permitted it only within a limited number of years after his entry, a new law could subject him to deportation. An alien can be deported for an offense which was no ground for deportation at the time he committed it. 

In the Bridges case, (1945) the Supreme Court passed by an opportunity to reconsider some of these fundamental questions or to rule on the extent to which the power of deportation is limited by the Bill of Rights.

(Justice Brandeis had refused to act because members of his family had interested themselves in Mrs. Sacco.) 

However, in Moore v. Dempsey (1923), the Court had accepted the contention that the trial of certain Arkansas Negroes had been no more than mockery because it was carried out under the dictates of a threatening mob. Accordingly it ordered a hearing to ascertain the truth. Justice Holmes said: "But if the case is that the whole proceeding is a mask,-that counsel, jury and judge were swept to the fatal end by an irresistible wave of public passion, and that the state courts failed to correct the wrong, neither perfection in the machinery for cor- 43

rection nor the possibility that the trial court and counsel saw no other way of avoiding an immediate outbreak of the mob can prevent this court from securing to the petitioners their constitutional rights." 

And more recently (1935) in the Mooney case from

California, the contention that the conviction rested on perjured testimony deliberately used by the prosecution, was held to require a similar hearing. The Supreme 

Court, however, did not permit this hearing to take place in a federal court, but in accordance with established usage compelled Mooney to seek redress in the state courts and after that redress was denied the Supreme Court refused again to interfere. And in the Hysler case the 

Supreme Court affirmed a state ruling that there was no substance to the convicted man's claim that the testimony of a confederate had been obtained through coercion or was false. Justices Black, Douglas and Murphy dissented on the ground that knowledge by the prosecution of the improper methods used was not essential. The majority opinion did not make clear whether such knowledge was essential.

The Supreme Court has also interfered with state convictions in a few other instances--as that in which a judge who imposed fines for the possession of intoxicating liquor was paid by being allowed to keep part of the fines; as in the first of two appeals in the cases of the Scottsboro, Alabama, boys (1932), when the accused had been denied opportunity to obtain counsel, and as in the case of Brown v. Mississippi (1936), where the only evidence against three Negro defendants was that which had been obtained from them by torture. Chief Justice 

Hughes thus reviewed the requirements of due process: "The rack and torture chamber may not be substituted for the witness stand. The State may not permit an accused to be hurried to conviction under mob domination where the whole proceeding is but a mask-without supplying corrective process. The 

States may not deny to the accused the aid of counsel. Nor may a 

State, through the action of its officers, contrive a conviction 44

through the pretense of a trial which in truth is 'but used as a means of depriving a defendant of liberty through a deliberate deception of court and jury by the presentation of testimony known to be perjured.' And the trial equally is a mere pretense where the state authorities have contrived a conviction resting solely upon confessions obtained by violence."

Recent decisions on the subject of confessions have varied greatly. In Chambers v. Florida the conviction of several Negroes was reversed because they confessed after prolonged questioning in the belief that they were threatened with mob violence. In Ward v. Texas the same result was reached in the case of an ignorant Negro house servant induced to confess after prolonged questioning while held away from friends and lawyer in violation of a state law which required immediate arraignment. In Ashcraft v. Tennessee the Court reversed a murder conviction because the confession had been obtained by the state police who kept defendant in a room in the jail for two days and questioned him in relays for 36 hours.

But in Lisenba v. California the conviction was affirmed because the majority of the Court believed that the confession resulted from defendant's learning that a confederate had confessed and not from the improper conduct of the police. In Lyons v. Oklahoma there were three confessions-one concededly improper, which was not introduced in evidence, a second made later on the same day which was challenged, and a third, made several days later, which was admitted at the trial without objection. The objection to the second confession was primarily that the fear instilled by the prisoner's treatment earlier the same day still influenced him. This precise issue was not submitted to the jury for its determination, but it was instructed generally that a confession. obtained as the result of threats could not be considered. 

Justice Reed said that where, as here, the facts were in dispute, great weight must be given to the findings of the jury and the state courts and the conviction was affirmed. 45

The recent Malinski case (1945) throws further light on the problem of confessions. A bare majority of the 

Court reversed a conviction based in part on a confession which the prosecutor had described to the jury as obtained because of defendant's fear of what the police might do to him if he did not confess. The majority, however, refused to reverse a conviction of a confederate against whom the confession had not been directly used. 

A curious case arising under the due process clause of the 14th Amendment was that of the gangster Lepke convicted for plotting a murder. Complaint was made that the jury was biased, that the prosecutor withheld evidence which might have helped the defense and that he made improper statements in summation. The Supreme 

Court unanimously rejected those contentions.

In cases arising in the federal courts the power of review of the Supreme Court rests on broader grounds of general justice. Therefore the Court has felt freer to reverse, as in cases where the prosecuting attorney had misconducted himself, or where the trial judge had characterized the defendant as unworthy of belief, or where, in the case of one Negro, the judge had refused to permit inquiry into the possible prejudice of talesmen against Negroes.

The Supreme Court has recently reversed several federal convictions because of improper conduct by federal agents. In the Viereck case the prosecutor's reference in summation to the war and the sacrifices of our soldiers was sharply criticized by the Chief Justice. In the 

McNabb and Anderson cases the Court condemned the practice of holding arrested persons incommunicado for several days while questioning them.

Whether similar reasons would be considered sufficient to justify reversal of state convictions is still uncertain. But the trend seems toward the view that the standards of fairness imposed on federal courts should apply as well to state courts. 46

Searches and Seizures

N THE relatively less important field of searches and whole liberally preted the constitutional prohibition. It has ordered the return of property illegally seized and prevented its use as evidence against its owner. Many convictions have been reversed for disregard of this principle. But in the case of tapping telephone wires for evidence, the Court, over the dissent of Justices Holmes, Brandeis, Stone and Butler, refused to apply the very salutary safeguards built up around searches and seizures in general. (Olmstead case, 1928.)

The same principle was applied, in the Goldman case (1942) to conversations overheard by the use of a detectaphone, a device consisting of a microphone so placed against a wall that what is said on the other side of the wall can be overheard by persons listening in on wires connected with the microphone. Chief Justice Stone and

Justices Murphy and Frankfurter alone believed that the Olmstead case should be overruled. 

Since Congress has made wiretapping a criminal offense the Supreme Court has barred as evidence conversations thus overheard, and even information obtained from conversations overheard as the result of illegal wiretapping. And it has been held immaterial that the conversations overheard were between points within the same state.

But only a person whose conversations had been listened to can complain. This was decided in the Goldstein case (1942) which involved an insurance fraud. Some of the conspirators testified for the prosecution after being confronted with the tapping of their wires. The convicted confederates contended that this testimony was "fruit of the poisonous tree." But the majority of the Supreme

Court held otherwise on the ground that the conversations of the convicted men had not been listened to and that none of the intercepted messages had been used at the trial. The Court, for the first time, approved the 47

similar rule laid down by many lower courts in search and seizure cases.

These safeguards to civil liberties are available, however, only when federal officers are involved. Nothing in the Constitution imposes any restrictions upon the states regarding searches and seizures. Thus it frequently happens that state officers seize desired evidence and turn it over to federal officials. The right of the federal authorities to use evidence so obtained has been upheld, provided the state officials originally acted with a state offense in mind.

Self-Incrimination 

HIS guaranty not only protects a person from giving testimony which might incriminate him, but also protects him from producing documents of such character. However, it protects only individuals, not corporations or associations, and it protects only against compulsion exercised by the federal government. So an officer of a corporation or a member of an association such as a labor union cannot claim the privilege when an attempt is made to compel the production of records of the organization.

The privilege, however, can be waived by disclosing too much. The Supreme Court recently avoided clarification of this subject in an unusual situation. A witness before a grand jury had admitted taking stolen money across state lines but had refused to disclose the name of the person from whom he had stolen it. This information was essential to any prosecution, because without it there could be no corroboration. The Supreme Court refused to review a conviction for contempt of court because by the time the case had reached that Court the witness had served the sentence imposed upon him.

Since the privilege against self-incrimination has no application at all to the States, the Supreme Court in the Feldman case (1944) held that testimony given under compulsion in a state court could later be used in a fed- 48

eral court. Justice Black, together with Justices Douglas and Rutledge, dissented. They criticized the result reached as cutting "into the very substance of the Fifth Amendment."

The privilege against self-incrimination has made it difficult in many cases to get testimony from one member of a group implicating others. To overcome this difficulty, Congress and most state legislatures have passed statutes which compel the giving of testimony in particular situations. These statutes grant immunity to the person testifying. The Supreme Court recently decided that unless the statute requires that the claim of immunity be asserted at the time the testimony is given, the immunity is not lost by a witness who makes no such claim. 

Jury Problems

HE Constitution provides that in federal courts proseand that trials take place before a petit jury. On the whole these safeguards have been protected by the Court. 

Because of the word "infamous" used to qualify "crimes," the Court has held that a crime punishable by imprisonment in a penitentiary or by hard labor requires an indictment. In the Wong Wing case (1896) the Court held invalid an act of Congress which deported aliens illegally in this country, and subjected them to imprisonment prior to deportation without the safeguards of an ordinary trial.

Questions have also arisen as to the application of these safeguards to territories such as Alaska and insular possessions such as Hawaii. The Court concluded that 

Alaska had so become a part of the United States that the constitutional safeguards operate there, but not so in Hawaii. Trial by a jury of twelve men is therefore mandatory in Alaska; in Hawaii trial can be had without any jury at all.

Questions have also arisen under the ruling of the Court that Congress could provide in the District of 49

Columbia for the punishment of petty offenses without jury trial. In the case of Callan v. Wilson the Supreme 

Court in 1888 unanimously held that a charge of conspiracy arising out of a labor dispute was so serious that it could not properly be tried in such a court. But in 1937 in the Clawans case the majority of the Court held that it was proper to try a person in a police court without a jury for selling merchandise without a license, even though the punishment was a fine of $300.00 or imprisonment for 90 days. 

The Sixth Amendment requires that the jury be "impartial." In the Glasser case (1942) the Supreme Court condemned the practice of calling up women talesmen only from a list furnished by a particular organization. 

Justice Murphy stressed the necessity of so choosing the jury that it should be "a cross-section of the community." 

As a somewhat related issue to trial by jury it may be, noted that the Supreme Court in 1899 invalidated an act of Congress providing that in prosecutions for receiving stolen property, the fact of theft might be established by the record of the conviction of the thief. In the Kirby case the Court held this law violated the Sixth Amendment which guarantees that defendants in criminal cases be confronted with witnesses. 

The Right to Counsel 

E have already noted that the first Scottsboro conas lacking in due process because of denial of counsel. But there is a difference in the attitude of the Supreme Court on this subject, depending on whether the case arises in a state or in a federal court. Since the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right of all persons prosecuted in federal courts to representation by counsel a denial of that right completely violates a federal conviction. But in the state courts, refusal of counsel will void a conviction only if the defendant can show that he was harmed because of the denial. 50

This difference was made clear in Betts v. Brady (1942). There the accused, on trial in Maryland for robbery, was without means to provide himself with a lawyer and asked that one be assigned to him. The judge told him that this was not the practice except in cases of murder or rape. The Supreme Court upheld the conviction on the ground that no unfairness had resulted. 

Justices Black, Douglas and Murphy dissented. Justice

Black insisted that it was impossible to determine whether a lawyer's help might not have made a difference in the result.

In any case, the right to counsel can be waived. Whenever it is claimed that the right to counsel has been denied there must be a hearing in order to ascertain the facts and determine whether or not there was a waiver of the right to counsel. This rule applies both to state and federal courts.

As the Scottsboro case showed, it is not enough that an accused person be given a lawyer by the court. The lawyer must have a real chance to represent his client. 

Convictions for rape were reversed in that case because the lawyers appointed by the state court had no opportunity to investigate the facts of the crime and the defendants were hurried to trial in an atmosphere of hysteria. In a later case, also from Alabama, a different result was reached unanimously because the Supreme Court believed that the trial had been a fair one.

It is not altogether clear whether a stricter rule applies in federal cases. The Supreme Court reversed the conviction of one of a group of defendants in the Glasser case (1942) because the trial judge had, over Glasser's objection, assigned his lawyer to represent one of the other defendants, a man whose situation in the case was hostile to that of Glasser.

He concluded that the place of employment is an appropriate one for the exercise of these rights. But he left open the right of the state to punish threats or violence or the effects of mass picketing.

The Court has recently applied these rules to injunctions issued by state courts. In A. F. of L. v. Swing (1941) it reversed an injunction against picketing where the state court had rested its decision on the ground that no members of the defendant union were employed by the plaintiff. 53

Mr. Justice Frankfurter, for the majority, differentiated between the two cases solely on the issue of violence. 

In the Swing case there had been no trial-merely an injunction based on affidavits which both asserted and denied violence. Said the majority: "A state cannot exclude workingmen from peacefully exercising the right of free communication by drawing the circle of economic competition between employers and workers so small as to contain only an employer and those directly employed by him." 

But in the Meadowmoor case there had been a trial, and a finding by the master that violence endangered peaceful picketing, rejected by the trial court, but accepted on appeal. This, thought the majority, was quite different from a statute, since it affected only a particular case. Discrimination by Unions 

N the field of labor relations, as elsewhere, the question of discrimination is assuming increasing importance. A number of cases on this subject were decided by the United States Supreme Court at the 1944 term. 

In Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. and

Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen and 

Engineers collective bargaining agreements made under the Railway Labor Act were challenged by Negro firemen. The agreements provided that only "promotable" firemen should be employed and denied Negroes the right to promotion. In consequence, many Negroes formerly employed as firemen lost their jobs. The Supreme 

Court unanimously held that the Railway Labor Act forbade the representatives of any group of employees from using their power in such a way as to discriminate against a minority group, at least where the basis of discrimination had no relation to service, but was based on race. 

In Wallace v. National Labor Relations Board the problem was a different one. There an independent union which had won an election made a closed shop contract and then refused to admit to its membership various employees who had belonged to the rival C.I.O. union which had lost the election. The rival then complained to the Labor Board. It ruled that the union which had won the election was company dominated and that the employer had been guilty of an unfair labor practice, because it knew that the closed shop contract was proposed for the purpose of getting rid of these employees. A bare majority of the Supreme Court upheld this order. Chief 

Justice Stone and Justices Roberts, Frankfurter and 

Jackson dissented. For the majority Justice Black said that any union which becomes a bargaining agent as the result of an election represents all the employees and 56

cannot deprive a particular employee of his position because of previous affiliation with another union. 

For the minority, Justice Jackson took the position that the company could not dictate to the union concerning its membership and that Congress had expressly authorized a closed shop, the natural consequence of which was that persons not eligible to union membership could not retain employment. He believed that a union must have the right to protect itself against persons hostile to it. 

In Railway Mail Association v. Corsi the Court unanimously upheld a New York statute which forbade discrimination by labor unions in the admission or treatment of members because of race, color or creed. The objection made by the Association in this case was that such a law violated the 14th Amendment because it interfered with the right of the association to select its own members. The Court characterized this as a "distortion of the policy manifested in that amendment."

Regulation of Unions 

EVERAL other labor cases decided at the 1944 Term dealt with some of the recent State laws designed to curb the power of labor unions. The first of these was the Thomas case from Texas. That State required labor organizers to register and prohibited the solicitation of membership by a person who had not registered. R. J.

Thomas, the President of the United Automobile Work ers Union, went to Texas to test this law. He was met with an injunction which had been issued ex parte and restrained him from soliciting members without first having registered. Thomas ignored the injunction, addressed a meeting of workers and asked those present to join the Union. The State courts found him, guilty of contempt for violation of the injunction. The U.S. Supreme Court, by a 5 to 4 decision, held that the injunction violated Thomas' rights of free speech.

For the majority, Justice Rutledge expressed the view 57

that the injunction was issued for the purpose of preventing solicitation of any kind, and therefore operated as a threat to free speech. He made a distinction between speeches and meetings on the one hand, and the solicitation of funds or securing of subscriptions on the other. A state might require solicitors to register and identify themselves; it could not impose such a restriction on meetings or speakers. The minority, composed of the Chief 

Justice, Justices Roberts, Reed, and Frankfurter, rejected this distinction. Justice Roberts said a state had the right to require anyone engaged in a professional or business activity to register, and that Thomas was only being punished because he had refused to register, not because of anything that he had said. 

In the Hill case, the Supreme Court held that a Florida statute was void because in conflict with the federal Labor Relations Act to the extent that it required business agents to be licensed, and required the union to file reports. Chief Justice Stone agreed that the requirement for the licensing of business agents was in conflict with federal law, disagreeing with the majority with regard to the requirement to file reports. Justices Frankfurter and Roberts dissented altogether.

The Court, however, in two cases refused to pass on the validity of a similar Alabama law, because the questions had been raised prematurely and because there might be room for the application in Alabama even of the provisions condemned in the Florida case, if applied only to unions representing employees engaged wholly in intra-state business.
