Military occupation of territory, starting with the occupatio bellica, the belligerent occupation of enemy territory in time of war, has developed to include certain other contingencies. Today, the following cases of military occupation may be distinguished: 

1. Occupation of enemy territory in pursuit of military operations. 

2. Occupation of enemy territory under the terms of an armistice. 

3. Occupation of neutral territory in the course of a war. 

4. allied ".

Occupation of foreign territory under the terms of a treaty of peace: (a) as a guarantee for the fulfillment of treaty obligations; (b) for other purposes. 

5. Occupation of foreign territory under the terms of a peace time agreement between the governments concerned. 

6. Occupation of foreign territor in peace time without the consent of the occupied State: (a) as a punitive measure; (b) as a measure of protection of citizens and other interests of the occupying Stato. 

7. Occupation of insurgent or disturbed areas within the occupying State's own territory.

-2- In practically every instance of prolonged military occupation, questions of military government will arise. 

Broadly speaking, these questions will come up whenever one goes beyond the specific fields of internal relations within the armed forces of the occupant or of relations between these armed forces and such members of the armed forces of the opponent--prisoners or others--as may still be within the occupied area. 

In other words, the sphere of military government includes the entire scope of relations of the occupying forces with civilians, civilian property, public officials and public property in the occupied area. 

No express declaration is needed to institute military government. The fact of military occupation of a territory proclaims that military rovernment is in force,¹ and this regime continues until either military occupation has ceased or a different regime has been instituted by the occupant State.2

Of all the situations that give rise to military government, belligerent occupation is the only one that has been thoroughly studied. 

It is in connection with belligerent occupation that the general concept of military government has been evolved. All other situations in which military occupation--and, therefore, military government--may occur, are dealt with on the basis of an incomplete analogy with belligerent occupation. The specific problem of military occupation arises in those cases, where mere occupation, rather than more general powers of jurisdiction, of which occupation is but a part, characterizes the relationship between State and territory. As has been seen in the enumeration of the main cases of military occupation, this condition arises in several types of situations. 

The objectives of the occupying State being necessarily different in one situation from those inspiring it in another, the regime of military occupation shows corresponding changes. 

Belligerent occupation of hostile territory The classical case of military occupation is that of belligerent occupation of a foreign country. Starting with the presumption that public authorities as well as inhabitants are hostile to the troops and interests of the conqueror, the regime of belligerent occupation is one under which the occupant has been traditionally held to be free from any regard for the established governmental and legal set-up, substituting its own military

As a matter of practice, the difference between occupation regimes in neutral and in hostile territory is even more pronounced. Where the preexisting local regime is regarded as sufficiently friendly or trustworthy, there is practically no interference with governmental affairs. A typical instance of this kind is the assurance given to the authorities of Iceland and communicated to the government of the United States in the British note of May 10, 1940, in which Great Britain gives assurances that the occupation of Iceland is intended "solely to ensure the security of Iceland against German invasion, that the forces would be withdrawn at the end of the war, and that the British Government would not interfere with the administration of the island." Where the regime is regarded as lacking in friendliness, the occupant, as we have seen, is at times not above using his position to induce a change of regime, after which, once more, the government of the territory is left to the discretion of local authorities.

In any case, the occupant assumes a lesser degree of direct responsibility for local affairs than in the case of occupation of hostile territory. litary occupation of insurgent territory By analogy to military occupation undertaken as part of belligerent operations, during actual hostilitics or under the terms of an armistice, two other cases of forcible military occupations are frequently encountered. The first is the military occupation of insurgent or disturbed portions of the occupant's own territory. An insurrection of any magnitude, while still permitting the State concerned to treat the insurgents on the sole basis of its domestic criminal law, very likely involves actions which imply recognition of belligerent status as between legitimate authorities and insurgents.

It is not quite clear whether the rules relating to the conduct of war, as crystallized in domestic and internationel law, nccessarily apply in such cases, but suffice it to say that in some cases of insurgency and civil war such rules have been applied. This was the case both as between Great 

Britain and the United States, during the War of Independence, and as between the Federal Union and the Confederacy, during the Civil War. In both cases, such rules of warfare as existed at the time were, on the whole, scrupulously applied, and it will be remembered that the classical Instructions for the government of armies which established the modern standards of military government under belligerent occupation as well as of the laws of war in general, were drafted precisely for use during the Civil War. 

Indeed, a large part of American practice of belligerent occupation and military government is derived directly from the occupation of the 1 Southern States during the Civil War and immediately after. The second case of a forcible military occupation analogous to belligerent occupation is the one generally known as "intervention". reasons for intervention are extremely varied. The most important of these reasons are: protection of the lives and property of citizens of the intervening State; protection of its domestic or of international interests; prevention of anarchy; desire to prevent domination of an area by an undesirable regime or by an unfriendly foreign Power; reprisal for wrongs committed or alleged; enforcement of demands; and humanitarian motives. Not every intervention takes the extreme form of occupation of territory, but in many cases occupation is resorted to, and when this is the case, any prolonged military occupation raises questions of the extent to which the occupying forces interfere with the government of the occupied arca. It should be realized that in some cases of intervention, the hostility of the local authorities and population is so pronounced, and actual hostilities take place on a scale so large, that conditions which the occupying forces face are not essentially different from those of a foreign war. 

From the point of view of domestic law, the occupying forces in an intervention enjoy the same fundamental latitude and are subject to the same restrictions as in actual warfare. Indeed, the conduct of these

forces is determined by the principles of military government and martial law, and the lack of an international state of war is of secondary importance. 

The international rules of war, including the Hague Regulations, do not, strictly speaking, apply to mere interventions. 

However, insofar as these rules represent well-agreed contemporary ideas of the limitations upon the activities of troops abroad, these standards are usually conformed with.

One of the classical instances of American military government abroad is the instance furnished by the 1914 intervention in Mexico. 

On the other hand, the wide variety of circumstances under which interventions occur and the corresponding variety of objectives at which interventions are aimed, result in an extreme divergence of regimes of military government under intervention. There are instances when the occupying forces refrain from any interference with local government, while in other instances this interference is far more thorough than that usually exercised on the occasion of a belligerent occupation. Indeed, while belligerent occupation merely suspends existing political relations without having authority to change it, intervention frequently aims at the change of existing political relations: changes of regime by action of intervening 

Powers are quite common, and even changes of territorial sovereignty are at 1 times achieved by this means.

Consensual occupation We now pass from forcible occupation to consensual occupation. 

As a matter of theory, the situation here is quite different from that encountered before. The fundamental promise of forcible occupation-greater or lesser hostility of the home government to the occupant's action--is lacking. No doubt, this difference does not always correspond to realities: history knows cases of "forcible" occupation based on connivance of the controlling authorities of the occupied arca, just as it knows cascs of "consensual" occupation in which the "consent" was an enforced one and hardly concealed the hostility of local authorities and inhabitants. Nevertheless, the distinction is one of importance. is a result, consensual military occupation is far less likely to interfore with the government of the occupied zone. 

The precise extent to which the occupant's forces are entitled to control the government of the occupied erca is determined, in the first place, by the terms of the agreement under which occupation is taking place. Some of these agreements are quite explicit regarding this matter: thus, the exchange of letters between the Prime Minister 1 of Iceland and the President of the United States, of July 1, 1941, under which American troops have occupied Iceland. Nonetheless, it should be remembered that such agroments do no more than limit the general principles of military occupation. These principles, developed through the practice of belligerent occupation and extended to similar situations, cover in their essentials the cases of consensual military occupation as well. The customary rules of military occupation and

The occasions for consensual occupation are many. Even occupation of a surrendered zone or occupation following an armistice are, strictly speaking, instances of consensual occupation. However, occurring as they do as incidents of warfare, the belligerent character of such occupations is far more important than the consensual procedure by which they have been achieved. peace with one another.

The cases enumerated above do not exhaust the entire range of possibilities. They suffice, however, to indicate the main types of circumstances in which consensual occupation occurs. This enumeration brings out quite clearly the extreme diversity of purposes and objectives

aimed at by the occupying Power-- diversity which necessarily is reflected in the practice of the occupant in making use of his privilege of installing military government in the occupied zone. Indeed, aside from the limitation or prohibition of such a regime that may be contained in the text of the agreement, the very nature of the occupation will, in many cases, set definite limits to the practical application of a military regime. When present in well-administered friendly territory to assist against danger from abroad, occupying troops will hardly embark upon setting up a military government over the area and its civilian inhabitants, even should the agreement remain silent on this issue. A leased territory will, of course, present a different problem, with a further distinction between a territory leased for military purposes, in which military government will be the rule, and territory leased for general purposes, in which the occupant will maintain a civilian regime save in exceptional circumstances. Territory occupied at the request of a government faced with internal disorder will again call for a measure of military government in extremely delicate circumstances. 

Occupation of former hostile territory during a transitional period raises special issues of its own, and so on. 

As a result of this diversity of circumstances, it is impossible to speak of a definite scheme of military government in territories under consensual occupation. The scheme adopted varies from case to case, and must be studied individually by pcrusing available instructions of governments to the commanding officers and the reports submitted by the officers. It is true, as a general proposition, that within the 1

limits of valid international agreements regarding the conditions of occupation and subject to the circumstances of the individual case, military occupation of a consensual nature leaves essentially untouched the privilege of the occupant to apply the regime of military government as developed by analogy with belligerent occupation. But this general proposition must be qualified by the diversity of circumstances set out above.
