FOREWORD 

In Los Angeles, Hollywood, and San Diego, where the climate is semi-tropic, and where people from all parts of the world mingle together, it is absorbing to watch the effects of this climate on these people, and their resulting reactions on each other. These reactions are particularly traceable in the younger people, who are just beginning to find their places in the world of adventure and love.

This novel traces the pattern of their hours of happiness, and their hours of gloom, and reproduces that pattern as accurately as the observation of the writer would allow. These reactions are recorded frankly and sincerely, and the story is one taken from the life itself.

CHAPTER I DELL 

It was the sort of a day that all true Southern Californians termed "unusual weather". A raw ugly wind beat against the stiff leaves of the palm trees. Dell sat in her room in the Biltmore and gloomed with the elements. Mondays were mountains of boredom anyway, and then to have one like this! "I don't see why we stopped at this hotel anyway," she fretted at her mother. "The Ambassador is much more fun. 

Or even the Roosevelt," she added, "only those picture people bore me silly; they think they're the last word." "You're never satisfied no matter where we put up," Mrs. 

Dartney replied, looking at her daughter as she lay upon the bed, presumably supine with melancholy. Her bright copper head was thrust savagely into the center of a huge pillow and her slim legs bicycled back and forth in the air as she talked. "I'm bored, bored, bored!" she fairly screamed. Her mother, a large placid woman with skillfully touched-up gray hair, went on calmly powdering her nose in front of the dressing table mirror. "Now don't start in telling me how hard I am to please."

The voice from the bed went on, "I know I am and I'm proud of it." "Well really, Dell dear," protested her mother between pats with the huge puff, "I don't see what more you can want. I'm sure your father has been more than lenient, particularly since that last well came in. He just gave you that lovely new car and your allowance is more than liberal-’

A faint flush spread over the girl's face. Yes, she knew a lot of people called her that. Perhaps she did seem arrogant. 

Generally she wouldn't even admit to herself that her highhanded manner was merely a protective coat to be worn in a curious world. People with money-especially people who haven't always had money, are continually open to criticism of the most devastating sort. Dell had long ago decided she'd rather be called high-hat and a snob than be known as a milk and water baby of humble birth. She'd rather trample than be trampled on. "Ask them to bring my car around, will you?" Mrs. Dartney asked the young man.. "Mother's going out to trump her partner's ace," Dell explained.

Up in her room gathering together handkerchief, purse and gloves, she suddenly paused and then walked slowly to the window. Her room overlooked Pershing Square, noted for its discussions and its derelicts. On the benches that lined the walks sat many men.

In Pershing Square all the weighty world problems were debated and successfully settled; all the battles on land and sea were fought over and over again. There morons, perverts, felons and ex-extra people sat and soaked in the sunshine or surveyed the passing populace. 

Today, though, the park was unusually empty. Resenting the unaccustomed cold all those who could stayed indoors. 

The less fortunate ones, however, were in their accustomed places, shivering and grumbling into their ragged coat collars. "Queer to think of all that just across from such a nice hotel," mused Dell pressing her nose thoughtfully against the window pane. She looked up at the tall palms and then down at the few straggling figures on the walk. 

Something about one of them reminded her of the man who had just been shown out of the lobby downstairs. Or perhaps she had been thinking of him. Her fingers drummed on the glass as her eyes darkened with purposeful intensity. Then with a quick movement she turned, snatched up her fur coat and went downstairs to where Stuart Holden was waiting. "I'm sorry," she told him, "but I've changed my mind. I'm not going with you." "Suit yourself," he replied nonchalantly, eyeing her curiously nevertheless. "Well, I'll be seeing you." She bade him a brief farewell and walked briskly out the door Two blocks down the street she turned and entered a bank. 

People said of Dell Dartney that she had charm, that she was reckless and strong-willed, that she had talent but little

direction. They said she was selfish, that she was headed straight for destruction-that she was this, that she was that. 

The truth of the matter was that she was a number of things that her associates least suspected. Perhaps she didn't know herself. One thing everyone agreed on; she was very good company. Though she often professed to being bored herself, anyone with her seldom was.

She was an only child and even before oil was found on her father's ranch she was known to be terribly spoiled. The sudden access of wealth did nothing to diminish that trait. She used to overthrow any attempt at domination of her will. Neither of her parents could control her. She used to ride the fastest horse her father owned into a lather as she ranged the Texas plains. Now she drove high powered cars at an alarming speed over the smooth California highways. The same restless urge for movement was there, as when a child, only the means were different.

She was eighteen when the family fortunes suddenly changed. Her father had always been comfortably well off; in fact, Dell was in a private school in San Antone when it happened. She came home immediately upon hearing the news. Nothing her parents could say would persuade her to stay at her studies. She liked school all right, but she liked the world better and now that the black gold made it possible she intended to see something of it. So far she had really seen very little. Her father had refused to set forth at once upon an extended trip and her mother believed in seeing America first.

Even so, Dell was just a little tired now of traveling around -and doing nothing else. She found that pleasures and palaces pall eventually if there are no alleviating digressions into reality—not at all an original discovery. She was still restless, but in a different way. She had an urge for something more out of life than she was getting. She couldn't put what she wanted into words; it eluded her consciousness, but she knew it was there and the more she tried to formulate her desire the wilder, more reckless, more ruthless she became.

She felt that she was complex, an enigma more to herself than to anyone else. No one had ever ruled her and no one had ever understood her. She was like some hard, tightly closed bud whose soft center no one as yet had found. 

There was nothing artificial about Dell Dartney, but there was a great deal that was reserved and secret. She invariably donned a mask of mockery for those who desired to know too much, and behind it all was a fund of humor wherein probably lay, to a large extent, her attraction.

The Dartney house was full to overflowing and Dell was kept so busy helping her mother that she had little time to think about Than, which was a good thing for the sight of him each evening so sort of drawn and desperate looking wrung her heart. She would have liked to have helped him in some way, but it seemed a man's fight in which she was of little use.

At last on Friday, towards evening the wild well experts shut the gate for the third time by a new combination control head that closed with extreme difficulty on the two hundred feet brown geyser that at last protestingly subsided. 

A tired smile of impersonal triumph shone on Than's face that night when he came to see Dell. He had gone home first and changed the oil-saturated clothes that he had practically lived in the last few days and his eyes as well as his looks told her that now he could be her lover, now those things that had been buried in the strain of the excitement and workfilled hours could be done, waiting words could be said. 

But they talked little as they leaned on the balcony with clasped hands and looked dreamily out into the dusk. The night seemed strangely quiet with that vast throbbing roar no longer disturbing its womb, but still heavy on the warm air came the thick scent of the crude that caught in the throat and made muscles contract protestingly. But the man and girl sitting there noticed none of this. They were looking inward on each other.

The deep tones were husky with tenderness and the little pulse in the girl's throat began to beat excitedly with its promise.

The old happy discoveries of lips and hands in the dark was disturbed by her father's booming voice breaking through the wall of isolation that surrounded them. His huge bulk was outlined in the doorway against the faint hall light. "Huh," he grunted with gentle maliciousness, "I see you've got this thing capped too."

The two laughed happily there in the darkness and he turned, saying over his shoulder, "God bless you." "And God love you," intoned Than with a reverence that made it a prayer, and he put his seal in the soft little place behind her ear. "As long as you do I don't care," laughed Dell with something of the old recklessness in her voice. Its eager note broke the flood of emotion the earnestness of the moment had been stemming in the man and his kisses burned hard on her eyes and lips and in the little hollow of her throat with a sort of sweet ferocity that charged exquisitely all the little nerves of her being. 

A misty nun-like dawn was saying good morning to the stars and they were twinkling a dim good-night when the two arose and went within. "We must get married soon, within the month," the man was saying. "We have lost so much time."

Dell assented mutely as they turned at the door to look outward again, reluctant to leave. "When an emerald may mean so much," she murmured, and drew forth a slender little chain on the end of which sparkled the fateful ring, warm and twinkling from its resting place between her breasts. "My dear, so it is ours again. May it bring happiness to my son and yours, as it has brought it to us." "Though pray to Allah, not by such a devious, tortuous way," supplemented Dell. "But love today is like that."
