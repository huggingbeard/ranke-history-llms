TREATMENT OF GERMANY AFTER THE WAR Introduction and Summary 

The problem of what to do with Germany after the war will be the central problem of the peace making. The block of 80,000,000 

German-speaking people planted in the center of Europe far outnumbers any other national group (except, of course, the Russians) and must necessarily tend to dominate Western and Central Europe both politically and economically, unless some restraints are set up. When one adds to this the fact that the German nation, whatever the underlying cause, has a long and consistent history of aggression, that it has, indeed, disturbed the peace of Europe five times in the last 80 years, it is easy to see the potential menace to European and world peace.

Some way must, then, be found of removing the threat of future German aggression so that Germany and her neighbors may live side by side in peace and security. In other words, European peace--and in consequence the peace of the world--depends upon a wise solution of the German problem.

What is to be the criterion for treatment? There are two principal schools. of thought, one. which makes retributive justice the criterion and one which lays greater emphasis upon future stability. 

This assumes that extermination of 80,000,000 people of a strong and gifted race can be ruled out as a solution; it has been seriously proposed by only the most violent extremists. 

A peace that would mete out to Germany the treatment that she has imposed upon her neighbors has considerable support, especially among Germany's victims. "To make the punishment fit the crime" is an idea that appeals. Two major arguments have been advanced against it.

In the first place, punitive measures applied to a country as a whole necessarily fall upon the innocent as well as the guilty. It may be argued that there are none innocent, on the basis that the whole German nation is responsible for the action of its government. 

This point will be considered in more detail later, but against this it has been urged that this method is opposed to modern practice and smacks too much of totalitarian idealogy. There is another more potent reason advanced for not attempting a punitive peace, however just. This is that it holds out no hope of a true peace founded •upon" reconciliation and consent.:

Professor Fay of Harvard University bases his discussion of post-war treatment of Germany on the assumption: (among others) that no settlement for Europe will be satisfactory unless it includes a settlement that is satisfactory for Germany also, i.e., unless it holds out to the German people the prospect of eventual participation, on substantially equal terms (as was not the case in 1919) in the political and economic life of the new world order.1. ·

If the treatment of Germany is merely negative or repressive, most writers believe that it would be effective only as long as it is actively enforced. This would mean that the great powers must maintain a state of armed superiority over Germany--at best, a world permanently armed; at worst, recurrent wars. This is not the future envisaged by the Atlantic Charter as the democratic ideal. "We may sum up the main objections to the proposed policy of penalization, dismemberment and permanent coercion of Germany by saying that such a policy would prove in the long run morally repugnant, physically impracticable and economically retrograde. It could not be maintained except by the permanent use of force on a scale which Great Britain and the United States would be unwilling, and no other Power or group of Powers would be able to apply; and it would bring economic disaster to Central Europe, which would have damaging repercussions elsewhere, "l 1 "But the punishment of the Nazi leaders, and their accomplices, does not mean that we are entitled to avenge ourselves on the German people, still less that we shall do ourselves any good by such vengeance...we shall have learned nothing from the grim lessons of the inter-war years unless we realize two things. First, no prolonged control over a major power like Germany is possible without transforming nationalism into chauvinism, and thus preparing the ground in which potents like Hitler grow.... And second, unless, with a 2 defeated major power, there is a rapid recovery of self-respect, the danger is real that the internal relations will lack the stability necessary to give it an ordered place in the life of the international community. This again, will operate to prepare the ground for the advent of men like Hitler."

Consequently it has come to be widely accepted that the criterion for the solution of the German problem is that, if possible, it shall lead toward reconstruction and a lasting peace. This is the policy to which the United Nations seem to be committed by. the Atlantic Charter, the proclamation of the four freedoms and other pronouncements. "On the international plane, said the Earl of 

Selborne, British Minister of Economic Warfare, "the principles that govern our policy are laid down in the Atlantic Charter which recognizes the need for constructive measures after the war,"3 "They seek no aggrandisement, territorial or other"; they offer Germany equal access to trade and raw materials and the fullest collaboration with the object of sharing economic prosperity and social security, and, "after the final destruction of the Nazi tyranny, they hope to see established a peace which will afford to all nations the means of dwelling in safety within their own boundaries, and which will afford assurance that all the men in all the lands may live out their lives in freedom from fear and want" (Article VI).

This means that after the unconditional surrender demanded by the Casablanca and Moscow Declarations, followed by the overthrow of the Nazi regime and the removal of the Nazi leaders, the policy to be pursued should encourage the German people to reverse their political ideals, to give up their dream of world, or even European, domination and to collaborate with the other nations in establishing a peaceful world. 1 Whether a new and democratic leadership will eventually emerge, or whether the state of peace will continue only as long as Germany is under external control is another question and one that must be considered seriously. Moreover, the question is not merely one that concerns the great powers. It is a matter of life and death to Germany! s. smaller victims, and since most of them are members of the 

United Nations, they may have something to say about the decisions reached. At the present time the primary concern of the governments in exile in London is the removal of the threat of German aggression from 2 post-war Europe. This is regarded as the necessary condition for all post-war plans, political, social or economic. 

Therefore, unless there is to be a punitive peace, the problem of what to do with Germany may be stated as the problem of finding the proper adjustment between the restrictive measures necessary to prevent future aggressive action on her part, and measures designed to encourage her to live as a peaceful member of the European community. More simply stated, it is to discover how far Germany can be treated on terms of equality with other nations without at the same time destroying the sense of security of her European neighbors. 

This is an extremely difficult and complicated question, to which there are many different answers. Each of the proposals is based upon its own interpretation of history and analysis of the German character, the most drastic treatment being advocated by those who regard the Germans as inborn aggressors, while those who account for the long course of German aggression as the result largely of external circumstances and events, together with a reactionary and militaristic leadership, rather than due primarily to inherent characteristics, rely upon a process of friendly treatment and education in democratic ideals, to counteract expansionist and aggressive ambitions. The great majority of the latter group, however, would admit the necessity for a period of transition and probation of varying length during which discriminatory treatment would have to be applied.

At the other extreme are those who reject this doctrine in toto, and are opposed to any discrimination or punishment of the Germans, chiefly on the ground that such treatment contains the germs of future war. They regard the Vansittart propaganda as Goebbel's greatest asset, tending as it does to create a greater solidarity among the Germans through their fear of drastic treatment by the victors in case of defeat. 

This fear has been exploited to the full by Goebbels.1 Those who hold this view make a strict differentiation between the Nazis and non-Na zi Germans and denounce the idea of indicting a whole people for the sins of a minority. While they hold no brief for the Nazis, they are inclined to place the responsibility for their rise to power, and even the cause of World War II, largely upon the policies of the democratic governments and the Treaty of Versailles. They insist that any reforms that are to be made in post-war Germany must be left to the Germans themselves after the overthrow of the Nazi regime, which overthrow should itself be brought about through internal action. The proponents of this point of view belong to a wide variety of groups. They are to be found among the pacifists, the Quakers, many of the church organizations, and also the socialists and extreme left. In this country 

Oswald Garrison Villard and Norman Thomas are among the most widely known exponents.

Between these two extreme points of view, based not only upon different conceptions of German character and history, but also upon different estimates of the actual situation within German today (as, for example, the strength of the anti-Nazi underground movement) there exist many gradations of thought. The majority, while rejecting the Vansittart race theory, yet recognize that Germany's geographical position and the events of history have tended to develop certain traits of character; that Prussia's subjugation by Napoleon had its natural reaction in a desire for a powerful central state, that the foreign policies of Frederick the Great and Bismarck, to gether with the teachings of the German philosophers, Kant, Fichte, Hegel, and Nietsche, had a powerful influence in shaping German thought, and prepared the soil in which Nazi doctrines took root; that, indeed, Nazism in its relations with the outside world is nothing more nor less than a revival of the Pan- 

Germanism which led to World War I. Recognizing that it is this common characteristic, expansionism, carried out by Prussian militarism and efficiency, that constitutes the threat to world peace, they hold that certain drastic measures will be necessary to meet this danger. The degree of harshness of the treatment recommended depends upon the particular analysis of the problem. 

The expansionist aim runs consistently through the picture. As Dorothy 

Thompson puts it, "the third Reich is attempting a synthesis of all the Germanies of history, and is able to synthesize them in only one common characteristic: 'expansionism'.

It was at that time that the great emigration of German liberals to America took place. Similarly the German revolution of 1918-19 was directed against the political influences of the army and other reactionary elements who were responsible for German aggression in 1914, but, with the help of the Allied powers, was ultimately defeated because of the fear of bolshevism.2 President Ebert of the Weimar Government called upon the Reichswehr for its support. 

The new Republic, therefore, though nominally under the Social Democratic Party, was actually seldom, if ever, in complete control of power. It was dominated in the background by the Reichswehr, the Junkers, i.e., the Prussian landed aristocracy, whose sons furnished the officers of the Reichswehr, and the big industrialists. It was these three elements who conspired to put Hitler in power at the close of the year 1932 at a time when the Nazi Party had just suffered a major defeat at the polls. The move was actuated by the threat of the division of the Junker's estates, the class to which President Hindenburg belonged, and certain other political developments which tended toward social reform.3 

It is these reactionary elements, so the argument runs, whose power has never been broken, that are responsible for the expansionist, aggressive policies. manifested in German history. If the democratic elements in the population could gain control, the country could be swung towards a policy of cooperation with other nations.

One other view, based upon a fresh diagnosis, must be specifically mentioned: This is the view voiced by Dr. Richard M. Brickner, Assistant Professor of Clinical Neurology, Collège of Physicians and Surgeons, Columbia University, who finds the German nation a victim of mass paranoia and prescribes corresponding treatment.1 This view will be summarized at some length because it is difficult to understand otherwise. 

Thus far, the view has attracted attention rather than support. 

Dr. Brickner points out that in the years preceding 1914 Germany was behaving in virtually the same way as in the years preceding 1939. 

We heard the same complaints about encirclement, being a "have-not" nation, etc. Yet she was no military step-child then. She had colonies, immense military power, tremendous intellectual prestige and a standard of living among the world's highest,

The reason for German behavior, therefore, is farther to seek than in the Treaty of Versailles. The psychiatric diagnosis, of Germany, he says, is that the emotional core of German culture is: and, has long been growingly and menacingly paranoid in character. These trends have been developing steadily through more than a century of German history, and the behavior of the German group shows all the symptoms of paranoia as the doctor sees them in individual patients. The Nazis are only a symptor of this paranoid core, they cannot be dissected out from the Germar group as specially responsible for Germany's conduct.. They are extrem because the symptoms are growing steadily stronger. 

From this diagnosis Dr. Brickner deduces the answer to the problem of what to do with Germany. He rejects what he calls the method of 

Versailles and the Atlantic Charter, both of which assume that aggression develops solely from material factors and that readjustment of boundaries, markets and social strains may provide a solution. While naturally complete disarmament is necessary for safety, as well as social and economic rehabilitation, the job cannot stop there.

Transitional Period 

Whatever the permanent policy adopted and whatever the steps toward that end to be undertaken toward Germany, it is not expected that these would be put into operation, or even initiated, immediately. Practically everyone foresees a period of transition between armistice and peace treaty, varying in length from a year or two to five or ten years, or even a generation, dependent necessarily upon the actual situation and the purpose for which the period is utilized. 

The United States State Department divides the post-war period into three parts: The immediate or armistice period, the period of reconstruction and the permanent period.4 More commonly, however, a division is made in terms of two periods, the transitional and that of long-term solutions. 5. 

There is considerable difference of opinion as to the character of the transition period, especially as the use to which it will be put. At first, statesmen and experts spoke of it as a "cooling off" period between the end of hostilities, or the making of peace, as it is sometimes called, and the setting up of more lasting arrangements to serve as long term solutions of post-war problems. During this period, while the necessary first steps were being taken to establish order and set life on a more or less normal footing again, time would be given for the cooling off of 'heated passions. This would make possible the conclusion of a peace treaty or treaties which would have a better chance to survive than those of 1919.6

By the end of the transition period, however, permanent governments will have been set up, plebiscites, if any, will have been held, and permanent boundaries fixed, (See Chapters IV and VIII). Decisions should have been reached concerning the future organization of Europe and its relation with the rest of the world, (See Chapter X). 

The generally accepted position among post-war planners is that some form of world organization will be necessary in order to ensure a durable peace, 1 although some experts see the future overall political organization as an out-growth of international economic collaboration through specific commissions. According to various Gallup Polls, 75 per cent of the American people now recognize a world organization as necessary and are prepared to support United States participation in such an organization. By a vote of 85 to 5 the United States Senate (November 5, 1943) adopted the Connally resolution incorporating the 

Moscow Declaration (paragraph 4) as follows: 

That the Senate recognizes the necessity of there being established at the earliest practicable date a general international organization, based on the principle of the sovereign equality of all peace-loving states, and open to membership by all such states, large or small, for the maintenance of international peace and security.

Within the universal organization, in order to make it effective, regional organizations are also proposed. The European Council proposed by Winston Churchill in his speech of March 21, 1943, or some form of European federation like the United States of Europe which was laid before the League of Nations in 1929 by Aristide Briand, are examples. Steps have already been taken toward smaller regional confederations, such as the intention to form a confederation between Poland and Czechoslovakia officially announced in the joint Polish- 

Czech Declaration of November 1942, and the Greek-Yugoslav Agreement of January 15, 1942, concerning the constitution of a Balkan Union. 

These questions of European and universal organization do not fall within the scope of this report, except as they affect the solution of the German problem in setting up the basis of security in Europe. A number of those who are working on this problem, especially in England, see the only constructive answer in terms of some form of European federation or confederation. However loose such a confederation, it appears to offer the only basis for equality of treatment for Germany, while at the same time making her subject to restrictions.. 

In their view a federation could provide effective political and military guarantees against German aggression and thus would tend to meet the fears of Germany's less powerful neighbors. Failing such a solution, the peace conference is likely to be faced by a demand from the formerly occupied countries for a policy of dismemberment, permanent unilateral dismemberment; eoercion and economic discrimination such as would require some permanent military organization to enforce and would furnish the makings of future wars.

There remains the question of the admission of Germany to membership in such international organizations as may be set up, universal or European. While it is generally assumed that she will eventually become a member, there is difference of opinion as to whether she should be admitted without delay on equal terms with other nations, and even participate in the establishment of these organizations, or whether membership should be permitted only at some future time and then only upon the fulfillment of certain conditions which will testify to her good will. With regard to a European. federation, there arises at once the question of Great Britain and Russia. Should they also be members, and if not, would there not be a risk of domination by 

Germany, economic if not military? While this risk cannot be denied, the proponents of this plan claim that it can be met by constitutional means; that all members should be treated as equals. 

Undue predominace of any large country in a European federation must be checked by means other than partition of discrimination. 

Among the means proposed are a proper composition of the federal legislature and the distribution of votes, general European disarmament and common control of foreign and military affairs, general educational endeavors, and freedom on the part of any of the smaller states to form subsidiary combinations within the general framework. Two of such combinations are already envisaged in the agreements between Poland and Czechoslovakia and between Yugoslavia and Greece, already mentioned, both of which are open to accession by other states.

While the questions involved in the federalization of Europe or smaller regions remain highly controversial, there is general agreement among those who believe that we must make peace with Germany, rather than against her (to use Dorothy Thompson's phraseologyl) on the simpler proposition that the problem of Germany cannot be isolated, but that Germany's future is bound up with international social, economic, and political developments as a whole. 

This conviction is expressed by E.H. Carr as follows: 

"Indeed, the more the problem of rehabilitation of Germany is studied, the doarer does it become that it. remains insoluble until it is transplanted into a broader setting. We cannot solve it by treating Germans as Germans and nothing more. If the problem of Europe is the problem of Germany, it can be settled only with à European framework in which Germans can become not merely objects of policy but partners in carrying it out."2
