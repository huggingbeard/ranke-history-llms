FOREWORD 

This book is a set of formulas, a series of blueprints telling how to construct verse in such a way that it will sell. It considers verse only, and not literary poetry. True poetry is not made by following directions. Salable verse is. 

I have not discussed such things as kinds of verse, meter, rhythm, etc. All of that can be found in any high school or college textbook, or in any public 

I have quoted from my own published verse for two reasons: I did not need to secure permission to quote it, and I prove by using it, that even verse that is no better than mine, will sell.

I have used the pronoun she often, assuming that more women write verse than men, and that more women than men will read this book. 

My sole purpose in writing the book is to share my crumb or two of experience with others; to give them a desire to sell their own verse, and then tell them how to go about selling it.

B. S. A.

FIRST CATCH THE RABBIT 

An old English recipe for rabbit stew begins in this manner, "First catch the rabbit." We will follow those directions to this extent: we will catch the rabbit verse, examine it, and try to determine how it differs from its second cousin twice removed, the Belgian-hare poetry.

It is possible to grow both on our mental farms, but there is a difference.

When you write a poem, you are filled, saturated, with an emotion; so moved with a thought that you are driven by a force almost outside yourself to put it on paper. You have no thought as to what an editor will say or think. You follow no formula. 

You aim at no particular magazine target. You allow the words to come as they will, to flow easily. 

You create the poem in the best manner possible, as any honest craftsman would. It bears the stamp of 1.

your individuality. It is bone of your bone. It is a personal, individual thing. It is the best that you can write at that time. Later you may polish it, change a word here and there, or add to it. You may sell it; if not, the joy of creating it is no small reward. After all, perhaps you did not write it to sell. 

The verse writer does not necessarily feel any emotion; he builds up a synthetic emotion if the real one is lacking. He has one eye on the verse he is writing, and the other on a particular editor on whom he intends to inflict the finished product. If that editor prefers vanilla, he gives him that; if he wishes mint or raspberry, he offers that.

While the poetry writer is spending two hundred years building a beautiful cathedral, the verse writer is setting up a pleasant little camp for twenty-four hours.

The verse writer's aim is a small check day after tomorrow, and other small checks next week. His secondary aim (and not a bad one) is to give someone a little chuckle as he reads the effusion.

The great artist, poet, or musician is usually ahead of his time. It sometimes takes fifty or a hundred years for the world to catch up. Poetry that becomes popular immediately, and that is read by the masses, 2

FIRST CATCH THE RABBIT 

is usually not the kind that lasts through the centuries.

Ben Jonson ranked higher in his own day than Shakespeare. Goethe was not counted as one of Germany's greatest poets during his lifetime. Anne 

Bradstreet, early Americana, was rated as one of the greatest poets in her day. It was said that her poetry would "outlast the stateliest marble"; it didn't. No one reads Bradstreet now except the student of early American literature. Browning was fifty years being recognized. E. A. Robinson is less widely known than Edgar Guest, but his poems will probably last longer.

This book is for those who want a little check now; who want to make a business of writing; who care nothing for fame one hundred years from now. Fame is an elusive thing anyway.

One cannot take a salt shaker and attempt to put salt on the tail of fame as we were told to do with birds when we were children. There is only one way to find out which one of our contemporaries will be considered the best poet a hundred years from now. That is: buy a ringside seat in Father

Time's amphitheater, take a few blankets and cans of sardines, and camp there for the hundred years. 

At the end of that time when the list of good poets for 1940 is read, you may be surprised. Your pet 3

poet may be number 67 on the list; he may not be on the list at all. Some 1940 unknown may head the list. That is the way it has often happened in the past. Many things go in cycles; it may happen again. 

For that reason, let us not worry about the then; let us be concerned with the now. A little check will buy a new spring hat, pay a small bill, buy a concert ticket, pay for a subscription for a writers' magazine.

II ATTITUDE 

Your attitude has a great deal to do with your success in anything. If you feel that you would be disgraced to sell verse, then do not try to sell it. 

First, let us see whether any of the ancient models sold or wrote verse. Longfellow wrote poetry and a little verse. The "Mr. Finney and the Turnip" of his is pure verse, so is his "Doctor Fell." 

Robert Browning, who certainly wrote poetry, wrote verse on occasion. He had a little sick friend in the hospital and wrote some verse to amuse the child and to take his mind off his pain. 

The verses that Shakespeare is said to have tacked on the park gate of Sir Thomas Lucey were certainly not poetry. The lines on his tomb are verse too, and not very good verse. 

Most of our model writers wrote some verse, either for fun or recreation. It is good relaxation.

Verse writing does not necessarily interfere with your writing of poems. While you are refining and polishing a poem with your right hand, while you are holding it a few months until it is as nearly perfect as you can make it, why not write some verse with your left hand?

Some writers like to write one form exclusively, others many kinds. We can find exponents of both schools in models of the past. 

Many great writers have not only used different mediums in writing, but also have been famous in some other profession. 

Scott is as well known for his novels as for his poems. Van Dyke wrote essays, plays, poems, sermons, and short stories. Willard Huntington Wright became famous first for writing heavy scientific articles, later under another name (S. S. Van Dyne) for his detective stories. Shakespeare is as well known for his sonnets as for his dramas. Da Vinci was famous as scientist, artist, and sculptor. Michelangelo is equally as well known for his sculpture as for his painting. Browning was undecided for a long time whether to devote his talents to music, to art, or to poetry.

Don't feel that something is radically wrong with you if you want to clean the attic one day, write a sonnet the next, do a little play the next, compose

ATTITUDE 

a minuet the next, and talk at the P.T.A. the next. 

You are a well-balanced individual; you are not onesided. Each one of your talents acts as a feeder for the others. You will never die of monotony. It should be gratifying that your versatility is such that you do not have to confine yourself each day to the task of writing a lyric, or painting a picture. 

Now, assuming that your attitude is right, and that you are not ashamed to admit that you write verse and sell it; assuming that you can look anyone right in the eye and say, "Willie Shakespeare did it -I can too," then read on. Anything that Shakespeare and Browning can do, can be done by Ebenezer Philander Jones, and Penelope Charity Greensward, and you and me.

If you still think verse a stepchild of the muse, don't write it. 

If you want to write a sublime heroic poem that will be proclaimed as the best of its kind two hundred years from now, write it if you can. I'd like to, too, but I can't. I can't write a Paradise Lost. I can't even read all of it. (Oh, you didn't either? I thought as much!) I admit that it is one of the greatest poems of its kind in the world, but I don't want to read that kind.

III WHY DO YOU WANT TO WRITE? 

If your purpose in writing is to gain fame, if your aim is to have a three and one-half-inch space in the English books fifty years hence, you are wasting your time reading this.

People write for various reasons. This book is for those who wish to write to sell; who are willing to follow a formula, to observe the do and don't signals, and to receive a few little checks from little magazines at first, then more and larger ones later. 

Books a plenty have been written on the technical side of poetry; books bristling with eighteen-dollar words like amphibrach, assonance, canzonet, onomatopeia, etc. And good they are. But many of them assume that the reader is on speaking terms with many prerequisites, when he is not. Others assume that he already calls editors by their first names, when actually he does not know their names.

WHY DO YOU WANT TO WRITE? 

This little book is for the beginner, and hopes to dispense with most of the technical terms. It is for the beginner in selling, not necessarily the beginner in writing.

It contains no recipe for literary success; there is none. You either can or you can't, and that's all there is to it. No one can tell you how to be a Markham, a Frost, a Teasdale, a Browning or a Shakespeare. They became famous in a literary way, but not by following a formula.

This book is for those who want to write commercially. There are formulas for that kind of writing. One can follow them as one does a recipe for biscuits. A spoon of this, plus a cup of that, plus a handful of the other, equals a check. It is as easy to follow specifications in writing verse as in building a garage, or making an apple dumpling.

Perhaps your motive is to entertain people, give them a minute of fun, make them chuckle. You can do that with verse. True, they may forget the verse day after tomorrow, but what of it? You forgot the strawberries and ice cream you ate last week, but it was good while it lasted.

IV MAKE IT A BUSINESS 

Make verse writing a business; use business methods. Don't be dependent on praise, flattery, coddling. Don't be too easily crushed; don't be too sensitive.

When a manufacturer sets up a factory for making screws and bolts he takes a business attitude toward it. He doesn't need to have some gushing individual come in and say, "What an adorable rivet," in order to keep up production. He turns out the best screw he can and lets it stand on its own spiral.

When a plumber gets a call about a broken pipe he goes at once, even though it is at ten o'clock at night; else the kitchen will be flooded and he will lose the customer. The doctor goes to see the patient when he is called and does not wait to finish the 10

MAKE IT A BUSINESS 

paper he may be writing to read at the medical association meeting come next June.

Don't pay too much attention to your friend's estimate of the verse. He is not an editor, usually; he does not have the editorial attitude. He may have taught English ten years; he may be president of the "literariest" club in town, and still not be able to write salable verse.

When an idea comes and dances around on your chest, or weaves in and out among your brain coils, grab it then and there and jot it down on paper, or it may leave never to return. True, it may come in the middle of the night; it may come when you are right in the middle of making a lemon chiffon pie, but if you are in business take it, and be glad that it came to you at all. 

Another thing: you can never sell much verse if you are overmethodical and punctilious about your housekeeping. If you are the kind that must always have the washing done on Monday, always do the spring housecleaning on a definite calendar date, wash dishes immediately after each meal, you will lose many verse ideas. 

You must attain to a state of abstraction: reach such heights (or depths) that when you are writing verse and happen to look up at a corner in the room and see a spider web, a lovely festoon, you will not 11

run, grab the broom and remove the blot from your domestic 'scutcheon. You will calmly keep on writing; you will wink and say to the spider, "That is very nice, but the drapery on the left side could be made more graceful. See if you can make a prettier one while I finish this."

Don't worry about the friend who may come in and see the web. If she is a real friend, she will understand. If she is a false friend you should not be playing around with her anyway; good way to get rid of her.

Get a little file box and cards. Keep an accurate record of each poem-each on a separate card. You must know where each verse is at all times. You must never send the same poem to an editor twice. 

Work out your own system of keeping records and files. Each person has her favorite. 

Have some large filing cases for your files of back numbers of writers' magazines; for sample copies of different magazines that you will collect and need to have close at hand for reference. 

If you can have your workroom at the back of the house, it is best for these reasons: you need not let callers see its untidiness; you need not put things away each day, but can leave them out to use. My husband and I both write, and we "see alike" about the workroom. Our first article in the by-laws is: 12

MAKE IT A BUSINESS 

never put anything away, and never destroy any little scrap of paper found on the floor. It may have an important address on it; it may contain the germ of a five-dollar verse. Our filing case for magazines is a stack of orange crates seven deep, with open face.

Not so much for looks, but perfect for convenience. 

We can reach over at any time and get the magazine desired.

Your desk need not be a piece of period furniture either. A Queen Anne is no more conducive to good verse writing than a Mary Anne. I prefer the Mary 

Anne, and a battered one at that. When I want to be a "doodler" and draw little goats with five horns, or daisies with seventeen petals, I can.

Alabaster inkwells do not promote verse-atility. 

Have regular hours to work, and produce something, whether it is good or not. You will soon find that you can write whether you are in the mood or not. The clerk doesn't say, "I'll not go down to the store this morning; I am not in the mood to sell granite pans." Edison's remark about perspiration and inspiration is still true. It works with verse as well as with other work. 

Two hours may be all that you can spare for creative work in a day. If you are tired writing then, spend the other two or more hours working on your 13

files, studying writers' magazines, studying markets, making fresh copies of poems to send out. 

If the copy is shopworn, if there is a clip mark at the top announcing that it has been out before, if there are too many folds in the manuscript, make a new one. Remember the collar story. A man asked his wife if she thought his collar was too much soiled to wear another day, and her caustic reply was, “If it is doubtful, it is dirty." So is a manuscript. 

Learn to use the typewriter. Get a book of instructions and follow carefully, practicing much.

Having your poems typed by someone else is expensive, and too much trouble. Often the typist wants to improve your punctuation, and likes to slip in her favorite adjective instead of yours. 

Don't wait until you have finished all of your other work, and then sit down to write. There will never be such a time. I have found that when I am busiest with other things, that is the time that I can write best. It seems that the wheels of my mind are already running fast, and I can divert some of the power to writing verse, while using the main part for the thing in hand. When all the work is finished, the tempo of the mind is slow. 

Were you ever awakened by the cream separator some morning on the farm? Buzz-zz-er-er-er-. It goes faster and faster and becomes louder and louder. 14

MAKE IT A BUSINESS 

But it throws off no cream until it has reached a certain speed, attained its maximum pitch-its coloratura soprano high note. Then the cream flows.

When your mind is at a lazy low, when it is in a summer afternoon mood, there will be little cream.

But when you are busy, keyed up, at a white heat, then there is cream.

The first few occasions that you attempt to force yourself to write at regular times, you may compose little. But soon the habit will be formed. Most famous writers keep office hours. They write something each day, even though they may destroy some of it the next day. 15

INGREDIENTS OF THE VERSE 

Verse is not different from other things that possess feet. Horses look better with four horse feet than they would with a foot of an elephant and one of a dog, with two of their own.

See that the feet of your verse are mates, or if you use two kinds, see that they don't fight.

Often two kinds are used in the same verse. Here are two kinds that blend well: He came with a jump; he came with a bound. Anapestic and iambic usually marry and live in harmony. Some kinds of feet are incompatible.

Watch the words. Some words in a line seem to be melted and run together, and they read smoothly. 

Others fight and are antagonistic. If you can, choose words that sound well together; at least select those that are not too belligerent.

Alliteration has gone out of style; use it sparingly 16

INGREDIENTS OF THE VERSE 

in commercial verse. If something "throws a thrill through you," say it in other ways.

Be careful about punctuation, but don't be too much distressed about it. Underpunctuate rather than overpunctuate. Punctuation is the pepper; don't season too highly. The semicolon is the most misused mark, I think. Let's put it this way-if your two statements are not joined by an and or a but, use a semicolon; if the connective is used, put a comma between them. As: 

I miss the fine October; I miss the autumn haze. or I miss the fine October, and I miss the autumn haze Don't be a dash-hound. Some people punctuate by feeling. Their one blanket rule is, "When in doubt, use a dash." They feel one coming on about every five words, and their finished pages are stabbed through and through with little dash swords. The dash has its uses: to show change in thought, to mark an abrupt break in a sentence, etc., but do not use it just because you are too lazy to read the rules and find out what mark should really be used. 

Now as to rhyme. Rhyme is not using two words that sound somewhat alike. It is using two words that sound exactly alike, from the accented syllable on out to the end of the word. Coming and beginning both have ing, but they are not rhymes, because 17

they do not rhyme from the accent on out. Glow and throws are not rhymes because one ends in s and the other does not. Home and come are not exactly alike; therefore they are not rhymes. The words must sound alike; they need not be spelled alike. 

Days and haze rhyme perfectly, but are not spelled alike. Rhyme is a matter of sound, not spelling or meaning. Loan and home are not rhymes. One ends in an n and the other in an m sound. 

Rhyming dictionaries are a great help in finding rhymes. They are timesavers, and if published by a reliable publishing house, are accurate and dependable.

The structure of your poem-rhyme, meter, form, etc.-make up only part of the job. Form alone won't make a poem. You might take a list of words from the dictionary and arrange them in such a way that the rhyme, meter, etc., would be correct; you might even build a sonnet form in that way. 

That is only the husk; that is the frame for the picture; that is the setting for the jewel. It is still not a poem or verse unless it says something. After all, the thought of the poem is the main tent. The message it carries. Don't let the tail wag the dog.

Don't polish it to death. Don't sacrifice meaning to faultlessness in form. Clement Wood says, "Remem- 18

INGREDIENTS OF THE VERSE 

ber this important fact: flawlessness in metric verse is its chief flaw; perfection in metric poetry is the major imperfection." He also says, "Any artificiality of speech, of any kind is a blemish; an artificial wart on the face of your poem." 19

STREAMLINING THE VERSE 

In vaudeville, the joke must be timed; in short stories the plot must be brought to a climax, and there must be a build-up. This is true of verse. 

There must be a build-up; there must be a punch in the last line or two. You must reach a higher point at the end than you have reached in any other part of the verse. You must spring a surprise; give it an O. Henry twist; bring the chuckle to a head. 

You must place your strongest statement there; you must arrive.

Don't spill the point in the title or first lines. 

Lead up-build up. Rise and rise until you reach something. Don't let the reader fall like a sad cake in the oven.

One good way to be sure that you get the punch in the last lines, is to write those two lines first, then work back. 

Have a good beginning to capture interest. Have 20

STREAMLINING THE VERSE 

a good last couplet to leave a good taste in the mouth of the reader, and make him want to read some more verse written by that same gal. Fill in the middle with weaker timber if you must, but slave on the start and the finish. Think hard; chew several pencils if necessary.

Don't make the sentences too long in verse. An eight-line verse made of only one sentence is too long, even if the phrases and clauses and semicolons are grammatically correct and march along in regular formation. 

Beware of inversions; most editors hate them like poison ivy. What is an inversion? It is a cheap contortion. Here is one: 

My country 'tis of thee; 

Quite well the song sang he.

The second line is twisted out of its natural order. 

It is very evident that I broke its spine and twisted its neck in order to get the he at the end so it would rhyme with thee. People often perform major and minor operations to get a certain word at the end. 

The rhyme is perfect, but it sins in too many other ways. All lines should be arranged as they would be said in prose, in real life, and not contorted and warped.

Yes, I know that it is sometimes hard to avoid an inversion. You may have a perfectly precious rhyme 21

with the inversion, and be unable to find one without it. You must often tear the whole verse to pieces and rebuild from the foundation, just because you remove one little inversion. But do it at any cost. 

Once in a great while, you may be able to sell verse with inversions and contractions if it has some other strong quality, some clever denouement that carries it in spite of the inversion. But you are safer if you remove them. 

Contractions are out of date too. E'er, o'er, isn't,

I'd, etc., are not used as much as they were long ago. 

It is true that one sees them sometimes. When you are well enough known, you can make your own rules. Beginners must conform to accepted taboos as long as they are beginners. 

Someone once complained to Beethoven, saying the consecutive fifths in a composition of his were incorrect. "Who said they were?" asked Beethoven. "Albrechtsberger," answered the other. "Well, I say they are correct," said Beethoven.

And thereafter, they were correct. He was big enough to break an old dictum, make a new rule, and set a precedent. Consecutive fifths are common now.

One of the first things you need to decide in writing verse is to decide about the length. You like to 22

write long poems? Oh, but that is the first don't.

Many editors like them short; four-, six-, or eightline ones sell twice as fast as those of twenty or more lines. Why? Many of them are used as fillers.

There's a little space at the bottom of the column. 

Twenty lines would be too much; four lines may be just right. You don't want to be a filler, you say?

VII MARKETING 

When your little cousin Eliphalet decides to become a banker, he does not begin as president of the bank. Usually he is errand boy or something like that. The boy who has just been graduated from medical school does not usually become head of the hospital the next day. He starts at the bottom and works up.

Some verse writers want to hit Satevepost, Forum, Atlantic Monthly and Good Housekeeping at once.

It has been done by beginners, but that is the exception, not the rule.

The first thing of all, assuming that you have never had anything printed, and have been placing your verse in desk drawers, shoe boxes, and baking powder cans, and under the dresser scarf,-the first thing is to get yourself in print in any way that you can. Do it by hook or by crook. Maybe the small 24

MARKETING 

town newspaper will oblige. Perhaps the verse magazines, the little ones, the not-so-well-known ones, will take your poem and publish it. 

The better "Little Poetry Magazines" use good poetry, and it means something to have your poems printed in them, even though they may not pay you for them. Some of the littler little ones use most anything they can get. They may even ask you to pay them to let you appear in their pages. 

The mere bursting into print does something to you. It renews your efforts; causes you to want to do bigger and better things; causes your stock to go up among your friends and neighbors-all of which is good and right.

The next step is to sell something. That does still more to reinforce your belief in your own ability. 

If some one pays you, even five cents a line, you can truthfully say, "I sold a poem last week," and your next-door neighbor will want to touch the hem of your garment when she returns the salt she borrowed.

The next step is to sell more at higher rates. Still later, bring out a book if you can. You will probably have to pay for it yourself. But the bringing out of a book has its effect on the dear public. They don't know that it is just a business transaction. You take your sheaf of poems in one hand, your money 25

in the other, and the publisher will publish it, if it isn't too bad. Just as the milliner will make you that hat for ten dollars-made to order-a commodity. But it is the psychology of the thing that counts. It raises your estimate of yourself and gives you courage. There's always the chance that the publisher some day will take it on his own risk. Then you will be justified in displaying a mite of cockiness. "I love to write; I hate to market," says the beginner-seller. "It is so hard to know where to send what." Yes, it is. To some people, marketing is the dishwashing of the verse-kitchen. Why not make it a game? It can be done. See if you can hit certain markets as a target. If you are now selling one out of every twenty-nine that you send out, work harder to know markets and see whether you can sell one out of every nineteen that you send out. You can if you follow the blueprints.

Get the dope sheets as you would for a race. Writers' magazines tell you all that you need to know about any market. They are the social register. 

They tell you of the Virginia-or-Bluffville ancestry, the financial status of each magazine. They tell the type of magazine, rates paid, likes and dislikes of editors, whether reliable, slow paying, unsatisfactory, etc. 26

MARKETING 

Deal only with reputable magazines and you will never have a poem lost or stolen. Not long ago I had a certain poem ready to send to a particular magazine. I looked in the Author and Journalist list of markets and saw this at the end of the information about the magazine, "unsatisfactory." Of course, I did not send it to that place.

Journalist, both excellent, are dependable. They invite all writers to report to them any unfair dealings, any unpleasant experiences they may have had with any publication. If true, they tell the world about it through their pages. They exist for the purpose of helping writers. They welcome anything that will expose cheats, etc. You will not be a tattletale if you tell. You will be doing your fellow craftsmen a favor.

Besides all this, they tell new editors, changes of format in magazines, or changes of policy, birth of new magazines, death of old ones (the birth and death rate is enormous) .

VIII SECRET OF SUCCESS 

The secret of true success is bound up in the two p's, perseverance and postage stamps. If a poem has been out several times, ten perhaps, it may sell on the eleventh trip. I sold one on the thirteenth.

Don't keep the verse in the house longer than overnight if it comes back. Make it better if you can; otherwise send it right out as is. 

Don't wait for one lone poem to come back before sending others out. Keep at least fifty in circulation all the time, more if possible. Then you will be sure to have some checks coming all the time. Group them differently sometimes; the same ones need not be envelope-mates on each trip. But do keep them traveling.

Don't pay too much attention to how the other. fellow does his work. Get all the light you can on the subject, then formulate your own manner of procedure. 29

Keep an accurate filing system, so that you will not find that you have sent a group of verses to two editors at the same time.

Take one or more good writers' magazines and read them to pieces, every issue. Familiarize yourself with markets; learn all about them. The more you know about their requirements, peeves, etc., the less postage you will waste.

Learn to slant. Don't try to make the editor like the kind of poems you write. You write the kind of poems that he likes, and he will buy them. Build them by formula, just as a stonemason builds a wall. 

Remember, you are out after checks, and not to remake the literary taste of the world.

Watch magazines for changes in editors and changes of policy. Often, about the time you become familiar with a certain editor's requirements, along will come a new editor with a different set of peeves. 

Learn them. Keep up with the band wagon. Every other craftsman takes the journal of his vocation; you should take yours if you intend to be a commercial writer. The beautician, teacher, preacher, mortician, baker, etc., takes his and profits by reading it. 

Take the ones that best suit your needs. Each has his preference. There are several good ones on every news stand. 30

IX SURE-FIRE SALES 

Seasonal poems are sure sellers; that is, Christmas, Easter, Mothers' Day, Valentine, etc. But send them to the editor about five or six months before the occasion.

Many magazines and papers use as many as five of these at any particular season. The denominational and Sunday school papers buy quantities of them each year. They are easy to write. 

Poems that are inspirational sell well. It is almost impossible to sell verse about death, unhappy things, disasters, etc. People like to read about happy, cheerful things.

Juvenile poetry is the easiest of all to write and sell. It requires less polishing than verse for adults. 

Nothing is too trivial to use as a cornerstone for a juvenile poem.

Verse that is obvious rather than obscure, sells well. People like to read as they run. They want to 31

race through the verse, gain the idea the first rapid reading. They are annoyed if they must read it a second time to determine what it is about. Obscure verse is somewhat like futuristic painting. The readers do not all like it. They want the picture to be sufficiently obvious so that they will know definitely whether it is a baked halibut or a storm at sea. Some critic said of Longfellow's poetry that, "There is not sufficient obstruction to the stream of thought." But the average reader prefers it without obstructions. 

Some magazines like obscure poetry, but beginners are safer if they use only the obvious kind.

Human interest verse, verse making a general appeal, sells well.

Instead of striving to find new subjects, try to say the old thing in a new way. New ideas are rare things. If you can say the old truism in a new way, give it a new twist or slant, you can sell it. If it is too strange or too different, it will not sell, unless you can find a magazine that handles off-trail stuff.

Gertrude Stein is not universally popular. 

Try the little farm magazines, the Sunday school papers, the smaller publications at first, and work up from those as you learn more about slanting and marketing. Most of these will be glad to send you a sample of their publications if you ask for it. Study them, and try to fit your verse to their requirements. 32

SURE-FIRE SALES 

If we arrange subjects in the order of their selling quality, our list will, no doubt, look something like this: 

1. Humorous verse. 

2. Verse for or about children. 

3. Seasonal verse. 

4. Religious verse-not pious, but sincere. 

5. Nature verse. 

6. Love verse. 

7. Philosophic, racial and social, war, symbolic verse. 33

GROOMING YOUR VERSE 

Before you send your brain-child out into the cold cruel world to try its fate among the editors, be sure that it is physically fit and that it is groomed properly.

When it is as well composed as you can make it, it is still not ready to travel. Examine it carefully; make a last inspection tour for inversion-adenoids; see about contraction-tonsils; make certain that it is garbed meticulously in harmonizing rhyme; assure yourself that its rhythm necktie is not off key; don't turn it loose until you are sure that it is entirely ready to go.

Its coat is important. Don't use a tiny envelope that looks like a too-small-hand-me-down. Use a number ten envelope that requires only two folds, or a still larger one that needs only one fold. 

Does the manuscript look professional? Is your name in the upper left hand corner, and your ad- 34

GROOMING YOUR VERSE 

dress? Have you written "usual rates" or whatever kind of rates you want in the upper right hand corner? Have you double spaced your lines, with triple space between stanzas? Have you enclosed a stamped addressed envelope, so that if no one wants the little brat it will have a round-trip ticket to come limping back home? Have you used enough postage?

Have you made a fresh copy so that when it arrives, it won't embarrass you by shrieking out at the editor, "I have been places; I have been around!"?

It must seem to be untraveled, innocent and unsophisticated, whether it actually is or not. 35

XI REJECTION SLIPS 

If you have a great big sensitive bump in your nature; if you become angry when your brain child comes home with a clip mark over its left eyebrow, and with its dress soiled; if you feel crushed and embarrassed when you receive a rejection slip and resolve never, never to send out another verse—well, you will not get many checks.

Rejection slips are no disgrace. All of the great writers who have sold poetry have received them. 

The manuscript that sells the first trip out is the exception, not the rule. 

Magnificent Obsession, a best seller, went out many times before it found a home. Fannie Hurst sent dozens of stories to the Saturday Evening Post before it accepted one. Wilbur Daniel Steele wrote many stories before he became a model in the English textbooks. 

When the rejection slip says, "Rejection of poem 36

REJECTION SLIPS 

does not imply lack of merit," it is usually the truth. 

There are many reasons for rejecting manuscripts besides the lack of merit of the lines. Maybe the editor has already accepted a poem on the same general theme; maybe he is overstocked; maybe he has used the money provided for verse for that year. 

Don't feel that a rejection slip is a cold, clammy slap in the face. The editor has no time to write personal letters to each contributor. If he did he would need several more stenographers. He may get thousands of offerings each year. Think what that would mean the writing of letters to all those people telling exactly why he didn't accept the verse. 

Grow up! Be your age! You are in business. Do you always explain to the man at the door selling oranges or pot holders, just exactly why you are not buying? No, you don't. 37

XII TITLES 

Titles must be short for verse. They ought to suggest the theme, but must not spill the beans. Don't tell the trick in the title, or in the first two lines, for if you tell it there, the reader will have no cause to read the remainder of the verse. 

Don't promise more than you deliver. Don't mislead or cheat.

Don't use a title as a show window in which you display something that the customer is unable to buy inside.

Usually one-, two-, or three-word titles are better than five- or six-word titles.

Don't use a strange word that the reader, Mr. and 

Mrs. Average Citizen, will not be likely to understand. This applies to the body of the verse as well as to the title. Not long ago I saw the word prestidigitator used in the body of verse. That might be permissible in a poem, but not in verse. It annoys the reader; makes him feel inferior. 38

XIII SOURCES OF MATERIAL 

Poems and verse are all around us; they are on the street, in the kitchen, at church, at the grocery store, everywhere. Keep a notebook and pencil in your purse (both purses) all the time. When an idea comes write a key word down to hold it until you have time to develop it. Keep a notebook and pencil handy in the house for the same reason. A pencil tied to a string, and hanging on the wall with a pad of paper is a good way. A tied pencil stays put better than a loose one. The loose one is always gone when you need it.

Write about the common things, the things that you are familiar with. Nothing is too small to put into a verse. 

Read books of proverbs, sayings, quotations.

These make good starters. They often set the poetic wheels turning. They suggest ideas for you.

Keep scrapbooks of poems that you find in papers and magazines, particularly of the kind you wish 39

to write. These are not to copy, of course, but to gain ideas from.

Sit down occasionally alone; clean the slate of your mind and start at zero in the thinking process -think of nothing. Wait, and nine times out of ten, something will come, an idea, a first line, something on which to build.

Read much verse and poetry. Take it apart and see how it is constructed. 

Don't spend all of your time in your ivory tower reading and writing. Associate with people; learn to analyze situations; hunt for poetic angles. In the middle of the sermon you may get the germ for a religious poem. At the pickle counter you may get the cornerstone for a bit of domestic verse. A stray remark in a restaurant may develop into a five-dollar verse. I gained one there recently. I saw a large man holding his little finger out daintily while he drank his coffee. I worked it out into verse, calling it "My 

Pet Peeve." 40

XIV DEBUNKING THE VERSE PROPAGANDA 

Examine all of the dictums that are handed you so glibly, before incorporating them into your own personal constitution and by-laws. Josh Billings said, "It's better not to know so much, than to know so much that ain't so."

One dictum you hear most frequently (from those who have not sold much!) is this: you must know the editors personally in order to sell. Not true, of course.

You will be advised to start at the top and work down; to send first to the Satevepost, Harper's, Atlantic Monthly, American, etc. If your motive is helping to lessen the postal deficit, that is correct; if you want to sell, that is poor advice for the beginner. Your chances for sales are better if you begin at the bottom and work up. The one who sells to the larger and more literary magazines first (and it has been done) is an exception and not a rule. 41

You will be told that you must be a member of the particular denomination whose magazine you are striving to hit. "Taint so. I have sold to Methodist, Catholic, Nazarene, Evangelical, Mormon publications and, in fact, about all the denominations. 

I couldn't very well be a member of all of those churches. They still don't know what my church preference is, and do not care. 

Another dictum is that if you subscribe for the publication at the time you submit your verse, the editor will take it. Ridiculous! That is a false prop on which to lean; it will let you down hastily. 

Then, some one will tell you that you must belong to certain clubs and societies. That is not necessary. Editors won't know whether you do or not.

You will want to belong to some literary clubs perhaps, but not for that reason. You will find some of them helpful for the contacts, inspiration, news of the craft, helpful hints from fellow workmen, etc. 

Their help is indirect. Editors, frankly, don't care whether you do or do not belong. 

You will hear this: that the verse came back because the editor did not really read it. He actually reads all contributions, he or some member of his staff. There is always that chance that there will be a "find" in the morning mail, and he wouldn't want to miss it. However, it may not be necessary to read 42

DEBUNKING THE VERSE PROPAGANDA 

all of your verse to tell that it is impossible. An editor once made the remark that it was not always necessary to eat a whole egg to determine that it was rotten.

The argument as to whether you should write more than one kind of poetry, whether you must follow it all of your time and have no other vocation, and as to whether you must actually see the magazine before submitting to it, have all been discussed elsewhere in this book.

There is a belief in some circles that publishers have scouts in all towns; that they write to one of these when someone from a certain town submits a poem; that they take his opinion as to whether the poet is on the black list or the white list; that these scouts are dictators, and put thumbs down or up as they like. Absurd. Most editors are capable of doing their own deciding.

Then there is the belief that if you write two kinds of things, feature articles and verse, we shall say, you must use a pen name for one. That your verse editor wants you to write only verse; that if he finds that you are known for anything else, he strikes you off his list. Absurder and absurder! Don't flatter yourself that he will even know whether you write features or not. If he does know it, why should he care? 43

If you believe any of those things mentioned above, for goodness sake don't tell it. It labels you as an amateur. People suspect that you are using those dictums as an alibi for not selling. 44

XV REASONS EDITORS GIVE FOR REJECTIONS 

1. Falling interest, rather than rising interest, as the lines go on. 

2. Too trite; same old clichés; same old phrases, etc. Threadbare thought. 

3. Taboos; you stepped on one of those. Some

Sunday school papers will use nothing that mentions a circus. Some verse will offend the advertisers of certain publications. Learn the taboos of each. 

4. Bad meter, rhyme, rhythm. 

5. Inversions, contractions. 

6. Appeals to wrong age group; too old or too young for particular juvenile magazine to which you submit it. 

7. Too literary, or the opposite-too much slang, depending on type of magazine. 

8. Overstocked at present. 

9. Too late for the occasion (Christmas, etc.) . 

10. Labored, forced, unnatural. 45

11. Lacks singing quality. One editor said, "I like verse that sings." 

12. Lacks universal appeal. Appeals only to small group. Based on too trivial incident or thought. 

13. Too morbid. 

14. Too long. 

15. Not timely. 

16. Lack of restraint. Too many adjectives strung on a string. Overstatement, which weakens the effect. 46

XVI DO'S, DON'TS, AND PEEVES OF EDITORS 

Some editors have a personal dislike for certain words. One dislikes the word silver, another, ecstasy. 

Keep any peeves that you discover, in a notebook and do not use them when submitting to that person.

Do not tell the editor where your work has appeared. If it is widely circulated he will know it; if it isn't, he may not believe you. He usually doesn't care, anyway.

Neither does the editor care what clubs you belong to. If you are president of the World Alliance of Lyricographers, or if you were founder of the 

Bald Knob Pan Pipers, he still doesn't care. As one editor said, "Your verse must stand on its own poetic feet."

Most editors dislike letters accompanying manuscripts, unless there is some definite reason for the letter. It is useless for you to say, "I am sending 47

four poems for your consideration." He knows why they are sent.

Neither is he interested in how many times your verse has been used on radio programs, recited on club programs, been the high light of pink teas, etc. 

He dislikes longhand; it is too hard to read. Also single spacing in the typewriting. 

Stamp skimpers are his pet peeve. Usually you can send four sheets and an enclosed envelope for three cents. More than that usually need two stamps. If in doubt, use the extra stamp.

Don't arrange your contributions in some sweet, unique form of submission, such as a fancy booklet tied with blue ribbon. This might be advisable in some contests, but not in business like verse selling. 

Don't overwork the adjectives. Let the verbs do some of the work. A good verb gives strength to the statement. Too many adjectives weaken the effect. Understatement sometimes accomplishes more than overstatement. 

Don't use several kinds of meter in one poem. 

Fifty-seven varieties are welcome in pickles, but not in poems.

Fold your manuscript correctly. Use a large envelope.

Some religious publishing houses like to have the contributions sent to the separate editor of the 48

DO'S, DON'TS, AND PEEVES of EDITORS 

particular paper; others want them sent to the general publishing house, and they give them out to editor. Find out which wants which and the proper act accordingly.

Some editors have violent dislikes for certain forms of verse. When you can find one who hates sonnets, don't send any to him; if a certain one wants ballads, let him have them. 

All editors like clean typewritten ribbons, clean type, and professional-looking manuscripts. 

All this, and many other things, must be learned by experience, by you personally. Some writer of verse may tell you that a certain thing is true; you may have found the opposite to be true. You dealt with different editors, and their opinions and likes do not coincide. They are human beings, after all, and human beings are not all alike. 

Don't send twenty poems in one packet; from three to five is better. One poem is not best, because that gives the editor no range of choice. He may have accepted one on that theme a few days ago, but may like your style. If you send three or four poems, he has a wider choice range. 

Don't send a long poem unless you know positively that this particular editor wants long verse. 

Don't call the sheriff or sue for damages if an editor changes one of your poems when he publishes it. 49

Don't you often change the flowers on the hat you buy, or the lace on the dress? Remember we are talking about commercial verse now. You want the check. If you complain about the change, you will lose that market most assuredly. 

Now, one more thing. When you have accumulated a fine stack of reference material; when you have assembled sample copies of papers you want to hit; when you have marked certain prize articles in certain writers' magazines that you want to reach over and get often-when you have done all this with much labor, time, and some expense, don't lend them to anyone, even to your best friend.

Things will happen to them. She won't intend to misplace them, but some other member of the family will destroy them; they will be lost. You can never replace them. 

You needn't be stingy with them. Half the fun of selling is to share the news with others; help them get started; give them the crumbs of knowledge you have gained. Ask them to come to your home, as to a library, and read the thing they care to read, but do not let them take it out of the house. That is fair. If they really want to read it, they will be willing to do this, and you run no risks. 

Read much. Try to develop a feel for words. Keep 50

up with the band wagon; collect general information on what is going on.

Lead a normal existence. Don't shut yourself up in your ivory tower, and don't try to look like a poet. Be a human being. 

And here's to you. May you live long, and may the checks grow bigger and better. 51

XVII POEMS ILLUSTRATING POINTS BROUGHT OUT IN THE PRECEDING CHAPTERS 

Here are a few examples of types that sell well: 

1. This one might be called a poem instead of verse. It sold to Ave Maria, and it does not use verse: 

OLD HOUSE 

You clutch with faltering, blue-veined hand 

Your robe of ragged vine, And draw it closer, to conceal 

From curious eyes, all sign 

Of crumbling wall, decadence, death; 

Still proud of shining hours 

When brilliance, wealth, and beauty met 

Beneath your haughty towers. 

With but a vestige of your past 

You build a splendid dream, And all who pass, respect your wish, And accept you as you seem. 52

2. One with a suggestive title, that might cause the reader to read on:

SHOOT 'EM 

I like to walk in the cool dim woods 

And over the brook to the hill; 

I shoot little birds and animals too 

Whenever they keep very still. 

You think I am cruel? But wait, please wait; 

I'm afraid you don't understand; 

I carry no rifle, nor slingshot, nor rock; 

It's a camera you see in my hand.

In spite of contractions, this type will sell, because it has other qualities that sell it. 

3. These two have build-up; they have a sort of 

O. Henry twist that readers like:

OLD AGE 

I do not weep because old age 

Will leave my face deep-lined; 

I grieve because I dread to have 

Age wrinkles in my mind.

RECIPE Take one ripe trouble, medium-sized, That works in your behalf; 

Then try to find its funny spot, And when you find it, laugh. 

If you can see the humorous side 

You've cut its strength in half;

Of writing your epitaph. 53

4. This juvenile sold the second trip out:

TREES 

I'd like to know the reason why, If anybody knows,

That when the winter comes, the trees 

Take off their leafy clothes.

All summer long they wear green robes, With blossoms in their hair, And when the ice and snow return 

They stand out in it bare. 

5. This religious verse sold the first trip out, because it was slanted to a definite publication: 

HANNAH'S SACRIFICE 

She journeyed back to Ramah silently; 

Not yet aware of lonely hours that lay 

In wait for her; of stabs of poignant pain 

At sight of toys, or little clothes each day. 

But time brought forth a quiet happiness, A singing peace, a radiant inner glow;

For she had kept her promise to the Lord; 

The child was His! But each year she could go 

And visit him; could take some foolish toy;

Could make a coat to keep him from the cold. 

And he would not forget her, even when 

God made of him a prophet brave and bold. 54

6. A light love poem that usually sells easily:

HIGHER COURTS 

My head decreed that I forget the wrong;

That I conceal the scar, and sing a song; 

Then cast you from my heart with lifted chin, And soon another love would enter in.

My heart, a higher court, took this decree 

And tore it up, then smiled, for knowing me

It knew I loved you still; it also knew 

That I was quite as much to blame as you!
