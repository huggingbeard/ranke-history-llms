INTRODUCTION 

It is hardly necessary to present arguments in favor of a thorough grounding in comparative anatomy as an introduction to the technical study of human anatomy, since it takes but little experience with anatomy as taught by the modern comparative method, to see that this method furnishes a rational basis for an absolute knowledge of fundamental relationships, while the old system was largely one of mnemonics. A student of the older anatomy must perforce remember that two given structures were related in a specific way; if his memory failed him he was wholly lost. But the student who is familiar with their simpler prototypes will know that two given structures can only be related in a certain way. 

Many of the problems associated with human anatomy have thus found their solution in a study of the lower forms where one may see the stages through which man has passed in arriving at his present degree of complexity: This The physical difficulties attendant upon any attempt to survey so extensive a subject as the phylogenetic history of the vertebrates has compelled those entrusted with the responsibility to develop the "type study" scheme of presentation. scheme utilizes a variable number of representative chordates. in illustration of the evolutionary processes that have culminated in the 'modern vertebrate. Although "type study" has been sharply criticized in recent years, and certain institutions have introduced "comparative" or "systemic" variants, physical factors favor retention of the original plan in many laboratories. It is for these latter that this manual has been prepared. In its preparation the author has drawn freely from many sources, and whatever originality it may exhibit is of organization, not of subject matter.

C. LABORATORY EXERCISES. 

The word "anatomy" immediately suggests to the average student of biology the correlative, "dissection", a term that etomologically means "to cut apart"; and in too many instances preliminary training has emphasized this root-meaning of the word. 

In the anatomical laboratory, we have largely lost sight of the original significance of "dissection" and have substituted the idea of separation of parts, with the removal of only that connective tissue which tends to obscure the various structures. The probe, forceps, and fingers are far safer instruments for this type of dissection than are the scalpel and the scissors. Never, in any case, should a structure be cut unless one is sure that the proposed cut will damage no other structure; in all cases, dissection should begin in a readily accessible location; thence gradually be extended to those regions that are less accessible. Inasmuch as a part of the laboratory grade will depend upon the quality of the dissections, it behooves each student to do his own work, and to do it well. Discussion is permissible so long as it does not interfere with your own or your neighbor's work; but do not permit another's views or those expressed either by word or illustration in a text to influence your own observations.

The second part of the laboratory exercise consists in a demonstration of those structures that have been laid bare by patient and painstaking dissection. In preparation for a demonstration it will be

necessary to read carefully and analytically all that the manual has to say concerning the part under consideration. It is, also, advisable to peruse the text and all of the reference works to which the student may have access. It is not desirable, however, to learn by rote the factual matter thus obtained, but a thorough digest thereof should be attempted and the results presented in the student's own words at the time of demonstration.

D. DEFINITION OF SOME FUNDAMENTAL TERMS. 

In describing the spatial relationship existing between various parts of the animal body certain classical terms must be adopted. For example, the head marks the anterior end; the region opposite is the posterior. These terms are also employed to indicate the relative position of other structures with reference to one another. 

Thus, we say that the forelimbs are anterior to the hind limbs. The back or upper surface is the dorsal side; the lower or under surface the ventral side. Centrally located structures are designated as medial; those toward the sides or margins as lateral. Parts at or near the surface are spoken of as peripheral, superficial, or external; those situated in the interior are central, deep, or internal. All of these terms are also used to indicate relative position of parts. 

Chordates, without exception, exhibit the characteristic of bilateral symmetry; that is, their parts are so arranged and spaced about certain imaginary. lines, planes and axes of symmetry that they balance one another. An antero-posterior line in the middle of the dorsal surface is the median dorsal line; a similar line on the ventral surface is the median ventral line. 

The plane determined by these two lines is the median sagittal plane. Planes parallel 

Planes parallel to the median plane are described as parasagittal planes. An imaginary line in the median sagittal plane extending from the extreme anterior end to the posterior limits of the body is the antero-posterior axis. Other planes often referred to are the horizontal frontal plane, which is any plane parallel to the dorsal and ventral surfaces, and the transverse plane, which is a plane at right angles to the anteroposterior axis.

Parts of the body or of any of its organic units that are relatively remote from their point of origin or of attachment may be described as distal in position. Proximal is used to designate that end of a limb or other part which is nearest to the point of attachment. Cranial and cephalic imply movement toward the head; these terms still continue in force for structures beyond the head. Similarly, caudal implies movement in a posterior direction or toward the tail.

Homologous structures are those which, however unlike in function or superficial appearance, have the same origin, as demonstrated by the study of their embryonic development and their palaeontological history. Analogous structures, conversely, are those which resemble each other either in superficial appearance or in function, but have had different origins.

BALANOGLOSSUS or PTY CHODERA PHYLUM Chordata. 

CLASS 

Hemichordata. 

Until quite recently a single genus,1

Balanoglossus, was the only known representative of a class to which the name Enteropneusta was applied. There seems reason to believe, however, that two remarkable deep-sea animals, Cephalodiscus and Phoronis, though not close allies of Balanoglossus, may yet be sufficiently close relatives of it to justify their being placed in the same class. It, therefore, has seemed advisable to make Entero pneusta, Pterobranchia, and Phoronidia orders in the class Hemichordata. 

The status of this group in the chordate phylum2 is at best very insecure.

While the Enteropneusta, as exemplified by 

Balanoglossus or Ptychodera, seem because of certain resemblances to Amphioxus, to have some rather well-founded claims to vertebrate relationship, the Pterobranchia and Phoronidia are admitted to the phylum Chordata largely by reason of the almost unmistakable affinities of Cephalodiscus with Balanoglossus, and of Phoronis with Cephalodiscus.

The Enteropneusta, of which Ptychodera is a more recently recognized example, are soft-bodied, worm-like, burrowing forms with numerous paired gill slits and an alimentary canal which runs straight from mouth to terminal anus. There are three main body divisions: an anterior proboscis, a ring-shaped collar, and a cylindrical trunk which resembles the body of an annelid worm due to certain surface markings suggestive of segmentation.

These animals live in the sea, burrowing in sand or mud by means of the proboscis. 

Numerous integumental glands secrete a viscid substance to which grains of sand adhere in such a manner as to form fragile temporary tubes.

A SIMPLE ASCIDIAN PHYLUM Chordata. 

CLASS 

Urochordata. 

The class Urochordata, or Tunicata, in contrast with both the Hemichordata and the Cephalochordata each of which contains but two known genera and only a few species, constitutes a large assemblage of highly diverse marine forms, ranging from purely sessile forms, and from solitary forms of large size to colonial forms of comparatively small size. They are abundant in all oceans, from the shallow shore waters to the deepest abysses. 

That the tunicates are degenerate descendants of primitive chordates admits of little doubt; the history of development of the ascidians, taken in connection with the occurrence of permanently chordate members of the group, is quite sufficient to point to this conclusion. But the degree of degeneration which the class has undergone, the point in the line of development of the higher chordates from which it diverged is open to question. According to one view, the tunicates have descended from ancestors which possessed all of the typical features of craniates; according to another, their ancestors had not yet acquired the distinctively higher characteristics of the craniates. The nearest existing ally of the tunicates, among the lower forms, is probably Balanoglossus. The similarity in the character of the pharynx, which is perforated by branchial apertures, is alone sufficient to point to such a connection; further evidence is afforded by the presence of a notochordal structure in both, and by the similarity in the development of the central part of the nervous system. But the alliance is by no means a close one. 

The same structural evidence that links the Enteropneusta with the Urochordata even more definitely indicates the relationship of the tunicates with Amphioxus, whose affinities to the vertebrates are no longer questioned.

EXTERNAL ANATOMY 

A simple ascidian, such as Ciona or

Corella, is a sac-like creature, scarcely recognizable as an animal. During life the animal lives permanently attached to rocks, piles, or floating logs that have been a long time in the water. The basal, or attached end may be recognized by its rough and irregular form, and by the fragments of wood, or other materials which adhere to it. The distal end is marked by the presence of two apertures, usually somewhat asymmetrical in position. These openings are the siphons; the upper one is usually the oral or inhalent siphon and marks the degenerate anterior end of the animal; the lower siphon is the atrial or exhalent siphon and represents the posterior end. 

Thus we see that the anteroposterior axis of the animal is bent into a U-shape. The region between the two siphons is, therefore, dorsal; the remaining and larger part of the surface is ventral. When undisturbed in the water, the siphons are widely expanded, and a steady current of water passes in at the oral aperture and out through the atrial opening. If disturbed, the animal almost closes both siphons, at the same time ejecting streams of water from both; hence the name "sea squirt" popularly applied to these animals. 

Analysis 

The outer layer of the body wall consists of a tough translucent substance forming a thick test or tunic. has shown this to be a lifeless protective layer composed of animal cellulose, a substance almost identical with wood. This tunic is secreted by mesodermal cells that have migrated through the ectoderm and remain imbedded in the tunic which they secrete. The tunic is attached to the underlying body at the siphons only.

INTERNAL ANATOMY 

The test of Corella is so transparent that practically all of the internal organs are visible through it. That of Ciona is not so transparent, but the general organization of this form even may be seen. When the student has mastered the general plan, he should dissect the specimen in accordance with the following directions. 

Make an incision through the base of the tunic and peel off the tunic. Avoid injury to the underlying body wall.

Carefully cut away its attachments about the siphons and discard the tunic. Its removal reveals the soft body wall or mantle in which may be observed the muscle fibers that control the siphons.

Fasten the animal in a wax-bottomed dissecting pan by inserting pins in the rims of the siphons, and through the extreme basal end. Fill the pan with water so that the animal is covered. 1 The mantle incloses the viscera, which tend to adhere to it so closely that its removal without injury to the underlying organs is extremely difficult. Make a longitudinal incision in the mantle from the margin of the atrial siphon to the extreme basal end, and remove the mantle in small strips, separating each strip gently from the underlying parts. Within the body wall is a cavity, the atrial cavity, also called the peribranchial cavity, which communicates with the exterior through the atrial aperture. This cavity is not a coelom because it is formed by in volution from the outer surface and is probably lined with a prolongation of the ectoderm.

THE RESPIRATORY SYSTEM. The oral siphon leads into a large, thin-walled pharynx, or branchial chamber. Its walls are pierced by numerous minute gill slits, visceral clefts, or stigmata," as they are variously named. The gill slits, which are arranged in transverse rows separated by thick horizontal bars, have their edges beset with numerous strong cilia.³ It is the function of the cilia to keep a current of water flowing from the pharynx, the walls of which are richly supplied with blood vessels, into the atrial cavity and out through the atriopore or exhalent siphon. The feature of this respiratory system that marks it as a distinct advance over that of the Enteropneusta is the inclosure of the gill slits within a protective atrial cavity.

THE DIGESTIVE SYSTEM. The oral siphon and pharynx have already been noted in another connection. The lower end of the pharynx narrows abruptly into a short oesophagus, which opens into a widened stomach located just below or at one side of the basal end of the pharynx. The enteric canal is so curved that the long axis of the stomach lies at right angles to that of the pharynx. In some ascidians brown digestive glands may be seen covering the walls of the stomach. The stomach leads into an intestine, which immediately doubles upon itself so that it lies parallel to the stomach. It then curves upward toward the atrial siphon, where it is terminated by an anus situated just within the atrial cavity.

Make a longitudinal incision in the side of the pharynx; extend the incision through the oral siphon, and spread out the pharyngeal walls. In the midventral line of the pharynx is a conspicuous white cord extending the entire length thereof. This is the endostyle or hypobranchial groove. 

Its walls are composed of glandular and ciliated cells. Directly opposite the endostyle, 'in that which may be designated the mid-dorsal line of the pharynx, is located the dorsal lamina. This, in some tunicates (Ascidia, Cynthia, and Molgula) consists of a delicate membrancus fold of the pharyngeal wall; but in others (Ciona and Corella), it is represented by a row of processes called languets, and resembles a fringe. Anteriorly, the endostyle is continuous with a ciliated ridge which encircles the anterior end of the pharynx.

Dorsally, this ridge merges with the dorsal lamina. A second ridge, which lies just in front of the one mentioned, forms with it a ciliated groove, the peripharyngeal groove. Mucus, secreted by the glandular cells of the endostyle, is driven forward by the cilia of that same structure. As it

AMPHIOXUS PHYLUM Chordata. 

CLASS 

GNE Cephalochordata.

The Cephalochordata consist of several similar species grouped in two genera, Branchiostoma and Asymmetron. The differ ences between the genera, however, are comparatively insignificant. The group is cosmopolitan in distribution, occurring almost everywhere in the temperate zone where sloping sandy seashores exist. This wide distribution and slight variability are interpreted by some authors as indications that the group is extremely archaic. 

Amphioxus, the best known species in the group, was first described by Pallas in 1778.

On account of its resemblance to a slug, it was given the name Limax lanceolatus. In 1804, Costa redescribed it as a fish allied to the lampreys and hag-fishes. 

Because of his erroneous diagnosis of the oral tentacles or cirril as gills he applied the name Branchiostoma (gill mouth), which name is still retained by taxonomists. 

But the name. Amphioxus, though given a year or so later by Yarrel, has been in general use for so long that it will be difficult to displace.

The Lancelet, or Amphioxus lanceolatus, is a small transparent animal, occurring along the sandy shores of the Mediterranean and other temperate seas, where it leads a semi-sedentary life, burrowing into the sand so that only the head end remains visible, with its oral hood and tentacles outspread to test and draw in the food-laden water. There is no active searching for food; but water, which is swept in quantities into the pharynx and out through the pharyngeal clefts, gives up its minute organic particles which consist largely of protozoa2 and pelagic larvae.

INTERNAL ANATOMY 

No dissection will be undertaken in the study of the internal organization of Amphioxus. The student will depend upon prepared slides for verification of as many points of structure as is practicable. 

Other anatomical features will be assumed to be present.

THE DIGESTIVE TRACT. The mouth, which was noted at the bottom of the vestibule, is a small circular aperture surrounded by a membrane, the velum.1 This membrane has its free edge produced into a number of velar tentacles. The pharynx, into which the mouth opens, is a large laterally compressed chamber extending through the entire anterior half of the body. In its floor is a longitudinal groove, the endostyle, lined with ciliated epithelium containing groups of mucus cells. A somewhat similar structure, the hyperpharyngeal groove, extends along the dorsal wall of the pharynx. At the anterior end it divides, curving downward as the peripharyngeal bands which reunite in the anterior end of the endostyle.

The posterior end of the pharynx opens into a tubular intestine that extends backward in an almost straight line to the anus. From the ventral wall of the anterior intestine a diverticulum extends forward along the right side of the pharynx. This is the liver, or hepatic caecum.

THE SKELETAL SYSTEM. The chief skele tal or supporting structure of Amphioxus is the notochord, a cylindrical rod pointed at both ends and extending from the anterior to the posterior end of the body. It lies immediately above the digestive tract and between the right and left myotomal segments. It is composed of a peculiar notochordal tissue formed of large vacuolated cells, and having the nuclei3 confined to the dorsal and ventral regions of the chord. The oral hood is supported by a ring of separate rod-like pieces of cartilage arranged end to end. These rods correspond in number with the cirri, and each piece supports its related cirrus with a slender cartilaginous extension.

Likewise, this primitive chordate possesses those morphological features thought to be characteristic of all vertebrates. It would appear, therefore, that Amphioxus must be looked upon as a link connecting the vertebrates with the degenerate Urochordata, and more remotely with the extremely primitive Hemichordata.

PHYLUM CLASS 

THE FRESH-WATER LAMPREY 

Chordata. Cyclostomata.

The cyclostomes are a minor class of vertebrates as compared with the other five classes, since there are but a few genera and species. They are also of secondary significance phylogenetically, for they are believed to represent a comparatively unsuccessful lateral branch of the vertebrate ancestral tree, which probably came from an Amphioxus-like stock prior to the origin of the true fishes. Although it is not safe to trace detailed homologies between the two groups, it would appear that the cyclostomes have departed less widely from the Amphioxus-like prototype than have the fishes, and in that sense they represent a lower grade of vertebrate organization.

The Cyclostomata are distinguished from all other Craniata by the possession of a suctorial mouth devoid of functional jaws; by the presence of a single olfactory organ; and by the absence of lateral or paired fins. The name "round-mouth eels" is often applied to these fish-like forms to distinguish them from the "jaw-mouth fishes," the gnathostomes. The group consists of two distinct types, the lampreys and the hagfishes. Both of these have a superficial resemblance to the eels, but they differ from the class Pisces in many respects. Some of the differences are to be interpreted as evidences of primitiveness, and others of specialization and degeneration. 

Three species of lamprey are common in the Northern Hemisphere: Petromyzon mari nus (Sea lamprey); Petromyzon fluviatilis (Common fresh-water lamprey); and Lampetra wilderi (Lesser fresh-water lamprey). The second forms the basis for this study of the cyclostomes. Two genera, Mordacia and 

Geotria, are found in the Southern Hemisphere.

THE RESPIRATORY SYSTEM. Note the seven gill pouches which, unlike those of all other vertebrates, do not open directly into the enteric canal in the adult, but into the respiratory tube which lies just beneath the oesophagus. The gill pouches open to the exterior by the small gill slits already noted. Each pouch contains numerous gill lamellae. The normal course of the respiratory current is from the mouth into the buccal cavity and respir atory tube; thence into the gill pouches and out through the gill slits. During such times as the lamprey attaches itself to other objects by its suctorial mouth, distinct respiratory movements may be observed by means of which water is pumped into and out of the gill pouches through the gill slits.

The most significant characteristics of the 

Amnocoetes larva are: (1) a hood-like upper lip which resembles the oral hood of Amphioxus; (2) a well-defined parietal or median eye suggestive of the pigment spot in Amphioxus; (3) a food-concentrating apparatus consisting of an endostyle and a dorsal mucus groove; and (4) continuous and unspecialized fins. Amnocoetes is more advanced than Amphioxus in: the paired eyes that lie deep in the head; a small cranium; a much more advanced brain; a reduction in the number of gill slits; a functional pronephric kidney; a distinct heart; and a liver provided with a gall bladder.

After living in the larval state for three or four years it undergoes a profound metamorphosis, during which the buccal funnel is developed; the eyes come to the surface and begin to function; the median fin becomes specialized; the skull and branchial basket become more elaborate and the gall bladder is lost, except for vestiges.

The most remarkable transformation, however, is that of the pharyngeal region, in which the dorsal groove is pinched off from the old pharynx to form a new oesophagus that breaks through into the mouth. In the same manner, the endostyle is pinched off from the floor of the pharynx and becomes the thyroid gland. The middle part of the pharynx remains as the blind lung-like respiratory tube.

All of these facts seem to point to an affinity between the cyclostomes and the cephalochordates. Such a relationship helps to bridge the gap between the vertebrates and the lower chordates, even though it is extremely difficult to determine certainly the phylogenetic relation of the cyclostomes to the other classes of vertebrates.

PHYLUM CLASS 

THE SHARK Chordata. 

Pisces.

This class, which includes the cartilaginous and bony fishes and the Dipnoi or lung-fishes, is characterized by the possession of true gills that are persistent throughout life; paired pectoral and pelvic fins, and unpaired dorsal, ventral and caudal fins; and an endoskeleton in which the notochord has been more or less replaced by vertebrae.¹ The skull is a distinct advance over that of the Cyclostomata, and there is a system of well-formed visceral arches, of which the first comprises the upper and lower jaws. 

Of all this collection of true fishes, the elasmobranchs are probably the most primitive. They constitute a group that first appeared in the early Devonian period, and has survived the vicissitudes of the intervening ages with very little modification since Tertiary times. Most of the species are relatively sluggish in their actions, but they may become very active and do a great amount of damage to valuable sea life.

The small sharks which abound along the coasts of the United States are commonly called "dogfish" by the fishermen, who suffer keenly from these marauders. The dogfish of inland waters belong to an entirely different group. Two species of sharks are caught in numbers, and used in laboratory work; the spiny dogfish, Squalus, and the smooth dogfish, Mustelus. The first named species is easily distinguished by the sharp spine in front of each dorsal fin, and serves as the subject of this study, but the spiny and smooth dogfishes are so alike that the latter may be dissected according to the following directions.

NECTURUS PHYLUM Chordata. 

CLASS Amphibia. 

The Amphibia are distinguished from the fishes by their possession of dactylic limbs instead of paired fins, and by the absence of all skeletal support in the unpaired fins, which are mere folds of skin. 

Though amphibian larvae nearly all breathe by means of gills, and many representatives of the class retain these organs throughout life, lungs are present in the majority of adult forms. It is this feature that makes the members of this class so interesting to the comparative anatomist, for it marks the change from aquatic to terrestrial life; the greatest evolutionary crisis in chordate history. 

Classification of the group varies somewhat, but two subclasses are usually recognized: the Stegocephalia, an extinct group of tailed amphibia which roamed the earth during the close of the Carboniferous period; and the Batrachia or modern amphibia. The Batrachia are separated into three orders, the Apoda, the Caudata, and the Salientia. The first order includes those blind and limbless forms which pass their entire existence in subterranean burrows. Dermal scales, believed to be an inheritance from their stegocephalian ances tors, are present in the slimy skins of these degenerate animals. The Caudata are those amphibia which bear a well-developed tail throughout life. They range from forms that live permanently in the water, and breathe by means of external gills in addition to lungs, to forms that undergo a metamorphosis during which the gills become vestigial or are entirely lost. The third order is that which embraces the tailless amphibia, the frogs and toads. 

Necturus, or Menobranchus, commonly called the "mud-puppy", is one of the largest and best known of the perennibranchiate amphibia. It is distributed throughout the Mississippi and Saint Lawrence basins, whence it has found its way in recent years into the Hudson and other waters. This member of the class Amphibia represents the rather simple tetrapod type, and is a curious mixture of specialization and primitiveness, with the latter predominant. The markedly primitive features are those in which Necturus resembles a fish. 

The body is fish-like in outline, with myocomma showing as zigzag lines separating the myotomes. The external gills, which in most amphibia are larval characters and disappear as the animal becomes adult, are retained throughout life by Necturus. 

EXTERNAL ANATOMY 

The body of Necturus, like that of all Caudata, is cylindrical in shape, but is dorsoventrally flattened, except at the hinder end where it is laterally compressed. 

The body of the animal may be divided into three regions, the head, trunk, and tail.

The diphy cercal tail is surrounded by a caudal fin. The neck region, which is wanting in fishes, but is so characteristic of land vertebrates, is represented by a cervical vertebra with which the skull articulates. 

The head is bluntly triangular in shape. The mouth is rather small, but the lips surrounding it are prominent. Note how the upper lip fits into a fold of the lower lip. Examine the two nostrils at the anterior end of the head; each can be tightly closed by a fold of skin. Carefully probe the nostrils and determine their communication with the mouth cavity by way of the posterior nares. This arrangement permits air to enter the mouth cavity through the nostrils, and differs greatly from that found in the majority of fishes, where the olfactory sacs end blindly and have no connection with the mouth cavity. 

A pair of small eyes devoid of eyelids may be observed. The skin passes without interruption over the eyes, but is transparent in front of them. The ear, as in fishes, is entirely within the skull. 

A transverse ventral fold of skin and three laterally placed pairs of gills mark the posterior limits of the head. Each gill consists of a dorsal process from which depends a fringe of filaments. These are external gills and are not homologous with the gills of fishes which are internal gills, but are merely extensions of the

outer integument. Between the first and second, and the second and third gills on each side is a gill slit. Probe these slits and note their communication with the pharynx. The animal, however, does not pass water through the gill slits, but respires through the skin; by means of external gills; and to a limited extent by means of its lungs.

The trunk passes almost insensibly into the tail, although the large cloacal aperture, located in the midventral line, might be said to mark the junction of trunk and tail. The trunk bears two pairs of appendages. These correspond to the paired fins of fishes, but have evolved into typical walking limbs. The two pairs of eyes are of nearly the same size, and are widely separated from each other. Each leg is composed of three divisions, a proximal, a middle, and a distal division. In the fore limb these segments are the upper arm, forearm, wrist and hand; in the hind limb they are the thigh, shank, ankle and foot.

Both hand and foot bear four digits, although five is the typical vertebrate number. The first finger and first toe are missing. The position of the limbs, with reference to the body, is very primitive and should be carefully studied. 

The skin of Necturus is smooth and without bony or horny scales or other hardened integumentary structures, such as are possessed by all of the other groups of vertebrates. It is, however, provided with numerous integumentary glands that secrete a protective slime. Note the color of the animal, and its variation on the dorsal and ventral sides. The animal is well-fitted for a life in the dimly lighted waters where it is found.

THE DIGESTIVE SYSTEM. This consists of the mouth cavity, oesophagus, stomach, small and large intestine, cloaca, liver and pancreas. The mouth cavity has already been examined. Carefully follow the short 

Oesophagus forward from the anterior or cardiac end of the stomach to the point where it opens into the pharyngeal cavity. 

Note the constriction which marks the pylor ic end of the stomach. From the pylorus, the small intestine makes an abrupt turn to the right. In this bend rests a white gland, the pancreas, which also extends onto the dorsal surface of the liver. That part of the small intestine in contact with the pancreas is known as the duodenum. 

Lift the duodenum and observe the extent of the pancreas.

The remainder of the small intestine is a much coiled tube extending to the posterior end of the abdominal cavity where it widens to form the short large intestine. Observe the cloaca, the common receptacle into which the genital and urinary ducts, as well as the intestine, empty their products. Probe through the anus into it. The liver has already been located. Observe again its lobate nature, and note the spherical gall bladder between the lower and median lobes. The bile duct emerges from the gall bladder, receives a branch from the liver, and traverses the pancreas to the bend of the duodenum where it pours the biliary secretions into the small intestine. The pancreatic secretion is also transported by the common bile duct.

MALE 

Ventral to each long brown kidney is an elongated cream-colored body attached to the dorsal wall of the coelom by a mesentery, the mesorchium. These bodies are the testes. Lift a testis and examine the mesorchium for numerous delicate ducts, the vasa efferentia, which unite the testis with the kidney. Spermatozoa are produced in the testes and pass through the vasa efferentia into the pronephric portion of the kidneys, whence they find their way into the mesonephric ducts or vasa deferentia. 

Farther back note the urinary tubules leading from the mesonephros to the mesonephric ducts. The latter thus perform the double function of conducting urine and spermatozoa to the exterior. It is only in the elasmobranchs and the amphibia that the mesonephric ducts serve this double function. The posterior portion of each duct is usually referred to as the ureter, though incorrectly so.

FEMALE 

In the female the lobate sac-like ovaries occupy a position comparable to that of the testes in the male. They are suspended from the dorsal wall of the body by mesenteries known as mesovaria. Note through the ovarian walls the eggs of varying size indicating different stages in development.

Dorsolateral to each ovary is a long convoluted tube, the oviduct or Müllerian duct; trace it forward to the anterior end of the pleuroperitoneal cavity where it opens directly into the body cavity by way of the funnel-shaped ostium tubae. Follow the oviducts posteriorly to the cloaca which they enter laterally. Open the cloaca and locate the papillae by which the oviducts enter the cloaca. THE CIRCULATORY SYSTEM. The blood vascular system is much like that of the shark, but shows certain adaptations to terrestrial life. In Necturus the blood that goes from the heart to the gills does not return again, but goes directly to the systemic vessels by way of the efferent arteries. The blood from the lungs enters the left side of the heart through a common pulmonary vein.

The pericardial cavity has already been opened and the heart exposed. The parts of the heart that are visible are the thick-walled, conical ventricle, a pair of thin-walled atria anterior to the ventricle, and the conus arteriosus, a tubular structure arising from the base of the ventricle and passing forward between the Patria. Anteriorly, the conus passes into an enlarged basal portion of the ventral aorta which is known as the bulbus arteriosus.

Carefully examine the dorsal surface of the heart for the thin-walled sinus venosus which receives two large vessels, the Cuvierian ducts, that lie in the dorsal wall of the pericardial cavity. These vessels are formed by the union of the two common cardinal veins with the two hepatic sinuses. The latter are the two large veins that emerge from the transverse septum and pass into the sinus venosus. The common cardinals join the hepatic sinuses on their lateral surfaces. Veins

Two systems of veins are present: the systemic veins which return the blood from the various tissues and organs of the body to the sinus venosus; and the pulmonary veins that return oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium. The systemic veins include those which go directly to the sinus venosus, the hepatic portal vein carrying blood to the liver, and the renal portal veins that enter the kidneys. Blood from the portal systems is afterward collected by other veins and transported to the heart.

Turn back the ventral body wall and follow the abdominal vein from the pelvic region forward to the point where it enters the liver and joins the hepatic portal vein. It receives blood from the pelvic veins and the anal region.

The large hepatic portal vein is formed by the union of vessels from the intestine, pancreas, spleen, and stomach.

Stretch the dorsal mesentery of the small intestine and note the first of these vessels, a conspicuous mesenteric vein, which receives numerous intestinal veins throughout its course. The mesenteric vein enters the substance of the pancreas, where it receives the small pancreatic veins from that organ. At the level of the pancreas the large gastro-splenic vein joins the mesenteric vein from the left. Trace the gastro-splenic vein to the spleen, where. it is formed by a union of splenic veins from the spleen and gastric veins from the stomach. 

Follow the abdominal vein posterad to the point where it is formed by the union of the two pelvic veins. Note that each pelvic vein is joined by its corresponding femoral vein from the hind limb. The vein formed by this union speedily gives rise to a large side branch that is the renal portal vein. It passes at once to the dorsolateral surface of the kidney where it gives off a series of finer veins into the kidney tissue. At the point where the renal portal vein leaves the body wall to enter the substance of the kidney, it is joined by a branch of the caudal vein which returns blood from the tail region. 

Turn to the pericardial cavity and locate the common cardinal veins where they enter the hepatic sinus. Trace one of them laterad, removing the muscles between the pericardial cavity and the base of the forelimb. Just outside of the pericardial cavity, the common cardinal receives the

jugular and subclavian veins. Follow the latter vein into the forelimb, where it is formed by the union of a cutaneous vein from the skin and the brachial vein from the arm. Trace the jugular vein forward to the point where it is formed by the union of external and internal jugular veins.

The postcava is the largest vein in the body. It arises between the kidneys, from which it receives numerous small branches, and runs forward to the middle of the dorsal surface of the liver. At this point it enters the liver, whence it receives several hepatic veins. These relations may be observed by carefully picking away the liver tissue. At the anterior end of the liver the postcava emerges as a large vessel which pierces the septum transversum. It immediately divides to form the hepatic sinuses which, after being joined by the common cardinal veins, enter the sinus venosus.

At the point where the postcava turns toward the liver, communication is established with a pair of postcardinal veins. 

Trace these veins anteriorly through the transverse septum to the point where they join the common cardinal veins. Posterior ly, the postcardinals are connected with the renal portal veins which formerly were the caudal ends of the postcardinals. 

Locate the pulmonary vein on the mesial side of each lung, and trace it forward to the septum transversum, where it unites with its fellow of the other side. 

The common pulmonary vein formed by this union then enters the left atrium by a very small aperture that may be found by opening up the atrium.

The first visceral branch of the dorsal aorta is the gastric artery which supplies the major portion of the stomach. 

Some distance posterior to the origin of the gastric artery, the coeliaco-mesenteric artery springs from the aorta. Numerous small branches from the dorsal aorta supply the gonads. The continuation of the dorsal aorta into the tail is known as the caudal artery.

THE RESPIRATORY SYSTEM. In the floor of the pharynx, midway between the second gill slits, you noted the glottis. The walls were observed to be stiffened by the delicate arytenoid cartilages which are the first laryngeal cartilages to appear in the phylogenetic series, and probably represent reduced gill arches. The small cavity inclosed between those cartilages is the larynx. Slit the glottis posterad and note that the larynx leads, into a narrow flattened passage, the trachea. The posterior 1 end of the trachea is widened and contains two openings. Probe through these apertures, and note the emergence of the probe into the lungs. Slit open one of the lungs and examine the smooth interior, devoid of air sacs. It has already been pointed out that the gills and skin serve as accessory respiratory organs.

Branchial Skeleton 

The hyoid and three succeeding branchi al arches are present in Necturus, and are almost entirely cartilaginous. The hyoid arch, a broad V-shaped arch supporting the tongue, consists of a small anterior hypohyal and a long posterior ceratohyal. THE TURTLE PHYLUM CLASS 

Chordata. 

Reptilia.

It is generally conceded that, of all the vertebrate classes, past and present, the class Reptilia stands foremost in numbers, in size, in range of specialization, and in dominance in the organic world. Although the reptiles of the present occupy a position of comparatively minor importance in the realm of nature, they constitute one of the most interesting, though least known, classes of the subphylum Vertebrata. They are cold-blooded animals, yet breathe exclusively by means of lungs. The reptilian body is usually covered with horny epidermal scales, and bony plates are frequently present as well. When scales are not present, the epidermis is cornified so as to form plates of horny material. The popular idea that reptiles are slimy is without foundation and, contrary also to general belief, very few reptiles, at least in the United States, are harmful to man. 

Of the vast hordes of reptiles that roamed the earth during the late Palaeozoic and almost all of the Mesozoic eras, there are living representatives of but four orders; and one of these is represented by a single species which is rapidly nearing extinction.

Despite the fact that a lizard is in most respects more representative of the Reptilia, and considerably less specialized than is the turtle, the latter is usually selected as the reptilian type. The first reason for this choice is its wide distribution and plentiful numbers. Moreover, its compact nature and convenient size make the turtle a satisfactory laboratory specimen. 

A third, and more fundamental reason is that the turtle structurally is more closely related to the group of reptiles from which the mammals are believed to have arisen than is any other living reptile. 

EXTERNAL ANATOMY 

The body is divided into a flattened triangular head, an unusually long and flexible neck, a broad trunk in which dorsoventral compression is pronounced, and a very short tail of small diameter. The turtle is one of those animals in which the transverse axis is elongated at the expense of both the anteroposterior and the dorsoventral axes. The skin of the legs, tail, and other exposed parts of the body is provided with horny scales or plates. The skin of the head of most of the common turtles is bare, but in some species is marked off into large head shields. The trunk is enclosed in a hard shell, whose outer surface is covered with a series of definitely arranged, large horny plates, which correspond in neither size, number, nor position with the underlying bony plates that are also a part of the exoskeletal structure.

The dorsal roof of this chelonian armor is the carapace, the ventral floor, the plastron.

Study the head, noting the large mouth in which, instead of teeth, horny plates form the margins of the jaws. The nostrils, or external nares, are located on the anterior end of the snout. The prominent eyes do not protrude, but usually are protected above and below by bony ridges of the skull. Each eye is guarded by three eyelids: the thick upper lid; the movable lower lid; and the nictitating membrane, a translucent lid which can be withdrawn into the anterior corner of the eye. Just behind the angle of the jaw is a slight circular depression marking the position of the tympanic membrane which lies on the outer surface of the head. 

The trunk bears two pairs of stout appendages, each of which consists of the same principal divisions that were present in Necturus. The limbs usually possess five digits each. All of the digits, except the fifth digits on the hind legs, bear large claws, and are connected one with another by a more or less complete web. The short conical tail bears on its under surface the cloacal aperture, which is incorrectly named the anus.

Circulation of blood in the turtle's heart takes place as follows. The deoxygenated blood is returned to the sinus venosus which passes it on through the sinatrial aperture into the right atrium.

From the atrium the blood enters the right side of the ventricle, by way of the right atrio-ventricular aperture. Meantime, oxygenated blood has been returned from the

lungs through the pulmonary veins which enter the left atrium. This blood passes through the left atrio-ventricular aperture into the left side of the ventricle. Due to the incomplete separation of the two ventricular chambers, a slight mixing of oxygenated blood and that deficient in oxygen takes place in the ventricle. When the ventricle contracts, the pressure practically closes the inter-ventricular aperture, and the septum is pressed over the opening of the left aorta so that most of the blood present in the small right ventricle is forced out into the pulmonary artery, which conveys it to the lungs where it is aerated. At the same time, the richly oxygenated blood of the left ventricle passes out to the head regions, through the right aorta. As the pressure is diminished toward the close of contraction, the septum opens up, and the blood remaining in both ventricles is forced through the left aorta, which carries it backward to the point of union of the right and left aortic arches. Thus the dorsal aorta always carries a mixture of so-called arterial and venous blood. It is universally true among vertebrates that the circulatory system is so arranged as to convey the blood richest in oxygen to the head where the oxygen requirements of the nervous structures are unusually high.

THE PIGEON Chordata. 

CLASS Aves. 

In many respects the birds are the most highly specialized vertebrates. Fundamentally, however, they are flying reptiles which have undertaken extraordinary structural modifications in order more perfectly to conform to their peculiar environment. They are easily distinguished from all other animals by reason of the fact that they alone possess feathers. All birds, moreover, are bipeds whose forelimbs have been modified as wings. The replacement of teeth by a horny beak is another nearly universal avian characteristic. to these characters the loss of the left aortic arch, and the right ovary and oviduct, and we have a series of strongly marked characteristics such as distinguishes few other classes.

Add The secret of that great and sustained power so essential to successful flight lies in the capacity of birds to convert potential energy into the mechanical energy of motion through the rapid and complete combustion of organic compounds. Such thorough oxidation makes unusual demands upon the respiratory system, and this system, in the avian forms, has become profoundly modified in response to the increased oxygen requirements. Correlated with the high rate of combustion is a proportionate increase in bodily temperature, which may run as high as 44°C. even in a resting bird.

The origin of the birds is still shrouded in mystery, for palaeontologists have discovered but one link which definite ly connects the true birds with that sauropsidian stock from which they probably arose. This single link is Archaeopteryx, a fossil distinctly intermediate between the bird and the reptile. This ancient bird has been placed in the subclass Archae ornithes; all other birds, living or extinct, belong to the subclass Xeornithes.

Near the medial border of the kidney, a large vein emerges from the ventral surface of that organ, and joins the iliac. This is the largest of several renal veins, all of which join the iliac vein. A second vein from the thigh, the sciatic vein, joins the renal portal on a level with the posterior lobe of the kidney, and a caudal vein and two internal iliac veins enter the anastomosis to which reference has been made. In addition to the paired renal portal veins, this anastomosis gives rise to an unpaired posterior mesenteric vein which extends along the wall of the large intestine, and contributes to the hepatic portal circulation. 

The right iliac and portal veins are like the left, except that in the female the asymmetry of the genital system produces a corresponding asymmetry in the genital circulation of the right side.

Turn again to the dorsal surface of the liver and identify the large hepatic portal vein which branches into the liver substance. The main part of this vein enters the right lobe; the remainder traverses the dorsal surface of the left lobe into which it sends its branches. In its course it receives the median and left gastric veins from the left side of the gizzard and proventriculus. Follow the right branch of the hepatic portal vein backward to the point where it is formed by the union of three veins; an anterior mesenteric, a gastro-duodenal, and the posterior mesenteric vein whose place of origin in the anastomosis of the renal portal veins has already been observed. from the greater part of the small intestine. Clear away the connective tissue which obscures the bases of the great arteries that spring directly from the base of the ventricles. It will be noted that there are two such trunks: the larger, medially located trunk is the aorta; the smaller one, dorsal to the aorta, is the pulmonary artery. The ventral aorta has been divided in birds to form these two great vessels. 

The pulmonary artery passes to the left side of the aorta, where it immediately bifurcates to form right and left pulmonary arteries. Trace the left pulmonary artery into the left lung.

The aorta almost immediately gives rise to two large innominate arteries.

Follow up one of these branches to the point where it divides into an anterior common carotid artery, and a lateral subclavian artery. The subclavian is very short, and soon gives rise to a small internal mammary artery, two pectoral arteries, and an axillary artery which enters the wing. Within the wing the axillary artery is known as the brachial artery.

Carefully separate these muscles and follow one of the common carotids forward. Note the vertebral artery entering the vertebrarterial canal of the cervical vertebrae, which it follows to the brain. Remove that portion of the postcaval vein which obscures the aorta, and note the prominent arch that the aorta, now known as the dorsal aorta, makes over the right side of the heart and around the right bronchus to a dorsal position midway between the two lungs. It then turns caudad, and extends to the hinder part of the peritoneal cavity. 

Free the aorta from all obscuring structures and follow it on its course. On its entrance into the peritoneal cavity, the dorsal aorta gives rise to the large coeliac artery, a median vessel extending backward along the proventriculus into which it sends numerous branches. The coeliac artery then gives rise to the relatively small left gastric artery which goes to the left side of the gizzard. This branch sends an hepatic artery into the liver, and then, as the anterior pancreatico-duodenal artery, runs along the duodenal loop to the pancreas. Blood, which has been relieved of its store of oxygen, is returned from the tissues by the systemic veins. It enters the right atrium, passes into the right ventricle, and leaves the heart by the pulmonary arteries which convey it to the lungs. 

After aeration in the lungs, the blood is returned by the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, whence it enters the left ventricle. From the ventricle, it is forced out through the aorta to the organs of the body, and so completes its circuit. Thus there is a perfect double circulation, both oxygenated and vitiated blood flowing simultaneously through the heart, with no possibility of intermingling. Perfection of this double circulation has largely been achieved by the separation of the primitive ventral aorta into two trunks, aortic and pulmonary, which communicate with the left and right sides of the heart respectively.

Shortening of the overall length has been brought about by a series of three flexures of the brain axis. The primary flexure is a flexure toward the ventral side, and involves only the midbrain region. This flexure causes the forebrain to form nearly a right angle with the posterior portion. 

The second or nuchal flexure occurs in the region of the medulla. This, too, is a ventral flexure which causes the medulla to form an obtuse angle with the spinal cord.

Axial Skeleton

The skeleton of birds, in keeping with the other structures of the body, is highly specialized in those features associated with an aerial existence. Lightness and strength are the two outstanding characteristics of the avian skeleton. The first feature is secured by the substitution of hollow, spongy bone for compact, marrowfilled bone. Rigidity and strength are, however, insured through the fusion or ankylosis of various skeletal elements. 

Note the very natural division of the vertebral column into five regions, of which the most anterior is the cervical region. The four other regions are the thoracic, the lumbar, the sacral, and the caudal regions. The first two of the thirteen or fourteen cervical vertebrae constitute the reptile-like atlas and axis. The former is a ring-shaped bone from which the centrum and neural spine are absent; its transverse processes are broad and winglike. The centrum of the axis bears a cylindrical odontoid process upon its anterior face, which probably represents the missing centrum of the atlas. Examine one of the typical cervical vertebrae, noting particularly the elongated centrum. Its anterior and posterior faces are saddleshaped, the anterior face being convex. dorsoventrally and concave laterally; the posterior face is laterally convex and dorsoventrally concave. Such an heterocoelous vertebra provides for maximum flexibility of the vertebral column. Laterally, each cervical vertebra bears paired transverse processes to which are fused rudimentary ribs. Note the neural arch which arises from the dorsal surface of the centrum. It is composed of a pair of lateral neural processes and a median dorsal neural spine. Projecting from the anterior and posterior faces of the neural arch are paired articular processes called the prezygapophyses and post zygapophyses, respectively.

The five or six thoracic vertebrae are those vertebrae whose ribs extend to the ventral side. They have prominent neural spines and well-developed transverse processes; but the spines, centra, transverse processes, and zygapophyses of the first three out of five thoracic vertebrae (the second, third, and fourth vertebrae in those specimens possessing six thoracic vertebrae) are immovably fused together. In either case the first thoracic vertebra is ankylosed with the last.cervical, and the last thoracic with the first lumbar vertebra. To the latter ankylosis are added the two remaining lumbar vertebrae, the four sacral, and the first five or six caudal vertebrae to form one continuous piece, the 1 synsacrum. By examining its ventral surface, it is possible to recognize absence of ribs from the lumbar and sacral vertebrae, and the unusual size of their transverse. processes. Posterior to the synsacrum are six free caudal vertebrae, and an irregular terminal bone, the pygostyle, formed by the fusion of several vertebrae. 

Haemal arches are absent from all of the vertebrae.

The large flat sternum of birds is an highly specialized bone which covers the ventral side of the breast and a large part of the abdomen. Note the deep keel or carina4 upon its ventral surface.
