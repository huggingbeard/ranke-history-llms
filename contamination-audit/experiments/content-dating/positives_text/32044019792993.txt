REPORT ON AUDIT OF ECONOMIC AND TECHNICAL ASSISTANCE PROGRAM FOR VIETNAM INTERNATIONAL COOPERATION ADMINISTRATION DEPARTMENT OF STATE FISCAL YEARS 1955-1957 SCOPE OF EXAMINATION 

As part of our audit of the activities authorized by the Mutual Security Act of 1954, as amended (22 U.S.C 1750), and related prior legislation, we have examined the United States economic and technical assistance program for Vietnam as administered by the 

International Cooperation Administration (ICA) of the Department of State and its predecessor, the Foreign Operations Administra tion (FOA). 

Our examination included a review of pertinent records and procedures at the Washington office of ICA and a field visit in October 1956 to the operations mission in Saigon (Mission). 

The principal objective of our examination was to determine, by reference to major activities and projects within the over-all country program, the adequacy of financial and related procedures in the use of mutual security funds appropriated for fiscal years 1955 and 1956. Our report also gives effect to certain prior year assistance activities in the Indochina area and to transactions and procedures in fiscal year 1957 which are relevant to the matters covered in our examination.

A draft of this report was submitted to the agency for review in July 1957. We received the agency's comments thereon in January 1958 and, insofar as we deemed appropriate, reflected them in the pertinent sections of this report. Because of the delay in receiving the agency's comments, it was necessary to extend our audit work at the Washington office of ICA in order that the subjects of our report would be as nearly as practicable on a current basis. 

The scope of this report does not include the military assistance program for Vietnam which is under the administration of the Department of Defense and the subject of separate audit and re porting by the General Accounting Office.

INTRODUCTION 

Following the outbreak of hostilities in the area of French Indochina at the close of World War II, the United States furnished substantial military and economic assistance to France and the three Associated States of Indochina: Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos. 

The Geneva agreement of July 1954 terminated the war in Indochina and partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel creating the separate state of North Vietnam under control of the Communist Viet Minh.. 

South Vietnam (hereafter referred to as Vietnam), as well as Cambodia and Laos, dissolved the political and economic association among themselves and with France and became the recipients of separate and enlarged assistance programs from the United States.

SUMMARY OF FINDINGS 

General observations 

The emergency conditions prevailing in Vietnam following the armistice agreement in July 1954 have determined in considerable measure the nature of the assistance program and the manner of its administration.

The country's former economic, political, and military ties to France having been dissolved, Vietnam became largely dependent on assistance by the United States to meet the crisis brought about by the division of the country, to protect it against the external military menace, and to overcome threats to its internal security. Also, American technical help and advice to the newly established Vietnamese Government assumed particular significance after French administrators and technicians had left the country. 

In the administration of its assistance activities, ICA found the financial procedures generally employed in other foreignaid programs too slow and restrictive to accomplish the desired objectives. The agency deemed it advisable to relax certain of its usual controls and safeguards, made extensive use of broad cash grants, and liberalized its import program, so as to generate promptly the large amounts of local currency considered necessary to meet emergency conditions.

The agency points to significant accomplishments in the first 2 years of its expanded program for Vietnam. An estimated 600,000 refugees have been evacuated and resettled, and progress was made toward their economic integration. The authority of the central government appears to be firmly established and internal security

largely assured. The agency states that the program has helped to maintain economic stability and laid the groundwork for agricultural recovery and industrial development. Special assistance efforts were made in highway improvement, public administration, and information, in line with the emphasis on political and security criteria as being distinct from strictly economic criteria in the allocation of project assistance funds. Beginning with fiscal year 1957, the ICA program specifically seeks to meet Vietnam's needs for investment and economic expansion, in addition to financing its security costs.

The program for Vietnam has become, in the last several years, one of the largest assistance efforts in the entire Mutual Security Program. As economic and political conditions become more stable, the agency may be expected to be guided more and more by considerations of sound financial management in addition to those of urgent political or military objectives. Only in this manner can the United States Government realize results commensurate with the magnitude of its financial contribution. 

It is the purpose of our report to apprise the Congress and the agency of the principal program transactions and projects which have been the subject of our examination, to identify areas of deficient or undesirable administration and the conditions which caused them, and to encourage a determined effort to apply considerations of sound financial management in all important phases of the assistance program.

The conditions noted by us are summarized below and discussed more extensively on succeeding pages as indicated.

Economic and technical assistance to Indochina, 1951-54 (p. 18)

A significant amount of assistance activities carried out as part of the Indochina program prior to the Geneva agreements of July 1954 benefited the northern part of Vietnam which subsequently came under the control of the Communist Viet Minh. We were informed that certain United States-financed equipment had been evacuated to the South, but the agency had made no accounting for salvage or loss of such aid goods.

Military budget support program for French and Associated States forces, 1954-55 (p. 19)

The budget support program assumed by the United States Government in fiscal year 1954 did not become fully effective in view of the Geneva accords of July 1954 which terminated hostilities in Indochina. No savings developed in the 400 million dollar program for the French Expeditionary Corps, because of reported firm commitments by France which antedated the armistice. The support program for the Associated States forces, originally programed for 345 million dollars, realized savings of 91 million dollars which were reprogramed for other countries, including 14 million dollars for Vietnam.

Because of the significant political and military changes in the area, it became impracticable for the agency to determine whether proper end use was made of United States-financed goods and services.

Military support agreement in fiscal year 1955 (p. 24)

A broad military support agreement executed in April 1955 between the United States and Vietnam was used by FOA/ICA to

obligate 1955 aid funds in the amount of 234.8 million dollars. 

This method of obligating, funds was a major deviation from established agency procedures and, in our opinion, was not consistent with good financial management since funds were committed prior to a reasonably firm determination of their use. 

1955 aid level overestimated (p. 25)

The agency obligated 1955 assistance funds substantially in excess of Vietnam's requirements for budgetary support, as subsequently determined. The amount of 82 million dollars by which the program had been overestimated was carried forward and applied to the 1956 support program. 

Commodity import program (p. 26)

The administration of the commodity import program has been affected by the special conditions in Vietnam following the dissolution of long-standing commercial ties with France. ICA found the procedure of the Vietnamese authorities in the issuance of import licenses inefficient in various respects and in need of developing fair and nondiscriminatory practices. The Mission' assisted in the simplification and improvement of procedures and controls.

The agency deemed it necessary to liberalize its procurement regulations and control procedures. In particular, ICA waived certain limitations on goods normally not eligible for ICA financing, such as automobile accessories, household appliances, and bottles for the brewing industry. Also, the agency waived notification of proposed procurement by Vietnamese importers to the ICA Office of Small Business. Some of these waivers were

gradually eliminated; others are being continued. 

We found a need for follow-up of refund claims, which ICA had filed against Vietnam for contravention of agency regulations, and for effective procedures to accomplish a reconciliation between shipments and arrivals of aid goods as required by established ICA rules. The agency advised that improved procedures have been, or are being, worked out. 

Dollar cash grants and loans (p. 31)

Over one third (119 million dollars) of the assistance funds for fiscal year 1955 was made available in the form of dollar cash grants. ICA advanced dollars, by United States Treasury checks, to the Government of Vietnam which, in consideration therefor, set aside corresponding piaster funds principally to pay the costs of refugee assistance and military budget support. In 1956, a grant of 7 million dollars and a loan of 25 million dollars were made in a similar manner. This method has led to a number of unusual, if not undesirable, conditions: 

1. The expenditure of large amounts of piasters, based only on dollar reserves but without the offsetting immediate availability of additional goods, tended to aggravate inflationary pressures in the Vietnamese economy. 

2. Some of the dollars were authorized under a tripartite agreement with France for financing "invisible" payments,1 including profit remittances of French business interests leaving Vietnam. 

3. Vietnam received dollars in excess of its foreign exchange requirements.

4. To overcome a lag in the use of direct dollars in the Vietnamese import program, ICA authorized a number of commodity categories generally not eligible for ICA financing, such as tobacco and malt for beer. 

5. In April 1956, ICA made a 40-year loan of 25 million dollars to Vietnam under a letter of administrative arrangements, while the formal loan agreement was executed in July 1956. Since the greater part of the 1956 aid program involved military budget support and non-revenue-producing activities, the financial soundness of the loan is open to question.

The agency has recognized that cash grants are a less desirable means of furnishing aid than its normal system of financing commodity imports. In fiscal year 1957, the agency made no cash grants to Vietnam. 

Triangular trade programs (p. 38)

Under triangular trade arrangements, the agency shipped surplus agricultural commodities to France and allocated the franc proceeds from such sales for financing Vietnamese imports from France. Agreements with France for the sale of cotton met French buying resistance with a resultant lag in the program and the need to reprogram some of the cotton to other surplus commodities. 

In allocating the franc proceeds for Vietnamese imports, ICA authorized several generally not eligible commodity categories similar to exceptions allowed for the regular import program and the direct dollar program. ICA informed us that, starting in 1957, the licensing of most items which are ordinarily not eligible for 

ICA financing was discontinued. 

Local currency program (p. 42)

The official exchange rate of 35 plasters per 1 United States dollar, which is used for the purpose of converting dollar aid 11

funds into local currency, overvalues the piaster, compared with current market rates of 70 to 1 or higher. The rate of 35 to 1 not only adds unduly to the demands for United States aid, the greater part of which is justified by ICA for the purpose of generating local currency, but it also encourages speculation and leads to windfall profits for private importers. We believe that the agency should use its best efforts to negotiate a more favorable exchange rate with the recipient country.

The Mission needed stronger accounting control over the deposit of counterpart funds to assure that full commensurate deposits are made of the proceeds from all ICA-financed imports; also, it needed more reliable reports on the use of released counterpart funds and on the status of certain loans to Vietnamese agencies financed from the counterpart account. since been instituted.

ECONOMIC AND TECHNICAL DEVELOPMENT (p. 61) 

The ICA program in 1955 continued various projects previously undertaken under the joint program for the Associated States, but with increased emphasis on technical assistance and on a substantially larger scale. Dollar contributions during the 3 years 

1955-57 totaled 70 million dollars, supplemented by piaster contributions from the counterpart account programed in the aggregate of some 90 million dollars.

We observed efforts on the part of the ICA Mission to overcome various administrative weaknesses, some of which date back to the earlier program and are related to the emergency conditions prevailing in Vietnam: 

1. Assistance in the provinces was intensified through the stationing of technicians in key locations. 

2. Consolidation of projects reduced individual undertakings from some 200 to 43 at June 30, 1957. 

3. Special surveys and cooperative efforts with Vietnam were made to absorb unused supplies and equipment. Our field examination disclosed considerable quantities of medical supplies stockpiled in warehouses in Saigon. 

4. Deficiencies in the reporting and documentation of project contributions were corrected in 1957. 13

5. Transfers of funds determined to be in excess of project requirements to other projects, contrary to prescribed procedures, were discontinued. 

6. Progress has been made in reducing ICA funds for budgetary support to Vietnamese agencies.

Internal audits conducted by ICA in 1955 and 1957 disclosed certain deficiencies in the Mission's financial management and a need for improving property and other control procedures. 

Financial reporting (p. 93 )

Financial reporting, both at the Washington office and at the overseas Mission, has not disclosed in a clear manner the accomplishments and status of principal program activities, nor of the program as a whole. In view of the complex nature of the program for Vietnam, the agency's standard fiscal reports appear to be too technical and not sufficiently informative to permit a clear understanding of the financial results of the program. For greater usefulness to the agency management, we recommended the development of a comprehensive but concise report which should show, as a minimum, the obligation and expenditure of dollar funds classified by principal program activity and the intended use of local currency funds which are to be generated by dollar expenditures. 17

BACKGROUND OF PROGRAM ECONOMIC AND TECHNICAL ASSISTANCE TO THE ASSOCIATED STATES OF INDOCHINA, 1951-54 

With the initiation of the Marshall Plan for Europe in 1948, the area of Indochina received economic assistance through France as part of the United States program of aid to dependent overseas territories. The amounts provided in this manner are not readily available.

In May 1950, the Economic Cooperation Administration (ECA) 

opened a technical and economic mission in Saigon and commenced, effective in fiscal year 1951, direct economic aid to the Associated States of Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos, administered to the three countries on a unified basis. The Government of the United 

States signed, in September 1951, separate economic cooperation agreements with each of the three states. These agreements are still the basic documents governing the terms and conditions of assistance presently administered by ICA.

From the inception of this program to 1954, when the Associated States terminated their association with France, ECA and its successors, MSA and FOA, committed program funds totaling 114 million dollars. These funds were made available pursuant to section 302(a) of the Mutual Security Act of 1951 and subsequent amendments (65 Stat. 373), which authorized economic and technical assistance in those portions of the Asia and Pacific area which the President deemed to be not under Communist control. 

Following are the obligations of such mutual security funds for fiscal years 1951-54:1

The allocation of these funds between the three Associated States is not determinable from the records; however, the agency estimated that approximately 70 million dollars of economic aid was attributable to Vietnam.

For the three states combined, agency records show that some 30 million dollars represented project assistance, covering the costs of equipment, technicians, and related support of development and technical assistance projects. The remaining 56 million dollars of economic aid was applied to finance the import of salable commodities; in consideration, the recipient countries deposited local currency counterpart funds which were used to defray the local costs of projects. 

A substantial part of project funds provided support to activities having military significance, such as maintenance and improvement of transportation facilities (11.7 million dollars). Other important uses of funds were health and sanitation projects (8 million dollars) and assistance to agricultural activities (4.9 million dollars).

A significant amount of project activity was carried out in the northern part of Indochina which, pursuant to the Geneva agreement of 1954, came under the control of the Communist Viet Minh. 

The agency informed us that, when the Franco-Vietnamese authorities evacuated this area, FOA salvaged certain project equipment which could be readily located and moved. The agency has made no accounting for the quantity or value of equipment salvaged, nor of the losses in equipment or other investments which had to be abandoned at that time.

To the extent that project activities took place in the areas within the present boundaries of the States of Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos, FOA and its successor, ICA, continued these projects as part of the expanded aid programs starting in 1955. In our discussion of individual major project undertakings for Vietnam, we have given recognition to these earlier activities where appropriate. 

MILITARY BUDGET SUPPORT FOR FRENCH EXPEDITIONARY CORPS AND ASSOCIATED STATES FORCES, 1954-55 

In addition to military assistance, consisting of United States-procured weapons and equipment financed from mutual security funds under the administration of the Department of Defense, FOA undertook, in fiscal year 1954, a special program of budgetary support to assist France in maintaining its Expeditionary Corps in Indochina as well as in building up the forces of the Associated States. This program was designed to enable France and the Associated States to sustain a vigorous military effort and, by following the so-called Navarre Plan, carry the war with the Communistsponsored Viet Minh to a successful conclusion. 19

Memorandum of understanding 

The terms and conditions of the support program are governed by a memorandum of understanding signed on March 1, 1954, following an earlier exchange of diplomatic letters dated September 20, 1953. The United States pledged assistance totaling up to 785 million dollars, of which 400 million dollars was for the support of the French Expeditionary Corps in Indochina and 385 million dollars was for the Associated States forces. The memorandum contemplated 

745 million dollars in dollar payments and 40 million dollars in

French francs by release of counterpart funds in France. Three categories of French expenditures were eligible for United States financing. 

1. Certain chapters of the 1954 French defense budget for 

Indochina, and specified line items contained therein, covering the support of the French Expeditionary Corps in Indochina. 

2. French expenditures in France in support of the forces of the Associated States. 

3. French advances to the Associated States covering expenditures in Indochina in support of their field forces. 

The chapters in the 1954 military budgets of France and the Associated States under which expenditures were eligible for United States reimbursement were detailed in an annex to the memorandum of understanding.

On an over-all basis, the United States undertook in this program, together with military end-item assistance, to finance about 66 percent of the total estimated cost of the French and the 

Associated States defense effort in 1954.

Funding plan for 1954 Section 304 of the Mutual Security Act of 1951, as amended in 

1953 (67 Stat. 153), specifically authorized the sum of 400 million dollars for the procurement of equipment, materials, and services necessary for the support of the forces of the Associated States and the forces of France located in such States. Between 

January and June 1954, FOA allotted an additional 345 million dollars from the 1954 mutual security appropriations for military assistance. The use of the 345 million dollars was based on the broad power of the President, contained in section 301 of the 

Mutual Security Act of 1951 (65 Stat. 375) and section 303 (a) of the Mutual Defense Assistance Act of 1949 (63 Stat. 716), for carry ing out programs "in the general area of China."

With the cessation of hostilities in July 1954 and the subsequent changed conditions in Indochina, actual demands for United States payments to France successively decreased until at June 30, 1957, total requirements under the support program had been reduced from 785 to 707 million dollars. 20

Effect of armistice on United States aid commitment

ICA informed us that no savings developed in the 400 million dollar program for the French Expeditionary Corps because France had firm commitments through contracts with suppliers which antedated the armistice agreement. In fact, the United States considered it necessary to pledge an additional 100 million dollars in 1955 to sustain the Corps in Indochina pending an adequate build-up of the national armies of Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos and until eventual withdrawal of the Corps.

The original understanding between France and the United States regarding the commitment of the 345 million dollars for the support of the forces of the Associated States contemplated a resumption of negotiations if the French Government's military plan proved incapable of execution or if other unforeseen circumstances should arise which negated the basic understanding. Also, the understanding provided for a continuing review of the United States contribution and for appropriate adjustments thereof. Accordingly, in May 1955 France and the United States negotiated a cost-sharing agreement which limited maximum United States dollar expenditures applicable to the Associated States forces to 278.4 million dollars. 

This reduction was made possible partly by an increased use of French counterpart funds equivalent to 13 million dollars. The agreement noted that United States savings would have been greater except for the fact that the Governments of the Associated States were not able to contribute as great a sum as had been contemplated under the exchange of letters in September 1953. The resulting savings of 66.6 million dollars were reprogramed in June 1955 for a number of other countries and programs, pursuant to the authority contained in sections 401 and 501 of the Mutual Security Act of 1954 (22 U.S.C. 1921 and 1753), and after consultation with cognizant committees of the Congress.

During fiscal years 1956 and 1957 ICA realized further savings totaling 24.3 million dollars as French costs for the program 21

were below previous estimates. 

The sum of 13.8 million dollars was reprogramed and made a part of the 1956 assistance program for Vietnam; the balance of 10.5 million dollars was deobligated in 1957. 

It should be noted that the United States Government continued in 1955, 1956, and 1957 programs of budgetary support for the military forces of Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos; however, these programs are no longer administered through the French Government but directly by each of the recipient countries.

Special conditions in Vietnam

Noteworthy are the special economic and political conditions in Vietnam which gave rise to various administrative difficulties. 

The Mission and the Vietnamese authorities faced the task of overcoming obstacles to a more rapid implementation of the import program, deemed necessary to offset serious inflationary pressure and to generate local currency required for budgetary support of the Vietnamese Government. The Mission found the procedures of the 

Vietnamese authorities in the issuance of import licenses to be inefficient in various respects and found a need of developing fair and nondiscriminatory practices. ICA deemed it necessary to liberalize its own procurement regulations and control procedures. 

Until January 1955, by virtue of long-standing commercial and financial arrangements, the economy was intimately tied to France. 

Banking, commerce, and industry were almost entirely in French hands and French goods supplied most of the required imports. 26

Consequently, the ICA program, with its emphasis on world-wide procurement, had to overcome different habits and lack of experience on the part of importers. Moreover, the French Government pursued a policy seeking to retain the Vietnamese market to the greatest possible extent by means of preferential tariffs and export subsidies.

ADMINISTRATIVE DIFFICULTIES 

The administration of the import program was difficult because of the inadequate and inexperienced Vietnamese staff assigned to the program, as well as certain practices of the Vietnamese Government which were inconsistent with a prompt and effective use of dollar aid funds. In addition, stringent credit policies by the commercial banks, particularly at the inception of the import program, made it difficult for importers to obtain funds. 

Discrimination against certain classes of importers

ICA found that Vietnamese licensing practices discriminated against French and Chinese importers in favor of Vietnamese nationals, even though the latter often had no previous commercial experience. It was suspected that certain favored nationals acted as "fronts" for these importers and obtained licenses for the prejudiced firms at premium rates ranging as high as 17 piasters (about 

50%) for each dollar of import authority. The low exchange rate at which importers acquire the dollar exchange encourages this practice. The Vietnamese Government is believed by the Mission to have sharply reduced this activity, although at the time of our field visit we were informed that some discrimination still existed.

Arbitrary reduction in applications 

The Directorate General of Commerce (DGC), which is the Viet namese agency charged with the issuance of import licenses, has made arbitrary reductions in the amounts of applications. This has led importers to request greater amounts of exchange than they reasonably required, in the expectation that after giving effect to the reductions the amounts granted would correspond to their actual needs. Action in 1956 in limiting the number of eligible importers from 20,000 to 1,300 was expected to remedy this situation. A subsequent offering of funds for textile imports revealed a situation as bad as that prevailing before; 17,000 applications were received aggregating 200 million dollars. 

Lack of coordination between Vietnamese agencies

The authorization of aid dollars for import transactions has been subject to separate action of two independent Vietnamese agencies. The DGC reviews and approves applications for import licenses, whereas the National Bank of Vietnam allocates available dollar funds. There has been insufficient cooperation between the 27

two agencies. We found that special Mission efforts were needed to explore with the appropriate Vietnamese agencies possibilities for streamlining the functions of the National Bank and eliminate unnecessary control procedures in view of those which the DGC appears to exercise effectively. ICA subsequently informed us that duplication of activities has been virtually eliminated. 

Lag in procurement process

The various inefficient practices contributed to a substantial lag in the procurement process which was found to increase unduly the aid pipeline and to jeopardize the attainment of important program objectives. We observed that the ICA Mission, at various times, assisted the Vietnamese authorities in finding ways of speeding the licensing process and overcoming administrative problems. This was accomplished by simplified procedures, reduced paper work, and improved financial controls. 

LIBERALIZATION OF CONTROL PROCEDURES 

In view of the special conditions encountered in Vietnam, ICA found it appropriate to deviate from various financial control pro-. cedures ordinarily applicable to commodity import programs. 

Commodity justification

The Mission did not receive adequate information from the Vietnamese authorities regarding the various commodity requirements. By approving broad categories only, such as industrial machinery, the Mission did not know the specific items of equipment to be purchased. We suggested to the agency that it would be advisable to require somewhat greater detail in support of requests for procurement authorizations. It is the position of the agency that justification for individual commodity imports was supplied to the greatest practicable extent but improvements were made in this respect during 1957. 

Following the dissolution of the economic union between the Associated States, Vietnam at first was not able to prepare an independent analysis of its import requirements and the ICA Mission had no reliable information to serve as the basis for the commodity program in 1955 and 1956. A complete analysis of Vietnam's commodity requirements became necessary and the Mission informed us that such a study would be undertaken and would be ready sometime in 1957.

NATURE AND PURPOSE DOLLAR CASH GRANTS AND LOANS 

In fiscal year 1955 the agency considered it necessary to grant United States dollars on a large scale directly to the Vietnamese Government which in turn deposited piasters to the counterpart account at an agreed exchange rate. These piasters were needed to meet the emergency situations of the refugee and military support programs.

The Government of Vietnam, in order to contribute the agreed amount of plasters for each cash grant made, generally borrowed the required sum from the Vietnamese National Bank. ICA considered the financing of imports essential to offset the inflationary effect created by the large-scale expenditure of plasters without a simultaneous corresponding increase in commodities. Compared with cash grants totaling 119 million dollars in 1955, similar grants in 1956 were substantially less (7 million dollars) because the major part of local currency requirements could be met under the regular import program. EXCHANGE RATE DEPOSIT AND USE OF LOCAL CURRENCY 

For the purpose of converting dollar aid funds into local currency, ICA has accepted the official exchange rate of 35 plasters per 1 United States dollar. This rate was originally established in 1953 on the basis of a Franco-Vietnamese monetary arrangement by reference to the value of the franc; the relationship between 

French franc and Vietnamese piaster was set at 1 to 10, while that of the franc to the United States dollar was 350 to 1.

Use of the official rate adds to the cost of United States dollar aid

Subsequent inflationary conditions have reduced the real value of the plaster to one half or less of its original value. 

This is evidenced by the rates used in other exchange transactions. 

For example, in April 1956, the Vietnamese Government was using a rate of about 70 piasters per dollar for transactions in its own free dollar exchange. Vietnam approved a "limited access free market" rate whereby individuals as well as the United States Embassy may purchase piasters from the Vietnamese National Bank at a rate varying around 75 piasters. Prior to the establishment of this "free" market rate in 1956, the Embassy was able to purchase plasters for its own use and that of American personnel at a rate considerably higher than 35 to 1. At the time of our visit to 

Vietnam, rates in the money market of Hong Kong were quoted around 90 piasters. The use of the official rate of 35 compared with current market rates results in an expenditure of approximately twice as many United States aid dollars in order to generate a given amount of plasters.

In the field of commercial imports, the artificially low rate, besides increasing over-all United States costs, encourages speculation and results in "windfall" profits to private importers.

The commodities are purchased at the official rate, which underprices the goods, but are sold at going market prices, reflecting the depreciated value of the plaster. The resulting profit is offset only partly by custom duties levied by the Vietnamese Government equivalent to approximately 18 plasters per United States dollar.

Both the Mission and the Embassy have recognized that the rate now in use should be revised. However, they are concerned about the impact of a devaluation of the piaster on the position of the government and the confidence of the business community. 

Also, it is feared that a devaluation might stimulate further inflation and increase costs and prices to an extent that little, if any, savings in United States aid would be realized. 42

We believe it pertinent to refer to the findings of other United States official observers who have recognized and advocated the need for negotiating a more realistic exchange rate with Viet- In view of the magnitude of the current program for Vietnam and the wide variation between official and free market rates, we believe it incumbent upon the responsible United States officials to study the possibilities of negotiating, for use in the aid program, a rate more nearly in line with the current purchasing power of the plaster in relation to the dollar.

DEPOSIT PROCEDURES 

The economic cooperation agreement signed in September 1951 provides for the deposit of local currency funds (plasters) accruing to the Government of Vietnam from the sale of commodities or services furnished with United States grant assistance. These plaster funds, known as "counterpart" funds, are deposited in a special counterpart account maintained by the Minister of Finance in the National Bank of Vietnam, and may be withdrawn only for programs approved by the United States, through its economic and technical mission in Saigon. The agreement further provides that the 

Government of Vietnam shall, at the request of the United States, deposit supplementary amounts of plasters if the sales proceeds deposited to the special account are not commensurate with the dollar value of United States aid and are insufficient for the purpose of the aid program. 

Control over counterpart deposits needs strengthening The deposit of counterpart funds in consideration of cash grants and triangular arrangements with France has presented no accounting problems, since the Vietnamese Minister of Finance is obliged to make immediate and full deposit of the required funds. 

On the other hand, the deposit of local currency in consideration for the financing of commodity procurement is more complex as it is based on dollar payments made pursuant to numerous procurement authorizations issued by ICA. The large number of individual transactions, frequent amendments of terms and amounts, and subsequent adjustments of payments create administrative and clerical problems. For general control purposes, administrative procedures of ICA and its predecessors require the Mission to undertake a periodic reconciliation between dollar payments by the Washington office and plasters deposited in the special account by the Vietnamese Government. RESETTLEMENT AND REHABILITATION 

By the end of the evacuation phase (May 1955), more than 600,000 Vietnamese civilian refugees from the North had arrived in South Vietnam. An even greater number of persons in the South had been displaced from their homes by military action. The Government of Vietnam was unprepared for so many refugees. It had no plans for their resettlement or relocation outside the Saigon area and was not in a financial position to assume this burden. Some attempt was made during 1955 to relocate these refugees in areas suited to permanent settlement, but, to carry out a systematic program of this nature with proper consideration of the requirements of economic integration, the Vietnamese Government relied substantially on financial and technical assistance from the United States, to be furnished in fiscal year 1956. 

1956 plan of operation

The beginning of the major resettlement effort in 1956 found some 30 percent of the refugees living in the closely populated provinces adjacent to Saigon where little agricultural land was unoccupied. Some resettlement villages already in existence were located at the edge of inaccessible land requiring proper access roads. The general plan was to find suitable settlement areas for all refugees, relocate settlements where necessary, furnish settlers with basic tools, and otherwise take those steps which would facilitate eventual integration into the Vietnamese economy. In the meantime, subsistence, medical care, and other kind of relief had to be provided until the settlers became self-sufficient. 

For the support of this program, ICA agreed to make available from fiscal year 1956 funds the sum of 7.6 million dollars for procurement of equipment and services from outside of Vietnam and grants totaling 28.4 million dollars for the generation of counterpart funds to meet piaster costs in Vietnam. The ICA Mission organized a staff of technical advisors and support personnel, including headquarters personnel in Saigon and field staffs in various key locations throughout the provinces. 

The Mission set up a special advisory group to assist Comigal in accounting and financial matters. The dollar costs of the American personnel amounted to $284,000 in 1956. The services of United States-employed

In this manner, the contracting parties signed subproject agreements for 114 resettlement centers and 30 special activities. The control of funds was further subdivided by villages and resettlement committees establishing well over 300 separate control points. 

The system devised for 1956 has the advantage of (1) providing fairly detailed cost budgets for each center of operations and (2) placing financial responsibility on definite officials in charge of villages or centers. On the other hand, the system has created certain administrative difficulties because of the fragmentation of fund control among numerous control points. There was a shortage of trained local personnel to exercise supervision ani guard against irregularities.

The system has not been sufficiently flexible to accomplish necessary changes in funding. Within the agreed budget for each center or village, funds could be transferred to a limited extent, but changes beyond this point required formal agreement between the Mission and Comigal. The Mission reported that the Vietnamese authorities were reluctant to use the transfer authority and follow 

Mission recommendations for liberalizing procedures because of lack of accounting control criticized with respect to the 1955 program. 

It became apparent that many projects were overbudgeted and necessary downward adjustments had to await on-the-spot surveys. For example, the agreement for the Gia Kiem center provided 19.2 million piasters, but only 6 million piasters were required. At the time of our field visit in October 1956, the excess funds were still committed to this subproject and could not be used elsewhere pending formal amendment of the agreement. .59

GENERAL REVIEW ECONOMIC AND TECHNICAL DEVELOPMENT 

A program of economic and technical development has been under way in Vietnam since fiscal year 1951, when ECA initiated such a program on a unified basis for all three of the Associated States of Indochina. Initially, by agreement with France, the three countries were not to seek technical advice from any other country until they had first attempted to obtain such assistance from France. Following the Geneva agreement in 1954 which brought about dissolution of the ties between France and the Associated States, the United States-sponsored development program was greatly expanded, with increasing emphasis on technical assistance activities. Starting in fiscal year 1956, ICA supplemented defense support funds by a separate allotment for Vietnam from the mutual security appropriation for technical cooperation. 

United States aid funds were made available in two ways: (1) by approving dollars for the procurement of equipment and services for specific projects agreed to between Vietnam and the United States and (2) by authorizing piaster funds from the counterpart account (generated by ICA-financed commodity imports, or by cash grants), for the payment of local project costs. In addition to contributions for individual project activities, the category of economic and technical development comprises the costs of United States technical personnel stationed overseas, certain related support costs, and the costs of training Vietnamese nationals in the 

United States or in third countries.

In the 7-year period 1951-57, a total of over 230 million dollars of mutual security funds, directly or via the counterpart account, was authorized for economic and technical development in Vietnam; this included approximately 70 million dollars representing the portion of the Indochina program for 1951-54, which is attributed to Vietnam.

Project documentation 

Several project agreements were signed in 1955 and 1956 by the Mission and the Vietnamese Government before the Washington office of ICA had approved the project proposal document. Under agency regulations, proposal documents are required to be submitted by the Mission to the Washington office for technical and financial review. In the cases examined by us, the required approval was obtained subsequent to the signing of the agreements; this procedure is contrary to sound management controls as contemplated within the framework of the ICA financial system. In the 1957 program the agency adhered to the established procedure of prior Washington approval.

Transfer of funds between projects The Mission has transferred project funds, upon determination that they were in excess of funding requirements, to other projects, contrary to prescribed agency procedures. The rule is that project contributions obligated in a prior year cannot be reprogramed for other projects in a succeeding year unless referred to the Budget Division of the Controller's Office in Washington; this office will reallot the funds upon request of the Mission only if in the light of project and country priorities such reallotment appears advisable. Effective in fiscal year 1957, instructions by the Director of ICA have further limited the transfer of project funds to those cases where funding requirements have been overestimated for one project and underestimated for another; reprogramed funds shall no longer be used for new or additional activities which ordinarily would be financed from current year funds. 

We were informed subsequent to our examination that the Mission now handles transfers of prior year funds in strict conformity with agency regulations.

Nature of project

In 1955 the project consisted of three separate undertakings, which were merged in fiscal year 1956 and brought under one overall project agreement. The project continues and consolidates various programs carried out in 1951-54 under the former Indochina program to provide credit assistance to farmers and foster their self-sufficiency. As resumed and expanded in 1955 and 1956, the project had three major objectives: 

1. Provision of food, shelter, tools, work animals, and seed on an emergency basis for displaced farm families returning to the land. 

2. Establishment of demonstrational rice storage coopera tives. 

3. Establishment of a strong central organization to (a) coordinate credit activities, (b) provide necessary supervision and capital, and (c) assume responsibility for orderly liquidation of all previously established credit organizations.

While in 1955 some of the activities under this project were in conjunction with those under the refugee program, for example the procurement of work buffaloes, the project was limited in 1956 expressly to the benefit of nonrefugees. Dollar contributions were used chiefly for purchases of work buffaloes and oxen ($1,273,000), roofing and building materials needed for warehouse construction, burlap bags, rice milling equipment, trucks, and office equipment. Counterpart funds were used for housing materials, rice, and hand implements; they were also applied to set up loan funds and establish an agricultural credit agency.

Administration of loan activities

Prior to fiscal year 1955, certain credit activities were administered by a Vietnamese agency known as the National Agricul tural and Handicraft Credit Cooperative (SNCAAC). This arrange ment did not function satisfactorily, and ICA expressed concern 67

At the time of our visit in October 1956, final action by the Vietnamese Government had not yet been taken, but we were advised that on April 1, 1957, a decree was issued for the creation of the new agency.

The Mission thought that purchase from sources outside of Vietnam would be preferable to loans of plasters for internal purchases, since local demand would drive up prices. Various questions arose as to the specifications requested by the Mission, and certain inconsistencies with agency procedures, similar to those in the case of the 8-inch dredges, required withdrawal of the original bid invitation and resolicitation. Eventually, the contract for the 12-inch dredges was awarded in February 1954, providing for a construction cost of $665,000 per unit. 

The second 12-inch dredge, which was the subject of the 1955 project agreement with Vietnam, was procured under an amendment to the earlier contract of February 1954 at a cost of $606,000. At

June 30, 1957, the Army had not yet billed ICA for this dredge, and, as a result, the project records of the agency do not show any expenditures although shipment and delivery are complete. 

While this dredge was under construction the Mission suggested that it might not be needed in Vietnam and the order be canceled.

However, such action was not taken, and the dredge was delivered in the early part of 1956. ICA found that there is a clear requirement for the operation of all available dredging equipment to eliminate the backlog of dredging which may take from 4 to 7 years. 77

Dredge assembly 

All dredges arrived in Vietnam unassembled, and the Vietnamese

Government contracted for the necessary assembly work. Some difficulties and delays were experienced in this operation. The erection contracts as written included no enforceable time limit for completion of the assembly work and hence failed to protect against undue delays in contract performance. These delays required the dredge manufacturers to keep technical supervisory personnel in the field beyond the 90-day services stipulated in the construction contract and caused extra dollar expenses to ICA. 

Such extra expense ran over $5,000 for the services required in the case of the 8-inch dredges alone.

Prior to and during the assembly work, storage conditions were reported to have been unsatisfactory. Equipment was left in the open unprotected against the rain and became damaged. During this period, the Mission lacked personnel to exercise proper supervision.

At the time of our field visit in October 1956, we found that all four dredges were in operation. 

Dredging operations

The Government of Vietnam contracted with a French firm for initial dredging operations and training of Vietnamese dredging crews. The Mission was of the opinion that the training was not sufficiently thorough and, as a result, the dredges were not operating at satisfactory capacity. Nevertheless, work of substantial benefit already has been accomplished. The channel has been dredged at the Port of Saigon to allow greater freedom of movement for shipping, and, in conjunction with this, a large area within the neighboring city of Cholon, formerly a health hazard, has been reclaimed by filling. Also, existing canals have been cleared and land has been reclaimed in conjunction with resettlement activities of the refugee program. 

According to the Mission, a major problem, besides adequate training of local personnel, has been to persuade the Vietnamese authorities to assume financial responsibility for dredging operations. The ultimate plan was that, once the training of crews is completed and the waterways are in good usable condition, Vietnam would gradually maintain the system without United States aid. 

The Mission subsequently reported progress in this direction.

ADMINISTRATIVE AND FINANCIAL PROCEDURES 

Following are our comments on the adequacy of certain significant administrative and financial procedures.

Housing for Mission personnel 

A substantial part of technical and administrative support funds has been used to provide housing for ICA Mission personnel. 

ICA has the option of providing its employees with Governmentfurnished housing or paying them a specified housing allowance. 

In Vietnam, because of the inflated costs of suitable quarters which would have exceeded the authorized allowances, ICA undertook to provide Government-furnished quarters. 

The unrealistic exchange rate used in converting dollars into plasters, and competition between various United States Government agencies seeking to acquire suitable properties, has tended to increase over-all housing costs. Using trust funds advanced to it by the Vietnamese Government from the counterpart account, the Mission purchased in the name of the Government of Vietnam 9 individual houses and 4 apartment buildings. The Mission also leased some 97 other residential units. The cost of the purchased houses ranged from the equivalent of $21,000 to $85,000 each; the purchased apartments cost from $67,000 for a 3-unit apartment property to $480,000 for a 25-unit property. In April 1956 the various United States agencies operating in Vietnam (ICA, Department of State, Department of Defense, and the Information Service) entered into an agreement to provide the most suitable means of getting housing for American personnel at a reasonable cost to the United States Government. However, the agreement did not set up joint administrative arrangements, and each of the respective agencies continued to maintain its own personnel and records to administer leased properties. 

We believe that ICA and the other agencies should give further consideration to the establishment of joint administrative arrangements, together with the pooling of other support functions discussed below.

Pooling of support functions The Mission pays for certain support services rendered by the United States Embassy in Vietnam. Charges for this "embassy support," which include both dollar and piaster costs, totaled 90

$93,in 1955 and $114,280 in 1956. For fiscal year 1957 a total of $163,000 was agreed on between ICA and the Department of 

State. 

The services rendered by the Embassy include hiring and classification of locally employed personnel, disbursing functions, security arrangements, communications including cable and pouch service, customs clearance, and health services.

In addition, there are a number of other services common to the two agencies which were handled separately. Among these are the preparation of payrolls and vouchers, purchasing and control of administrative supplies and equipment, building maintenance, maintenance of a motor pool, and travel arrangements. General ICA policy in regard to embassy support has been to integrate common functions to the maximum extent practicable. The Mission in Vietnam stated that, because of the Embassy's relatively smaller size, the Mission had to provide its own support until the Embassy was more adequately equipped to handle support for both organizations. 

We believe that the problem of pooling support functions in Vietnam is not confined to the Embassy and the Mission. Other 

United States offices operating in Vietnam, such as the Information Service and the Military Assistance Advisory Group, require similar administrative services, for example, residential housing. 

We suggested that these various agencies study the possibility for pooling those common support functions which would result in more efficient operation and over-all economies to the United States Government.

In response to our suggestion, ICA advised that the integration of administrative service functions has been the subject of special studies and is under continuing review. To date, it has been the considered opinion of the Mission and the Embassy that further integration of services was not practicable, especially considering the different character of operations of each agency and their dispersed locations.

Based on our observations in Vietnam and other countries receiving ICA assistance, we believe that further integration of services is practicable and that the Mission and the other United States agencies in Vietnam should exert a determined effort to overcome obstacles in the way of integrated and more economical operations. 91

Financial reporting
