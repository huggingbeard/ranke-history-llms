ELECTRIC UTILITY INDUSTRY Service and Facilities (Excluding Alaska and Hawaii) 

The electric industry continued its record-making growth in 1961, advancing to new levels of power-producing capability. Although the 

American economy followed a somewhat uneven path, residential and industrial utilization of electricity resulted in new records of service to the nation's life and economy.

At the close of 1961, the electric industry was serving more than 59.9 million customers, which was approximately 1.3 million more than in 1960. Today, more than 98 per cent of the nation's occupied urban and rural homes are connected for electric service. 

Electricity's contribution to better living continues to gain wide acceptance. The steady increase in the use of electricity in the home reflects the utilization of an ever-expanding range of electrical appliances and increased, widespread popularity of home heating and air conditioning. 

In 1961 the average annual use per residential customer was 4,016 kilowatt-hours, an increase of 165 kilowatt-hours over the 1960 average use. During the past ten years, average annual use per residential customer has increased 1,955 kilowatt-hours, which increase is more than the total annual usage per residential customer during the year 1950. 

As a result of increased consumption of electricity and continued improvements in operating efficiency, the industry has been consistently able to reduce the cost per kilowatt-hour to the customer. Average revenue per kilowatt-hour sold to residential customers during 1961 was 2.45¢, as compared to 2.47¢ in 1960 and 2.88¢ in 1950. This achievement has been realized despite the general increase in the cost of living. 

During the year 1961, the industry generated a total of 792 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity. This was an increase of 5 per cent over the amount generated in 1960. Steam accounted for 80.26 per cent of the total production, hydro generation 19.17 per cent, and internal combustion represented the balance of 0.57 per cent. Coal continued to be the most important fuel in thermal generation, accounting for 65.9 per cent of the total, while gas for fuel accounted for 26.4 per cent, fuel oil for 

7.4 per cent and nuclear for the remaining 0.3 per cent.

Following closely the trend of the postwar period, the year 1961 was one of growth and progress for the electric utility industry. Electricity has become more and more important to our rising standard of living, whether it is in the home, the farm, or industry. Needless to say, electricity also has become increasingly vital in our national defense. During the past nine years, the electric utility industry has more than doubled its sales from 343 billion kilowatt-hours in 1952 to 719 billion in 1961.

Today, the electric utility is actively occupied in planning and building for this record surge of national growth.

Customers, sales and revenues of the gas utility and pipeline industry rose to record levels in 1961. An average of 33.8 million customers received gas service during the year, an increment of almost 800,000 customers over the total served in the preceding year. Sales of gas to these consumers during 1961 aggregated 95.9 billion therms a gain of 3.2 per cent over the 92.9 billion therms sold in 1960. New discoveries of natural gas in 1961 were the third highest on record, bringing proved recoverable reserves in the United States to a new high of 267.7 trillion cubic feet as of the end of the year. Despite a record production of 13.5 trillion cubic feet, total additions to proved recoverable reserves resulted in a net gain of 4.0 trillion cubic feet over the preceding year.

Underground storage facilities were in operation in 21 states during 1961, with the ultimate capacity of these facilities increasng to 3,121 billion cubic feet by year end. Total non-coincident maximum day natural gas storage output of 12.48 billion cubic feet accounted for nearly one-fourth of the peak day requirements of the gas utility industry.

Over 28 thousand miles of main were added during the year, bringing the national total to 659.0 thousand miles as of the end of the year. Of this total, 191.8 thousand miles represented transmission mains, 410.4 thousand miles distribution mains and 56.7 thousand miles field and gathering mains. 

Of the 31.1 million residential customers served during 1961, 21.8 million, or 70.2 per cent were gas heating customers. Very modest gains were recorded in shipments of domestic gas appliances, reflecting the moderate recovery in business activity and residential construction. 

Shipments of gas-fired automatic water heaters during 1961 totaled 2,672 thousand units, an increase of less than one per cent over the preceding year. Gas range shipments of 1,830 thousand units were 0.9 per cent greater than in 1960. Gas central heating equipment shipments, aggregating 1,200 thousand units, were up 2.3 per cent from the year before. Accident Prevention in the Gas Industry 

The gas utility and pipeline industry of the United States in 1961 continued for the fourteenth straight year to reduce its frequency rate from the 1947 post-war peak of 21.86 injuries per million man-hours

worked, to its lowest level since the American Gas Association began to compile accident statistics in 1929. Reports from 581 gas utilities and pipelines, which represent 92.5 per cent of the industry's employees, indicate a frequency rate of 6.57. This is a 6.3 per cent decrease from the 

1960 rate of 7.01.

There were 480 days chargeable to disabling injuries per million manhours worked during 1961, as compared to 563 days charged in 1960.

This country has a tremendous asset for the protection of its vital communications in its multiplicity of telephone routes. As a further measure, there is now under construction a new cross-country buried coaxial cable especially hardened against blast. The eastern third of this blast-resistant transcontinental cable system was completed late in the summer of 1962 as a part of a continuing program to insure the survivability of communications. 

All the cable, amplifier stations and communications centers are being constructed underground, engineered to withstand any nuclear blast short of a direct hit. The system will be completed to the West Coast by 1964.

The buried cross-continent system will transmit telephone calls, data and other communications and will interconnect with existing microwave and coaxial express routes. These criss-cross the nation and include by-pass routes around potential target areas. The under-ground system, which also avoids large cities and major target areas, will represent a Bell System investment of about $200 million when completed. 

The subterranean complex will include almost 4,000 miles of coaxial cables, more than 900 intermediate amplifier stations and nine communications centers linking the new route to conventional facilities. The cable is being buried deep underground, and all amplifiers and terminal equipment are housed in reinforced concrete buildings protected by thick earth cover. Manned stations are protected from radio-active fallout.

The communications centers, buried one to two stories deep, have reinforced concrete floors, ceilings and outer walls 18 to 24 inches thick. In emergencies, each center can generate its own power and has reserve water and fuel supplies. Food is stockpiled, and there are emergency living facilities for operators, technicians, and maintenance men. Each center's ventilation system is controlled by a sensing device which would react immediately to nuclear blast. Blast valves in the underground buildings would close automatically, filtering equipment would cut in to prevent fallout from entering, and other equipment would constantly check radioactivity. 

The only part of each building above ground is a small entrance structure. Personnel enter through a guarded, two-door "entry lock" and pass through a fallout decontamination room before reaching the operations areas.

When fully equipped, the new transcontinental system will add about

9,000 telephone circuits to the 15,000 now spanning the country.

TELSTAR 

TELSTAR, the Bell System's experimental communications satellite, was launched from Cape Canaveral by the National Aeronautics and Space Agency on July 10, 1962. The success of this 170 pound sphere, filled with wires, transistors, and American ingenuity, opens a new era in communications that is probably even more significant than the installation of the first trans-Atlantic cable in 1866. It represents another first for America, and every effort should be made to press on rapidly with this opportunity, which the United States has for demonstrating leadership in the first practical use of outer space on a large scale.

TELSTAR, developed by the Bell Telephone Laboratories, and launched by NASA at the expense of the Bell System, has proved beyond a doubt the practicability of using artificial satellites for providing high capacity telephone, teletypewriter, data and television channels over transoceanic routes. In its first few hours of orbit, it provided the means for transmitting the first live trans-Atlantic television programs in history. Subsequently, voice, teletypewriter, facsimile, and other data communications have been transmitted across the Atlantic with eminent success.

In accordance with previously announced policy, TELSTAR'S transmission facilities have been made available to all of our country's international communications common carriers for experimental purposes at no cost. In addition, the Bell System has agreed to make available to the Government, the scientific knowledge which will be derived from the TELSTAR experiment. Data are now being collected for a full-scale communications satellite system to augment present underseas cables and overseas radio systems. 

There is no doubt that TELSTAR is a major technological achievement. Moreover, its greatest significance lies in its dramatic evidence to all the world that America's free enterprise system has produced benefits for mankind unparalleled by any other system throughout history. TELSTAR has demonstrated that America's private industry, given the opportunity to express its talents and initiative, can meet the challenges of today's complex world as it has in the past.

Safety 

The communications industry in 1961 continued with a lost-time work injury rate well below other industries. The all-industry rate, of those reporting to the National Safety Council, was 5.99 injuries per million work hours. The rates for individual industries ranged from a high of 35.86 to a low of .93 for communications. The rate for Bell 

Telephone Companies was .67.

For the seventh time since 1951, the Bell Telephone Companies in 

1961 qualified for the Council's highest award, the "Award of Honor."

Motor vehicle accidents continue to be a major cause of disabling injuries. In an effort to reduce these accidents, a film, "Anatomy of an 

Accident," was released by the Bell System during 1961. The film illustrates vital principles of safe driving. It shows how ignoring them contributed to a fatal accident involving a man and his family. The safety message is brought out through psychological impact rather than horror scenes. The film has been shown widely to employees and other groups. It has been made available to any groups that care to use it. 

The National Committee on Films for Safety named "Anatomy of an Accident" as one of the best safety films of 1961. It has earned a bronze plaque which will be awarded at the 1962 Safety Congress. 

Off-duty injuries are of major concern. The 1961 Bell System offduty injury rate was 8.81 injuries per million exposure hours, compared to 9.13 for 1960. Additional emphasis has been placed in safety programs to include the reduction of off-duty accidents. Slips, falls, and motor vehicle accidents contribute most to the off-the-job injury rate.

Projects such as the above illustrate how the water utility industry is meeting its principal challenges. The industry which now serves 140 million people in 22,000 communities through 18,500 utilities must catch up on deficiencies in water utility plant facilities in addition to providing water for the nation's rapidly expanding population. It has added challenges relating to standards of water quality, water resources, and water pollution. Also, it has a growing need for research in many areas.

The water industry has a sizeable backlog of construction, as indicated by the results of a survey made in 1955 by the United States Public 

Health Service as follows: 

1. One of five water utilities is deficient in supply. 

2. Two of five are deficient in transmission capacity. 

3. One of three is deficient in pumping capacity. 

4. Two of five are deficient in treatment capacity. 

5. Ground and elevated distribution storages show deficiencies of approximately 30 and 45 per cent, respectively. 

6. Distribution system improvements are required in roughly 60 per cent of the water systems. 

It is estimated that the United States population will grow from today's figure of 180 million to 250 million by 1980. Most of this increase will be dependent on public water systems because of the trend in metropolitan growth. Moreover, the future development of new sources of water will present added problems. More utilities will find that nearby, available sources of fresh water are no longer adequate to supply increased populations. It will be necessary to transport water longer distances or to rely on treatment of nearer, relatively undesirable sources.

In addition to correcting current plant deficiencies and seeking new sources of water supply, the water industry needs higher standards for the product it delivers to consumers. The industry has established an outstanding record for safety of water. Now it must concentrate on

producing a "quality water"; that is, one that is free of taste and odor, is free of color and turbidity, is soft, and does not possess staining properties.

It is estimated that it will cost $5 billion to correct deficiencies in supply, transmission, pumping, treatment, storage, and distribution. 

The cost to provide water supply facilities to serve the population increase to 1980 is estimated at $16.2 billion. The facilities needed to produce "quality water" will cost another estimated $1.9 billion.

There continued to be great concern with the nation's water resources during 1961. This subject received extensive coverage by press and other communications media. The findings of the Senate Select Committee on National Water Resources, which were published in a report released in 1961, should have far-reaching effects. In the Congress, numerous bills were introduced relating to all aspects of water resources. 

One bill which received considerable attention was the Administration's Water Resources Planning Bill, S 2246. This bill is still in Committee; however, it is expected to be reintroduced in the next session in a modified form as the result of hearings and discussions. 

Late in 1960, a National Conference on Water Pollution was held in Washington, D. C., to focus attention on Pollution of surface and ground water supplies. The water utility industry is directly involved because many water supplies are taken from sources which receive waste discharges. Water pollution is increasing as waste discharges from new population and industry are added to those already present. In 1961, the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, to provide a more effective program of water pollution control, was significantly expanded. 

Research and investigation in areas of importance to the industry continued to expand in 1961; however, much more needs to be done so the industry can effectively meet future problems. Support for water research is expected to rise significantly in the next decade. One aspect of water research which received considerable attention was the work carried forth by the Office of Saline Water of the Department of the Interior. This office proceeded with construction of saline water conversion demonstration plants as well as in pilot plant and research studies. 

Public Law 87-295, approved by President Kennedy in 1961, authorized $75 million for research and development activities of this Office during fiscal years 1962-1967, inclusive. The United States Congress is surveying current water resources and research needs, and a major bill covering water resources research is expected to be introduced in the next session of Congress.

The water utility industry has established a notable record over the past fifty years for the safety of public water supplies. It is facing new problems involving the increasing water demands of a growing population, the need for higher standards for water quality, and water pollution. Careful planning and enlistment of public support will be essential to success.

Safety 

A valuable method of on-the-job safety training is illustrated by the "tailgate safety session" for employees of the San Dimas-Charter Oak Domestic Water Company, California.

The American Water Works Association continues its efforts to make the water industry "safety conscious" and thus reduce the number of injuries in the industry. The Association has an active committee on safety practices; publishes safety articles in its JOURNAL and 

Monthly Bulletin; has prepared a manual entitled, "Safety Practice for

Water Utilities;" and has developed an award program for local sections and on the national level. Statistics compiled on its award program since its inception in 1957 show a remarkable increase in participation by water utilities and indicate an increased awareness of safety by the industry. In 1961, some 1,200 water utilities received awards based on outstanding safety records or notable progress in injury reduction. 

In 1960, the Bureau of Labor Statistics of the United States Depart-

ment of Labor released Report No. 166, "Injuries and Accident Causes in Water Supply Utilities." This report presents a detailed analysis and summary of data collected from 98 water utilities in cities of more than 100,000 population. Information was compiled on 1,723 disabling injuries which occurred in 1953. The injuries included nine fatalities, 35 permanent partial disabilities, and 1,679 temporary total disabilities. 

The AWWA Safety Practices Committee is expanding its efforts to stimulate more water utilities to develop active safety programs. It hopes to stimulate utilities through its statistics and awards programs, by safety schools and clinics, and by other efforts in cooperation with the National Safety Council. Although much progress has been made by the industry, much remains to be done. The industry's frequency rate in 1961 was 21 lost-time injuries per million man-hours worked. 

Reduction of this poses a big challenge; however, outstanding records which have been established by many individual water utilities demonstrate that safety can be achieved.
