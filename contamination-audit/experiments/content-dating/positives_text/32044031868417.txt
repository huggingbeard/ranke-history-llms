Foreword

The mission assigned by the Commission was the preparation of a report which would analyze, primarily from an independent engineer's point of view, certain activities of the Executive Branch of the Federal Government in the fields of power, flood control, and irrigation, placing particular emphasis upon the so-called multiple-purpose projects. In fulfilling this mission, careful consideration has been given to the detailed operations of 18 major Federal multiple-purpose projects, which are now in operation, but on which all construction has not been completed. In addition, brief surveys have been made of 27 other Federal projects now under construction and of all major, long-range programs announced to date. Altogether these authorized and projected construction programs envision an eventual 671 major power, flood control, and irrigation projects within the continental 

United States.¹

It is estimated that this ultimate program for multiple-purpose projects will cost in the neighborhood of 40 billion dollars.2 

This report represents an attempt to review and evaluate these programs and to recommend procedures by which such programs should be governed in the future. In the analysis of Federal power, flood control, and irrigation activities, the following matters have been given primary attention: 

1. The size and scope of the Federal multiple-purpose projects already constructed, now authorized by Congress, or planned for the future by the executive departments and agencies; and the implications of these programs for the citizens of the nation (ch. 1). 

2. A review and evaluation of the criteria presently used by various Federal departments and agencies in determining the economic justification for multiplepurpose projects and a brief discussion of alternative criteria which, it is believed, would be more effective in determining the economic justification of such projects (ch. II). 

3. A very brief discussion of certain specialized problems encountered by the 

Federal Government in connection with hydrology, soil conservation, and national defense and their importance in relation to multiple-purpose projects (ch. III). 

4. A consideration of certain elements involved in the relationship between public and private power; of the competitive value of Federal power; and of the fact that business enterprises of the Federal Government are only to a limited extent subject to taxation (ch. IV).

Chapter I SIZE AND SCOPE OF FEDERAL POWER, FLOOD CONTROL, AND IRRIGATION PROJECTS 1. GENERAL 

Direct expenditures for construction of multiple-purpose projects are now a substantial item in our peacetime Federal budgets, amounting to approximately 875 million dollars for the fiscal year 1949.¹ 

And giving consideration to present programs authorized or planned by the Federal Government, expenditures for this purpose can be expected to rise greatly in the future, as is shown at length in chapter VI hereof. There is, for this reason, an evident need for a careful review of past accomplishments and for a searching consideration of future plans. Only by doing this can the citizens of the Nation place in proper perspective these vast programs; and only by doing this can they determine the extent to which, and the procedures under which, they will support such programs in the future. 

The two largest agencies involved in such programs are the Corps of Engineers, Department of the Army, and the Bureau of Reclamation, Department of the Interior. Three other major agencies are also active in the field: The Tennessee Valley Authority, the Bonneville Power Administration, and the Southwestern Power Administration.

Eighty-five projects are being planned by the Corps of Engineers. 

The principal projects of this agency are planned primarily for flood control, but also include navigation, power, and other features, although not usually irrigation features. These projects are located in 32 States.2 3 The Bureau of Reclamation is planning 541 projects, all of which are, by law, located in the 17 States wholly or partially west of the ninety-seventh meridian. By statute the main emphasis of the Bureau of Reclamation was originally placed on irrigation, although the generation of electric power has become more and more an important feature in its projects and might now be considered almost the primary interest of this agency.*

Multiple-purpose projects are not only found in the "arid or semiarid west." They are also found in many other States as is shown by a list of 38 States in which the Bureau of Reclamation, Department of the Interior, and Corps of Engineers, Department of the Army, are doing or planning construction work on multiple-purpose projects.5

For an example of this tendency to "snowball," one can consider a typical case, that of the Garrison Dam Project on the Missouri River in North Dakota. 4. PROJECTED PHYSICAL INCREASES IN FEDERALLY OWNED ELEC- TRICAL GENERATING CAPACITY 

A careful study of present plans indicates a major growth in the physical volume of federally owned electrical generating capacity. 

At the end of 1947, the installed electrical generating capacity of power installations owned by the Federal Government was reported at 4,908,700 kilowatts, or a total of 9 percent of the total electrical generating capacity in the Continental United States. This reported federal capacity compared with a reported 5.2 million kilowatts of 

State and municipally owned capacity (10 percent) and 41.5 million kilowatts of privately owned capacity (81 percent); giving a total reported capacity from the three sources of 51.6 million kilowatts (100 percent).¹ 12 The present programs of the executive departments and agencies of the Federal Government contemplate an ultimate installed electrical generation capacity of 45,968,900 kilowatts which would be owned and operated by the Federal Government.13 Thus, the federal programs, if authorized and completed, would increase the installed 1947 capacity by 41,060,200 kilowatts, approximately equaling the total privately owned electrical generating capacity constructed in the United States from 1882, the year in which electric power was first distributed commercially, until the present day.¹4

5. CONFUSION IN MULTIPLE-PURPOSE PROJECTS 

The Corps of Engineers has been charged by law with primary responsibility for flood control. The multiple-purpose projects being constructed under its jurisdiction are generally authorized under flood control acts, and the primary emphasis of such construction is upon flood control features. The Flood Control Act of 1936, section 1, has the following declaration of policy relating to the work of the 

Corps of Engineers: 

It is hereby recognized that destructive floods upon the rivers of the United States, upsetting orderly processes and causing loss of life and property, including the erosion of lands, and impairing and obstructing navigation, highways, railroads, and other channels of commerce between the States, constitute a menace to national welfare; that it is the sense of Congress that flood control on navigable waters or their tributaries is a proper activity of the Federal Government in cooperation with States, their political subdivisions, and localities thereof; that investigations and improvements of rivers and other waterways, including watersheds thereof, for flood-control purposes are in the interest of the general welfare; that the Federal Government should improve or participate in the improvement of navigable waters or their tributaries, including watersheds thereof, for floodcontrol purposes if the benefits to whomsoever they may accrue are in excess of the estimated costs, and if the lives and social security of people are otherwise adversely affected.

However, the Corps of Engineers flood control projects frequently have made provision for most of the other features implicit in multiple-purpose projects with the frequent exception of irrigation.22 

The Bureau of Reclamation, the Federal Government's other large construction agency, is charged by law with a primary interest in irrigation and in the generation of electric energy. To only a minor extent does it place emphasis upon flood control. 

Thus, there is a lack of uniformity in the emphasis placed by law upon the flood control activities of the two largest construction agencies in these fields. For one, flood control is of major importance; in the case of the other, irrigation and power receive major emphasis. 

This leads to a difference in construction and management practices. 

Basically the control of floods is best effected by having empty reservoirs available for the storage of unwonted water flows. On the other hand, adequate provision for irrigation or power calls for maximum storage of water if these requirements are to be met. In theory, the

Corps of Engineers should have as its objective empty reservoirs; and the Bureau of Reclamation should have its reservoirs full. 

The inclusion in any one project of diverse features such as power, irrigation, navigation, and flood control must inevitably lead to conflict in administrative objectives and in the management of the individual features. This confusion is well illustrated by the history of the Tennessee Valley Authority. More important, perhaps, than this confusion is the resultant confusion in the public mind as to the major purpose and objectives of a project. 

6. FLOOD CONTROL 

a. Background.-The Federal Government's jurisdiction over river and harbor development and the control of floods stems from the commerce clause of the Constitution which awards to Congress the power "to regulate commerce with foreign nations and among the several States, and with the Indian tribes." Implemented by the decision of Chief Justice Marshall (Gibbons v. Ogden, 1824) which ruled that everything to do with waterways from the lifting of a snag on up was a subject of concern to the Federal Government, this power was first used broadly in authorizing the Corps of Engineers to clear a navigable channel in the Mississippi and Ohio Rivers (Act of 

May 24, 1824).

The Rivers and Harbors Act of January 21, 1927, and the Flood 

Control Act of May 15, 1928, first recognized the need for a comprehensive study of the Nation's water resources to the end that a Nationwide policy might be developed. These measures, recognizing the interrelationship of water control functions, required that the country's needs for flood control, navigation, irrigation, and power be determined. 

The surveys thus authorized have been the basis for development work by the Corps of Engineers ever since.

The Tennessee Valley Authority Act of 1933 provided for flood control of the Tennessee River. 

The Flood Control Act of December 22, 1944, turned over all generated hydroelectric power to the Department of the Interior for marketing. b. Lack of Uniformity in Flood Control Data.-There is a serious lack of uniformity in the information available upon flood control aspects of multiple-purpose Federal dams.

The pertinent data obtained from the Army Corps of Engineers are very complete in their tabulation of reservoir volumes in acre-feet provided for flood control, for power, for inactive storage, and for totals.23 On the other hand, the Project Data Book of December 

1945, prepared by the Bureau of Reclamation, makes no reference to the storage space provided for flood control. It does, however, list inactive storage below a given elevation and active storage above the inactive or dead-storage space which gives some indication of flood 10

control aspects. The Tennessee Valley Authority, in spite of the wording of its basic statute, does not even mention flood control storage in its reports on total storage. Consistency in the presentation of flood control information by all agencies would be appropriate; for, otherwise, intelligent evaluation of flood control aspects of projects by the Congress is rendered extremely difficult. 

c. Lack of Uniformity in Principles of Flood Regulation.—There is also a lack of uniformity among Federal agencies in the principles of designing multiple-purpose dams to provide for the flood control feature. These practices might well be subjected to more use of uniform principles of design.

A review of the project reports of the Corps of Engineers indicates that this agency proceeds somewhat as follows: 

1. The height is determined by the terrain. 

2. The total storage is determined by the terrain. 

3. The flood control space is determined from a study of past floods and estimates of the probable maximum flood to be expected. The volume of water determines the flood control space, the bottom of which then becomes the top of the conservation pool. 

4. Data are obtained from the manufacturers of water turbines on the range in operating heads for various designs of water wheel. From this range the bottom of the conservation pool can be determined. 

5. The space below the conservation pool (dead storage) and the adequacy of the space remaining is checked wtih the silting rate of the river at that point. 

6. Studies are then made of the various potential uses of water stored in the conservation pool for the following purposes: (a) power, (b) irrigation, (c) domestic or industrial water supply, and (d) navigation needs. 

The design data in the Bureau of Reclamation Data Book of 1945 indicates that the Bureau of Reclamation, if it does take the basic steps necessary for adequate flood control, does not so indicate it in its published documents.24 It is believed that a similarity of approach in this matter would be in the public interest, particularly in view of the disasterous floods last spring in the Columbia River Basin. 

There also appears to be lack of uniformity in the methods of regulating river flow for flood control purposes. This matter is accentuated by the division of authority among several agencies and might well be subjected to more centralization and uniformity of management practices. d. Projects not Emphasizing Flood Control.—In general, the projects being constructed by the Army Corps of Engineers are principally for flood control with provisions for the ultimate installation of electrical generating equipment. Yet, even in certain of the projects of the 

Army Corps of Engineers, flood control does not appear to be the major consideration.25 Bonneville Dam and McNary Dam are, for example, authorized under Rivers and Harbors Acts, although more generally multiple-purpose projects are authorized under flood control acts.26 An example of the lack of emphasis upon flood control is the 11

7. IRRIGATION 

In order to develop the arid West so that its fast-growing population could be supported, Congress passed the Reclamation Act of June 17, 1902, which provided for a survey of potential developments and construction of irrigation works. Appropriated for this purpose were receipts from the sale of public lands in what are now the 17 Western 

States.30 The Secretary of the Interior then established the Reclama tion Service which later became the Bureau of Reclamation. In 1906 

Congress provided that, whenever a power development was necessary for the irrigation of land, the Secretary of the Interior could lease for a period of not more than 10 years, any surplus power or power privilege. The reclamation policy was broadened and advanced when the 

Congress approved the Boulder Canyon Project Act in 1928. This measure expanded the concepts of reclamation to include irrigation, flood control, navigation, power, and other features. 

The estimated ultimate cost of programs planned (table III, p. 6), amounting to over 40 billion dollars, includes projects for irrigation and power involving the ultimate irrigation of 19.8 million acres. 31

As of December 31, 1945, 4.2 million acres had already been irrigated by the Bureau of Reclamation.32

It was originally contemplated in some statutes that the irrigation activities in connection with multiple-purpose dams would be selfsustaining and self-liquidating. To be self-sustaining, the revenue from water sales must be at least equal to the sum of the costs properly chargeable against such revenue. To be self-liquidating, the revenue must be sufficient not only to cover the costs, but also to afford repayment of its capital. Chapter II ECONOMIC JUSTIFICATION OF MULTIPLE- PURPOSE PROJECTS 

The economic justification of individual multiple-purpose projects is the keystone upon which will be built the entire future power, flood control and irrigation program of the Federal Government. Unless a project is economically justified, there is no point in building it. 

Money spent on uneconomic projects is "money down the rat hole." 

In view of the huge sums of money ultimately to be involved in these programs, the people of the nation cannot afford to be careless in authorizing unjustifiable construction projects. 

In this chapter, consideration is given to the two major facets of this problem: 

1. Allocations of construction costs to features of a project. These ultimately determine the amount of money the Government can expect to recover on its investment and the net cost to the taxpayers. 

2. Cost-benefit ratios. These indicate the net benefits expected to be realized by the nation as a whole from the construction of the project, whatever may be the relationship between annual revenues and annual costs.

They represent two of the most complex problems faced by the Federal Government. This chapter is an attempt to analyze them and to make recommendations, so that they may be administered more effectively in the future. Only by clearly defining national policies with respect to economic justification of new projects, can the public be assured that their funds will be properly expended in the future.

A. Allocations of Construction Costs 1. GENERAL 

Allocations of construction costs, as made by Federal agencies, cannot be precisely paralleled by any activity in private business. In private business, all facilities are assumed to be productive of revenues; otherwise they would either be discarded or they might eventually lead to bankruptcy. 817284°-494 15

This is not true of all business enterprises of the Federal Government. For instance, with respect to multiple-purpose projects, only some of the facilities or features are productive of direct revenues comparable with those of private business enterprises. Others of the facilities or features are productive of no direct revenues whatsoever; nor was it ever intended that they should produce such revenues. 

The latter facilities are built on the grounds that, although they produce no direct revenues, they do increase national wealth or productivity and hence warrant construction. 

Allocations of construction costs are an attempt to determine which facilities of a project have given rise to which construction costs. 

They are made in order to determine the amounts which are repayable to the Federal Government. This is made enormously difficult by the fact that many, if not most, federal projects are multiple purpose. 

2. THE MULTIPLE-PURPOSE ASPECT AND ITS IMPLICATIONS 

The term "multiple-purpose project" means that large dams with their appurtenances are built for various or multiple purposes, including control of floods; generation of electric energy; sale of water for irrigation and other uses; improvement of navigation; improvement of recreational facilities, etc. Some of the features, primarily those built for power and irrigation, are productive of substantial revenues; and others, such as flood control and navigation, are not. 

Some Federal multiple-purpose projects make certain substantial contributions to the nation by increasing productivity or wealth through features the effects of which cannot be translated directly into revenues as in the case of damage prevented by flood control. Therefore, the theory has been widely promoted that only those features producing substantial revenues should be required to carry their own weight financially; and, thus, the nonrevenue producing features should be built and, in some cases supported, by subsidies from the public treasury.

The basic confusion inherent in the multiple-purpose pattern is illustrated by the following quotation from page 7 of a booklet entitled "A National Water Power Policy," published by the Isaac 

Walton League of America in July 1945: 

Multiple-Purpose Dams. This term has been conveniently adopted by the promoters of large river developments as the economics of hydro power and irrigation developments become less and less favorable at the left-over sites. 

The theory is that the large dam constructed, we will say, primarily for irrigation, will be of considerable benefit for hydro power, navigation and flood control so that under the multiple-purpose title sizeable percentages of the costs are charged off to these other three purposes, materially reducing the cost left to be charged against the irrigation project. Since no cash return to the Treasury is contemplated in Government expenditures for navigation and flood control, this furnishes a very convenient method of bookkeeping, making it possible to justify on paper an irrigation or a power project that could not stand on its own economic feet. 16

3. ALLOCATION OF CONSTRUCTION COSTS TO VARIOUS FEATURES 

Since Federal multiple-purpose projects have so many different features, it is considered necessary by various Federal agencies to allocate a project's construction costs to the various features in order to determine the value relationships as between features and the estimated costs of each feature. This allocation of construction costs among various features is assumed to be important; for, by doing so, it is thought possible to determine the costs for each portion of the project and to isolate those costs which must be met from various sources of funds.

After allocating construction costs to the various features, the specific and joint costs allocable to each of such features are then broken down into two major groupings, reimbursable costs and nonreimbursable costs. The reimbursable costs of construction are primarily those costs allocable to power and a portion of the costs allocable to irrigation. Costs allocable to the other features are considered to be nonreimbursable. 

The methods used by various construction agencies in computing the allocations of construction costs vary considerably. Among the theories which have been advanced are the following:¹ a. Allocation in proportion to benefits. b. Alternative expenditure method. c. Allocations based upon vendibility. d. Allocations based upon assessability. e. Proportionate use of capacity. f. Separate projects method. g. Residual value method. h. Equal distribution method.

The sharp divergencies inherent in different methods of allocating construction costs can be illustrated by the following extracts from the 

1939 Hearings on the Tennessee Valley Authority (Report of the Joint Committee Investigating the Tennessee Valley Authority, S. 

Doc. 56, pt. 1, April 1939, p. 154):

The testimony given before the committee revealed a wide divergence of opinion on allocation theories, which may be summarized below:

The incremental power investment theory was advanced by Dean Moreland in his testimony as a witness for the Commonwealth & Southern Corp. Under this theory, power is charged with the total investment balance after deducting the estimated cost of the most economical alternative plan for accomplishing the other purposes.

In a system devoted to several different purposes, such as the Authority, the cost assigned to each purpose must necessarily be to a large degree a matter of opinion. The problem is similar to that which would confront an architect if he were asked to allocate the cost of a house among its various uses. The best allocation is that which appears to be the most reasonable in terms of any proposed alternative. The difficulties of allocation, which cannot be based on exact scientific considerations, have created confusion and lack of uniformity among engineering and accounting experts, in both public and private fields. This problem is particularly aggravated in the Authority's operations by the magnitude of the common costs.

Proponents of divergent theories, similar to those shown above in the case of TVA, have argued vehemently over these theories for years; and the controversy remains unsolved. The reason for their vehemence can be explained by reverting to the discussion of reimbursable and nonreimbursable costs.

Reimbursable construction costs are those on which the Government anticipates repayment of the capital it provided (frequently with interest) from the revenues of the project. Nonreimbursable construction costs, on the other hand, are costs attributable to those features which produce no revenue and, hence, on which no repayment of the Government's investment is even contemplated. This means that, if the allocations of construction costs to nonreimbursable features like flood control are heavy, the reimbursable features like power and irrigation need not produce such large revenues in order to warrant construction. A corollary of this is the fact that it is sometimes possible to sell electrical energy or water at lower prices, if the allocations to nonreimbursable costs are heavy. 

Given the large number of alternative methods of allocating construction costs, confusion and inconsistencies have been inevitable. 

Among the criticisms which can be made of the present situation are: a. Allocations comparisons between various projects are difficult to make. b. Material presented to Congress regarding allocations is frequently either so inadequate or so technical that judgments are seriously hampered. c. The extent to which a project should be required to carry its weight financially is influenced greatly by an arbitrary decision of the construction agency on cost allocations. 18

To put it more simply: Allocations ultimately determine the amount which will be repaid on the Government's investment. Unless they are reached by a clear, fair and standardized method, Congress and the public will not have adequate information to permit intelligent evaluation of a proposed project. Unless cost allocations are made properly, it is virtually impossible to determine the true economic justification for a project. 

B. The Present Concept of Economic Justification and the Cost-Benefit Relationship 1. GENERAL 

Given the diverse objectives of multiple-purpose projects, it is admittedly difficult to determine whether a specific project is feasible or justifiable from the standpoint of the national economy. Therefore, it has proven necessary for the construction agencies to devise a method whereby the feasibility of a project can be measured. In order to do so, it has become customary, first, to estimate the average annual benefits to which the project would give rise. Against these benefits are matched the average annual costs of the project; and, if such benefits exceed such costs, a determination is made to the effect that the project is economically feasible or justifiable. 

This procedure is susceptible of widespread misinterpretation, because the average annual benefits do not represent cash income and are not available to meet the average annual costs. 

Only power and irrigation (and including the sale of municipal water) are productive of substantial cash income to meet costs and are considered to be reimbursable, and even the costs to be met from these two sources depend on the arbitrary and extremely variable allocation of construction costs described in the previous section of this chapter. If less than a fair proportion of the construction costs is allocated to power and irrigation, then interest and amortization of the power and irrigation investment is unfairly low. In addition, the revenues anticipated to be received from the sale of water for irrigation have been frequently overestimated, so that much of the irrigation revenues may fail to be developed into cash income. The most important problems in attempts to determine the economic justification of a project are: The inexactness of the present method of comparing theoretical benefits, rather than revenues, with estimated costs, and the wide variations inherent in the allocation of construction costs, both throw serious doubt upon the validity of the economic justification of many multiple-purpose projects. 19

2. CURRENT PROCEDURES FOLLOWED BY CONSTRUCTION AGENCIES 

Under present procedures, the department or agency charged with originating multiple-purpose projects prepares the original detailed economic justification for each such project. In some cases, the initial motivation for the project may have arisen within the executive department or agency which requests Congress for study money or for authorization of the project; and in other cases, Congress itself may provide the initial motivation by voting study money and referring the project to an executive department or agency. Later, a statement regarding the economic justification of the project, frequently accompanied by a formal certification of authorization under the Reclamation Act or other legislation, is prepared by the originating department or agency; but the final judgment as to its economic feasibiltiy is that of Congress.2 Historically there has been no independent engineering or economic group in the Executive Branch with final authority to analyze and evalute the material so submitted by the originating executive agency, although sporadic attempts have been made to create such authority. 

3. AVERAGE ANNUAL BENEFITS 

The first step in the determination of the economic justification of a multiple-purpose project is the computation of the average annual benefits. In making this computation, there appears to be some lack of uniformity among Federal agencies, but, in general, the average annual benefits are hypothetical advantages expected to be derived either locally or by the citizens of the Nation as a whole from the project. 

They are expressed in dollars and are considered to be representative of the annual increment to the Nation's wealth arising by reason of the project.

Speaking broadly, the average annual benefits may be computed as follows: 

a. Power benefit is computed by various methods. Sometimes it represents the anticipated revenue from the sale of the power at the "bus bar" at competitive rates. In the case of the Missouri Basin it is computed by estimating the energy to be generated which, stated in kilowatt hours, is then multiplied by 4%½ mills. b. Irrigation benefit represents the increase in the estimated value of the production of the land to be irrigated. c. Flood control benefit represents the savings in property values attributable to the cessation or diminution of floods. d. Navigation benefit is the estimated savings to the national economy from water transportation when compared with the cost of transportation of equivalent quantities at higher rail or road rates. e. Recreation benefit is computed by estimating such items as additional license fees, the value of new vacation sites, etc. 20

f. Fish and wildlife benefit is the increase in the number and value of fish and wildlife in the land and water affected by the project. g. Malaria control, silt control, and salinity control benefits are computed by estimating the increase in national wealth attributable thereto.

Under this procedure, a statement of the average annual benefits is prepared, generally in detail, as a basic consideration in the computation of the economic justification of each project. 

There is a lack of uniformity in the principles followed in computing benefits. An example of this lack of uniformity can be found in the case of irrigation benefits. In some cases, the benefits arising from irrigation are based upon the estimated increase in the gross crop income which it is anticipated will be obtained by the furnishing of the new irrigation supply to the area serviced (e. g., Colorado Basin development); and, on the other hand, the benefits from irrigation may be estimated on the basis of the net crop income (e. g., Gila 

Federal Reclamation project). 

4. AVERAGE ANNUAL COSTS 

Having computed the average annual benefits, the department or agency then estimates the average annual costs of the project after full development is reached. This is the second of the two fundamental steps involved in determining the economic justification of the project, and it is done in order to determine whether this estimated annual cost is less than the average annual benefits expected to result from the operation of the project. 

In computing the estimated annual costs, there again appears to be a lack of uniformity in the practices of the executive departments and agencies, and even among the field offices of some of them. This lack of uniformity has given rise to serious controversy in some areas, including, for example, the two following matters: a. In the computation of operating costs, resplacement accounting is used in most cases by the Bureau of Reclamation and by the Corps of Engineers as well as by the Federal Power Commission, but estimates of replacement provisions differ among the above. The method used by the Bureau of Reclamation is not described in the published reports studied. The Corps of Engineers uses a sinking-fund method based upon a 3 percent interest rate, while the Federal power Commission uses a sinking-fund method with a 2%½ percent interest rate. 

It is interesting to note that the Bonneville Power Administration uses the replacement-accounting method in its repayment studies but uses depreciation accounting in its annual reports of revenues and expenses. b. In the computation of other charges, there is wide diversity in the treatment of interest on the Government's investment, repayment of that investment, and payments in lieu of taxes. 21

Thus, average annual costs, as a factor in determining economic justification, are subject to variations in treatment. Unlike the forecasting of benefits, the forecasting of costs may be subjected readily to firm guiding principles; and it might be wise to inquire whether there should not be uniform methods established for all forecasting of the average costs of multiple-purpose projects. 

5. PRESENT METHOD OF COMPUTING ECONOMIC JUSTIFICATION 

Under most of the procedures currently in use, if the average annual benefits computed as set forth above exceed the estimated average annual costs, the project is assumed to be economically justified. 

In other words, if the ratio of benefits to costs is more than one to one, it is assumed that the people of the Nation will profit from its completion. 

In the preparation of this economic justification, the cash income or revenue to be derived from the project is not the major consideration. 

The ability to meet costs from cash revenue is minimized, and the probability that the project will support its own operations financially is considered a secondary factor. 

For the taxpayer, this means that his contribution is either deemphasized, or virtually impossible to determine. 

6. LACK OF FLEXIBILITY IN COST-BENEFIT RATIO DUE TO CHANGES IN PRICE LEVELS 

In addition to the lack of uniformity and the tendency toward arbitrary opinion found in the computation of average annual benefits, the computations of estimated average annual costs might well be questioned for another reason; because, although the costs are subject to fluctuation in the price cycle, allowances for this factor are rarely made. Construction costs and operating costs have increased materially in recent years, with the result that many of the original estimates, made in 1940 or earlier, are no longer valid.

Present operating costs (August 1948) are almost twice the estimated 1940 operating costs; and the estimated average annual benefits in some cases have not increased comparably (e. g., the power benefits). Therefore, the actual ratio of benefits to costs, when based on 1948 prices, may be less than the orginally projected minimum ratio of one to one. Due to this failure to revise cost-benefit ratios periodically in a flexible manner, some projects which were originally assumed to be economically justified could not be so justified at present price levels.

Treatment of this factor, however, varies among agencies. In the project reports made by the Corps of Engineers, estimates of construction costs include: Provisions for contingencies; the cost of preparation of definite project reports, plans, and specifications; supervision; inspection; and district and division overhead costs. 22

In contrast, Project Data Book, published by the Bureau of Reclamation in 1945, makes no mention of these items. 

There have been criticisms made against this lack of flexibility in handling costs, and more systematic revisions would appear to be required. It would, in addition, appear to be also highly desirable that cost estimates should include all items, including interest during construction, and should be developed according to a set standard. 

Otherwise, Congress and the public will be operating under a major handicap.

Unless power and irrigation revenues are greatly in excess of those indicated in the report, the $741,100,000 of reimbursable costs cannot be repaid.

It might be added that, when the above justification figures were presented to the Congress in 1944, construction costs computed at 1940 prices has already increased 22 percent by the time the figures were presented, and, as of August 1948 construction costs had increased 97 percent. 

8. CRITICISMS OF PRESENT Procedures 

There have been numerous criticisms of the practice of determining economic feasibility on a basin-wide basis rather than on an individual project basis; of the present tendency to use benefits rather than revenues as a basis for determining the feasibility of projects; and of the allocations to various features in individual projects. Pertinent quotations follow: a. The State of California, on February 28, 1947, went on record with the following statement regarding consideration of projects on a basin-wide basis:

There is no justification in existing reclamation law for the consideration of economic feasibility of proposed projects on a basin-wide basis, or by a comparison of estimated benefit and costs. Existing reclamation law requires that each proposed project be considered individually on its own merits and that a showing be made of engineering feasibility and of economic feasibility, based on the sufficiency of water payments and revenues form all sources to meet reimbursable costs and other necessary charges and expenses. All the projects previously authorized and constructed by the United States on the Colorado River system, including large developments such as the Boulder Canyon project, have been considered individually as to engineering feasibility and economic justification on a repayment basis. There appears to be no reason at this time for creating new projects on a different basis (p. 49 of interim report of the Secretary of the Interior, July 1947, on the Colorado River). 

b. Stephen B. Ives, director, Corporation Audits Division, General 

Accounting Office, made the following statement on the treatment of 26

1948, with reference to the Tennessee Valley Authority (p. 448):

The power program is an income producing program * * * However, the navigation and flood control programs are not revenue producing and, consequently, are not income producing. On the basis of our review of the 

Authority's evaluation of the navigation and flood control tangible benefits, the portion of the cost of multiple-use facilities allocated by the Board to the two purposes is not justified. In view of the foregoing and the weight possibly attached to the alternativejustifiable expenditure theory, it is possible that insufficient costs have been allocated to power. Relation Between Benefits and Values.—Benefits and values may be, but are not necessarily, synonomous. As is developed in more detail herein, the real economic value of a component project may be less than the benefits derived therefrom. 

For instance, if, as regards a given multiple-purpose dam or one of its components, the same benefits can be obtained at less cost by some other practical means, the true or real value, as distinguished from the benefits resulting from that multiplepurpose dam or one of its components, can be no greater than the cost of obtaining those benefits by such other means.

In general, other critics have emphasized the fact that even though the revenues from irrigation and power may be substantial, the taxpayers of the Nation, without even realizing it, will be called upon to finance to a large extent the navigation, flood control, recreation, fish and wildlife, silt control and salinity control benefits, whether real or fancied, which may be derived from multiple-projects. It is not the subsidies themselves that are criticized as much as it is the difficulty in estimating them from the inadequate figures presented by the construction departments and agencies. 

C. Possible Criteria in Determining Economic 

1. GENERAL SUGGESTIONS 

Justification Given the numerous criticisms of the current system for justifying projects economically, it becomes evident that more satisfactory criteria must be found. The determination of benefits is far from an exact science and largely a matter of opinion. The important thing 27

would seem to be that Congress and the people be accurately informed as to the total and annual costs to be borne by the taxpayers. To revert to the Missouri River Basin project hereinbefore discussed, the annual costs including interest and amortization are shown at $65,- 413,000 and the indication is that but $25,091,000 of revenues will be available to meet them, which means an annual cost to the taxpayers of $40,322,000. The fundamental question is whether or not the undeterminable benefits from irrigation, flood control, and navigation are worth the out-of-pocket costs to the taxpayers. 

While benefits are not susceptible to accurate determination, revenues and costs and consequent profits or deficits can be more readily forecasted.

Essentially the problem of establishing criteria for determining economic justification comes down to the following matters: a. Standardized forecasting of anticipated average annual costs (including interest and amortization) on both an overall and a feature-by-feature basis for the normal life of the project and the establishment of appropriate accounting and forecasting principles. b. Standardized forecasting of anticipated average annual revenues on both an over-all and a feature-by-feature basis. c. Deduction of anticipated annual costs from anticipated annual revenues on an overall and a feature-by-feature basis in order to determine the possible surplus or, if there is to be a deficit, the amounts which must be equalized or absorbed by the Treasury. d. In the cases where there is an anticipated deficit, standardized computation of the anticipated benefits, broken down by features of the project, so that the Congress and the citizens will be aware of: (1) The benefits anticipated in relation to the over-all costs and in relation to the over-all deficit, (2) the same on a featureby-feature basis, so that the deficit can be traced to the features responsible therefor. e. A proper review within the Executive Branch to insure proper consideration of the economic justification on the following bases: (1) Average annual costs to average annual revenues; (2) average annual benefits to any average annual deficit; (3) the possibility of eliminating certain features or a redesigning of the project to make possible a reduction of the deficit. 

2. NONALLOCATIONS 

There is considerable force to the argument which has been advanced that when a Federal project involves the sale of power in competition with private industry, the project should be treated in a way more comparable to that which would apply to an independent operator (State, municipal, or private) who obtained a license under the Federal Power Act. This act requires a licensee to construct and maintain all navigation facilities at his own expense and to operate the levels of the reservoir in accordance with instructions from the Corps of Engineers. While the act provides for cases in which the 

United States Government might subsidize the construction cost of navigation works, this has been done in relatively few cases. There- 28

fore, such an operator must treat the project on an over-all basis and record revenues from all sources together with expenses of all kinds, without recourse to allocations of investment or annual charges to other than power features.

Some such procedure would seem to make clearer the true costs to the taxpayers of large multiple-purpose projects. 

3. REVENUE BONDS AS A CRITERION OF FEASIBILITY 

Various suggestions have been recently made that revenue bond financing be used to provide funds for the construction of Federal projects which have power or irrigation features; and certain techniques used in the sale of revenue bonds might be adaptable to Federal multiple-purpose projects. This method of financing has been used successfully for a number of municipal and state projects. Various 

State laws covering the issuance of revenue bonds provide a convenient method for determining certain phases of economic feasibility; for they include provisions that the revenues derived from the operation of the system for which the bonds are issued shall be sufficient to provide for operating expenses, depreciation, interest on all bonds and to retire the bonded indebtedness by maturity; and some States require that the interest and debt retirement requirements be covered more than one to one by revenues. 

Revenue bonds imply one of the strictest criteria for determining economic feasibility, for, if revenues are not sufficient to support the project, the bonds could not be sold. However, it is believed that the techniques of revenue bond financing cannot be applied to multiplepurpose Federal projects without modification, because of the features. which, such as flood control, give rise to no direct revenues for the project.

Probably it cannot fairly be suggested that power and irrigation revenues be sufficient to support all features of these Federal projects. 

In fact, were this to be required, it would usually cause power and irrigation rates to be so high that the potential market would disappear. But, on the other hand, it might be reasonable to so set up Federal projects that the power and irrigation features in themselves would be self-supporting and self-liquidating and to finance these features with revenue bonds not guaranteed by the Federal Government. It is believed that this procedure would greatly assist in determining economic justification by subjecting these two important features to the cold glare of the market place. 

4. CONCLUSION 

This report does not imply criticism of the actions of the Congress in attempting to develop the power and water resources of the Nation. 

To the extent that private industry is unable or unwilling to develop our resources, the Government should do it, particularly in the un29

developed areas of the West. But, if the Congress is to do this in a logical manner, procedures must be established whereby Congress will have available the information to make the proper decision. 

The essence of this is the determination of proper procedures for establishing economic justification.

This determination of economic feasibility lies at the heart of all developments of our power and water resources. Unless it is computed in an unbiased manner and according to well-defined standards, the possibilities of wasting public funds will be great indeed. With taxes and prices at their highest levels in peacetime history, the Nation cannot afford to be wasteful; and money spent on a nonfeasible project is money wasted. Given a program of 40 billion dollars, the number of possible opportunities for waste may be very large. 30

3. EFFECTS OF SILTING 

When the silt space in a dam has become filled, the silt begins to pass through the water turbines, resulting in the clogging of downstream channels. The gritty silt tends to increase the wear on the sealing rings and accelerate erosion of turbine blades, both of which result in a reduction of efficiency. The finely divided silt, if spread on farm lands, tends to fill the voids in the soil and form an impervious surface. At times when there is plenty of water, attempts are made to flush this material into the drainage ditches which in turn results in filling the ditches rapidly with excess material. 

The alarming siltation rates shown above indicate the inherent difficulties of this problem; and siltation should be a consideration in deciding upon the feasibility of a project. 32

1. GENERAL B. Soil Conservation 

One of the major values claimed by advocates of multiple-purpose projects is the conservation of soil and the retardation of erosion. 

In this matter there is a strong divergence of opinion among experts and proponents of different techniques.

The Soil Conservation Service of the Department of Agriculture has expressed its theory of conservation as: "Every blade of grass is a reservoir." It believes that soil conservation can best be effected by damming small upstream tributaries and by the adoption of modern farming methods. Its theory is that erosion should be pre- 

T vented before the soil washes off the farm or before it reaches the major river flow.

The proponents of large downstream reservoirs agree in principle, but maintain that irrigable land should be reclaimed before it is rendered useless by erosion and that large reservoirs offer a more practicable solution to the problem of excessive water flow and its concomitant, erosion.

It is not the purpose of this report to arbitrate between these two schools of thought, but it is believed that closer coordination between the Department of Agriculture and the construction agencies is imperative. 

2. ADVANTAGES OF SOIL CONSERVATION PRACTICES 

In some cases, application of the practices advocated by soil conservationists will have a major effect and will cost less than many of the extensive irrigation projects proposed by the Bureau of Reclamation. 

Soil conservation practices can be applied to all parts of the country. 

In some cases, expenditures for soil conservation may have benefits exceeding those offered by reclamation. For example, by proper drainage practices and their use in rich areas not now drained, many acres of very fertile land in river bottoms, deltas, and marshlands can be brought into production; and this land may have an agricultural value far greater than that obtainable, at equal or greater cost, from reclaimed land. Drainage must be planned with care in order not adversely to affect flood storage. 

3. CONCLUSIONS 

From the analysis of water resources problems, it is concluded that more attention should be given, in considering new projects, to the following soil conservation matters not now generally considered by those agencies primarily concerned with construction: a. Installations of more upstream "dry" reservoirs on small tributaries. These small reservoirs would be used for flood control and silt catchment basins. This would not only retard erosion and conserve soil but would also prolong the useful life of the large reservoirs now in existence. 3333

b. Further development of ground storage, thereby reducing serious evaporation losses (particularly in the California area) and augmenting municipal and industrial water supplies. c. Some informed engineers believe that the types of construction used for irrigation works in the past, consisting of extensive canals and laterals, are an obsolete design and that in the future it will be more effective and cheaper to pump water from rivers or underground storage and deliver the water through pipe lines to sprinkler systems located on the farms. The use of sprinkler systems reduces the amount of required ground leveling and does not require the construction of long irrigation canals, which have high evaporation and seepage losses. 

Sprinkler irrigation can be useful in locations where an intermittent supply of water is required, particularly in place of irrigation ditches that are used intermittently for the latter are apt to become clogged with silt and vegetable growths and expensive to maintain. d. Development of modern farming methods such as terracing, contour plowing, proper preparation of the fields for planting, strip planting, rotation of crops, use of proper fertilizers, use of hedge-rows to provide refuge for various birds, and thereby reduce troubles from insects of all kinds, and proper drainage. 

In addition to the matters discussed above, careful consideration should also be given to the economics of proposed flood control projects, for many of these projects may result in the submergence of valuable farm land or the flooding of more valuable acres than are protected by the flood control projects. In considering any project, the value of land lost should be a major consideration in its final approval. 

1. GENERAL C. National Defense 

The policies in connection with provision of an adequate power supply in the event of an emergency should also receive serious attention in any consideration of any multiple-purpose project. 

The demands for power are increasing at a rapid rate, deliveries of electric equipment require from 2 to 3 years, and power shortages are occurring in some sections of the country. It may be several years before spare capacity will be available sufficient to meet the accelerated demands which might result from an industrial mobilization program. 

In the case of many projects, national defense is a major argument advanced by proponents during peacetime and during wartime.

Chapter IV CERTAIN ASPECTS OF THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN PUBLIC AND PRIVATE POWER 

With the increase of Federal participation in the power industry, there has developed a serious controversy between advocates of further Federal expansion and those opposing it. Most recently it has waged over the Tennessee Valley Authority's request for funds to construct a steam-generating plant at New Johnsonville, Tenn. The Authority maintains that it needs this plant in order to have the firm energy necessary to meet the demand. Its opponents have questioned the propriety of its building a steam plant as being an invasion of the province of private industry.

This chapter represents an attempt to analyze briefly two phases of the relationship of the Federal Government to the private utility industry. The analysis is broken down into two parts: a. The competitive value of public power. b. The question of taxes foregone.

The importance of this problem should not be underestimated, for the future adequacy of the Nation's power supply will, to a major extent, be determined by the relationship of public to private power. 

Large areas of the Nation will require further power development if their growth is to continue. Private companies are hampered from filling this need by the fear of Federal competition. Public agencies are hampered by the difficulties in obtaining appropriations sufficient to expedite the huge projects described in chapter I. This impasse is typified by the New Johnsonville steam-plant situation. Until the relationship is stabilized, the nation will not be able to do everything possible to develop its power resources. 

In the last 15 years, Federally owned power generating capacity has risen from almost nothing to being about 10 percent of the national total. Given the programs planned at the present time, a further growth is obvious. More and more Federal generation of electrical energy is becoming a key factor in our economy. Its relationship to the energy generated by private companies must be worked out in a unified program. 35

A. The Competitive Position of Public Power 1. GENERAL 

The popular opinion has been that hydroelectric power is inherently cheap, because it is being generated by falling water. However, in many cases, the high construction cost of elaborate dams and generating equipment results in high annual fixed charges and total annual costs higher than would be the costs at a modern steam plant. 

Since many of the proposed Federal hydroelectric power plants are located at long distances from existing markets, as in the case of the Missouri River Basin, transmission costs also become an important factor. 

2. TRANSMISSION COSTS 

To transmit power from Hoover Dam to Los Angeles, a distance of approximately 266 miles, a 287,000-volt transmission line is required. 

This is the highest transmission voltage used in the United States, although there is a 380,000-volt line under construction in Sweden and some 400,000-volt lines are being built in France. It is a rough rule that the transmission distance in miles can be from 1 to 1.1 times the voltage in thousands. Thus, if the market center is 440 to 480 miles from the dam, as is the case with certain markets proposed to be served by the Missouri Basin project, a 440,000-volt line would be required. The cost of such a line is estimated to be at least $40,000 per mile (total $17,600,000 for 440 miles) of single-circuit steel-tower construction, without substation equipment. 

The tie-line between the Fort Peck project in Montana and the Gavins Point project in South Dakota will be approximately 600 miles long with connections at the intermediate projects at Garrison, Oahe, Big Bend, and Fort Randall. Transmission distances to market are about 175 miles from Fort Randall to Omaha, 400 miles from Fort 

Randall to Duluth, 450 miles from Garrison to Duluth, and 500 miles or more from Garrison to the Mesabi Range, Minn.

B. Taxes Foregone

There is little uniformity in the treatment of taxes, or payments in lieu of taxes, or taxes foregone for multiple-purpose projects; yet both in determining competitive rates and in determining true costs to the taxpayers, the element of taxes should be given consideration. 

In Administrative Memorandum No. 12, prepared by the Federal

Power Commission in January 1947, for the use of its staff in determining the cost and value of power, allowances of 1.4 percent of the 

Federal hydroelectric power investment are adopted for payments in lieu of property taxes to reimburse State and local governmental units for taxes lost as a direct result of the construction of the project (for privately owned steam plants this memorandum adopts 1.75 percent for State and local taxes and 1.00 percent for Federal taxes).

The Boulder Canyon project makes payments of $300,000 a year each to the States of Nevada and Arizona plus $500,000 a year for the benefit of the upper basin States. This aggregate of $1,100,000 can be compared to the $1,697,400 which would have been required if the Federal Power Commission formula of 1.4 percent of the investment allocated to power were used. The Tennessee Valley Authority in 1947 paid $1,668,000 in lieu of taxes although the Federal Power Commission formula of 1.4 percent of the investment allocated to power would have required a payment of approximately $7,314,900. 

For purposes of comparison, it should be noted that many municipal power systems are required to make payments to city funds in lieu of taxes. The Uniform Classification of Accounts for Electric Utilities, issued by the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio, effective January 1, 1915, provides an example of this.

2. OVERLAPPING BETWEEN FEATURES OF A SINGLE PROJECT 

Also, there is a major internal overlapping as between the water storage requirements for various functions of multiple-purpose projects. In general, conflicts in requirements are due to the fact that maximum floods occur during the spring rainy season, and are augmented by melting snow; power demands are usually at a maximum in December or January; while the maximum demands for irrigation water and municipal water are in August or September. If a dam were primarily for flood control, it would be built and regulated with one set of principles in mind; if it were primarily for power, another 

1 See note on page 54. 42

set of principles would hold; and, if primarily for irrigation, still a third. The regulation of reservoirs for these uses, in addition to taking care of navigation requirements, malaria control, recreation, fish and wildlife, is extremely complicated, and the need for unified control in the regulation of dams is self-evident. 

3. OVERLAPPING WITH FACILITIES OF PRIVATE INDUSTRY 

Not only are there major overlappings as between areas of administrative jurisdiction and as among the principles of management governing the various features of a single project, there is also a third type of overlapping—that between governmental activities and those of private industry. This is particularly notable in the case of transmission facilities and is discussed at some length in chapter IV hereof. 

4. RIVER BASINS 

There have been many suggestions that there be unified control of power, flood control, and irrigation projects in the major river basins within the continental United States. In the opinion of some groups, there should be authorities comparable to the Tennessee Valley Authority; in the opinion of others, an established department or agency should have primary jurisdiction. The effect of having two or more coequal agencies has proven to be unworkable in practice, even when interagency committees are in existence, and this method of operation has been open to criticism. 

The Natural Resources Committee of the Commission has discussed this matter adequately. Here let it only be reiterated that, in the national interest, this problem of overlapping and duplication of administrative jurisdictions in river basins must be resolved. 

5. SUMMARY 

The description set forth above illustrates the departments and agencies involved in jurisdictional problems relating to power, flood control, and irrigation. No properly informed, or technically competent, control is exercised at the highest echelon by the Executive Branch in order to determine economic justification for, or appropriate management for the operations of, these projects. As a result, the evaluation of all projects initiated by the constructing and operating personnel of the departments and agencies depends upon the final judgment of a Congress not equipped technically to analyze the enormously complex problems involved in multiple-purpose power, flood control, and irrigation projects. 43

Chapter VI FUTURE TRENDS IN EXPENDITURES ON MULTIPLE-PURPOSE PROJECTS 

Authoritative estimates have been made that the Federal Government will have annual expenditures of 50 billion dollars by 1951, an increase of roughly 10 billion dollars over 1948. Bearing this in mind, it is important that the taxpayers should realize the extent of annual expenditures which will be required for the projects covered in this report.

Expenditures for construction of multiple-purpose projects differ from many other items in the Federal budget in one major respect. 

Whereas the bulk of appropriations to departments and agencies are on a year-by-year basis and can be closely controlled by Congress, those for construction are on this basis in name only; for, once the 

Federal Government has commenced a multiple-purpose project, expenditures for years, or even decades, are implicit in the early appropriations. Cessation of construction on such projects usually entails "pouring down the drain" past expenditures; and in practice, it has proven to be almost impossible to halt such projects "in midair," as was shown so clearly when Congress cut off the Passamaquoddy appropriations.B

Chapter VII CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 1. FEDERAL POWER POLICY 

A definite Federal power policy is needed, in which the Federal Government cooperates with its citizens in the development of hydroelectric power, irrigation, and water-supply projects instead of competing for markets. It is recommended that major considerations in such a water policy include: 

1. A return to the policy of requiring local contributions. 

2. Shift of emphasis from development of channelized water to water conservation in connection with land use and soil conservation. 

3. Control of nonnavigation uses of water at Government reservoirs by States. 

4. The Federal power policy should provide for the utilization of the facilities of privately owned utilities in marketing the power from Federal dams. The privately owned company could: (a) Build the power house and install the generating equipment at each Government dam where feasible, paying taxes on these facilities and also paying the Government for the use of falling water and the dam, with the Government retaining full control over each dam and operating it for the primary purpose of flood control, reclamation or navigation or a combination thereof; or (b) lease the powerhouse and generating facilities, as is done now at the Hoover Dam; or (c) purchase the power output under long-term contracts, entered into in advance of construction; and, in any case, should (d) build the transmission facilities needed to market the power from Government dams, and pay the taxes on such transmission facilities.

The regulatory commissions having appropriate jurisdiction would see to it that fees and rates are fair and that any applicable benefits resulting therefrom would, after a fair return, be distributed and passed on to all consumers. 

5. The rates charged by the Federal Government for electric energy should not be higher than the cost to the purchaser for energy received from an alternate source. 

6. The Federal Government should not build, or acquire in the future, any thermal power plants for the generation of electrical energy that is to be sold. (This excepts power plants for Navy ships, Army or Navy posts, and similar installations.) 47

Since the projects now planned may require an aggregate investment in excess of 40 billion dollars (of which approximately $2,186,- 500,000 has been spent up to June 30, 1947), it is important that a thorough review of plans, economic justification, etc., should be made by a source not connected with the Bureau of Reclamation or the Corps of Engineers, in order to screen out unsound projects. The writer's recommendation is that the Federal Power Commission or the proposed Water Resources Board should make such review. 

An independent report should be made in advance of the authorization of each project, supplemented by reports at the time of each request for appropriations. In the event that cost levels have increased between the time of authorization and the first or subsequent requests for appropriations, consideration should be given deferment of the project or even deauthorization. 

It is recommended that in determining "economic justifications” greater emphasis should be based upon the relation of revenues from all sources to expenses of all kinds as is discussed more fully in chapter 

II, C.

It is further recommended that the Federal Power Commission be authorized to initiate rate studies on all Federal power and water supply projects, and have final regulatory jurisdiction over rates and contract terms, in cooperation with local State utility commissions. 

3. DESIGN AND CONSTRUCTION 

It is recommended that the engineering design and construction now carried on by the Civil Works Division of the Corps of Engineers and by the Bureau of Reclamation be merged into a Department of Public Works, or into a Department of Natural Resources, as proposed by other task forces of the Commission.
