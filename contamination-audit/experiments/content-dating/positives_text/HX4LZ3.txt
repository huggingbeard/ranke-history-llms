The "Going-Away" 

HE WORD has come! Either the selective service number has been drawn or the young man is definitely set to enlist. He is getting ready to go to camp. And right away you are all steamed up to take some action.

You must do something to let him see how you feel about this brave and patriotic thing he is about to do. You will give him a bang-up party and hang the expense.

That may be just the thing to do. But it's wise to make sure before you get too deep in it. Size up your man first. If he is itching to get into the Army, if he rather fancies the thought of being a soldier, then you couldn't do better than throw a grand celebration. But if he gave up his job with a twinge, if he is going to be lonely without his girl, if he feels rather forlorn and is trying to hide it, then you had better skip the hullabaloo. Let the going away be as matter of fact as possible. ...

At least half the boys we talked to were for the less-fuss-the-better. 

And their families had this to say. "The best farewell party you can give 

George is none at all.” . . . “No parties, please. He went in good spirits like going off to work." "They were talking of having a surprise party at the church for several of the boys. The surprise was that it wasn't given! 

Decided it would be too embarrassing for the boys." . . . "No send-offs. 

Personally it's an emotional upset for both the boy and you." . . . "He went away just as he wanted, quietly, very quietly."

That's the way half of the boys and their parents feel. But the other half says in effect, "Sure we want parties. We want all the fun that's coming to us." 2

Some enjoyable going-away parties have been given, too. What the boys seem to like best is the regular type of party their crowd always givesdancing, or a group over to dinner-and presents. 

POST-OFFICE SHOWER One recruit rated this pretty keen. That doesn't mean the old-fashioned type of Post Office, although that's not ruled out either. At this party his friends brought everything that had to do with letter writing: stamps, air-mail and regular; packs of penny postcards; packages of stamped envelopes; air-mail stationery (light weight so it will not eat up postage); fountain pen with military clip; pencil with double supply of leads. 

A DANCE AT HOME FOR TEN Around midnight the postman, identified by the word in cardboard letters on her visored cap, said the mail had come which was a signal for everyone to take a place at the table. The place cards were small envelopes with name and address. As soon as all were seated, the postman brought in some fat manilla envelopes bearing each one's name and address and marked Special Delivery. She put one at each plate. Opened, it proved to be a packet of delicious sandwiches. The mints were square and supposed to be stamps, and so were the individual cakes, iced carefully even to the black marks across showing they were canceled. Postage was due on the coffee. And the ice cream came registered, and everybody had to sign for it on a card which became an autographed souvenir for the guest of honor. 

DINNER FOR EIGHT 

Another girl gave this party. The guests paid high afterward for the elegant meal. She gathered together a hundred words of Army slang (see next page). A list was given to each one to translate into English. There were insignia and sleeve and specialty marks to identify. In fact, the hostess had everybody busy most of the evening showing how little they knew about the Army. The party proved a lot of fun. While ordinary games might not have been interesting, these timely ones went over big, since everybody was eager to know the right answers. When the party was breaking up, the hostess brought out a tiny suitcase. "For your clothes," she said. He started to put the suitcase in his pocket. "Open it," everybody urged. It was filled to the brim with dimes - compliments of the crowd. (The hostess decides the amount to be paid for failure to answer the quizzes, depending on the number and the type of the questions. The receipts are changed into shiny dimes. If the guests should know all the answers, the hostess will have to devise some other way to gather up dimes.)

A CAUTION ABOUT THE PARTIES! Sometime during the "gala," 

somebody ought to say something like, “If they turn you down, Bill, come back and we'll celebrate that.” Or, “Tell 'em you have flat feet, Jim, and they'll let you come back home." In his army medical examination he may be turned down. And there may be a lot of heartaches piling up if he feels foolish, being returned like a bad penny, after all the splendid celebration of his leaving. So put him at ease by tossing a few casual notes into the party atmosphere just in case he is rejected. 

Here is how it sometimes works. A boy, popular in his set, was given

a big send-off: five or six parties, lots of friends flocked to the train, stacks of gifts, all the trimmings. At the induction center he took his Army physical examination. The doctors strongly advised him to go back home. 

His was a border-line case, so that the decision was up to him. He would have given anything to go home. He was just getting started in a job that could turn into defense work, and he had obligations. But he was ashamed — rather stick it out than face all of those kind people who had poured money into his farewells. 

Another boy with about the same setup became really ill in camp almost a nervous breakdown. Now he has been discharged. 

So the point is, see to it that the party talk takes some little recognition of the fact that he might not really get in; that instead of good-by for a year, he might be back home in a couple of weeks. 

WHAT ABOUT GOING-AWAY GIFTS? Cigarettes, a good safety razor, a toilet kit, writing supplies, and money (handed over in any clever way) are all excellent. A scrapbook is fine for a record if it can be left at home and the girl friend, mother or sister keeps it up to date. Boys are allowed to wear their own underwear if they like instead of the Army regulation kind, and a lot of them do. Mothers and sisters often find that underwear is a most acceptable parting gift. 

But whatever the presents, see that he does not take them with him at first. When he leaves, he wants to pack light and cheap, and take only what is absolutely necessary. He should wear his old suit, take a change of socks, shirt, underwear, minimum toilet accessories. He is going first to the induction center, where he will remain for a brief time, then to the reception center, where again the stay is short. Then he will be sent to his permanent camp. Meanwhile, he will have to lug everything around, which is a big nuisance. And with all the arriving and leaving and the confusion, he has a good chance to lose his stuff. Let it be the kind that if he does lose it, it doesn't matter much. After he is settled, there will be plenty of time to forward his things to him.

Write Him Soon and Often

AIL the word should be written in capital letters - is the high point of the soldier's day. The Army even says it's practically the high point of the soldier's life. The letters he gets, the gay, newsy, homey, friendly, gossipy letters, do much to keep up the young man's spirits. And if you're in any doubt about that, stand and watch as we did, when the boys crowd around at mail time, and take a look at the keen disappointment of the lads who turn away with no mail. You'll see that letters mean a lot more than you ever thought they could. One army officer told us that mail is delivered to the men as often and as rapidly as possible because, he said, "It is a prime factor in morale."

All right then. What kind of mail will do the most good? Letters from home come first; they are tops. Letters from friends come next. And letters from strangers come third and rate a special little discussion of their own.

HOME LETTERS 

The big, important, present connection the soldier has with his normal life is the letter from home. Letters can mean so much, if instead of just dashing off a few lines, you put extra time and thought in them. These letters must take the place of his family and friends, and they ought to be good. We know a number of mothers who have a piece of paper and pencil always at hand. And any time they think of a special something in which the boy would be interested, they jot down a little note about it.

Jim likes to hear all about the business, said one mother. He was his father's right-hand man and the business will be his one day. I tell him about new business that comes in, often ask his advice, so he'll feel a part of it. This doesn't make him homesick. It makes him feel he has roots.

I telephone several of his friends before I write, said another mother. 

Then I can include a lot of personal news about them in addition to all the family happenings.

In writing Ralph, said a third, I always give him a picture of the family at the time of writing. I'll tell him that Spot (his adored young puppy) is lying on my feet. Father is scowling at something he's reading in the newspaper. And Elsie, the youngest member of the family, is talking to the Conner boy on the telephone. Ralph says the letter makes him feel as if he just stepped in at home — and he loves it. 

So the first rule for the home letter is: Make it as full of pleasant, interesting bits of news as possible. 

And just as important, never refer to something you said in a previous letter without going into full detail about it again. Maybe you only thought you had mentioned it.

Families who do not obey this rule have caused their young men a lot of grief. Your father was allowed part of a soft cooked egg today, wrote one relieved mother. But she forgot that she had said nothing about the father's serious illness, hoping to spare the boy if the father did improve. 

That sentence brought a costly long distance telephone call from the terrified son. .. We had to get a carpenter after all to break open the desk. What desk? Why? When? If you do change your mind, let us know about it. About what? Upsetting, to say the least, are such letters. If you remember that the boy's work and thoughts have taken him entirely away from you temporarily, that the chances are he is dog tired from drill or maneuvers, you'll realize what a relief it is for him to receive newsy, cheerful, comprehensible letters. ...

Of course, any young soldier will tell you he can't get too much mail. But most of them will add that they like best of all to know the letters will come regularly. One girl who corresponds with five soldiers (two brothers and three cousins) always answers their letters the day after she receives them. One, two, or three times a week is a schedule that most families follow. A few write every day. Of course, that's fine if there is enough news. Or if it's a fiancée, it's probably fine anyway. 

But one young boy who would rather cut off his right arm than hurt his mother has won a lot of horseplay and a stupid nickname because that kindly, affectionate lady writes him sixteen-page letters every day of her life. 

It has become a sort of company joke. He is practically always in the act of reading through one of those booklike epistles, which brings us to the question of length.

A few of the boys we talked to said, the longer the better. Most of them, however, felt that more letters and shorter ones were what they wanted.

Generally a four-page letter with all the news crammed in like plums in a pudding is the most acceptable size. But don't, don't write four or five a week the first several weeks after he leaves, and then taper off to one or two. When you start a letter-writing program, adopt one that you can and will follow.

MAKE IT EASY FOR HIM TO ANSWER Many mothers, sisters, and young wives include in each letter a stamped, self-addressed envelope with a fold of blank note paper. Then all the young soldier has to supply is a stub of pencil to write his message. When the men went on maneuvers, a couple of postcards were often enclosed. For writing facilities at that time are likely to be poor, and it is easy to scribble a few lines on a postcard, when a real letter might be out of the question. 

And, please, don't sound blue. . . . We haven't had chocolate cream pudding (his favorite dessert) since you left. (So what?) . . . Mother hasn't been well from the day you went away... She worries so about you. 

Perhaps the greatest satisfaction the young soldier has is in picturing home, the normal, comfortable, happy place he has left, exactly as he left it. 

In letters, do everything in your power to make that pleasant picture stand. 

LETTERS FROM FRIENDS Such letters run a gamut; from what's going on at the old plant and what's happening in the crowd, with emphasis on the latest about the girl friend, to what's new at church. We're not talking about a fiancée, but just the girl who is glad to write to her friend in the service. Snapshots, clippings, jokes, any kind of surprise is more than welcome in addition to the interesting local tidbits. Postcard showers on a birthday have been greeted with ringing cheers. 

One girl said she tried to find or invent a holiday each month to be the reason for some kind of gay or original card. Hallowe'en, Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year's, Valentine's Day, Washington's Birthday, Saint Patrick's, Easter, 4th of July, Labor Day, Flag Day, birthday, all sorts of anniversaries. And from the appreciative letters she gets, they seem to go over.

Stamps are something most of the girls and many other writing friends put in the letters they send along. Small books of ones or twos or threes. 

Sometimes just a single "three-center," or even an addressed stamped envelope. 

One young woman always includes an air-mail stamp when she wants a fast answer. So far she always has received it, too. 

Another girl told us: "All I do is try to have my letters funny. I just want him to get a laugh out of them." She has made up a couple of crazy little figures that she scatters around on each page. And if anybody wants

to know whether letters are shared, and the boys read each other's mail, here's one answer. Our young friend calls these cartoons by a crazy made-up name. On her first visit to camp, all the boys in the company greeted her by that as her nickname, showing themselves familiar with the goofy episodes. 

This suggests a small note of warning. If you are a young girl with a soldier friend, it might not be a bad idea to limit your letters to the kind of chat you wouldn't mind hearing quoted. 

LETTERS FROM STRANGERS Are there any rules to govern them? 

Patriotism, unselfishness, adventure, and romance are all mixed up in this situation which seem to us to bear a little sorting out. Nationally, we are in the throes of the biggest letter-writing crusade this land has ever seen. A soldier, quoted in an editorial, told how lonely he was, and ninety-seven girls wrote him warm, friendly letters to cheer him up. Lists of soldiers who would like to receive mail are published in some daily papers as a patriotic gesture. 

A convoy going off to maneuvers stopped briefly for gas at a small country town. Cards were dropped like manna on the streets, saying virtually in every case: "My name is John Smith. Write me -" and the address was given. One young lady has already entered into brisk correspondence with seventeen. The whole feeling is that every soldier must get mail; and on the part of the girls, that every girl must do her share to supply it. There is nothing wrong with this reaction, provided it's spiced with a little common sense.

Exchanging letters with strangers is not new to us. American and French school children, American and British school children, have exchanged letters for years, with advantages to both sides. Teachers read the most interesting ones aloud in class. 

Right there is your basis. Write to your "mail-friend" soldier if you like. But be sure the letters are the kind you would be pleased to have read aloud – friendly, gay, and amusing. But don't go sentimental on him and don't make rash promises you can't keep or haven't any intention of keeping. "If you were here," one seventeen-year-old word artist wrote in her letter which was sent winging over twenty-two hundred miles to the appreciative recipient, “we'd ride horseback this morning, and after a brisk canter, come back to one of Delia's good breakfasts!" Imagine this young lady's surprise when one Sunday morning, by one of Uncle Sam's miracles, the soldier was on her doorstep! But there were no horses, no canter, and no Delia. There was, however, an embarrassing situation and a short-lived breakfast. 10

A young friend of ours who gets delightful replies from her soldier boyswhether home friends or correspondence ones - wrote a first letter like this. 

Dear R,

Since Monday everyone has been reading about the convoy from Fort Dix which is headed for Virginia. Tonight our local paper gave your complete route and also the time you would pass through each city and town. So I made up my mind I would be one of the many to cheer you on.

Well, at 9 p.m. I was standing on the corner of C Pike and 

A Road, (half a block from home) waving to all the boys. I was even dressed up for the occasion, red sweater, white shirt and navy slacks. 

I was with my younger sister L, and our friend, G.

For a while our main object was to get a rise out of the officers leading each group. But that was difficult. Many looked as though they would like to wave and say hello - but they stopped with a half smile. It was fun. 

Finally you came along, and I'll never forget it. Quite a group had collected by this time, and we all saw the metal disc fly out with the white card attached. But none of us had the nerve to get it. The next thing I noticed, an elderly lady whom

I know stepped out and picked it up. 

She and her friends gathered around the light of the store window and proceeded to examine the card. Then she called me over and gave it to me. "Why don't you write?" she asked. 

So that is how I came to know of your existence. You say you didn't get the name? It's E-- -— M▬▬▬▬▬. Hello, how are you?

Then in the same gay, informal vein this girl went on to describe herself, to tell something about her job which she likes very much, touch briefly on her family and her hobby, and sign off. 

This type of letter she has sent four times, and in every instance by return mail came an interesting, amusing reply. The young man gives his background - tells something about his family, his former job, where his regular home is, what he has been doing and is doing in the Army. "Dear E-," wrote one. "This is my first experience in writing to a young lady without actual introduction. But you have set me at ease with your cute, informal letter. So it seems as if I must have really met you. I am going to be as friendly as your letter makes me feel you are." When he came to the end, he signed himself "Your pen pal.” 11

A LONG RANGE ADOPTION 

Writing to young "stranger" soldiers is not limited to young girls. Many mothers are doing it. Here is a typical case: In one of his letters home, the son said, "I read most of your last letter aloud to George and James and Frank. They are so interested, because I have told them all about you. And they don't get any mail at all." 

In her reply to the boy, she made some inquiries why this trio should be "mail-less." Satisfied with the explanation, she wrote each one a friendly, homey letter. They all answered at once - not just routine answers - but full of information about themselves and what they were doing. Now she writes them all every week, sends them cookies and cigarettes and candy from time to time, shows them various little attentions - birthday cards, small gifts.

ADOPTING A COMPANY A Ladies' Aid society has adopted a whole company. They send letters, stamps and food to every one of the men. 

And if you would like to know whether they are popular with their boys or not, take note that every so often each letter contains in addition to news and chat, a crisp new one dollar bill.

Incidentally, if you think he is going on maneuvers and you are not sure where, send the letter to the place from which he entrained. The mail will be forwarded as quickly as possible, and this includes third-class as well as first-class mail.

Mail is delivered once or twice a day. The time of delivery varies with the different camps: every day at noon; every day at 11 a.m. and 4 p.m.; every day at 6 p.m.; every day at 5:50 p.m.; every day at noon and at 5 p.m. 

But whatever the time, the boys are all on hand. 12

The mail comes in regularly from the Army Post Office, is sorted and broken down by regiments. Then it is separated by companies, and handed over to the top sergeant who blows his whistle, perhaps, and the men come running.

If you are in a hurry for him to get your letter, use air mail but not special delivery. He'll get the air mail as fast in camps as the planes can bring it: A letter from Fort Sill, Oklahoma, to Philadelphia arrived by air mail in one day. But so far as special delivery mail is concerned, don't go to that expense. When a letter arrives, the chances are he will be out on drill. When he returns he'll get all his mail-including the special delivery. We found this rule in fifteen test cases in which we sent specials to fifteen young soldiers in fifteen different camps spread over the whole country. Telegrams naturally are treated a little differently. They are put in the hands of the men as speedily as possible. Sometimes word is sent to the boy to call for his telegram at the company office. Sometimes it is telephoned from receiving stations to the company orderly room. Sometimes it is delivered personally. 13

The Gift and the Giver 

IGARETTES, candy and Hedy Lamarr" was the way a draftee at Fort Sill, Oklahoma listed his gift preferences. Then he explained that what he really meant was cigarettes, something sweet to eat, and a visit from his girl friend.

We had written or interviewed personally scores of young soldiers in camps from New York to California. And it was increasingly evident that a lot had been left unsaid about gifts for the draftees, especially when we were meeting on all sides girls in offices and behind counters who were gladly skipping lunch or cutting it down to a milk shake in order to send little presents to their soldier friends. 

What do the boys really want? How can you find out? We are going to report exactly what we learned. But first a word of caution. All sorts of sources are giving out advice about soldier presents and much of it is excellent. But since it's your money you are spending in this very special cause, it is wise to make sure that what you are reading is not just clever merchandising but sound information on what to buy. (An extreme case was a small merchant who piled on a display table all sorts of things he had not been able to sell and put up a card reading FOR THE SOLDIER. They were snapped up at once. There is little of that. But you can't blame any merchant for thinking his wares are exactly right. You just make sure what's right for your own particular soldier.)

FOOD Food came at the top, or near it, on practically all lists. And that's not surprising. In even the most expensive colleges, boys are constantly begging for boxes from home. And in camp, while they are adequately fed, the home-box looms even more important. It's the news from home par excellence. But there are certain rules about food that are well to bear in mind: 14

The box must be able to take punishment. 

Here is our idea of punishment: a good strong kick wouldn't hurt it; or being dropped suddenly; or standing four days in one place. We saw a huge carton of cookies reduced to powder by the time it was opened. We saw and smelled a roasted chicken sent airmail, but evidently grounded somewhere because of weather. Keen disappointment was registered in both of these instances. 

Have the food unit complete when opened. If there are glasses of jelly, put in an inexpensive spoon or knife to spread it. If it's a cake, put in a knife to cut it. If there are tins of food, see to it there is also a can opener. 

Take climate into consideration. A young soldier who had been asking for and getting chocolate bars by the dozen while back at his regular camp base, learned by experience that the bars melted down in your pocket and got all over your uniform when you were out in the sun on maneuvers.

Hard candy was what he wanted after that-the kind you can easily and invisibly carry in your mouth when marching, or cram in your pockets, too, and find that it is still edible hours later. 

Don't waste any space. 

Pack the box with roasted peanuts instead of crumpled paper, or with popcorn, if the container is airtight, or with hard candy wrapped separately in waxed paper.

When the young man receives your box, it goes without saying that he is going to write and thank you. But was it really what he wanted most?

Was it the kind of food he would have chosen himself? 

CHECK UP ON HIS PREFERENCES 

Here is where your ability as a letter writer comes in. One mother, after sending a couple of boxes, began to wonder and wrote this letter: "I'm going to send you a box each month. Next time would you rather have all cookies, or cookies and a fruit cake, or an assortment of boxes of cheeses, pickles, jelly, crackers, sardines, olives, candy (if so, what kind), and cookies?" She had forgotten all about these double cookies. And how delighted she was to know she was sending what he really wanted! Organizations Help You to Meet Soldiers 

NY DISCREET GIRL who lives within commuting distance of any of the A Army camps has the opportunity of her lifetime. She has limited chances of meeting many young men, and entertaining them at her home, and who knows what permanent friendships may develop?

But how does this agreeable situation come about? Certainly not by the method direct. You don't pick up the soldier in camp, or on the street, or at a movie. There are much better ways than that, and much more dignified.

THROUGH YOUR CHURCH One way is through your church. Let's follow through how one girl went about it. She got two other girls and the three went to their minister and told him they each would be glad to entertain a couple of soldiers at Sunday dinner. He said, all right, and asked the girls to wait a moment after church on the next Sunday and he would introduce them to six soldiers, if that many were in attendance. 

And that is what he did. He had not known the boys before, but at least they were interested in church service. And he felt that was a kind of recommendation. In a short time scores of members let it be known that they would also be delighted to have soldier guests for Sunday dinner. Until today it's a rare soldier that goes to that church who does not dine out. And quite a number go to the same homes Sunday after Sunday. 

SERVICE DANCES Another way to get acquainted is by attending the service dances. You don't wait for a bid and you don't crash the gate either. Your town has some organizations right now that are looking for girls like you. They are arranging dances for men in uniform and putting girls on their special lists. 26

For example, Camp Stewart is near Savannah. The Camp Morale Officer 

(under whom entertainment is arranged) visited all the nearby towns and talked to the parents and got their permission and made arrangements for the daughters to attend the dances.

In Louisville, near Fort Knox, twenty organizations of women take care of the dances which are held each week. Each organization has chaperons, and the chaperons invite ten girls each time-girls for whom they can vouch. 

In Richmond, near Fort Lee, one hundred women have made themselves responsible for ten girls each, to attend the men's dances. In Houston, the 

Recreation Department is behind the service dances. To be on their list, a girl must get a dance ticket, have it signed by a sponsor and some executive in the Recreation Department. You probably know what organizations are interested in your town. It might be the Chamber of Commerce, and of course, the U. S. O. In many places also the Y. W. C. A. is running a registry list. 

If you feel a little shy about letting it be known that you would like to attend the dances, please don't. There are not nearly enough girls to go around, and you would be doing everybody a favor by showing a willingness to help out.

Go to the church socials that are so often given for the soldiers. And even if you're not on the entertainment committee, remember that every girl there is really a hostess and act like one. What does that mean? We attended one of those parties recently, and some of the girls stood around in groups of four or five and just looked. Probably, they felt timid. But so did the soldiers. And those girls weren't having any fun and they weren't making it enjoyable for anybody else either. 

Of course, it isn't easy to walk up to several strange young men and start an interesting conversation. But we know a pair of girls-and they are not more clever or braver than you-and they can start talk any time. How? 

They carry their topic of conversation with them, the starter at least, the ice breaker. Once it was little light bulbs hidden in their hair. They began turning them off and on, and right away the boys were asking them how they did it. Another time, they did a Gracie Allen, looking everywhere for their nonexistent brother, asking everybody if they knew where he was, giving crazy descriptions of how he looked and where they had last seen him.

Leaves and Furloughs 

HE BOYS write home, "I think I'll get two weeks after maneuvers." "I expect to be home ten days at New Year's, it looks like ifteen days' furlough in March." Or, 'I'll be home for the week end." And maybe they are right and maybe they are wrong. For the one sure thing about a soldier's leave of absence or furlough is that he is never sure. 

An order may come through that cancels every single leave or postpones thousands of furloughs. 

As it stands, soldiers accumulate two-and-a-half days a month of furlough which in a year amounts to thirty days. They are also allowed week-end leave whenever they ask it unless they are assigned some special duty or are on guard duty.

A leave 

DIFFERENCE BETWEEN LEAVES AND FURLOUGHS 

is any absence up to three days, and of course you must have a pass and tell where you are going. A furlough is any time over three days and you are allowed money for your food. Getting the two straight has been quite an education for some families. Ralph, for instance, had week-end leave and was going back Sunday night. "I report in, in the morning," he said, "then lie around all day, until I really go back on duty tomorrow night." "Why not stay here then until tomorrow?" was the question. "Leave expires," said Ralph. "If they had let me stay until then, it would have constituted a furlough and they would have had to pay me food money." WHILE HE'S AT HOME One mother has what seems to us an ideal way of handling her boy's leaves. "When Charles comes home," she said, "I never have a group in. He has such different kinds of friends, and they are not friends of each other. And he wants to get all caught up. 

So we see that the car is at his disposal all the time he is at home. After he comes, I bake a ham and see that there is plenty of fruit and cookies and bread and butter. And he can trail in with anybody he likes. He has 29

open house the way he wants it. We see him, too, but we don't hang around his neck."

Come here for dinner? Or shall we go to the hotel, for a spread? You can be as extravagant as you like — it's all on father." Two points are made very tactfully: he can be as gay or as quiet as he pleases, and there will be no expense. 

This brings up another point about thoughtful hostesses. You have doubtless read about the lower railroad rates allowed soldiers on leaves and furloughs. The railroads are doing all they can to cooperate and help out. But there are many rules and tremendous detail, and, of course, some trains are restricted.

According to one mother, here is how it worked out for her son: "Every time he has come home on leave, he has had to pay full fare. The so-called "special rates" seem obtainable only for furloughs and are good on one special train going and returning. He may be going about it wrong, but that's how it works out for him." She mentioned her son's circumstances because she had been under misapprehension. She, feeling very generous and patriotic; had been inviting two or three friends to come up with her son. She found out that instead of a trifling cost for soldiers, each one of her week-end guests was out $3 or the regular round trip fare - for his visit. Now she invites fewer boys, but with each invitation goes a round-trip ticket, so the hospitality is really hers, and not a big slice out of the boy's income. 

Most boys do not care for big parties on their leaves or furloughs. 

They don't feel like heroes; they feel like boys who are drafted to do a job and are doing it. One young soldier stepped out on the porch away from a dance being given in his honor. “What are they celebrating?" he asked.

POINTS TO REMEMBER When in uniform, men are not very keen about carrying armfuls of bundles or an umbrella. So on their leaves, try not to load them down as carryalls.

If you want the leave to be perfect, have the house shining with neatness and cleanliness. The young men are housekeeping-conscious now that they do their own, and their training is something to give a wife or mother pause. One young soldier whose room when he left it, always looked as if a hurricane had just passed, spent a whole week end getting it shipshape. 

Everything is not only scrubbed almost to the bone, but all is squared off with arithmetic precision. As he walked through the rest of the house, the alarmed family could see him eyeing other rooms for his later attention. 30

Questions of Etiquette

TIQUETTE BETWEEN boys and girls has swung around a lot today. Many young people have asked us about certain points, "Is this proper?"

Etiquette itself has not changed. It is still the kindest, most common-sense thing to do. Remembering that, it will be easy for you to make any new etiquette rules for yourself as the occasion arises. 

Suppose though, we repeat some of the questions we have heard asked most frequently.

Is it proper for a girl, visiting a soldier friend at camp, to pay the bills for their food and fun? 

Any number of soldiers said, "If she doesn't, we won't eat." But you would certainly know that, unless the boy had independent means, there would be mighty little left of the $21 or $30 for entertainment. 

You know how far he has to make that money stretch. Out of that sum must come his laundry, Post Exchange expenses - cigarettes, candy bars, magazines — movies, and transportation to town. The way we heard it, most of the boys are in funds the first week after payday, and broke from then on. 

So how about your week end near camp, or your day at camp? First, feel out your ground. Once in a blue moon you will meet a young soldier who has the cash in hand and expects to do the honors. Your testing ought to be handled easily and frankly. You could hand him an envelope and ask: "Is that enough for dinner and the show?" And if he says, "This time it's on me!", and you see he means it, and is not just embarrassed about having to take your cash, why then the treat is his. Or you could say, "I'm rich today! We can spend up to eight dollars. What'll we do?" 31

And here's one special way not to do it. Any way you face it, it is not too much fun for the boy to let you do the treating. And it should be as painless as possible. One girl, kind as they come but without a morsel of tact, walked around camp with her pocketbook open half the time. She was constantly doling out a few cents for whatever they were buying. 

They got two magazines at the Post Exchange and she handed her soldier thirty cents right change. They stopped for hot chocolate at the Hostess

House, and she gave him a dime. They took the bus to the nearby town and she gave him eighty cents. She was making it her treat – but it wasn't done very skilfully. 

Shall a girl with a cur give a soldier a lift? There are two phases to this question. If you are at camp and are ready to drive back to town, go right to the Hostess House or to an officer and say you have space in your car for two or more soldiers and you'd be glad to drop them off at any designated place in town.

But suppose it is not at camp and you're driving down the highway, and you pass a soldier who wants a ride. This is the place for your own innate good sense to come to the fore. This soldier is just a strange boy with a uniform on. And in the past you never risked picking up strange young men. And don't think this does not come straight from headquarters.

Many mothers have said: "Why, yes, I have a son in camp and I'd like to have him picked up and given a lift when he was tired. But I've got a daughter, and I've got to think about her, too." ·

Should a girl foot the entertainment bill when the soldier is home on leave? Why not? He's still broke, just the same as he was at camp. 

And if you're broke, too, you can certainly make candy in your kitchen and see that he has at least one good meal at your house. 

What about starting a correspondence with soldiers you haven't met?

It's perfectly all right; just be careful not to get it too personal. You can have a lot of fun out of those letters and he can, too. But remember, again, don't let your imagination run away with you. If you tell him how you look, let it be the way you look. And if you presently urge him to drop in on you if he ever gets the chance, be prepared to back up that hospitality one of these days.

If a soldier whom you have met only through correspondence, invites you to camp to visit him, should you go? There's no one who can really answer that except your parents. And they might feel it would be better for him to come to your home first. 32

But you may be holding a job away from home, or it may be impossible for the soldier to get to your home. In such cases, the feeling seems to be that it's quite all right to accept the soldier's invitation, but make it a party affair. Get some other couple or your sister to go down with you, and plan the day so it won't develop into a two-some affair. Everybody can have lots of fun, and your soldier will see that you have a sense of fitness as well as personal dignity. And if that's the kind of girl he's really interested stock takes a rise. in 

your If What about throwing cards with your name and address to soldier convoys as they pass through your town? That depends on you. you're a gay little creature, don't take things too seriously, and always are seeing the funny side of life, go right ahead. Your letters will be a welcome diversion, and you can charge time, postage, and paper to patriotism. 

Is it proper to send gifts to a soldier you know in name only? Certainly!

But use your good common sense. Don't embarrass or unconsciously put him 'in a spot' by sending expensive gifts a costly military wrist watch or items of personal jewelry. Naturally nothing is too good for a soldier boy. But if you want to make a bull's-eye, how about a book of stamps in your cheery letter; a couple of tins of his favorite cigarettes; or a simple box of eats?

Then the soldier doesn't feel these presents require anything more than a grateful letter, no obligation is involved.

If your correspondence soldier seems to be misinterpreting your gay notes, how can you let him know graciously you have no idea of becoming serious? 

For you, that shouldn't be too difficult. It's just a question of getting a few facts over to him clearly. Your notes can contain brief, gay but personal references to your home-town boy friend, or fiancé, at Camp So-and-So or in home-defense work. Any boy would soon understand. And it will not hurt the pleasant friendship at all. 33

The Marriage Question 

O MARRY or not to marry a soldier? Half a million boys and girls are today wondering what is the right answer to that question. Many are deciding yes. If it's draftees, likely the marrying is taking place before they go off to camp. For once in the Army, it's a question of getting the permission of their superior officer, and such permission is all but impossible to get. 

This feeling of haste and hurry, of uncertainty about the future, the drive to clutch any happiness you can while you can, has pushed many young people into unplanned matrimony. And how is it working out? 

A FEW EXAMPLES FIRST. A young private, newly married, was lucky enough to find himself stationed a few miles from home. He was able to get home most nights and nearly every weekend. Then orders came through. 

He was sent to Texas and has not seen his wife since. But he's not the only one. So many indeed have had identical experience that we feel almost like saying to new draft wives: Take a map of the United States and put your finger on the army post farthest from your home town. That's probably where your new husband will be transferred within a couple of months after your marriage. Put baldly: you can't make any plans at all about anything. Another young wife is living with her husband's people. "I wish,” said the new mother-in-law, "that Jim could be transferred a thousand miles away. He's too far now to get home, so Elizabeth clips off the three hundred miles every Friday in his car. She's getting to be a regular nervous wreck. I wonder what it's doing to him."... A third young couple plighted their troth during his three-days' week-end leave. (He had secured permission, the other boys thought, because his girl had a good job.) So they were married in a little town nearby, and he went back to his camp and she to 34

her job, 900 miles away. That was eleven months ago, and they have not seen each other since.

It sounds romantic and dramatic, that swift marriage to a soldier who is going to defend his country and your being the young wife who keeps the home fires burning.

But let's take a look at it a couple of months afterward and glean a few pros and cons. And don't forget, at camp it's the married fellows as well as the single boys who are talking; and back home, the new wives. Line up the cons and the pros.

The cons first from the soldier's viewpoint: (1) Salary $21 a month, then $30; later perhaps more. But still not enough to support a wife or finance a home. (2) The future uncertain. You've no idea how long you will be in the army, or what may happen to you before you're through. (3) You can't be sure about your job or what you'll find waiting for you when you come out. (4) You can't be with your wife; she's miles away. go with any other girl-you're married! 

The cons now, from the girl's viewpoint: 

And you can't (1) None of the fun that should go with marriage. Where is the little new apartment that is the first home of the bride and groom; the drapes, the rugs, the breakfast nook; the struggles with the cookbook and can opener; the excitement of furnishing and making the home together? All of that missing in the bride-withouta-bridegroom arrangement. (2) You have a job-so did lots of other girls-but oftentimes they had to give them up to have the baby. (3) You can't have dates with boys-you're married!

But, you say, we haven't even touched the pros. All right, let's have the pros: (1) If you've been planning your marriage for some time, saving for an apartment and furniture and a future, and if you'd rather have a month or so of marriage than perhaps none at all, then marry. (2) If the girl has a job and a little money besides, and you both are strong for it regardless of consequences, then marry. 35

Each individual case must rest on its own merits. Many girls today are fully capable of supporting themselves. (3) If the bride can have a comfortable home with her people or his, and is willing to take it that way, then marry. (4) If you believe strongly that this is the one, then your marriage will survive in spite of the long separations. 

So much depends on the qualities and temperaments of the individuals concerned. But don't marry your soldier because you're afraid someone else might beat your time if you don't. Someone else might anyway. For a photograph bride, a picture husband-it's pretty hard. 

Parents, the ones most concerned about the welfare of their son or daughter, take a long range view of marriage. A few, who are financially able to support the new wife if she is not already secure may feel that she will be a stabilizing influence for their son and favor marriage, particularly if it had been planned for some time.

Some of the parents to whom we talked, had this to say: "Marriage just gives the family one more thing to worry about." . . . “Decidedly bad bet." ... "Wives have to live with “in-laws” or go back home." . . . "The boys can't contribute to their wives' support with the salaries they get, and they get a lax idea of marriage responsibilities.” . . . “Might be "Might be years of absence.

Heartaches for the girls, ties them down. She can't have a good time; neither can he.” . . . "They see the single boys having dates-hard not to fall in line."

This advice, of course, is not particular but general. Your case may be entirely different, one apart. But in general, our strong urging is wait for the marriage until after your young man is through with being a soldier. 

It's a purely masculine life he's leading now-it's hard to make a place for a wife in it. 36

Jobs for Soldier's Wives Near Camp 

HOUSANDS of young draft wives want to get jobs near camp to be Tclose to their soldier husbands. Can it be done? If the question had to be answered straight out, yes or no, the answer would have to be no. 

For no girl, marrying a soldier, can have any assurance that she can find employment near her husband's camp. Making that broad statement, we can go into particulars, for in lots of cases, girls have found jobs. 

The type of job depends upon the location of the camp, whether it is pretty well away from big population centers; or whether it is near a large city.

We'll take first the camp that has only small towns in the vicinity. 

Of course, job opportunities there are naturally much less than near a big city. One thing that has sprung up like a big mushroom is a whole flock of eating places. Another thing, a lot of small shops to cater to the soldier and his cash. And, likewise, new people have come in — or the people there have more money. 

So there are three types of jobs that are possibilities: waitress in one of the cafes or restaurants; sales clerk in one of the stores; cook or maid in one of the houses. There is very little demand for stenographers or bookkeepers or for girls with professional training. 

Red Cross chapters concern themselves directly with all problems that affect soldiers' wives. If you are on the ground, the thing to do would be to go to the local chapter, tell your training and see if there is any market for it. And if not, ask them if they can put you in touch with the proper agency to get you a job as waitress, clerk, or housekeeper. That's if you are on the ground. If you are not, you can write the local Red Cross and give them a picture of your capabilities and your job problem, and 39

ask what they have to suggest. Give an extra special reason, if you can think of one, of why you should be nearby while most of the wives are back home, for there are plenty of requests like yours. 

Meanwhile, your husband in his free time can do a lot of asking around, too. The Red Cross agent in the camp can give him his first steer, and he can go on from there. If neither one of these leads seems to be able to find anything for you, and you want to see for yourself, then there is only one thing to do. Come on down. But by all means, have with you enough money to get you back home if you don't find anything.

It doesn't make the slightest difference whether you were a domestic helper or clerk or waitress or what you were in your other life. That's what there is. And a number of girls who had other kinds of training, are grabbing at the chance for these jobs.

A young college girl whose fiance was making fifty a week, married him a month or so before he entered the army. She is now earning enough to take care of herself by working as nursemaid in a home where there are three children. Her salary is small, but she has all her meals and she has a pleasant room which she is allowed to share with her husband on his free nights.

If the camp is near a large city and you would be living there and have your job there, then you do have a better chance to get the sort of job you held at home. Crack secretaries, and Al office workers, bookkeepers, salespeople, frequently find work to their taste. Still it's a good idea for them to stop at the local Red Cross and see if there are any helpful suggestions. They might know the best employment agency. Or they might have a tie-in with an employer, and telephone him to give the soldier's wife every consideration. It's certainly worth a try. But again, have the fare for the return trip just in case.

If you are leaving another job to be near camp, and you can manage it, you might ask your old boss if he can hold your old job just a week or two until you are sure of a new one. 40
