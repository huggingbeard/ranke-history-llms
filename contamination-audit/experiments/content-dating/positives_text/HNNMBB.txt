INTRODUCTION 

IN EDUCATION, the danger to the student does not lie in the field of pure science. Science has to do with facts as they exist. Philosophy is the realm in which facts are interpreted. It attempts an explanation of things as they are. The philosopher who leads his students into thinking that human beings are animals, and nothing more, is a menace to the human race. He is neither Christian nor Pagan.

Psychology and allied subjects give one an insight into human beings. They show, from one point of view, something of man's nature and how he undertakes to discover solutions of his problems. Why do human beings think and act as they do? Why do individuals, and groups as well, have the standards which they follow? What is mind? What is the significance of thought? Are empirically derived ideas and concepts the essence of knowledge? Are the great ideals which we have held dearest and which have meant most toward the improvement of the human family merely human creations? How are such questions to be answered safely and wisely? 

Certainly one must be sincere. One should not be a prejudiced, narrow, partisan dogmatist who refuses vii

to confront facts. A Christian, above all others, can afford to be courageous and optimistic. One should have an open mind, but not applaud every glamorous passing movement. A Christian should go forward, but he must not follow blind trails that lead nowhere. He should search for truth in all realms, and, if light breaks in, he should welcome it and follow the gleam.

Education is at its best only when Christ is at the center. Philosophy, with Christ at the heart, leads to emancipation. A better knowledge of human beings enables one to serve them more widely and acceptably.

In the Department of Philosophy and Psychology at Wake Forest College, with its unusually well equipped departmental library and its apparatus for psychological studies, Dr. A. C. In this volume, which Dr. Reid was requested to publish and which he now sends out, he sounds the note so often heard when he lectures in the classroom or from public platforms-civilization is safe only as Christian principles dominate the thinking of mankind. FOREWORD 

CHRISTIAN should have no vestige of apology for Christ, or for the high standards and inescapable obligations of Christianity. Moreover, a Christian must recognize the sacredness of personality and the importance of personality, both in its own right and in its social relationships. It is furthermore imperative that a correct appraisal of values be established. Advancement cannot successfully be made by stooping to mediocrity. If man is to walk safely and find the abundant life, he must look upward.

Nature embodies no falsehoods and practices no deceptions upon herself. If the yearning for personal immortality, so universal to the human family, is a delusion, man is then the only part of the universe made the hopeful-miserable victim of deception. The hope for personal immortality is an ineradicable characteristic of man's intrinsic being, and it would seem preposterous to suggest that he is only a conscious puppet made to appear for a brief moment on the naturalistic stage.

Then, why does man, as an individual and in groups, frequently stoop so low in thought and in practices? Perhaps one cause is to be found in the xi

The lectures printed in this little book are offered here with the same purpose with which they were originally delivered. They were designed as an effort to awaken interest in colossal problems and to suggest remedies for unwholesome and dangerous conditions that impinge upon our elemental interests and liberties. Criticism is employed as a means of attracting attention to grave needs, for there are conditions, values, rights, and ideals which must not be surrendered or forfeited in the crisis which obviously now exists.

If Christianity is successfully to meet the grave needs of the present time, Christians and Christian organizations must look beyond traditionalism, dogmatism, fundamentalism, modernism, creeds, and theology. 

They must discover the Living Christ, and, through His leadership, develop spirituality, wisdom, and sacrificial love.

Christians must establish a profound and active faith in God, realize the sacredness of man, recognize inescapable high human obligations, and discover the fundamental purpose and worth of Christian institutions. They should also identify and combat evils.

Christians against waste of their resources and opportunities in dealing with trivial matters when colossal issues are at stake. The struggle now, as then, is not so much with flesh and blood as with principalities and powers, and with evils in the high places of the home, industry, government, the law, education, and organized religion.

One primary source of danger is to be found in the widespread loss of confidence in man and in institutions. Fearful distrust, like creeping paralysis, now enervates many parts of civilization. This source of trouble must be removed, for confidence is necessary to genuine progress in any major field. This audience consists of members of several denominations.¹

I. HUMAN RELATIONSHIPS 

HUMAN CONTRACTS and mutual responsibilities are now so numerous and varied that local groups are inseparably knit together, and the world has become a network of interdependent thought and action. 

Business relations so integrate the whole world that whatever affects one part concerns the whole. 

Many relations are now severely strained or broken. 

We witness, for illustration, a great social struggle. 

Our American system has suffered a nervous breakdown. It is now receiving artificial stimulation which, by itself, is as inadequate, in many respects, as were the old pragmatic standards which aided in bringing about the collapse.

In the absence of a positive Christian spirit of brotherhood, force is now generally employed to level, to regulate, and to protect the social structure. Coercion is widely used in efforts to guarantee justice. 

Some religious organizations, as such, seem inclined to dictate to government agencies and industries. 

Secular groups, on the other hand, appear to ig-

There is a truth as old as Adam, as deep seated as heredity, as widespread as environment, and as sacred as personality, that must be reckoned with. 

Moreover, the international situation is tense. Nations are now engaged in a great economic struggle. 

Germany suffers from political madness. Italy is ruthless and unscrupulous in grasping new territory. 

Japan uses subtle and unprincipled methods in extending her borders. Russia promises to become a fearful menace to Europe and Asia, if not to America.

These strained international relations are perhaps our greatest immediate menace, for the next great war may be brought about by greed for land and what land holds and produces.

The acute revival of nationalism is restoring and building great walls of sentiment and armaments around nations. The cry has become, Britain for British, Germany for Germans, Italy for Italians, Asia for

Confidence in human relationships, local and international, will be reëstablished when, and only when, there prevails a deep conviction that these relationships are instituted and conducted in harmony with strict justice, and when they are inspired and interpenetrated by a high sense of personal honor and unselfishness. This ideal will be attained when Christianity ceases to be a mere veneer and becomes the dominating force in human life. The enormity of the Christian task in effecting a change of attitude is therefore obvious, for the problem not only embraces personal affairs but also is so vast as to include vital international relationships.

II. ORGANIZED RELIGION 

THE FINEST human agency which man can employ in reëstablishing confidence, and in the interest of human welfare in general, is organized religion. The church is Christ's; it was established in principle by 

Him to promote His kingdom. Let there be no mistake about the sacred place of the church. 

It is patent, however, that organized religion is losing much of its prestige and influence. It has neither adhered to nor succeeded in its original purpose,

REËSTABLISHING CONFIDENCE 

and it should therefore accept its share of responsibility for present dangerous situations. 

This condition of weakness exists largely on account of errors within organized religion itself. Organized religion has often permitted secondary matters to obscure the primary objectives. Organizations have frequently tended to become ends within themselves. 

They have emphasized secondary results, and neglected the essence of their origin and purpose; and, by doing so, they have lost much of their capacity to attract and hold our strongest and finest people. 

Some groups have tended to reduce the religion of Jesus essentially to the status of social service only, and, unwittingly, have thus done widespread harm. 

Types of fruit cannot be substituted for the tree itself. 

Other groups have been inclined to make humanism the essence of religion; as though humanity were an end within itself, and as if man could worship himself or the human race. Still others have devoted themselves to fundamentalism or modernism, pre-millennialism or post-millennialism, theology or scientific interpretations. Christ emphatically cautioned against building a house upon an unstable foundation. 

Furthermore, organized religion has been seriously compromised by various unfortunate practices. High standards have been abused; questionable methods employed; ridiculous conduct tolerated or applauded.

Religious practices have, in many instances, become so cheapened and so indicative of irreverence that they have provoked ridicule and disgust. For illustration and contrast, divine blessings are invoked upon a great cathedral in New York, political conventions in Chicago, and a pack of fox hounds in Baltimore. 

Some churches become places of entertainment. 

Some leaders preach a "gospel" of current events, and fluently solve economic problems about which they actually know very little. 

Moreover, religious organizations have too generally adopted standards of the non-religious world. 

Christian schools have become more and more secularized, and churches are all too often dominated by concepts of temporal power. Organized religion has a peculiar and sacred task. It is, by the very nature of things, in the world, but it must not become spotted by the world. Christian institutions will fail, if not lose even their right to exist, in proportion to the degree in which they become secularized in nature. 

We need to recognize and put actively into effect the principles for which a Christian institution exists, or admit we can hope to lay but vague claim to the respect of those whom we wish to attract. Efficiency, wealth, numbers, force, and systems of external prohibitions have all too often become the chief interest.

We need to be reminded of the chief purpose of the ministry of Jesus. He kept no rolls, made no surveys, sought to establish no official creeds; and, as Simpson states, "nowhere according to the records, does he explicitly condemn slavery and war." His work was more fundamental and consequently more effective. 

It was clearly the intention of Jesus to give man's life such sacredness, meaning, and value as to make man strive to attain to the highest possible standard of personal integrity and to make man wish to become Christ-like in spirit and motive. He knew that a genuinely good man would not knowingly approve of or participate in evil practices. Jesus' mission was to make men good, that they might live the abundantly good life. He denounced hypocrisy, selfishness, indifference, and other sins that corrupt body, mind, and soul. He drove the money changers from the

Of course organizations are necessary; and they, as well as individual men and women, must have objectives. Jesus approved of wholesome organization.

There is only a vague hope for revival of spirituality, or reformation of society, or righteousness, so long as we depend almost altogether upon such things as mere standards of efficiency and resolutions that have the spirit of dictation to individuals, government, and industry. It is futile, in the long run, for religious organizations to undertake to force reforms through employment of secular methods only. This does not mean the reduction of Christianity to dry technical theological discussions, emotionalism, or any thing of the sort. It does mean that one needs. first of all an actual experience of God. Then, if the experience is real, he will try to do his part. III. CONFIDENCE IN MAN 

TO RESTORE Confidence in man, there needs to be a realization of what man is and who man is. A low conception of man breeds disrespect and fosters compromise.

In the first place, man is fundamentally a soul. 

Man does not possess a soul, he is a soul. We must bring back the old Bible picture of man made in the image of God. I am not speaking of an anthropomorphic God, or of the physical form of man. I mean that man is essentially spiritual in his nature, and that he is an immortal soul, and I insist that these two facts must be steadily and strongly emphasized. 

The idea of man as a soul demands high conceptions of one's self and of others, and disallows evil practices. 

It is the fountain of wholesome pride and self-respect, independence, and liberty. It is a truth from which the springs of universal brotherhood and peace must rise. It, moreover, gives man a sacredness and an ultimate destiny which impel him to teach, to heal, to build and endow, and to become a missionary in spirit and in fact at home and in distant lands. It is 12

the heart of our great benevolent organizations, including our schools, churches, and missionary enterprises. 

In the second place, we must consider who man is. 

An institution or a cause is usually judged in terms of its leadership.

We must build character, and we must also seek our best men and women to lead us in politics, education, and religion. The example of leaders is a powerful influence. Reflect for a moment upon standards set by those in authority in places of public trust. For illustration, respect for the law in a given district is rarely higher than the standard set by the legal profession in that district. 

Confidence is never shaken by men of recognized honor, honesty, integrity, and principle. The country has not lost confidence in the Supreme Court, for they are select and picked men. The country never loses confidence in a patriot or in a martyr to a great cause. 

The shadow of Robert E. Lee, despite military defeat, grows longer with the passing years. A great humanitarian compels respect and arouses confidence. 

The grave of Louis Pasteur is constantly covered with fresh flowers. Then, too, people do not forsake a church where the pastor is a God-called, God-endowed man.

It is a custom with Oxford students to haze, with 13

It is said, however, that when David Livingstone walked down the aisle to receive his degree, the students saw the empty sleeve and remembered that an African lion had torn away an arm of this man who dared brave the wilds of the Dark Continent to preach the gospel, and, instead of jeering as was their custom, they arose en masse and stood with bowed heads-for they knew that they were in the presence of a man.

IV. CONFIDENCE IN GOD

Many things have occurred to weaken our faith. 

For illustration, let me cite five influences: 

The recent maelstrom of war between Christian nations, with consequent financial and moral bankruptcy. 

2. The fascination of science and of the world of practical accomplishments, which has led to the obscuring of great truths in other fields. 

3. Erroneous interpretations and applications of great scientific theories. For illustration, the doc- 14

4. A pragmatic philosophy that emphasized the self-sufficiency of man, and reduced the soul, immortality, right, truth, temperance, and good to mere opinion, and made God a creation of the human mind. 

5. The failure of religious leadership to interpret 

Christ properly to the modern world.

These and other matters have had a withering effect. The notion of soul, if not soul itself, has become atrophied. We seem to believe in God as natural law, a myth, Santa Claus, or Uncle Sam.

In a recent book against atheism, the author refutes arguments of atheists; but he does not offer a remedy for the vast indifference toward religion. 

From a recent book on English literature, I take the assertions that there is not among English young men the conviction of a spiritual existence, of a dominating Providence, or of a future life; that England is no longer Christian but pagan; and that the wave of irreligion might be called a tide. One ventures to guess that a survey of our American leadership in government, the professions, society, education, and 15

One of the most beautiful pictures in the Old Testament is that of Isaac reopening the wells of his father. 

The waters had not changed. Likewise, with the . passing of the centuries, man has not essentially changed, truth has not actually varied, God is the same yesterday, today, and forever. The greatest teacher in method and in thought, His words are universally true. He never condemned any worthy private or public endeavor, but, with the finest vision ever exhibited, He saw, beyond wealth, political kingdoms and organized religion, the Kingdom of God in the hearts of men. CHRISTIAN AMBASSADORSHIP¹ FRIEDRICH NIETZSCHE once referred to Christianity as "this hybrid image of ruin derived from nullity, concept, and contradiction, in which . . . all cowardices, and lassitudes of soul have their sanction." A distinguished religious leader recently said that the average Christian now counts for very little that is positive in religion.

Since a brilliant megalomaniac so bitterly hated Christianity, and since millions of professed Christians are so indifferent to their obligations, one should seek the causes of such attitudes. One cause is to be found in ignorance of the essential nature and demands of Christ.

Jesus never suggested indifference toward Himself nor discredited Christian responsibilities. He says, "I and the Father are one"; and states, "And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto myself." At the outset of His ministry, in speaking to His disciples, He said: "Ye are the salt of the earth. ... Ye are the light of the world"; "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven

The Christian task is, then, an incomparable one with respect to personal standards, scope, difficulties, and spiritual and social importance. Christ offers the most serious challenge that man has ever known, for it embodies every fine and noble enterprise of which man is capable. 

You young ministers, and every other Christian as well, are ambassadors of Christ, charged to represent Christ at home and in every other part of the world. 

It is your duty, and your privilege, to go to all types of men-men of diverse mental ability and training, men of every type of social stratum and attainment, men who are subject to various political beliefs and situations, who are of different colors, who follow every conceivable moral code, who live under high and degraded economic conditions, and who follow every imaginable religious belief and practice. And you should command respect and carry conviction.

A secular ambassador must have certain qualifications for his mission. He must be a citizen who possesses such personality, wisdom, character, information, and loyalty as will make him a worthy repre- 24

sentative of his own government and acceptable to the nation to which he goes. You should be no less well qualified for your commission. Let me cite four necessary qualifications.

I. PERSONAL INTEGRITY 

AN AMBASSADOR must be of such character as will give him confidence in his own worth and make him acceptable when his credentials are presented abroad.

Likewise, a Christian should have personal integrity, irreproachable in his own and others' eyes. Let me state some particulars. 

1. There must be uncompromising honesty. This is fundamental. An honest man commands respect, while suspicion of falsehood or duplicity breeds distrust and undermines wholesome influence. One's word, oral or written or implied, should be one's bond. 

No justification can be established for inconsistencies between promises and practices, whatever one's work may be. In general people agree with Pope when he says, "Who dares think one thing, and another tell, My heart detests him as the gates of hell."

Carelessness, that leads to over-indulgence in luxuries and often to financial irregularities, must not be tolerated. The code for a Christian exceeds mere 25

technical obligations. Sir Walter Scott labored, paid his debts, and won universal respect. You should labor, make no unnecessary financial obligations, and pay whatever you owe. Someone has said: "Carelessness with money is a fundamental vice, for it represents somebody's life blood. To be careless with money is to be careless with personality." From negligence of one's financial affairs come abuse of life, loss of reputation, and frequently incalculable institutional injury. 

2. A representative of Christ must be sincere in motive. We live in a maze of problems. Everyone knows how difficult it is, in our involved social and legal situations, for one to meet all assumed and imposed obligations. Students, including lawyers and young ministers, frequently inquire whether one can be honest and, at the same time, succeed in the face of existing competition. Yet, one can do one's best; one can be sincere in motive; and one can thus retain that self-respect which is prerequisite to fine leadership. It was this that made it possible for Sir 

Galahad to say, "My strength is as the strength of ten, Because my heart is pure." 

3. Furthermore, Christian leaders must be intelligent. If ignorance is not vice, it is the foul mother 26

from which issue all sorts of vile creatures. Cowardice, waste, morbidity, intemperance, lusts, and, worst of all, selfishness, are frequently the progeny of ignorance. The historical highways of religion itself are bordered by wrecks and tragedies caused by superstition, bigotry, fanaticism, intolerance, compromise, expediency, and opportunism. Sentiment, feeling, and gross emotions are too frequently interpreted as divine revelation. It borders on blasphemy to assert that God reveals himself under certain circumstances. 

Intelligence does not necessarily mean academic proficiency, although there is no danger that one may acquire too much information. One is wise to seek the best schools and great teachers and to remain with them until one has met successfully their high standards of scholarship. But some people, superficial and sophisticated, are educated in the worst possible manner, for, with a little information, their minds have become impervious to truth. The distinction between information and knowledge should be made. 

Plato is wise in suggesting that awareness of the eternal is essential to knowledge or wisdom. He says: "My opinion is that in the world of knowledge the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort; and, when seen, is also inferred to be the universal author of all things beautiful and right, parent of light and of the lord of light in this visible 27

world, and the immediate source of reason and truth in the intellectual; and that this is the power upon which he who would act rationally either in public or private life must have his eyes fixed." Paul is right when he asserts that faith is essential to knowledge. 

Says he, "The things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal.' وو Christian leaders should be our most highly trained men and women. It is a gigantic work to which they have committed themselves. They must meet every type of situation. I. THE HOME 

THE HOME is for man, as for many lower animals, a focus of industry, safety, stability, hope, and happiness. This summer, in the garden where these words were written, wood thrushes built their nest in a shrub, cardinals called to each other with lucent notes, and mated mocking birds sang and worked. 

The home is our greatest creative institution. It is not a sculptor's shed where marble is shaped into statues, or a studio where canvas is transformed into portraits, or a scientific laboratory where elements are revealed, or a forum where orators charm with persuasive speech. The home is more than any one or all of these, for in the home "the veined marble of flesh and blood" is shaped into living statues; the colors of righteousness are painted on living minds; the elements of eternal truth are discovered and bequeathed to undying souls; and speech may be as of the high places of eternity. Of all the institutions, 44

divinely endowed and seeking to conserve life's riches, the home is peerless. 

The dominant atmosphere of the home ought to be refinement; the primary creator of this refinement is woman, for the perfection of human attainment and creation is womanhood at its best. By the very nature of circumstances, the home has been, is, and will continue to be, woman's realm. Her tenderness, refinement, gentleness, and unassuming culture make her supreme in her world. It is in the home that she is supreme and serves best. 

One cannot deny, however, the existence now of many disintegrating influences about and within the home.

In addition to all of the external forces that attack the home, circumstances within the home itself have greatly changed. Woman is no longer enslaved by domestic responsibilities. As more leisure has come, her interests have become multiplied. Now she is lured by travel, business, professions, and careers.

The potential equal of man socially, politically and intellectually, she now experiences wretched unrest to become independent or to express an assumed independence; and as often as not she is in error in the method she employs to assert her rights and to follow her new inclinations. She has not yet adjusted herself to her new and deserved freedom. A woman's 45

nature is not that of man, and, irrespective of any wish or effort, her primary interests can never be the same as his. She almost invariably reflects unfavorably upon herself when she departs from womanly interests and ideals. And man's esteem for her is enhanced in proportion to the extent of her conformity to womanly standards.

If the home is to be saved, woman must save it by preserving the ideals and atmosphere of the home. 

Many women must build homes, and with grace and charm rule them with uncompromising firmness. 

Other women, of necessity, must enter the realm of business. And why not? How contemptible is a parasite! But those women who leave the home as such should make a home in spirit wherever they go. 

In the school, the office, the hospital, the spirit of the home should prevail, that it may "transform places and transfigure lives by its extended atmosphere." 

If woman fails in this obligation, she will have betrayed her greatest trust. Better that she forget careers and sever her relationship with public institutions than permit disintegration of the home. 

II. THE BEAUTIFUL 

THE OCCURRENCE of beauty is all but universal. It is found in star and atom, marsh and mountain, the tint of the rose, the symphony of birds at dawn, the 46

Beauty is essential to culture. Feeling and emotion, the mental counterparts of beauty, are as truly a part of mind as are processes of reason. They are as essential to thought as color to canvas, and tones and harmony to music. Without feeling or appreciation of beauty, life would be reduced to cold rationality, and man would become a Frankenstein-like creature who, having destroyed his means of happiness, would finally experience disaster. 

Since beauty must be conserved and developed, may we indicate, as illustrations, two types with which you are especially concerned?

I. Genuineness. Only the genuine and the flawless bring deepest satisfaction. One may pay twenty dollars for a dozen American Beauty roses; artificial roses can be purchased for fifty cents. A Rembrandt sells for a fortune; displays of "art" may be found in dime stores. But the imitation, in art or in human life, deserves no place in our esteem. Veneer may be attractive, but it is veneer; the superficial and sophisticated may be glamorous, but it is still the superficial and sophisticated.

Good character is the basis of confidence in one's own self and in others, and confidence is the parent 47

of respect, love, and progress. When you, through any means, have won complete respect for yourself and others, you may move with the majesty of a princess, unafraid and unashamed. 

2. Refinement. A few years ago someone proposed the invention of an instrument delicate enough to receive spiritualistic messages. Such an instrument is obviously impossible, but occasionally one does observe a person so gentle in bearing, so highly sensitive to values, and so considerate of feeling as to command respect and become a benediction. One may define a gentleman as one who would not, even unintentionally, unnecessarily embarrass another. Sensitivity is an index to culture. Woman's greatest power consists in the dignity and authority of her refinement, charm, and grace.

True refinement, the best criterion of civilization, must be preserved and cultivated. The responsibility for its preservation and development is primarily yours, for woman sets the standard for herself and for man.

I. THE CHRISTIAN COLLEGE AND NATIONAL PROBLEMS 

THE CHRISTIAN college is an indispensable aid in the solution of our national problems. 

A cause of the present troublesome situation is to be found in the fact that Western civilization has been educated to worship power. In the early sixteenth century, man discovered a new world and a new universe. He then turned eagerly from an inadequate, unsatisfactory, and turgid religiousness to the fascinating field of natural science and began to chart anew the course of Western progress. Francis Bacon, urging that old idols be smashed, himself set up a colossal golden image of practical science, sounded a trumpet, and proclaimed that all of the old academic gods should be utterly forsaken for this new one. 

In 1859,

Darwin published his epoch-making book, the Origin of the Species. In 1879, Wundt established the first laboratory for experimental, descriptive examination of the human mind. Disciples are usually not so great as their masters, and soon numerous dangerous and false interpretations appeared. Among them, nationalism and other concepts of power arose. The idea of the "omnipotence of force" challenged the interests of man, and won.

This idea of power has expressed itself in numerous 79

ways. Its unsavory influence may be observed in the super-state; in the bitter conflicts in the field of commercialism; in heartless capitalism; in ruthlessness in organized labor; in unscrupulous rivalry in politics; in monopolistic mergers in business and professions; in the soulless producers of certain forms of public entertainment; in the widespread power of the editor of one or a chain of publications; and even in secular trends in the field of religion, which bring Christian institutions all too frequently into unrecognized competition with Christianity.

The immortal Plato says that "when a man of brass or iron guards the State, it will be destroyed." 

Modern standards of efficiency and power have the sound of brass and iron. As an example of error, we did not stop with giving science the high place it deserves, and we did not clearly realize that scientific discoveries should be the oil and wine and beast of burden with which we may aid mankind. We deified science and worshipped technology, and now our use of certain products of science threatens us with destruction. Another war, and our civilization may be hopelessly crippled! Captains of industry were glorified, and they are bankrupt. We tried to live by bread alone, and now we are hungry. Paradoxically, there are wealth and poverty, abundance and 80

destitution, physical power and fear. Millions-poor or pauperized-clamor for relief; and governments, local and national, stagger under indebtedness to the verge of collapse.

Our weakness lies in our training. In Western civilization, information is a giant, wisdom a pygmy. 

Western civilization has been called a civilization of "ignorant knowledge," and a "compound of astonishing learning and incomparable ignorance." What does this mean?

Our social organism has been amazingly developed ; its soul has become enfeebled. It is like a giant animal with a tiny brain, or a Goliath with a dormant conscience. This abnormal condition has permitted the development of dangerous attitudes, demands, and practices, which, like unsuspected cancers, have gradually invaded customs, sacred contracts, laws, constitutions, ideals, and spiritual concepts. Now, unless a master surgeon is found, collapse is imminent. Local, national, and international conditions are symptoms which show that Western civilization may not much longer withstand the crimes and pernicious evils arising from half-truths.

It should be recognized that our economic and social structure must rest upon Christian principles. 

The codes governing capital and labor, professions, 81

public officials, and others, should be written in the brilliant light of Christ. A Christian college reflects this light.

Moreover, wholesome internationalism can arise only through acceptance of the principles for which a Christian college lives. One speaks of the reduction of time and space and refers to the world as only a unit. It is not a unit in spirit. One speaks of the economic advantages of leagues; but now jealousy and economic warfare predominate. Covenants are made, and under certain circumstances become, and will continue to be, mere scraps of paper. Peace conferences are held, and chains of forts are erected. 

We babble about peace, and build greater armaments; we glorify brotherhood, and, in the name of civilization, exploit and crush the weak. 

The springs of international brotherhood must rise, if they appear at all, from deep down in the feelings and emotions of the human family. One cannot think of a more difficult or important task than that of effecting a change from the present attitude of jealously and fear, and the desire for temporal power, to an attitude of fellowship and coöperation. Such a change can be realized only through education permeated by the principles of Christ. 82

II. THE CHRISTIAN COLLEGE AND THE HOME 

THE CHRISTIAN college is a means of saving the home and, through the home, society. 

The decay of the home, with reference to its ideals, practices, and authority, is obvious. One thinks of the lapse from sobriety, temperance, refinement, honor, and decency. Conventions are violated with impunity; the sense of decency is becoming atrophied; practical interests and excitement threaten to supplant culture, sensitiveness, and moral and ethical codes.

The gravest danger, of all the multitude of evils now confronting the home, exists in the attitude of woman. 

The highest attainment of the human family, its most God-like accomplishment, is motherhood at its best. 

If woman, with her new social and political freedom, chooses to sell her incomparable birthright for a mess of the pottage of superficiality, sensuous excitement and cheap imitation, the home and the next generation will be lost. The home, the members of the home, and social standards in general are no better than are the ideals which woman sets for them. If such a choice were necessary, better that woman forsake the church than neglect the home!

Some plain facts need emphasis in this connection. 

A normal child comes into this world with only a po- 83

tential mind. Generally speaking, his thoughts, his ideals, his motives, his standards, his intellectual likes and dislikes are a product, directly or indirectly, of his environment. He becomes a part of all that he meets. He is especially a part of his early home life. 

Children walk in the footprints of their elders. If nothing else were involved, the integrity and authority of the home should be reëstablished for the safety of our children.

From infancy on, the child should have, for illustration, positive training that leads to a feeling of repulsion toward wrong and a love of right. An adult is trained only with great difficulty. One knows of very few instances of adult reformation. The church itself rarely brings about the reformation of an adult. A thousand times more emphasis than now given needs to be placed on the training of children. 

The church has, moreover, made a colossal error in assuming that grace and reformation of body and mind are synonymous. Grace is free; reformation may be acquired in time. Desirable reformation of society will be attained, if it is ever realized, chiefly through intelligent and wholesome training of children from infancy to maturity. 

A Christian college, despite the belief of many parents, is not a reformatory. It should not be regarded as such. But a Christian college does strive to train 84

its students to become intelligent Christian parents; and intelligent Christian parents do more than any others toward making a good home. They show righteous indignation toward the unwholesome. They fight the insidious influence of cheap print, profane music, vulgar tastes, low ideals, insincerity, and compromise. They possess appreciation of the highest attributes of life—such as joy, love, loyalty, respect, patience, goodness, and self-sacrifice and impart these attributes to others. And from these, security and respectability grow.

Purely secular education often leads to one point of view, and, therefore, to extreme dogmatism. The goose-step as practiced by press and schools and Church in Italy, Germany, and Russia is a vivid illustration of what might occur in America. 

It is obvious that no more obnoxious forms of intolerance exist than those sometimes found in secular circles. To a Christian college, however, belong all forms of truth-scientific, literary, philosophical, spiritual. 

They are hers to possess, to examine, to criticize, to cultivate, to enjoy, and to use to find life and freedom. 

In a Christian college all truth should be recognized as God's truth, wherever and in whatever garb it appears. This leaves no place for bigotry, intolerance, or provincialism. Truth, as reflected in science, in literature, in philosophy, and even in pagan religions, never conflicts with Christianity and right living. 

Conformity to truth, not to creeds or license, assures freedom. A nation of intelligent Christians will not be enslaved in body, mind, or spirit. 

A Christian college recognizes in theory and in practice that knowledge leads to virtue, and truth to freedom. On the campus of the Christian college the only authority is the Christ; and one may assert that this fact affords the brightest hope for the attainment of intellectual and spiritual freedom.

V. THE CHRISTIAN COLLEGE AND ORGANIZED RELIGION 

FINALLY, the Christian college is a means of saving organized religion and the interests which it promotes. 

At the present time, organized religion is bewildered 88

by its enemies and by its own impotence. Some regard religion as an encumbrance of which civilization should be rid. Others say that it is only a political necessity. Still others ignore or ridicule religion. 

Types of atheism and agnosticism are very prevalent. 

The majority of people are indifferent toward religious organizations. 

Organized religion itself suffers from enormous debts, crushing suspicion, emasculated convictions, a panicky attitude, and the consequent loss of authority over even a large part of its own constituency. Poor training and weakness of convictions have produced a fearful dearth of Christian statesmanship. There are those who say that spiritual reformation will come, if it appears at all, from sources other than organized religion. Altogether, it seems that there is the threat of irretrievable spiritual bankruptcy.

Organized religion is in purpose, and can be in fact, our finest agency for the promotion of the Kingdom of Christ on earth. Excuses for criticism and sources of weakness must, therefore, be removed. 

The fact that organizations are a means and not an end must be recognized, for Christian institutions are 

Christ's institutions.

The Christian college contributes to the improvement of the present situation by training leaders adequate in scholarship, culture, and spirituality. And 89

there must be adequate training of religious leaders if organized religion is to command respect at home and abroad.

Clergymen should be our best trained men. They have the most difficult and exacting field to cultivate, and the hardest work to do. One's indifference to problems, standards, and obligations obviously disqualifies him for this sacred task. 

Laymen, including teachers, physicians, editors, lawyers, and others, are indispensable to the support of organized religion. Leaders in the development of religious homes must be had. Parents must have convictions, even if they are imperfect, about the Sabbath, religious services, and reverence. Foundational training for organized religion rests in the home. Missions are a Christian mandate. They demand the highest type of men and women who, in technical training and in spirit, may command full respect of the people with whom they live and work. The Christian Sunday school is indispensable to the Christian program, for its work is done in the most vital and formative period of the individual's life. How poverty-stricken the churches would be without this part of their work! 

There must be trained leadership, to the end that the effectiveness of this work may be doubled and redoubled. How may such leaders be had? 

Christian preachers and laymen are "the light of 90
