METHODS OF DISCRIMINATION By RALPH E. CURTISS Assistant General Counsel 

In the three preceding issues of Repeal Review, discriminatory liquor laws have been discussed and their inherent dangers pointed out. As a logical complement to these articles, this article will outline the methods that are, or might be, used by the several States in discriminating against the goods produced in sister States, to prepare the way for a state-by-state analysis of methods actually in use. 

Necessity for Classification

Court decisions under the 21st Amendment have indicated that the States are now empowered to place burdens on interstate commerce as long as the products concerned are intoxicating beverages.¹ 

Such a widespread variety of discriminatory liquor laws has resulted from the exercise of this power that a further classification must be made in order to attack the problem where it affects most adversely the Nation's interstate trade economy. 

As a basis for a classification, we use the purpose for which the discriminatory law was enacted. Those laws passed solely to aid in the administration of the liquor control acts of the States, we call "Justifiable" Discrimination. Those laws passed for the purpose of favoring local products or persons over those from outside the 

State, we call "Hostile" Discrimination. That a classification upon this basis is reasonable is supported by the language of the court in Evansville Brewing Association v. Excise Commission (1915) 2 in which a license fee on agents of out-of-state brewers equal in amount to that paid by local brewers was upheld, and the court in its decision stated that any disadvantage caused to foreign manufacturers by such statutes "did not appear to arise out of any intention on the part of the State Legislature to make a hostile discrimination against foreign manufacturers". 

Justifiable Discrimination 

This classification, based upon the fact that the laws and regulations therein were promulgated solely for the purpose of aiding in the administration of the liquor control laws of the States, would include provisions for: (1) prohibition against the exercise of any

control over a local retailer by an out-of-state producer (tiedhouse); (2) registration by nonresidents and inspection of their records of sales to persons within the State; (3) the licensing of out-of-state salesmen and missionary men; (4) the conformance to the State law concerning out-of-state corporations doing business within the State; (5) limiting importation to the State Alcoholic 

Beverage Control Board in those. States using the State stores system; (6) certificates of approval for an out-of-state producer to ship into the State, where such certificates are for the purpose of assuring standards required of local manufacturers. 

It is true that most of the above provisions were, prior to the 

21st Amendment, designated by the courts as discriminatory and were therefore invalid;¹ but because of the nature of the products involved and the purpose for which the provisions were enacted, we designate them as justifiably discriminatory laws. It cannot be doubted that there is a serious social problem inherent in the commerce in alcoholic beverages, and for this reason certain latitudes in its control must be allowed to the States. 

That justifiably discriminatory laws are purely devices by which the States may be aided in the administration of their liquor control laws is further borne out by correspondence which Repeal Associates has had with the various State Liquor Control Administrators. For example, one administrator has stated that certain of these provisions in his State were promulgated solely for the purpose of expediting the collection of the State's excise or luxury stamp tax, and that without these provisions enforcement of that part of the law would be impossible; another has said that certain restrictions of this type were imposed merely to enable his State to exercise the same control over out-of-state producers shipping into the State as the State already exercised over those located within its borders.

Thus it is that, within this mass of laws that are commonly called "Discriminatory", there is a classification of justifiably discriminatory laws without which the States could neither collect their properly imposed taxes nor insure themselves against the handling of alcoholic beverages by unresponsible parties within their State. boundaries.

Hostile Discrimination 

Under the classification of hostilely discriminatory laws would fall all those enacted primarily for the purpose of favoring local products or persons. Laws in this category provide for discrimina- 

1 110 A. L. R. 931.

tion upon either of two bases: (1) solely upon the geographical location of the producer over a State boundary; (2) upon the geographical location over a State boundary plus some other condition.

In the first instance would be placed a prohibition of importation of all out-of-state liquor while permitting the commerce within the State. This, the most severe of all methods, has never been used as yet, but it varies only in degree from a provision, which is in use at the present time, restricting the amount of liquor that can be imported.

Another method is a provision for a lower excise tax on beverages manufactured within the State or manufactured from products grown within the State, which in reality amounts to a protective tariff.

Still another method in this classification would be the enactment of special restrictions, e.g., license fees, on out-of-state dealers or on local dealers if they handle out-of-state products. Such restrictions would be hostilely discriminatory if enacted purely for the purpose of favoring local persons or products. When the fee is large, or the restriction is such that local persons or products are given an advantage over those from outside the State, then there is hostile discrimination. The amount of the differential in the fee would indicate whether a particular restriction should be classed as hostilely discriminatory or justifiably discriminatory. 

In the second classification of discriminatory laws, those based upon the geographical location over a State boundary plus some other condition, would be found such laws as those excluding a specific brand of liquor because it did not meet certain procedural standards, or standards of manufacture. For example, a law prohibiting the importation of alcoholic beverages unless certain labor standards were met in their manufacture (not yet used), or a law prohibiting the importation of any brand of liquor whose label was not registered in the patent office of the United States.¹

Also in this category should be placed the so-called "anti-discriminatory" laws. To describe this type of law the word "retaliatory" is much more appropriate than the phrase "anti-discriminatory". To persons familiar with the problems of liquor control, the phrase "anti-discriminatory law" connotes a law passed to prevent, by intimidation, other States from discriminating against the products of the enacting State. However, to many persons the term "anti-discriminatory" connotes a law preventing discrim- 

1 Minnesota law.

ination by the enacting State. For that reason the word "retaliatory" will be used throughout this article.

Retaliatory laws provide for (1) exclusion of all liquors originating in specific States because those States discriminate against the products of the enacting States; or (2) a protective tariff imposed by the enacting State equal in amount to the protective tariff assessed by the discriminating State; or (3) special restrictions on local or out-of-state dealers handling out-of-state products, equivalent to the special restrictions put upon the same persons in the discriminating States who handle the products of the enacting States.

The retaliatory law is ordinarily enacted for the dual purpose of preventing another State from passing laws that would discriminate against the enacting State, or if such laws are already in effect, to compel the discriminating State to repeal them. 

This method of obtaining interstate cooperation has proven to ´ be effective, but it is also dangerous because it opens an avenue for more discrimination. For example, prior to March 2 of this year the laws of Indiana provided that the importation of beer should be through "ports-of-entry". The port-of-entry permittees charged a fee for handling the beer, thereby creating a substantial differential in distribution costs in favor of the Indiana brewers. Missouri and Michigan had retaliatory liquor laws on their statute books, and both States would have enforced them against Indiana except for a temporary agreement between Michigan and Indiana and because the enforcement of the Missouri law had been enjoined, pending a court decision on the validity of its law. Other neighboring States had introduced bills providing for retaliatory laws. 

Consequently, in order to protect its export business, Indiana was forced to repeal its discriminatory law on March 2 of this year. 

Indiana will be an interesting State to watch in the near future because one week after the repeal of its discriminatory law a retaliatory law was enacted. This law charges the Control Board with the duty of investigating the laws of other States, and when in their opinion a State discriminates against the products of Indiana, certain embargoes and prohibitions become effective. 

Although Indiana's law is of the "anti-discriminatory" type, it can in effect become discriminatory because of the provision that the opinion of the Board may determine whether or not an embargo is to be put upon the products of a particular State. As a

matter of practice the Board may determine that a State which has a justifiably discriminatory law is in fact a State which discriminates against the products of Indiana. In such a case, the other 

State would be administering its liquor laws with no thought of favoring its own products, and thus not hostilely discriminating against Indiana products; but the Indiana Control Board might designate that State as one discriminating against Indiana and, therefore, prohibit the importation of alcoholic beverages from that State. Thus, through a misunderstanding, or a misinterpretation, of a State's laws a trade barrier would again arise. 

A situation similar to that described above has actually arisen in at least one case. Acting within the scope of its statutory authority, the Control Board of Michigan designated several States as discriminating, among which was Washington. Those features of the Washington law which were called discriminatory were actually within that class called justifiably discriminatory, as is shown by an excerpt from the Fourth Annual Report of the Washington State Liquor Control Board, which is here quoted : 

"CERTIFICATES OF APPROVAL AND BEER IMPORTER'S LICENSES: 

"Certificates of Approval and the Beer Importer's licenses recommended by the Board and adopted by the legislature were proposed for the sole purpose of securing control over the operation of outside breweries selling beer in the State of Washington, rendering them subject to the same control that was heretofore exercised over breweries licensed within the State. 

The fact that the fees adopted were at a nominal figure emphasizes the fact that neither revenue nor discrimination against outside beer were considerations. Because statutory provisions which have been adopted in other States were designed as discriminatory legislation, charges were made by outside breweries that the provisions adopted in this state were discriminatory with the result that misinformation and misunderstanding as to these provisions have developed. "The Certificate of Approval and Importer's license merely requires a showing of the beer sales in the State of Washington and in the event irregularities are uncovered involving the sale of such beer the Board has a licensee within the state on whom it can place responsibility. Outside beer may still come into the State of Washington without the burden of tax or license fees in excess of that required for beer manufactured and sold within the state. Provisions such as these are essential in order to achieve necessary control over the sale of beer. 

An examination of the statutory change and its administration will convince anyone that there is no element of discrimination in the law and that its sole purpose is regulatory."

It is true that the Michigan retaliatory law was instrumental in securing the repeal of the Indiana discriminatory law, but it is also true that, through a misinterpretation of the Washington law, it may very possibly give impetus to the passage of a retaliatory law by the Washington legislature which, if enforced against Michigan, would create a trade barrier. 

Summary 

There are so many varying types of discriminatory laws and regulations in effect today that there must be some well understood definitions and classifications set out before there can be any orderly discussion of the problems involved. 

Discriminatory Liquor Laws I. Justifiable Discrimination. 

(Those laws and regulations promulgated for the purpose of aiding in the administration of the liquor control acts.) 

A. Prohibition against the exercise of any control over a local retailer by an out-of-state producer (tied-house). 

B. Registration by nonresidents and inspection of their records of sales to persons within the State. 

C. Licensing of out-of-state salesmen and missionary men. 

D. Conformance to the State law concerning out-of-state corporations doing business within the State. 

E. Limiting importation to the State Alcoholic Beverage Control 

Board in those States using the State stores system. 

F. Certificates of approval for an out-of-state producer to ship into the State.

II. Hostile Discrimination. 

(Those laws and regulations promulgated for the purpose of favoring local products or persons.)

A. Based solely upon geographical location of the producer over a State boundary. 

1. Exclusion of all out-of-state liquor while permitting the commerce within the State.

2. Restriction on the amount of liquor that can be imported.

3. Protective tariff. 

4. Special restrictions on either local or out-of-state dealers if they handle out-of-state products. 

B. Based upon the geographical location of the producer over a 

State boundary plus some other condition. 

1. Exclusion of a specific brand of liquor because it does not meet certain procedural standards or standards of manufacture. 

2. Retaliatory laws. (The "other condition" being discrimination by another State.) a. Exclusion of all alcoholic beverages or all alcoholic beverages of a specific type originating in discriminating States. b. Protective tariff equal to that imposed by the discriminating States. c. Special restrictions on local or out-of-state dealers handling out-of-state products equal in amount to the special restrictions put upon the same persons in the discriminating States who handle the products of the enacting State.

State by State Analysis of Hostilely Discriminatory Laws 

In order to point out specifically the methods used by the several States to discriminate against the products of other States, that is to say, favoring home products or persons over those from other States, a brief discussion of the discriminatory features of each State law follows: 

Alabama.

The retaliatory law of Alabama provides that no alcoholic liquors or beverages of any kind shall be sold in this State which are manufactured in any State which, by its laws or in the opinion of the Alcoholic Beverage Control Board by its practices, discriminates with respect to the sale therein against alcoholic liquors or beverages manufactured, distributed, or sold at wholesale in Alabama. 

(Laws 1936-37 (Extra Session) Act No. 66, Sec. 63.)

This law gives a great deal of power to the Board to decide what State or States discriminate against Alabama products. It is the same type of retaliatory law now in force in Indiana and has inherent in it the same defects and benefits that were discussed in the section dealing with retaliatory laws. (Supra, p. 6) 

Notwithstanding the fact that this law has been in effect in Alabama since February 2, 1937, there was introduced in the legislature on February 14 of this year a bill (H. B. 262) containing, among other things, the same provisions for retaliation. To date. the bill has made no progress. Arizona.

Prior to this year Arizona has never had a law in force which would amount to hostile discrimination, but on March 13, 1939, a law was passed (S. B. 154, introduced February 7) providing that distillers shall register with the Superintendent of the Department of Liquor Licenses and Control each solicitor or salesman. Thus far the provision would fall within the classification of justifiably discriminatory laws, but there is a further provision that a fee of one dollar must accompany the registration when made by a local distiller and a fee of ten dollars when the registration is made by an out-of-state distiller. The differential in the amount of the fees charged indicates that the law was enacted not only as an aid to local control, but also as a means of favoring the local producer. Arkansas.

Arkansas discriminates by two methods in favor of wines manufactured within the State from products grown within the state: 

First, locally produced wines are subject to an excise tax of 5 cents per gallon, while those produced outside the State are subject to an excise tax of 50 cents per gallon; second, a local winery may wholesale its own products without a wholesaler's license, while a person selling out-of-state wine must secure a wholesaler's license, the cost of which is $700 per year. (Laws 1935, Act No. 109, Sec. 

3(b); Laws 1935, Act No. 69, Sec. 8; Laws 1935, Act No. 108; Article IV, Sec. 3.) California.

The California Alcoholic Beverage Control Act, as originally enacted, required payment of a fee of $100 for a distilled spirits and wines importer's license, and a fee of $500 for a beer importer's 10

license. Although these discriminatory provisions were upheld in the first case¹ under the Twenty-first Amendment to come to the United States Supreme Court, in 1937 they were rescinded. At the present time an importer must have a license, but there is no fee charged therefor. Connecticut.

There is a retaliatory law in effect in Connecticut. It provides that the tax commissioner shall impose, by regulation, an additional reciprocal tax on alcoholic beverages from States in which beverages from Connecticut are taxed at a higher rate than domestic products. (Laws of Connecticut, 1937 Biennial Supplement, Chap. 

151, sec. 626-d (s))

In the exercise of this statutory duty, the tax commissioner has imposed additional taxes on wines manufactured in, from grapes or fruits grown in, the States of Michigan, Georgia, and New Mexico at the following rates:

Michigan: Six cents per gallon when imported for blending purposes only, and 46 cents per gallon when imported for all other purposes. 

Georgia: Thirty cents per gallon on all wines having alcoholic content of not more than 14 per cent of alcohol by volume and 30 cents per gallon on all wines having an alcoholic content of between 14 per cent and 21 per cent by volume. 

New Mexico: Four cents per gallon on all wines manufactured in New Mexico from grapes grown in a vineyard in said State of New Mexico which vineyard is owned and which wines are manufactured by any persons except a corporation, firm, copartnership, or association.

In the present session of the Legislature H. B. 1260, providing for a tax of 7 cents per gallon on out-of-state beer, has been introduced. To date the bill has made no progress. Florida.

The retaliatory law in effect in Florida provides that the Board, Department, or Official charged with the duty of administering and enforcing the laws of the State governing the manufacture and

State imposes a greater occupational, excise, gross or net receipts, or sales tax, or any tax based upon the quantity, or the cost or price on Florida wines than it imposes on wines manufactured locally, then the differential is to be ascertained and certified to the comptroller and all officers charged with the duty of collecting occupational and excise taxes from wholesale and retail dealers in Florida. By this method, Florida seeks to put the same burden upon out-of-state wines that the particular State concerned im poses upon Florida wines. (Laws of 1935, Chap. 16774, Sec. 9) 

The regulations of the beverage department discriminate against out-of-state spirits by providing that neither distillers nor rectifiers are authorized to sell to anyone other than qualified distributors; except that those qualified to both distill and rectify, and who first distill from raw material and then rectify the beverage in Florida, may sell to both distributors and vendors such beverages as they both distill and rectify in Florida. Thus all out-of-state distilled spirits must pass through a licensed distributor who pays a fee of $1250 per year, while distilled spirits that are both distilled and rectified within the State, for which plants the fee is $750 per year, may be sold directly to the vendor without going through a licensed distributor. Georgia.

Georgia discriminates by imposing a lower excise tax on local products than on out-of-state products. The tax on local naturally fermented wines containing not more than 14 per cent of alcohol by volume is 5 cents per gallon, but if such wines are imported, the tax is 40 cents per gallon. If the wine contains between 14 per cent and 21 per cent of alcohol by volume, local wine is taxed at the rate of 30 cents per gallon, but if imported, it is taxed at the rate of 60 cents per gallon. (Laws of 1937, Act 378, Sec. 3)

There is also in Georgia a tax of 25 cents per wine gallon on all fortified wines made exclusively from fruits, berries, grapes, and other products grown in Georgia, while the tax is 50 cents per wine gallon on all fortified wines containing over 14 per cent of alcohol by volume where such wines are made from, or contain ingredients made from, products grown outside the State of Georgia. 

Distilled spirits manufactured in Georgia from Georgia-grown 12

products are taxed at the rate of 50 cents per wine gallon, as against a tax of $1.00 per wine gallon for imported spirits. Alcohol manufactured in the State of Georgia, not from Georgia-grown products, is taxed at the rate of $1.00 per wine gallon, while all alcohol imported into the State of Georgia is taxed at the rate of $2.00 per wine gallon. (Laws of 1938, Act 297, Secs. 11 and 12) Illinois.

The Liquor Control Act of Illinois contains no provisions for hostile discrimination, but on March 7 of this year, H. B. 377, which provides for retaliation, was introduced into the state legislature. 

The bill requires the Governor to examine the laws of the several States relating to alcoholic beverages to determine if any of the States have discriminatory laws, and further provides that beer manufactured in a State which the Governor designates as discriminatory shall not be allowed to be transported into or imported into Illinois. To date the bill has made no progress. Indiana. 

Prior to March 2 of this year, beer wholesalers in Indiana could buy out-of-state beer only through a licensed importing agent or a port of entry permittee, and because the two latter licensees imposed a service charge upon the beer as it came into the State, a differential in distribution costs was created in favor of Indiana beer. However, H. B. 166, which became law on March 2, 1939, abolished the licenses for importing agents and port of entry licensees, thereby revoking Indiana's discriminatory practice. 

On March 9 of this year a retaliatory measure was written into the laws of Indiana. (S. B. 38) By this law the Indiana Alcoholic 

Beverage Commission is charged with the duty of investigating the laws, rules, regulations, ordinances, and practices of the several States, territories, districts, political subdivisions, and municipalities of the United States to determine whether in the opinion of the Board said laws and regulations discriminate against the intoxicating liquors manufactured, produced, processed in, or exported from the State of Indiana. In the event that the Commission finds that a particular State is discriminating against Indiana, they may either prohibit the importation into Indiana of one or more classes of intoxicating beverages originating in the discriminating State, or they may assess additional taxes on the products of the discriminating State. The law specifically states that the 13

orders of the Commission shall not be restricted to the particular class of intoxicating beverages that are being discriminated against, but may include in their order any or all classes of intoxicating liquors produced in the discriminating State. Iowa.

The only provision in the Iowa laws or regulations which amounts to hostile discrimination is found in a regulation, promulgated June 1, 1934, and amended September 9, 1935, which provides that manufacturers of native wines from grapes, cherries, other fruit juices, or honey grown and produced in Iowa may sell on the premises where said ingredients are produced, in quantities not to exceed 5 gallons at any one time to any one customer, and not to exceed 500 gallons for any one year to all customers, for consumption off the premises. There is no license required to exercise this privilege, which amounts to a discrimination in favor of small producers. Louisiana.

The laws of Louisiana discriminate in favor of local wines in that they do not require a dealer to secure a license for the handling of orange or berry wines made from oranges or berries grown in that State.

Although the Attorney General has ruled that an out-of-state wholesaler is not required to have a license of any character in order to ship alcoholic beverages into the State, the Louisiana law has a potentially discriminatory provision. This provision states that a dealer must pay $1000 for a permit to do business if he operates from an established wholesale liquor business in a storehouse or warehouse other than a public or bonded warehouse within the State, but, if he operates from a public or bonded warehouse and does not maintain an established business within the State, he must pay a fee of $10,000. (Laws of 1934, Act 15, Sec. 4) Maine.

In the foregoing discussion of types of discrimination it was pointed out that restrictions on all importations to the Control Board in those States using the State Stores System were merely justifiable discrimination. The reason for placing such laws in the justifiably discriminatory classification was that there was no intention on the part of the legislature to favor home products by such a provision. However, in the case of Maine, their provision 14

restricting importation to the Control Board amounts to hostile discrimination because of the further provision that the Commission shall in its purchases of alcoholic liquors give priority, wherever feasible, to those made from the agricultural products of the 

State of Maine.

In addition, the laws of Maine provide that a license fee of $100 per year shall be imposed upon distiliers and brewers using Maine agricultural products exclusively, and the fee is increased to $3000 per year when the distillers or brewers use the products of other States exclusively. When distillers and brewers use in part the agricultural products of Maine and in part the agricultural products of other States, the amount of the fee is determined by the Commission and must be directly proportionate to the source and quantity of such raw material and based upon the foregoing differential.

Wineries using exclusively the agricultural products of Maine pay an annual license fee of $50, and wineries using in part the agricultural products of other States or foreign countries pay a fee of $50 plus an excise tax of 4 cents per gallon on liquid raw materials and 2 cents per pound on solid or semi-solid raw material. 

Thus by the use of a differential in the license fee imposed, and by the use of an express direction to the Commission to favor Maine products, the products of other States are subject to a hostile discrimination. (Laws 1933, Chap. 298) Massachusetts.

The present laws of Massachusetts contain no provisions that amount to hostile discrimination, although there were several bills introduced into the present session of the State legislature that if passed would discriminate against out-of-state products. 

H. B. 836 provides for control of importation of malt beverages by requiring approval of each order and assessing a fee of $1.00 per barrel for such approval.

H. B. 1505 provides for the establishment of a tax of $1.00 per barrel on all beer imported into the State. 

S. B. 153 provides for a tax of $1.00 per barrel on beer manufactured within the State and $2.00 per barrel on beer imported from any other State. 15

Michigan.

The Michigan law provides that there shall be levied and collected by the Commission on all wines sold in the State and manufactured from grapes or fruits not grown in the State a tax at the rate of 50 cents per gallon; but if the wines are manufactured in Michigan from grapes grown in Michigan for which the wineries, blenders, or rectifiers have paid in cash to Michigan grape growers $55 per ton or more at the shipping point, the buyer furnishing at his expense all necessary packages or containers and paying transportation charges beyond such shipping point, the tax shall be reduced to 4 cents per gallon. The same tax reduction is allowed on wines manufactured in Michigan from Michigan-grown fruits other than grapes; on such wines when blended with wine or wine spirits manufactured in Michigan; and when blended with wine or wine spirits manufactured from grapes or fruits not grown in Michigan, when there is used in the finished product not over 25 per cent in volume of wine or wine spirits manufactured outside the State of Michigan. This latter provision results in an absolute limitation on the quantity of beverages imported for blending purposes. 

Out-of-state wine, imported for the purpose of blending, is taxed at the rate of 10 cents per gallon. (Laws of 1933, P. A. 8, Sec. 16-a and 16-b, as amended by Laws of 1937, P. A. 281)

During the current session of the State legislature, S. B. 252, which provides for the repeal of the above discriminatory features of the Michigan law, has been introduced. 

The retaliatory law of Michigan provides that the commission shall adopt a regulation forbidding the sale of beer originating in any State whose laws or regulations (1), require a licensed wholesaler to pay an additional fee to purchase, import, or sell Michigan products; (2) deny issuance of a license authorizing importation of beer to any duly licensed beer wholesaler therein who makes application for such license; or (3) prohibit licensed beer wholesalers therein from possessing or selling Michigan beer unless the seller has secured a license and paid a fee therefor, when such seller neither transports the beer into the State nor sells the same therein; or (4) impose higher taxes or inspection fees upon Michigan beer than upon beer manufactured in that State. (Laws of 1933, P. A. 

8, Sec. 40, as amended by Laws of 1937, P. A. 281)

Pursuant to this Act the Commission, on December 14, 1937, designated as discriminatory the States of Maine, Maryland, Nevada, Indiana, New Hampshire, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Vermont, and Washington. The prohibition on importation 16

of beer from those States was scheduled to go into effect on March 14, 1938, but was lifted as to Indiana and Pennsylvania by mutual arrangement between officials of the States concerned. 

During the current session of the State legislature, S. B. 143 was introduced which provides for the repeal of this retaliatory law. To date the bill has made no progress. Minnesota.

Chapter 390 of the Minnesota Laws of 1935 provides that no licensed manufacturer or wholesaler shall import any brand or brands of intoxicating liquors containing more than 25 per cent of alcohol by volume ready for sale without further processing, unless such brand or brands shall be duly registered in the Patent 

Office of the United States. 

The United States Supreme Court in Mahoney v. Joseph Triner 

Corp. held this statute to be valid under the Twenty-first Amendment and the Young's Market Company decision, but the Court also said that this statute was clearly discriminatory. Thus, even though this discrimination is not an incident of reasonable regulation of the liquor traffic, the Twenty-first Amendment makes it valid. Missouri.

In April, 1937, the General Assembly of Missouri enacted a law providing for retaliation. (Laws 1937, H. B. 331) This law, after first setting out several tests for a discriminatory liquor law, charged the Attorney General of the State with the duty of examining the laws, rules and regulations of the several States, and if he found that any State discriminates against alcoholic beverages manufactured in Missouri, he was to certify the same to the Supervisor of Liquor Control who would then give notice that the alcoholic beverage products of the discriminating States were barred from importation into Missouri.

On October 20, 1937, a temporary restraining order against the enforcement of this law was secured in the United States District Court.

On February 24, 1938, the United States District Court reversed the prior decision and held that this law was reasonably related to the object sought to be achieved and did not violate the equal protection clause of the Constitution. The Court again said here that the Twenty-first Amendment and the decision in Young's Market

Co. thereunder has removed any implied prohibition upon such

legislation under the commerce clause. This decision was appealed to the Suprme Court of the United States, and on January 3, 1939, that Court upheld the decision of the lower court and said that the law was valid under the Twenty-first Amendment. 

On the same day that the United States Supreme Court handed down its decision upholding the validity of the law, the Circuit Court of Cole County, Missouri, temporarily enjoined the enforcement of this law as to importations from Indiana, Pennsylvania, Michigan, and Massachusetts.

On January 18, 1939, H. B. 55, which provides for the repeal of this law, was introduced into the Missouri legislature where it was passed and signed by the Governor on March 21. 

In his massage returning H. B. 55 to the General Assembly after it was signed, Governor Lloyd C. Stark said: "Two years ago the 59th General Assembly of Missouri enacted what is known as the Discriminatory Liquor Act. 

Some states had enacted legislation which it was thought would operate to discriminate against the sale in such states. of intoxicating liquor and nonintoxicating beer, manufactured in Missouri. The Act of the 1937 General Assembly of Mis souri was intended to at least ameliorate the evils of the said discriminatory legislation of other states. "The lapse of two years time has shown distinctly legislation of the discriminatory character of that referred to seems squarely counter to the great basic principles of our Federal Constitution which command commerce between all the States must always be free. "I am pleased that the General Assembly of Missouri promptly recognized the discriminatory act of 1937 was not in line with the policy of freedom of commerce between the States and has expressed its disapproval of said Act by submitting to me a Bill for approval, passed by both Houses of the Legislature. The historic setting of the Federal Constitution and the Articles of Confederation between the thirteen original states shows clearly the necessity of the States and the Federal Government maintaining absolute, untrammeled and free at all times all commerce between the States. . Montana. 

The laws of Montana at the present time contain no provisions that amount to hostile discrimination, but during the 1939 session of the State legislature H. B. 397, which provided for a tax of 18

$2.per barrel on out-of-state beer and a tax of $1.00 per barrel on domestic beer, was introduced. This bill died with the adjournment of the legislature. Nebraska.

The present laws of Nebraska contain no provision that can be called hostile discrimination. In the current session of the legislature, however, Legislative Bill No. 418 was introduced, which provides that the Nebraska Liquor Control Commission shall forthwith adopt a regulation designating as discriminating those States the laws, rules, or regulations of which are found to require a licensed wholesaler of alcoholic beverages therein to pay an additional fee for the right to handle alcoholic beverages manufactured in Nebraska or which deny the issuance of a license authorizing the importation of alcoholic beverages from Nebraska. The regulation adopted shall prohibit all licensees in the State of Nebraska from handling in any way any alcoholic beverages manufactured in any State which is designated as discriminating. The bill is still in committee. Nevada.

The laws of Nevada require the first person in possession of imported alcoholic liquors within the State to secure an importer's license. Such a license does not authorize the resale of the beverages and the fee imposed is $350 per year if the licensee handles. all types of alcoholic beverages, and $100 if he handles only beer. 

In order to sell what he imports, the licensee must secure a wholesaler's license for which the fee is $150 if he handles all alcoholic beverages and $50 if he handles only beer. (Laws 1935, Ch. 160, Secs. 2 and 15) 

New Mexico.

The 1937 Liquor Control Act favored the local grower-producers by allowing individual persons (not corporations, firms, co-partnerships, or associations) who operate vineyards to manufacture and sell to wholesalers and retailers wine made from grapes grown in their vineyards without the payment of a license fee. The excise tax on such wine was lower than that imposed upon all other wines. All other wineries were required to obtain a license (fee $350) and if they wished to sell to retailers as well as wholesalers they were required to secure a wholesaler's license (fee $250). (Laws 1937, Chap. 130) 19

The above act is repealed by the 1939 Liquor Control Act which will become effective June 9, 1939. This act still favors the individual person who is a grower-producer by not requiring him to pay a license fee although he must have a grower's permit (issued under rules to be prescribed by the Chief of Division). 

The differential in the wine excise tax has now been abolished so that the same excise tax is paid on wine made by the growerproducer as on all other wines. The fee for a winery license has been reduced to $300. (Laws 1939, Chap. 226) Ohio.

The law now in effect in Ohio allows the importation of wine by a winery for blending purposes (fee $20 per year) but the amount so imported must be blended with not less than 51 per cent of wine produced from grapes grown in the State of Ohio. Wine may also be imported by wine importers (fee $500 per year) and by distillers and manufacturers of bottled mixed beverages, but the fees for the latter licenses are $1000 per year. 

S. B. 74, introduced in the present session of the State Legislature, provides for changes in the winery permit requirements, raising the fee to $100 per year and raising the percentage of wine from grapes grown in the State of Ohio to 60 per cent. The same bill proposes to restrict the amount of wine imported by wine importers so that the importation in any one month will not exceed 40 per cent of the total amount of wine purchased during that month. At present the Bill has made no progress. (Ohio General 

Code, Sec. 6064-15)

Thus the law, as it exists today, provides for a restriction on the amount of wine that may be imported, and if S. B. 74 is made law, the importation of wine will be further restricted. 

The present law of Ohio also contains a retaliatory provision to the effect that, if the laws or regulations of another State provide for higher taxes or charges upon Ohio wine and beer than upon domestic products, the Tax Commission is authorized to levy and collect additional reciprocal taxes or charges upon such products from the discriminating State. Such taxes have previously been levied against beer from Indiana and Michigan, but both have been withdrawn by later orders of the Commission.

Pennsylvania.

The discriminatory laws of Pennsylvania are of the retaliatory type. Section 201 of the Liquor Control Act contains a provision requiring each Pennsylvania manufacturer and each non-resident manufacturer of liquors other than wine, selling such liquors to the Board which are not manufactured in Pennsylvania, to make application for and be granted a permit by the Board before such liquors manufactured outside the State shall be purchased from such manufacturer. Each such manufacturer shall pay for such permit a fee equal to that required to be paid, if any, by a manufacturer or wholesaler of the State, territory, or country of origin of the liquor for selling liquors manufactured in Pennsylvania. 

This same Section provides that the Board shall not purchase any liquor fermented, distilled, rectified, compounded, or bottled in any State, territory, or country the laws of which result in prohibiting the importation therein of liquor manufactured in Pennsylvania.

Section 29 of the Beverage License Law contains further provisions for retaliation. This Section empowers the Board to impose fees on out-of-state sellers or Pennsylvania licensees buying out-of-state malt or brewed beverages, reciprocal fees equal to those imposed on the sale of Pennsylvania beverages in the State of origin or to restrict or prohibit importation of such beverages from States where importation of Pennsylvania beverages is restricted or prohibited. Section 3 of the Malt Beverage Tax Law contains. 

a retaliatory provision which states that in the event that any State shall impose upon malt beverages which have been manufactured in Pennsylvania a higher fee than is imposed upon malt or brewed beverages manufactured within such State, then importations of malt beverages from that State into Pennsylvania will be taxed at a rate equal to the excess tax imposed by such State on 

Pennsylvania manufactured malt beverages.

Section 15 of the Malt Beverage Tax Law contains a general authorization to the Department to prescribe rules and regulations relating to the transportation of malt beverages through the Commonwealth and from points outside the Commonwealth to 22

points within the Commonwealth, and to prescribe rules and regulations reciprocal of those or laws of any other State or territory affecting the transportation of malt beverages manufactured in Pennsylvania.

During the current session of the Pennsylvania State Legislature, H. B. 682 was introduced providing for the establishment of standards to be followed by the Board in fixing sales prices of distilled spirits and also providing for a mark up of 20 per cent on all liquors made outside of Pennsylvania. There has been no progress made on the Bill.

Rhode Island. 

Rhode Island favors its local wines by imposing a lower license fee on certain wineries. The annual fee for a winery is set at $1000, but where a winery produces only still wines from grapes grown on premises in the State of Rhode Island owned by the winery, the annual fee is set by the Division at some point between $100 and $500. (Rhode Island General Laws of 1938, Chap. 163, Sec. 3)

The excise taxes which the State of Rhode Island imposes upon alcoholic beverages is imposed only upon those manufactured in the State. All importation is accomplished by the Division of 

Intoxicating Beverages and they collect a service charge for such importation in an amount equal to the excise tax that the local products pay. South Carolina.

The annual license fee for operating a winery in South Carolina is $100; however, if the owner and operator of any such winery consumes in the operation thereof only the fruits produced. on his own farm or premises, then the license fee shall be only $5 per year. This is, of course, an attempt to favor the local grower-manufacturer. (Laws 1935, Art. 599, Sec. 4 (a), as amended by Laws 1936, Act 794) 23

Tennessee.

The laws of Tennessee favor locally produced beer by imposing a lower license fee for the handling of such beer. An agent or distributor of beer at wholesale for a brewer who pays the brewer's tax ($300 in cities or counties of 100,000 population or over; $150 in cities and counties of less than 100,000 population) shall pay only one-fourth of the tax imposed for the privilege of wholesaling beer. This means that a wholesaler handling out-of-State beer must pay four times as much for a license as the wholesaler of local beer. (Laws 1933, Ch. 115, Sec. 1)

In the 1939 session of the State Legislature, H. B. 349 was introduced, providing for an excise tax of $3.20 per barrel on outof-State beer and re-enacting the present excise tax on local beer of $1.70 per barrel. The bill died with the adjournment of the Legislature. Washington.

Domestic wines are defined in Washington as wines manufactured or produced within the State of Washington in a licensed domestic winery from fruits or fruit products grown exclusively and entirely within the State of Washington. Although the license fees to operate a domestic winery are the same as those to operate any other winery, the wine produced from Washingtongrown fruits or products is favored by the provision that a domestic winery may sell directly to persons holding licenses entitling them to sell wine at retail, upon the payment of a tax of 

10 cents per wine gallon, while other wineries must sell only through the Board. (Laws 1933, Chap. 62 as amended by Laws 

1935, Chap. 158; Laws 1937, Chap. 217; Laws 1939, H. B. No. 172)

The Laws of Washington contain no provisions for retaliation at the present time, but during the 1939 session of the Legislature, S.
