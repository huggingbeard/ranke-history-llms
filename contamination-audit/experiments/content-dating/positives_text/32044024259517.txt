INTRODUCTION 

This biographical sketch is prepared for the information of young people in the Society of Friends. It is probably best adapted for use in senior high school grades. This series of Quaker biographies does not aim at giving complete information but rather at interpreting the lives of the men and women who have made important contributions to Quaker history.

ELIAS HICKS TRAVELING MINISTER 

Traveling in the ministry to Quaker meetings was common when traveling was frequently difficult and sometimes hazardous. Up and down the Eastern seaboard, into the valleys of the Shenandoah, Genesee and Susquehanna Rivers, across the mountains into the plains of Ohio and Indiana, Friends traveled by horseback, by boat and occasionally by carriage. Always they were sure of hospitality and entertainment in a Friend's home. And equally sure that an audience would gather to hear them in a meeting house, in some home or in a public place to which the community was invited. 

They were the makers and builders of the Society of Friends during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

A much traveled American Friend was Elias Hicks of Long Island. His journal tells of the extent of his trips but only occasionally does he describe the diffi-

culties. He writes to his wife from Exeter, near 

Reading, Pa., telling how on his way westward he had borrowed a horse of William Brinton of Lampeter and now has his own again but "she has a very bad sore on her withers." He had left York the Fourth-day before, and it was night by the time he crossed the Susquehanna River in a crude ferry. He landed in Columbia "where dwelt a few friends" and "appointed a meeting there the next day." On this trip in 1801 he visited practically all the meetings in New Jersey and Pennsylvania. His letter says, "It is now just six weeks and four days since we went from this place, which is about 48 miles from Philadelphia, since which time we have rode 813 miles and attended 35 meetings. Much of the way in this tour has been rugged, mountainous and rocky, and had it not been for the best attendant companion (Edmund Willis on this occasion), peace of mind flowing from a compliance with and performance of manifested duty, the journey would have been tedious and irksome. But we passed pretty cheerfully on, viewing with an attentive eye the wonderful works of that boundless wisdom and power by which the worlds were framed." 

Horseflesh was irked by this preaching circuit while the preacher, as enjoined by George Fox, went "cheerfully over the world, answering that of God in every man."

On a trip to Friends in Upper Canada in 1803 and to Friends of New York Yearly Meeting living in the Hudson and Mohawk Valleys, Elias Hicks again describes the mode of travel. "We traveled by land and water in this day's journey about forty-five miles. Very bad traveling over logs and mudholes, crossing two ferries on our way, each four or five miles wide, with an island between called Long Island. About six miles across we were in the middle thereof, the darkest time in the night, when we were under the necessity of getting off our horses several times to feel for the horses' tracks in order to know whether we were in the path or not, as we were not able to see the path, nor one another at times, if more than five or six feet apart. Some of our company began to fear we should be under the necessity of lying in the woods all night. However, we were favored to get well through and crossed the last ferry about midnight and after."

Great crowds flocked to hear Elias Hicks yet he notes little about them, in his journal. He tells how "his spirit was set at liberty" when he must have preached acceptably, sometimes how he found it his place "to sit silent and suffer with the seed," and occasionally comments on other ministry as "It was for the most part a favored season, but would have been more so, had not some in the ministry quite exceeded the mark by unnecessary

communication." He mentions "the most affectionate care and attention" of the family who entertained him, for he made many warm friends in his extensive travels. 

During almost fifty years of public service as a minister he must have traveled forty thousand miles. He had no speedometer to measure each trip but the number of miles and number of meetings addressed are the chief facts noted on his many excursions. 

His letters indicate he missed his family and is sometimes disappointed that he found no letters awaiting him. But when he hears that his children are ill, that news "would induce me to return home immediately if I was set at liberty from my religious obligations." 

The family of Elias and Jemina Hicks was large. Of four sons and seven daughters, two girls died early and all the sons before they were twenty-one. Four of the daughters married and in later years his sons-in-law managed his farm, but his wife must have been a good manager to keep the household running smoothly during the long absences of her husband. She may have been a poor correspondent for on one trip when he had received "but two small letters" Elias Hicks adds "and thou writest, my dear, as if paper was scarce, on very small pieces." He is concerned for her health and pens tender messages like "There is nothing while we continue in our right minds that can dissolve that firm and precious bond of

love and endeared affection, which from our first acquaintance united us together, and in which, while writing these lines my spirit greets thee with endeared embraces." 

Elias Hicks was born Third month 19, 1748, at

Hempstead, Long Island, and early moved to the south shore where he enjoyed hunting and fishing. His mother died when he was only eleven and he lived with a married brother until at seventeen he was apprenticed to learn the trade of a carpenter. After his apprenticeship ended, and his marriage in 1771 to Jemina Seaman, he went to manage the farm of his father-in-law at Jericho and lived there for nearly sixty years. As a farmer he seems to have made a comfortable living and had freedom to travel. At that time wheat was grown on many farms in Long Island. Once when harvests. were light and provisions scarce and high his own field had produced a good crop. Speculators offered him a good price but he refused to sell, even at the unprecedented price of three dollars a bushel, yet when his poorer neighbors were in need he invited them to come and get as much of his as they could use at one dollar a bushel.

Elias Hicks lived through two wars-the Revolutionary beginning in 1776 and the War of 1812. Yet he was so true to his convictions against war that he would not allow himself to profit by the advanced price

of food. The British controlled Long Island during the revolutionary period and for some time the meeting house in Flushing was occupied as a barracks by the king's troops. In 1777 the yearly meeting sessions were removed to Westbury. To attend yearly meeting and make other religious visits it was frequently necessary to pass the lines of both armies. Regarding this privilege freely granted Friends, Elias Hicks writes in his journal, "This was a favor which the parties would not grant to their best friends, who were of a warlike disposition; which shows what great advantages would redound to mankind were they all of this pacific spirit." 

Elias Hicks' first official visit grew out of the Revolutionary War. He and John Willis carried to 

Philadelphia Yearly Meeting for its consideration a matter which was causing dissension in New York. 

During the British occupation a large room under the meeting house had been used as a storehouse for military goods. Rent was offered by the military commissioner and accepted by some representative Friends. 

When the matter came before the Yearly Meeting in 1779, the peace party said the money should be returned as it was blood money, but a vigorous minority wished to keep this war revenue. The travelers started up the Hudson valley visiting meetings as far north as 

Marlborough in Ulster County, then turned southwest and visited other meetings before arriving in Philadel-

phia, Ninth month 25th, for the opening of the Yearly

Meeting. (From 1755 to 1798, Philadelphia Yearly 

Meeting was held in Ninth month and in 1779 it continued from the 25th to Tenth month 4th, a week and two days.) Philadelphia's decision was that the rent money should be returned and this was done by direction of New York Yearly meeting in 1780.

There is little reference to schools in Hicks' Journal but he seemed to have had "a hungering mind" and early acquired the reading habit. He was a careful and intelligent reader of the Bible, thinking through its teachings for himself. He probably read the journal of George Fox and of other Friends. The number of these journals written in the eighteenth century is very large and they all are written on the same model, ignoring nature or literature or world problems, and dealing exclusively with the writer's own inner experiences of the Spirit. Elias Hicks' Journal follows this pattern and so does not tell us many things we might like to know. It requires some imagination to see waving wheat fields in Long Island, trails and wagon roads where now are concrete highways, river fords now spanned by bridges, forests in the places occupied by villages. It takes even more imagination to understand the cultural climate of former centuries. Without newspapers, telephone or radio, people were more isolated and more dependent on travelers and visitors to hear

the news and know what their friends were doing and thinking. Letters moved with some regularity before the postage stamp was authorized in 1847 but not as frequently or as cheaply. Some people received their education in schools set up by and taught by schoolmasters before public schools were established in 1837. 

Books in the home were few but those few were prized and thoroughly familiar from frequent rereading. 

The language of the journals may seem quaint today as they record unique experiences in a conventional pattern. Yet they give us a picture of the Quakers of that day who by an inner authority became messengers and "publishers of Truth" like the first Quakers. One careless young man who slept peacefully through the meetings he attended was awakened by a vivid message from a young woman preacher who fixed her eye upon him in meeting and said: "A traditional Quaker; thou comest to meeting as thou went from it the last time, and goest from it as thou came to it, but art no better for the coming; what will thou do in the end?" As a result, Samuel Bownas was reached, his conduct was altered and he became another man. In a short time he experienced his own call to the ministry "being qualified by the operation of the Spirit in myself." He became an extensive traveler, underwent persecution and hard imprisonment but up to the end of his life in 1753, his ministry was "powerful and lively."

The difficulty of first speaking in meeting was an awful experience to most of these journalists (really filled with awe). Elias Hicks wrote, "I had many deep openings in the visions of light" and again "I felt an impressive concern to utter a few words, which I yielded to in great fear and dread; but oh, the joy and sweet consolation that my soul experienced, as a reward for this act of faithfulness." Friends seem to have been appreciative of his speaking and the Monthly Meeting of Westbury in 1778 recommended him as a minister.

His traveling began the next year with his commission and indirect trip to Philadelphia. In 1793 he had a concern to visit Friends in New England and attended meetings in Rhode Island, Connecticut, Massachusetts,

Maine and the Massachusetts islands, traveling 2283 miles by land or water in about five months. He seems to have recorded his mileage as exactly as some motorists. In 1798 he was away from home five and a half months, traveled 1600 miles and attended 143 meetings (an average of nearly one a day) in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland and Virginia. It was on this trip that he began his serious opposition to slavery. 

His appeal was to the conscience of slaveholders and in some cases was quite effective. Of a meeting held at 

Elk Ridge, Maryland, in 1798 he writes, "Truth rose into dominion, and some present who were slave-holders were made sensible of their condi-

tion, and were much affected . . . Since then I have been informed that a woman present at that session, who possessed a number of slaves, was so fully convinced, as to set them free, and not long afterwards joined in membership with Friends."

The state of New York provided for the gradual emancipation of its slaves in 1799 but this fearless traveler bore his testimony against slavery into states where it was the law of the land. 

Elias Hicks maintained other well-known testimonies of the Society of Friends. He was opposed to the establishment of a liquor-selling tavern in the Jericho neighborhood, so when he saw strangers approaching he invited them to accept the hospitality of his home and thus made unnecessary a tavern in the small country town. He advocated a change in the Discipline that would allow women a voice in making and amending the Discipline, for the women's business meetings of that day were not fully equal with the men's. He desired to give youth its share of opportunity for he cautioned age, "The old folks think they have got far enough, they are settling on the lees, they are blocking up the way." He seems to have allowed others liberty to form their own opinions. 

However the name of Elias Hicks is known today because of differences of opinion which were magnified and extended into a serious division. While the cause

and blame for this tragic separation cannot be laid upon any single individual, Elias Hicks was the most striking personality of the historical drama. He was one of the most extensive travelers and one of the most powerful ministers of his time. 

Walt Whitman's boyish impression of his Long 

Island neighbor written down years later is as follows: "A pleading tender nearly agonizing conviction, and magnetic stream of natural eloquence, before which all minds and natures, all emotions, high or low, gentle or simple, yielded entirely, without exception-not argumentative or intellectual, but so penetrating—so different from anything in books." 

He describes the man himself as a "tall straight figure, neither stout nor very thin, dressed in drab cloth, clean shaven face, forehead of great expanse, large clear black eyes, long or middling long white hair."

Elias Hicks was well known to many American Quakers and well liked both personally and for his preaching. He was an old man and had been preaching for almost fifty years before his doctrines were questioned. He like others was disturbed by the rationalistic inquiries of the age, by "the turning away of many among us from the law and the testimony." His way to combat this enemy of the faith was to make religion an inward affair and to turn from outward phrases to

the Christ who lives and works in the soul. He was accused of belittling the authority of the Bible and he did not use the evangelical language of many of the Quakers who had come in from the outside, often through a mature conversion experience. Positive assertions on either side widened the breach. 

Rufus Jones says both sides lacked "any real historical grasp of early Quakerism . . . Still feebler was the apprehension of early Christianity." A prophet was needed who "would bind both the inner reality and the outer truth into one harmonious whole." But no such leader arose and accusations and counter-charges increased, until a division was effected in many meetings, in 1827 and 1828. "Elias Hicks was a vigorous dynamic individual who spoke out in clear tones. . . He had no fears and he went straight through to his conclusions without any inhibitions or hedging tendencies."

In 1822 Elias Hicks had visited many meetings in 

New Jersey, in Pennsylvania, in Delaware and attended Yearly Meeting at Baltimore. Again in 1827 he received a minute to visit Concord and Southern Quarterly Meetings. Meeting houses were crowded to capacity when this well-loved minister was announced, some coming from curiosity to hear the man about whom so many charges had been made. He completed one of his longest and most extended journeys just before his eighty-first birthday, having traveled nearly

miles in an absence of seven months and ten days.

On this trip he visited the Yearly Meetings of Philadelphia and Baltimore where the separation had occurred, and on to Ohio and Indiana where the differences of opinion caused some disturbances, and even visited a few meetings in Virginia. This was his last extended visit before his death in 1830. His journal makes little mention of the widening controversy but once he writes "The situation caused an abatement in answering the query relative to love and unity." Yet the name of Elias Hicks has been indelibly associated with the separation, though he was never acknowledged as the leader of a faction. 

But neither "Hicksite" or "Orthodox" represents a single viewpoint and the names only remind us of a dead past. Where different groups gather seems to be a more distinguishing mark today. So we use the place of the Yearly Meeting, if not the street on which the local meeting house is located, when we wish to name a particular group of Friends. And there are no barriers to intervisitation. 

In the early days of the Society of Friends a real concern took members to all parts of the world,—to visit the Sultan of Turkey, to seek converts and establish meetings in the New World. In the second generation contact between scattered groups was maintained by the Friends traveling with a minute which indicated

their Meeting endorsed their concern for visiting distant Friends. The itinerant ministers drew the lines which differentiated Quakerism and marked it off from other types of Christianity and they keyed the members up to the task of preserving the spiritual ideals of the Society. Members were extremely individualistic yet in their group life showed a remarkable similarity in ideas, in dress and manner, and in conscientious scruples. That unifying influence was the continuous intervisitation of the spiritual leaders of the Society. 

Great roads the Romans built that men might meet And walls to keep strong men apart, secure. 

The centuries have gone and in defeat The walls have fallen, the roads endure. 

With differences forgotten, with quarrels outgrown, with knowledge of the past and understanding of the present, traveling across old barriers and along enduring roads will again unify the Society of Friends.
