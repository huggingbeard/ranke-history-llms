Getting Competent Management for Your Estate

Heavy legal and moral responsibilities go with the job of being an executor of an estate. It's a position of deep trust. Perhaps that's why some people think they "honor" a spouse, relative, or close friend by appointing him or her as executor or executrix of their estates. 

But if you give careful consideration to the difficulties and complexities that occur so frequently in the administration of estates these days, you'll likely reach a different conclusion. Those familiar with the administration of estates know that appointing as executor a relative or friend not properly qualified often puts a crushing burden of property management on the person so selected. More important, it may jeopardize the property in the estate and the future financial well-being of those interested in its distribution. 

Details, Details, and More Details

The task of an executor can be stated simply. It is his job to collect and safeguard the assets of the estate, pay all proper debts, claims, expenses and taxes, and then make distribution to those entitled to share in the estate. So stated, it doesn't sound too difficult or complicated. But in any actual case the specific details of estate administration may, particularly to an inexperienced person, be amazingly complex.

Take, for example, the matter of Federal estate tax alone. With the rise in property values in the last decade or so, and with life insurance being subject to this tax, more and more estates are required to file a return. It's the executor's responsibility to do this. 

The Federal estate tax return has forty pages. In addition to the return itself it's often necessary to 8606113

file supplemental pages containing extra information which the tax authorities require. To the person who has never settled an estate before, the task of furnishing acceptable information about such items as "Transfers made in contemplation of death," "Credit for tax on prior transfers," "Credit for State death taxes," and other technical matters, is a task of staggering proportions. 

In one case a husband made a will which named his wife executrix. Later the husband himself was appointed as executor for the estate of a business associate and lifelong friend. When the husband realized the difficulties he himself faced in assembling proper information for the tax return of his friend's estate, he pictured the difficulties his wife might have in regard to his own estate. He promtly changed his own will and named his bank as executor.

Some of the Problems 

Your Executor May Face 

There's really no such thing as a typical estate. 

The tasks of estate administration that will confront your executor will, to a certain extent, be unique and special. However here is a random list of a few specific questions that your executor might be called on to decide in the course of settling your estate. 

How should your estate raise needed cash? Estates need cash for the payment of debts, taxes, and expenses. Your executor may have to decide which assets of your estate should be liquidated and which retained. Should your estate borrow money and, if so, which assets of the estate should be pledged as security? Should estate funds be used to advertise and promote the sale of hard-to-sell assets like slow-moving real estate, heirlooms and antiques, or a business inventory? Which of the securities in your estate are likely to rise in value, which ones likely to fall? Should an automobile, not specifically bequeathed by will, be sold immediately or kept a while for the family's "convenience"? (In 4

one recent case an administrator was surcharged six hundred dollars for failing to sell an automobile promptly before it dropped in value.) 

Should your executor take an immediate or a long-range tax approach to your estate? Federal and State death taxes are based on the value of the assets of the estate. The higher their value, then, generally, the higher the estate and inheritance taxes that must be paid. Consequently you might expect that your executor should do everything possible to have the assets of your estate valued at the lowest possible amounts. But this may not be desirable from a long-range point of view. Should your beneficiaries later sell the assets, then, for income tax purposes, their "basis" for gain or loss would probably be the values determined earlier in the estate's tax proceedings. In other words your executor might save estate and inheritance taxes by having low values placed on the assets of your estate but these low values might boomerang when your beneficiaries later sell the assets.

Example: The estate of X, a widower, consisted almost exclusively of a stock interest in a plastics manufacturing concern and X left this stock to his son, an only child. The Federal tax authorities at first valued the stock at $100,000. Consequently X's estate faced an estate tax of about $4,800. But X's executor succeeded in having the stock finally valued at $65,000. X's estate paid an estate tax of only $150. The executor felt he had "saved" $4,650 for X's son. But later the son sold the stock for $100,000, paying a capital gains tax of $8,750 ... 25% of $35,000 ($100,000 minus the "basis" of $65,000). If the executor had accepted the tax authorities' original $100,000 valuation and the son had been able to use this figure as his "basis" for the sale, the son would have had no gain and no tax on the sale. The executor's misplaced zeal cost the son $4,100! 

How quickly should your estate be settled? “As soon as possible" is, of course, an obvious answer

to this question. But your executor must weigh many factors before deciding exactly how soon your estate can properly be brought to final settlement. 

Since he is charged with the duty of satisfying all valid claims that may be brought against your estate, he must retain sufficient assets to meet these obligations. Should he make distribution too quickly, he may be held personally liable for any unsettled claims such as a claim for Federal estate tax. On the other hand your beneficiaries may be requesting immediate distribution of their shares of the estate, may, in fact, be in serious need of funds. A competent executor should be able to strike a satisfactory balance between the desire for fast distribution and the need for retaining assets.

Your Executor Should Be EXPERIENCED 

There are many other questions that an executor is called on to answer, many vitally important decisions he must make in the course of settling an estate. What to do about investments, whether to resist or compromise certain claims against the estate, how best to settle some dispute among beneficiaries, these are questions that should be answered by an executor of proven competence and experience. Don't appoint as executor someone who will acquire experience in estate administration at the expense of your beneficiaries! 

Can Your Estate Afford

Part-Time Management? 

No estate can afford to be managed by an executor whose services are rendered on a part-time or haphazard basis. Consider the financial losses that occurred in the following two cases because, for one reason or another, an executor did not or could not give full-time service and concentration to the job of settling an estate. 

In one case an executor's five-day hunting trip cost an estate $27,000. A decedent left a will appointing a good friend and business associate as

executor of his estate. Right after the decedent's death the executor took a few preliminary steps towards inventorying the assets of the estate. The bulk of the estate seemed to consist of high grade securities and life insurance. Anticipating no immediate problems in regard to these assets, the executor felt there was no need for postponing a scheduled deer hunting trip with some of his friends. Both the decedent and the executor had made this trip for many years and, as the executor phrased it, "Bill would have wanted us to go ahead as usual." After the trip the executor made a more thorough inventory of the estate assets. It was only then that he discovered that the decedent had held an option to purchase, at a very favorable price, some land on the outskirts of town. At the time of the decedent's death he had been planning to exercise the option. He had originally acquired the option in the belief that a new state highway would be built adjacent to the land and his predictions were correct. Unfortunately the option lapsed while the executor was on the hunting trip. Had the estate been able to exercise the option, there would have been no trouble in reselling the land for at least $27,000 more than the option price. An executor who concentrated on the task of settling the estate would, no doubt, have discovered the option in time.

In another case a decedent had been in the plumbing supply business. His brother, who had been appointed as executor of the estate, was faced with the task of liquidating an unusually large inventory of lengths of pipe which included several hundred items of odd sizes. Rather than to attempt to carry out separate sales of the various items he sold the entire inventory to one purchaser, receiving a price which seemed fair and reasonable. Indeed, all those who were interested in the estate gave their consent to this bulk sale. Later, however, it was discovered that if the executor had devoted more time and effort to the liquidation he could have realized a much larger amount by making a number of sales of the various items to several different purchasers. But that would have

meant that the executor would probably have had to seriously neglect his own personal affairs for three or four months.

Your Executor Should Be CONSTANTLY AVAILABLE 

To attend thoroughly and efficiently to all the tasks of settling an estate, an executor should be able to give full time and attention to these tasks. 

An executor's sickness, death, or natural concern about his own business or personal affairs are factors that may prevent concentrated and uninterrupted efforts at settling an estate. Unless your executor can be constantly available there may be costly delays and difficulties in the administration of your own estate. Don't let the administration of your estate be a sideline activity. 

Will Your Estate Be 

Administered Fairly?

Obviously your executor should be completely impartial in carrying out your wishes as expressed in your will. A recent case shows to some extent the degree of impartiality required. The decedent was the head of a large family living under one roof. A son was administering the estate. Everything went smoothly until the executor presented his final account for court approval. At that point a cousin, who had a small interest in the estate objected to the account. His objection was that the executor had erred in failing to collect rent from himself and the others who had continued to live in the decedent's home pending the settlement of the estate. The cousin's objection was eventually upheld by the court. The matter caused ill feeling which persisted in the family for many years. Had the decedent appointed an executor completely unrelated to the family, such unpleasantness might have been avoided.

Sometimes a member of the family chosen as executor is "too impartial." He or she may bend

over backwards in an effort to be completely fair but this very attitude may result in unjust treatment of some of the beneficiaries.

Your Executor Should Be UNQUESTIONABLY IMPARTIAL 

The executor of an estate must treat all beneficiaries with complete fairness. This may be difficult to do where the executor or some member of his family may be personally interested in the estate. Even where a member of the family observes the strictest impartiality in administering the estate, motives may be misunderstood. For example, an executor must generally follow a certain priority in distributing legacies. Those who do not receive their shares right away may feel that the executor is biased or unfair in paying over other legacies but withholding theirs. In so doing the executor may actually be following the strict requirements of the law but his actions may be severely questioned or challenged.

In selecting an executor for your estate, recognized impartiality is an absolute "must." 

EXPERIENCE, AVAILABILITY, IMPARTIALITY, PLUS . . . 

SOME OTHER IMPORTANT ADVANTAGES 

After carefully reviewing the qualifications that the ideal executor should possess, many persons decide to have a corporate executor, that is a bank or trust company, settle their estates. Let's look at some of the reasons behind such a choice. 

As to Experience

The chief activity of the trust department of a bank or trust company is the administration of estates. This constant specialization results in everincreasing "know-how" and efficiency that assure competence and thoroughness in every detail of settling your estate. When you choose the cor-

porate executor, you choose practical and proven ability in administering estates both large and modest. You give your family the benefit of service from carefully trained and completely experienced personnel. 

As to Availability While some individuals are undoubtedly skilled in handling estates, they are subject to human disabilities. These may cause costly interruptions in the administration of your estate. If you choose an individual executor, the person selected may become ill, die, move away, or have the pressure of his or her own affairs interfere with the administration of your estate. These matters are of no concern when you select the corporate executor with its continued existence and concentration of effort. Another advantage of having a bank or trust company administer your estate is that the members of your family will have a convenient source of immediate information on all matters affecting the estate. This may be less true where an individual executor is handling the estate. 

As to Impartiality- In settling your estate the corporate executor's only interest is to see that your wishes are carried out. Thus the actual details of administration may be carried out in a pleasant atmosphere devoid of personal suspicion or misgivings. This recognized impartiality facilitates estate settlement, tends to prevent unnecessary disputes that might otherwise occur and cause delay and expense. 

Two Other Advantages 

You Should Know About

There are two other points to consider when reviewing the advisability of appointing a bank or trust company as executor of your estate. 

One is the financial responsibility of the corporate executor. Indeed many states have recognized 10

this factor by permitting banks and trust companies to administer estates without being required to post a bond. The estate is spared this expense. A more important advantage, perhaps, is the fact that those who deal with the estate do so with a clear recognition of the financial strength of the executor. 

The other advantage is the group judgment that a bank or trust company brings to the problems of estate administration. When important decisions affecting an estate are made, these decisions are the product of the considered judgment of a group of persons thoroughly versed in all phases of estate administration. Thus no vital steps are taken solely on the whim or unsupported opinion of a single individual.

Many of the problems, financial losses and delays that may arise in the administration of estates can be avoided through careful advance planning.
