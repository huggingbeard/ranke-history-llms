Introduction 

T is a pleasure for me to introduce this book to those whose eyes may fall upon its pages. 

For books, like people, should be introduced. 

The same is true concerning their authors. Perhaps the reason this pleasant task is assigned to me is because the author has long been my friend and I am one who has declared his unusual writings should circulate among the people rather than waste their fragrance on the desert air.

A great editor and writer recently delivered a message to the church editors, and the first thing he said was: "The people will forgive you for anything but dullness." George Stuart once said to a group of preachers that the greatest drawback to preaching was its dullness. There seemed to be a concerted action on the part of preachers and those who made preachers, to make sermons as dull as possible. Men who were attractive and human tried to be dull and super-dry when they came to the pulpit. 

Certainly the charge of dullness cannot be lodged against this book. It is "humanesque," and throbs with red blood of human nature. The author is a master in ironical criticism, but his

INTRODUCTION 

blade is always dipped in sunshine, and, while he is a real surgeon with a keen blade, he gives you laughing gas when he puts you on the operating table. His humor is of a fine type and it is far from the canned variety of 1920 jokes. His humor is not the cistern kind, but the artesian-well variety. 

These sketches remind you of your mother's lemon pie. The plate is not large enough-the piece is too small, and you want some more. 

Dr. J. H. Barnhardt was born in Cabarrus

County, North Carolina, some time in the past, and while he has left the Old North State at times, he has always gone back. He loves those hills and they love him. He also understands them and their people. He has a combination of gifts few men possess. He can see things clearly with a human eye, and he can tell in the most charming style what he sees. He surpasses many experienced literary cooks in his ability to flavor what he cooks. He knows how to put in the sugar, the pepper, and the salt; and, better still, he knows the use of vinegar and tabasco. You will know it when you begin to read.

He is a preacher but it hasn't dehumanized him. 

I never saw him with a high hat, or his collar on backwards. He has no "Hark From the Tombs" countenance, and no "Bear Me Away on Your 

Snowy Wings" voice. He graduated from what is ii

INTRODUCTION 

now Duke University, and has "pastored" and "eldered" in the pasture where he was born. He has represented his Conference four times in the General Conference, and led his delegation at 

Memphis in 1926.

It was my privilege to have him as a traveling companion through Europe, and the memory of it is full of sunbeams. I never traveled with a man who could put more humor in the tourist trail, and I know no one who could see more and get more human thrills out of what he saw. I hope to go around the world with him some day.

This book will circulate widely if it has a chance. It has the entertaining qualities we seek. 

It has a smooth and easy manner. It runs on all cylinders. It teaches wholesome lessons and weighs and measures this gas-burning age and the crowd that is jammed in the traffic. If you start it you will finish it, and then you will tell your friends about it.

J. M. ROWLAND,

Foreword 

OME men have been slain on account of their ambitions, and numerous others ought to SON have been, but escaped for one reason or another. I am a candidate for the latter distinction.

That anyone should aspire to write a book is a form of justifiable revenge for his having had to read so many books that other people have written. Even at that, however, there should be some semblance of reason-or excuse-for his doing so. 

This writer has had two rather distinct and somewhat painful ambitions almost from the beginning of his career. They have been discouraged, neglected, ridiculed, and sat upon generally because he could not seem to detect any particular urgency on the part of the public to lug them out into the open. Under such treatment, one of them has completely expired, but the other still persists. 

The dead ambition was a desire to run for Congress which passed away only after I had fully made up my mind never to be entirely out of a job. 

The latter, which survives, is a desire to write something which people will read instead of passing it on to the garbage collector. 

So it has come about that I am to occupy a small

FOREWORD 

place in the libraries of a good many of my friends, and enter with them, through the medium of this little volume, into the fellowship of a friendly fireside visit, which I sincerely trust may be as pleasing to them as it shall be to the writer of these stray thoughts. 

Naturally, every person thinks his own children are the cat's whiskers, whether they be children of his body or his brain. Therefore, we like to show them off. Of course, if anybody should call them "tow-headed brats," we get mad about it and keep them at home afterward, which helps some. But if the neighbors speak kindly about them, we feel encouraged to display them still further, and even to bring out the rest of the family to put them on exhibition, too.

That is what has happened in this case. The stories contained in this volume are made up in part of articles written at intervals for the press, which were received most kindly by the reading public, for which I wish to make due acknowledgment of my sincere appreciation and thanks. They, by a rather persistent request, more than I, are responsible for the appearance of this volume. 

These articles have been carefully re-written, to which has been added much other original matter, and put into suitable form for a publication of this kind. They deal with topics of current interest, vi

and were originally designed to furnish entertainment for the reader, with some concealed attempt to "point a moral or adorn a tale." This design is carried over into the present volume. In that spirit, these modest sketches are sent out in the hope that they may meet the favor of those who read them. 

Thus, having said "howdy," I am ready for a friendly chat with those who are willing to pursue these pages further.

CHAPTER I 

Among the Nation's Great and 

Near Great 

S a boy, I used to have a suspicion steal over As me on hot days down on the flinty knolls of the old farm, that I might some day be conscripted to serve as President of the United

States, and go to live in Washington, De Ceased. 

Later on, as before related, my political ambitions shifted from the Presidency to Congress at the other end of Pennsylvania Avenue. But never having been sufficiently urged to justify me in accepting either of these positions, or even a clerkship in any one of the government departments, I took it upon myself to show ungrateful humanity that I could go there on my own hook and pay my own expenses. 

So I grabbed my itinerant handbag and put out for the historic hub of this free and unterrified Republic, where history is being made, and joined the other nuts who trickle around over the country 1

without having any visible business anywhere in particular, and who usually turn up at the capital as a fitting finale to their aimless peregrinations. 

The Honorable Frank Smith, senator designate from the sovereign State of Illinois, was on the grill at the time of this particular visit. The Senate was so shocked on account of a suspicion that anybody should obtain an appointment to that body except upon the highest ethical grounds, that the members were in a perfect stew of excitement as they tried his case. You ought to have seen the corridors of the Capitol building that day. It looked like court week and a murder trial on docket. Being free born and of a fair complexion, I sauntered up to the stairway leading to the galleries and started to ascend with confidence in my venture to find a seat, only to be rudely halted by some functionary who demanded to scrutinize my credentials, and upon my inability to produce any such documents, I was informed kindly but firmly that I could not proceed further, as "nobody is being admitted today."

But this happened to be the day when everybody wanted to be admitted. The general trend of the guard's remarks seeming to apply to me, I retreated with what grace I could under the circumstances, considering my tendency to fire up under provocation. After a few minutes of rapid- 2

fire thinking, I suddenly arrived at the conclusion that, if I could get to Senator Overman, who was on the floor at that moment, he could help me out of my perplexity and arrange for me to get into the gallery. Acting upon this inspiration, I took my calling card in hand and approached the entrance to the Senate chamber in search of a page, but was destined to be halted still more emphatically than before by some guard or doorkeeper, who looked common enough, but evidently had a good deal to do with things around the place. I told him that Senator Overman was a special friend of mine and that perhaps he was expecting me to call upon him since I was in Washington, and, furthermore, that it was important that I should see him. I think that I must have impressed the man, for he told me to sit down awhile in the reception room where the Senator would probably be pleased to see me rather than hold a reception for me on the floor of the Senate chamber. Sure enough, after a very few minutes, the Senator came out and gave orders to admit me into the Democratic side of the gallery, and as I went off in triumph with the page who seated me with a flourish, I felt a pardonable pride in being able to high hat it over that crowd of doorkeepers who didn't know "peas."

As I sat there for a few minutes looking down 3

upon the men whose speeches I had been reading in the Congressional Record since kingdom come,

I was duly impressed with the dignity of that ancient body of law makers. I was a bit surprised to find the Senators about the average run of human avoirdupois, with a tendency to become fat. I noticed, too, that some of them were sprouting presidential wings, while others were shedding their senatorial tail feathers in preparation for retirement to private life after the adjournment of the pending session. In the midst of my meditations, I glanced around and discovered that my side partner was an Ethiopian who had preceded me to the gallery, doubtless having better luck getting in than the Caucasian element could boast of. The two of us were cuddled closely together, furnishing a lot of inspiration, and no little perspiration, for one of the liveliest debates that had taken place in the Senate in many a day. 

The official guides around Washington appealed to me strongly because they were so courteous. In this respect, they are a shining example to all others who have dealings with their fellows.

This quality is true of nearly everybody who is in uniform in Washington. Never once did I get the "aristocratic stare" from any of them, nor was I even called a Rube when I insisted upon carrying my umbrella through the White House. They

took it away from me, but they didn't "sass" me.

The only back chat I got from anybody in the city was from a fellow who represented a private corporation with whom I had to do some transportation business, who gave me a two-dollar bill in making change. I insisted that it was bad luck for me to carry the thing around with me, and wanted him to give me two one-dollar bills instead. He told me to carry a buckeye around in my pocket to ward off my bad luck, whereupon I said, "applesauce" to him, just that a-way.

If I were going somewhere to sprout a reputation, it would not be to Washington. There are so many celebrities thereabouts, living and dead, that one could hardly get in a bid edgewise for attention from anybody except at a hotel where tips are not prohibited by law, and the bell-hops hailing from your own home town. There is one personality around there who overshadows them all, and he is dead. They point out places where George 

Washington stopped, slept, caught a rabbit, etc.; they conduct you to the church where he worshipped and to the Lodge where he presided as 

Master; they carry you along the banks of the 

Potomac, and show you the place of his sepulcher, and they have built to his memory the tallest monument of masonry ever erected to any human being. In fact, he dominates every nook and cor-

ner of that city, just as his fame covers the whole nation, and nobody who has since come in contact with public life at the nation's center has ever appeared in his true proportions simply because he has been overshadowed by the man who first was on the job of building this nation and laying out its capital.

Within the beautiful and gripping estate of 

Mount Vernon is located America's foremost shrine. As I stood there before the tomb of the man whose name is known and revered the world over, I could not help contrasting the simple surroundings of his resting place with the elaborate tomb of Napoleon at Paris, where the body of that great general reposes in a magnificent sarcophagus facing the high altar, whose spiral columns of finest Sicilian marble are rendered exquisitely beautiful by the mellow light of the sun falling upon them through amber windows, the whole place being gilded with a splendor beyond the dream of man. But here, the simple country gentleman rests amid the silent hills he loved so well, in a plain vault overarched, not by the cunning workmanship of the artist, but by the eternal blue of the Virginia skies, and guarded, not by the liveried soldiers of a vanished monarchy, but by the free spirit of American democracy. It is fitting that he should sleep at the close of the day, as he

lived, amid the peaceful environs of his ancestral estate instead of reposing in some heroic pile, like the Abbey of Westminster where sleep the dust of 

England's great.

But even Washington does not completely dim the glory of other great statesmen and leaders of this young Republic, who have come to Washington for awhile, served their country, and then departed, leaving the imprint of their personality upon the national character. I noticed the instinctive tendency of men to lower their voices and uncover their heads in the pervasive, persistent presence of some of these giants, and the deeds of all of them are written in the annals of the nation's scroll of fame.

There is in S Street an unpretentious brick house, where Woodrow Wilson lived in pathetic invalidism after his retirement from the White House, and where he died. This house has quickly become a shrine to which many people journey when visiting the city, regardless of political affiliation, and before which they pause in silent contemplation of the man who bore, in the loneliness of his intellectual isolation, the unmeasured shock of a world at war, and who went down fighting for ideals which he conceived to be vital to international peace and good will. It is too early to appraise his place in history. The shadows will have

to lengthen before he is assigned to his niche. But all people of the present generation hail him, along with Lincoln and Washington, as one of the nation's supremest sufferers for the cause of humanity.

Until recently there was one ex-President sticking around Washington for whom I have a great deal of respect. It is a very hard thing for a man who has attained the highest position in the gift of the nation, to retire gracefully, especially if they hand him his hat too soon. But that is what 

William Howard Taft did, and he grew bigger and better all the time. He was unfortunate in his political experiences but if he ever whined about it, I have never heard of it. And he wasn't bitter, either. I have great respect for a person who doesn't squeal under misfortune. He had been in and out of Washington for a long time, but he was no intermeddler into other people's business. He had the modesty and good sense, as well as the capacity to live his life and do his work uncomplainingly wherever he was called to serve. I count him a big man who can live sweet and keep his head on his shoulders after all that happened to 

Taft. In his second campaign for the Presidency, owing to a split in the Republican Party, he carried only two states-Vermont and Utah. Senator 

Heflin of Alabama, commenting on this experience

of his, said that the difference between Moses and 

Taft was "that the Lord buried Moses where the people couldn't find him, but the people buried 

Taft where the Lord couldn't find him." Anyway, he found himself, and he made one of the best 

Chief Justices of the United States Supreme Court who ever sat upon that honorable bench.

The Capitol building is, to me, the most impressive and significant structure in the city of Washington. It is rife with the traditions of our 

American life and ideals. There are finer structures in ancient capitals, but none which are better fitted architecturally to represent the people of their respective commonwealths, than is this majestic building to shadow forth, by its serene and massive grandeur, the quality and character of our great nation. As one stands on the outside, in the evening, and beholds this modern pantheon of justice, radiant with the iridescence of a thousand electric suns which illumine its dome and transfigure its somber marble into a shining crown of splendor, one feels like saying of it what Michael 

Angelo remarked of Ghiberti's bronze door on the church of Florence: "It is worthy to be set up in Paradise." And then, when we go within and stroll through its corridors and halls, and explore its chambers of legislation and its departments rich with the accumulated evidences of the nation's

progress, and eloquent of her patriotism and service to humanity at large, we feel like prolonging our devotions at night in thanksgiving to the

Head of the nation for his providential guidance of her destiny.

I went over to the White House while in Washington, and knocked around awhile with the hired help, just to be pleasant, and inscribed my name upon the register for future generations to read and inwardly digest. I didn't get to see Mr. Coolidge, but looked through the family album in the front room and saw his picture. They said he was around there somewhere but they couldn't seem to find him just then. I think he was making out his income tax return and would be busy until after lunch, as it was taking him a lot of time to figure out his exemptions. Mrs. Coolidge was down at the Museum making arrangements with the authorities to donate one of her discarded dresses to the institution, when she is through with it, to be added to the collection of costumes which have been worn by the various first ladies of the land, of which they have there a complete showing from Martha Washington to Grace Goodhue 

Coolidge. That would be a great place to stage the National Old Clothes Convention at its next annual meeting.

The Bureau of Printing and Engraving is a 

10

most interesting place, if you know how to behave while in there, and can understand machine-gun dialect aimed in your direction by female guides, to the accompaniment of clicking machines and roaring presses. Whoever thinks he is through licking stamps, just let him look over the works where they are made. I gathered that they make a million dollars' worth of these postage stamps in a single day, to say nothing of the output of revenue stamps besides. Then they took us into the department where the money and bonds are printed. It was the first time I had ever seen enough money, and, like brown sugar, enough money is enough. We beheld this currency like 

Moses saw the Promised Land-from afar, they pointing the same out to us from a safe distance as we stood on convenient platforms erected for the accommodation of a curious public. But when they told me that I was looking at about eighteen million dollars' worth of money, I was knocked clean silly and had to be taken out and given some fresh air. The day I was there, Henry Ford had them working for him, but I thought I saw one tendollar bill down there which might possibly reach me under the call of the thirty-first question on my second round of quarterly meetings. But I was only mildly interested in that, for I had borrowed enough money on thirty days' time down in North 11

Carolina, to make the trip, and twenty-eight days remained before the note was due.

I was interested, and somewhat annoyed, in this building, by the persistent manner in which the attendants counted us. They had a mania for counting. Evidently they proposed to keep up with us and be sure they were rid of us when we left. They counted us when we entered the building and when we left it, and all between times as we rode the elevator or climbed the stairs or turned the corner. 

Never a thing could any of our party see or be told until the whole bunch had been lined up and counted. That crowd never would have quit telling the stars if they had been turned loose on the heavenly bodies like Abraham was. But it was really aggravating, for a fact, to be counted so often when a fellow just knew there was only one of him to be enumerated.

As we left this building, I noticed several folks sitting in a corner at some machines, with what looked to be several bushels of paper money around them, and asked what they were doing with it. I was told that they were destroying it because it showed some slight defects in the printing, and, therefore, could not pass the inspection. 

That was the most heartless thing I ever saw my government do, when several of us would gladly have taken that money just as it was, defects and 12

all, and made good use of it. Even at that, it was not half so defective as some I was lugging around with me right then, and which everybody seemed willing to take at its face value. I figure that a defective fifty-dollar bill is superior to a perfect fivedollar one, like some I knew to be reposing in my wallet at that particular time. But, anyway, I saw plenty of money that day and I know now how a millionaire feels.

There have been several times in my life when I had more hands and feet than I knew what to do with, but I could have used a few more eyes, if I had had them, while strolling through the Library of Congress. The first time that Washington City had the honor of looking into my face, was back some thirty years ago, when I was a young and callow youth doing time at Trinity College, now Duke University. At that time, the

Congressional Library was new, having been completed in 1897. It made a wonderful impression upon me then; it has not lost its charm through the intervening years. It is not surprising, therefore, that I should find myself devoting as much time as possible to this building upon each subsequent visit to the capital of the nation, where some of my seven dollars a year income tax goes for upkeep. Almost the first place I put into was the reading-room for the blind, by inadvertence, 13

where I was asked if I could see. Upon giving the questioner a rather free translation of some of the things I had beheld since I arrived in the city, I was adjudged out of place with the blind, and admonished to move along.

This structure is a dream of architecture, and I don't mean a nightmare. It is the finest library building in the world, the original cost being more than six million dollars. It covers three acres of ground and houses nearly everything of note in the book and periodical line, as well as the notable emblems and symbols of the nation's life, and the life of all mankind since Adam. I tried, while in there, to make out the original signatures attached to the Declaration of Independence. These people knew how to frame a constitution, but, if what I saw was a fair sample of their handwriting, their early training in penmanship was sadly neglected. 

The same remark might apply to the Presidents of the United States, whose signatures have all been assembled there from G. Washington to C. 

Coolidge. 

While in the city, I went around to the Corcoran Art Gallery where I had a chance to see

George White's Scandals without paying an admission fee, or running the risk of being seen by my Board of Stewards. There is a lot of statuary in there which seems to be left-overs from the 14

famous galleries of Europe, and some paintings from originals which repose in the public and private collections of the Old World. If these figures in marble correctly represent conditions back there when they lived, the cotton crop certainly was short that year and silk was too expensive to use for clothing. Only the fig leaf was in evidence, and some of these were pulled too young to be of any noticeable service.

But this is no slouch of a place, and the Government is doing a good thing by its constituency by maintaining so noteworthy a collection of art. It furnishes inspiration to the people for the expression of their aesthetic nature, and teaches them that there are other values in life except the material. Many of our people cannot visit the celebrated museums and galleries of the Old World where the famous paintings and sculpture of the masters are displayed in rich profusion, but some, at least, may go here and behold the outer fringe of this kingdom in which moves a mighty race of giants who point the way to glories not yet attained by the majorities of men and women. He who builds an empire is a benefactor of his race, but the person who paints a picture or chisels an angel transmutes a drab and materialistic world into a shining Paradise where the gods delight to dwell. I stood before some canvases that day and 15

felt my soul lifted into the heavenlies as I contemplated the silent grandeurs which greeted me from that source, speaking to my inner self a message more eloquent than was ever framed by speech of man or angel.

And then to go out on the street from such a sacramental experience, and have a brindle Ford car nearly run over me was a rude awakening, which brought me back to Mother Earth and

Father Satan, with a dull, sickening thud, which came near transforming me from a near cherub into an unlovely, barking hyena like some I had seen in the Museum that morning. But I am willing, at that, to admit that the street isn't a good place for one to do his dreaming. 

Arlington, "on the Virginia hills beyond the 

Potomac," is the loveliest place to be buried that

I know, provided one happens to be looking for such an attraction. No wonder that William Jennings Bryan, who traveled the world over, and who, so far as some people are concerned, might have found a welcome sepulcher much sooner almost anywhere he might have wanted it, decided that, man of peace though he was, this spot should guard his dust as the prettiest place he had ever seen in which to await the resurrection of the Just. 

To me, Arlington is a pathetic place, just because it typifies a phase of our life which is so use- 16

less were it not for the riotous passions of men. 

Here lie many of the nation's dead, distinguished and otherwise, who gave their lives in grim and horrible war in defense of home and native land. 

Many of these not only failed in their ventures of patriotism, but they lost their lives, and now their very names are forgotten. As we stand before row upon row of headstones bearing their grim testimony to the horrors of this deadly and savage method of settling the disputes of nations, one cannot help feeling that we shall never have our civilization based upon any permanently rational or stable foundation until the thing Arlington stands for has been abolished. 

In 1921, the body of an unidentified American soldier was taken from the soil of France, and conveyed to Arlington, where it sleeps today in the "Tomb of the Unknown Soldier" across the road from the magnificent Memorial Amphitheater. It is well, but America should henceforth forever dedicate her efforts to the abolition of that brainless performance called war, by which a patriot not only lays down his life, but may lose his very identity in pursuit of an end which is never realized.

This is not the expression of a spineless pacifism, as rampant military Jingoism would have us believe. One of the wholesome things that the mili- 17

tary leaders of the nation will have to learn sooner or later, is that a person may be a real patriot and yet, in principle, be opposed to war. Among thousands of our best citizens, and bravest as well, there is a growing dissatisfaction with war, which does not draw its incentive from a defective patriotism, but from a Christianized social order which says, "Mankind, the world over, shall brothers be," instead of fanatics flying at each other's throats upon the slightest provocation, and estimating their relative importance by the number of human scalps they bring home, at their girdles, at the close of the day. This is not said in repudiation of the sincerity of men who have fought in the wars of the past, nor as an underestimation of the principles for which they stood and fought and died, but until we renounce the principles of peace, we must denounce the principles of war. 

For my part, I had rather be living in the broom sedge fields of the South, surrounded by those whom I love, than to be sleeping with the great in beautiful Arlington, at the expense of the 

Government.

CHAPTER II 

"Like a Mighty Army-"

HILE in a bank the other day paying off a note which couldn't be renewed any longer, I was accosted by a total stranger who wanted to know if I had been "down to the Convention yet?" At first, I thought he was talking about politics and that he had mistaken me for a 

Congressman, but my curiosity was almost immediately allayed when the aforementioned stranger confided to me that he had unwittingly "taken me" for Brother Mason, an elongated specimen of 

Baptist preacher of doubtful pulchritude, and that he was referring to the Baptist Convention then in session in a nearby city.

No, I hadn't been to any Baptist Convention- I had just been caught in a shower of rain. But I have been to several Methodist Conferences recently, which is the same thing in principle, only the Baptists think they are democratic and are not, while the Methodists know they are not but let on like they are.

I have been on terms of reasonable intimacy with preachers of all denominations for several 19

decades, and I am of the opinion (with apologies to the creator of Elmer Gantry) that no other group of men among us, taking them by and large, will measure up more generally to the best standards of normal living than these leaders of the religious life of the people. I do not want to "make" over my crowd too much for fear somebody may think I am trying to be funny, and I have no doubt but that this may sound like brindle bovine to some people, coming as it does from one who is himself in line of apostolic succession by the saddle-bags route, but if one doesn't blow his own horn somebody will take it from him and blow it himself. 

Of course, some of these sky pilots are like

Vesuvius-more attractive from a distance-and all of them are reasonably endowed with a normal degree of human nature held somewhat in abeyance by an orthodox belief in the flammatory qualities of a certain mineral which is listed in the purgatorial unabridged dictionary as brimstone. 

But nobody need fear that the majority of them will not keep in the straight and narrow path, for their respective denominations are putting on so many drives and special campaigns that they haven't time to go crooked even if they wanted to. 

The particular group of preachers with whom I am best acquainted are those of the Methodist 20

Episcopal Church, South (of God). They are a jolly good set. Apart from their ministerial character and calling, they are just ordinary folks like the people they serve, and, sometimes, the degree of their popularity is in direct proportion to their ability to be "common," as they say out in the backward portions of Zion. Most of them grew out of the soil right here at home, and they know the folks-also are known of them. Some of them may be short of funds but they are expert financiers, contrary to a prevalent belief among their rich parishioners who have more money but less sense. They are ready to work or to pray, to preach or to play, laying themselves out unselfishly to serve the flock in season and out of season. 

They are keen analysts of human nature and, for the most part, are a jovial, rollicking, fun-loving set, whenever the proprieties of their calling admit of it. They get that way by seeing so many funny things before them on Sunday.

The Annual Conference is the leading religious and social event of the year for every Methodist preacher. It is the one great occasion toward which the whole creation moves. One may dodge his creditors for a good while, or put off his visit to the dentist, or postpone indefinitely his quarterly affair with the barber, but just as surely as the fall of the year rolls around, every Methodist 21

preacher expects to go to his Annual Conference and tell the assembled universe what he has been doing the last past twelve months, as Hambone would say. The regular fall supply of persimmon pudding and "chitterlings" not yet having come in at the time set for this annual gathering, and with the main pounding season several weeks ahead, these itinerant divines go up to this much-heralded meeting of theirs with full-grown appetites, and not bothered any to speak of with conventional notions about excessive table manners. 

Eating is one of the main industries of an Annual Conference, as it is also back home in connection with any "forward movement" designed to "save" the world. At any rate, even at that reduced figure, he broke into the catechism as the wisest man who ever lived prior to the present generation. I used to wonder how he got that way, but, really, when one considers the situation fully, it becomes perfectly apparent that nobody could have that many women around the house without finding out everything that was going on. The time in which a person lives has a heap to do with his character and general reputation. We think the present age to be completely out of balance when it comes to moral standards and practices, but a lot of those old former saints and ecclesiastical "bellwethers" couldn't even prove a good character today in the courts of some of our modern just-asses of the peace.

The devil used to be on the defensive in this country, but, of late, he is sitting pretty, with very little to do. Nobody is mean any more-he is just crazy. I just hate to have that question raised among the ministry, anyway. 

People have been saying that about some of us for a long time, and I just naturally cringe to hear one of the boys admit it. 

If a fellow is as mean as the devil used to be before he reformed, and gets caught up with, it would speak much better for the honor of the human race for him to admit his error frankly, without whining or lying about it, and try to make amends by turning over a new leaf and taking a fresh start. Nearly everybody is ready to sympathize with and help a person who has fallen down through weakness, if he shows a penitent spirit and desires to do better, but nobody can entertain anything but contempt for a sheep-killing pup which begins to eat grass and vomit, as a bid for sympathy, when he sees the whip in his master's hand.

We are about to run this insanity plea into terra firma, as a means of escape from justice whenever everything else fails. Our old self-defense standby 38

has about played out, since we have gone to killing babies and other subjects who are not supposed to offer any great resistance to brute force. We are ransacking the earth in search of technical pleas to save the worthless lives of chronic criminals. 

We used to go to a party in the evening if we wanted amusement. Now, we go out and waylay some innocent pedestrian, rob him, and leave his mutilated body by the side of the road, and then turn up in the court with the plea that we always were a little "quare," and that we ought to be turned loose because we wanted a thrill and thought we were entitled to it. 

The first step we take, after a blood-curdling crime has come to light, is to set all the soothsayers and wise men in the land to trumping up some fictitious defense, regardless of the facts, so that the perpetrator of said crime may be liberated from the penalty of the law. Testimony is juggled, evidence manipulated, and witnesses carefully coached as to what they shall and shall not say, until truth becomes so utterly disgusted with the whole farcical proceeding that it flies away to the North Pole, or somewhere else not easily accessible, and allows legal technicalities to rule justice off the map, leaving society at the mercy of lawlessness, rapine, and murder.

One trouble is that he doesn't have any steady family doctor, but goes around and calls in every quack in the country to treat him. The wonder is that he is living at all when they all get through with him. The remedies are so varied and radical and contradictory that the treatment becomes more of a menace than the disease.

The alienists have arisen to the situation nobly, and they have cleverly fixed it so that anybody can be crazy at will for periods of fifteen, thirty, or forty minutes, as the conditions may require, and then lapse into sanity again and go right on with his business as though nothing at all had ever happened. It is getting to be so that a fellow is afraid to go out any more, unaccompanied, even in broad open daylight, with so many lunatics abroad, lest he should meet one of them and be sent back to the bosom of his family in a suit case.

All of which leads me to say that somebody has opened a Pandora Box in our beloved midst.

Down in my native section of the country, some time ago, a certain farmer found himself greatly overstocked with cats. Being a kind-hearted man, he did not find it in his heart to kill them, so he studied out a humane way in which to dispose of them. He gathered up a sackful of these surplus juvenile felines, representing all varieties and shades of backyard ancestry, and carried them over to the nearby village during the night, while the people slept, and turned them loose on a neighboring hill. The next morning found these kittens parked upon numerous doorsteps in the community, waiting to greet paterfamilias when he came out after the milk, and watching for a chance to walk in and donate themselves to the families concerned. When I was down there a little while ago, they were feeling so much at home that one could not safely sit down in the old family rocking-chair without first dislodging several of the intruders. The farmer had simply shifted his problem to other people whom he had no right to involve in his difficulties, and had 41

solved nothing except his own selfish predicament.

The most striking single feature of the whole business under discussion, is the rapidity with which all of these criminally insane people regain their sanity as soon as they have slain their victims, or, at least, by the time they have been canonized by a jury of their peers and given their freedom, with apologies for having subjected them to the inconvenience of being detained overnight in the town calaboose.

The thing the state ought to do for these poor lunatics who are incurable by any other method, would be to provide somebody for each one of them to kill, as a means of their restoration to mental normalcy. What is the use of building large and expensive hospitals where these unfortunates may go and have all kinds of serums squirted into them, when we could cull enough folks from our population, at a nominal cost per head, and without ever missing them from the streets on Saturday night, to furnish each patient with one or more subjects whom he could shoot, hang, strangle, electrocute, or otherwise dispose of as his particular form of lunacy might dictate for the time being, and thus furnish him a chance to get well immediately. It might be necessary to charge a little more for these specially prepared victims, in order to cover the expense of the extra service, but this could not be objected to by any reasonable lunatic who wants to do the right thing by the state, as he should not want the latter to operate at a financial loss. Thus ends another travesty on justice, and yet we wonder why decent people should become restive under such a system of recognized knavery and incompetency which breeds in the minds of the criminally inclined a contempt for all law because the courts of the land are too weak or too corrupt to administer justice, even when it is recognized as such.

Well, we've got to do something in this country to curtail crime, or the old national bus is going to be ditched. What it is that we must do will require the earnest attention and wisdom of all elements of our civilization to work out. If capital punishment is wrong, let's abolish it and set up other penalties capable of enforcement; if it is right, let us enforce it in cases clearly apparent. If a fellow is crazy, and so many people know about it a long time in advance, let's put them in jail for not telling the authorities before the criminal gets on a rampage and depletes the population. Or, if crime is the result of underdevelopment in the subject, as some claim, or if he acts under constitutional ab- 47

The trouble with most of us is that we are incapable of considering a subject like this fairly and without bias. Our speech is intemperate. We are consistent only in spots, and when consistency serves our personal advantage. In so many court cases, people do not consider the case itself, on its merits, but fall to lambasting one another until the evidence is overlooked. Such a mêlée always results in a lot of sore heads. Our officers are made the goat, times beyond number. We base our theory of justice upon the old English law, and our practice of it upon the whim of every Tom, Dick, and Old Harry who decides to defy it upon arbitrary technicalities. The result is that many a neck has been saved by whining that never could have been saved by evidence, the success of which venture has been transmitted to every thug and criminal in the land until our prisons have become wailingplaces for those who do not like the fare and want to get out.

This is not written in any vindictive spirit. It may be that capital punishment is wrong. Many think it is, and that the genius of enlightened man should find some other and better way of dealing with those who commit gross crime. Then, we ought to abolish it, or practice it, one of the two.

CHAPTER IV Dry Remarks on a Moist Subject 

ATHER NOAH is one of those twilight celebrities who has come down to us in a somewhat unlovely light because, as Rupert 

Hughes remarked about the Father of our country, he turned out to be right much of a soak.

While possessing admirable traits of character in most respects, he was a distinct disappointment to his prohibition friends.

Probably it could be established, if the quarterly conference records had not been lost in the flood, that he was lay leader of his church at Bethel and a member of the board of stewards, but he had "spells" of backsliding which made him very much of a problem to the congregation. He had trouble carrying out his New Year resolutions, and it was whispered around quite freely that his tendencies were not always celestial. But just as, in these modern days, we learn to chew tobacco for our rheumatism, and take the children with us as an excuse for getting into the circus, so friend Noah always had a reason for his bibulous inclinations. 

Perhaps he had been troubled for several cen- 52

turies, more or less, with halitosis. That is the reason the dove never came back after he got loose and had any other place to go. At any rate, on that account, he felt it incumbent upon himself, in order to maintain his social standing, to sweeten his breath occasionally by imbibing a certain popular and well-known brand of antediluvian hootch.

This was not a safe thing for him to do, for it got him in bad with his friends of arid habits, and created a sensation in W. C. T. U. circles, of which 

Mrs. Noah was an active and valuable member.

For fear that somebody might accuse me of being unbrotherly enough to be personal in my remarks, I hasten to say that this particular Noah of whom I speak, is not now living. This is not a fictitious name which might stand for almost anybody who happens to belong to the country club, but it stands for a real person who lived several millenniums ago, and, therefore, it is safe for me to talk about him.

It so happened also that this man was in charge of the government weather bureau. He was never happier than when foretelling storms and floods and squalls. He could predict more storms, and miss it oftener, than our own Mr. DeVoe of recent meteorological fame. His neighbors often consulted him about the right time in the moon to cut their hay, or whether the signs portended a dry 53

spell so that they could ditch their branch bottoms. 

We have it on good authority that he was the first man to introduce scientific methods in making up weather reports. He made light of his contemporaries who contended for the goose bone and the ground hog as expert weather prognosticators. 

Naturally, this course made him unpopular with the hoi polloi, as nobody wants his superstitions to be upset. But he got it back on them; he lived to see science triumph over superstition. One day the barometer fell down almost to the bottom of the tube. He rushed out and warned the people of an approaching storm of marked intensity, but they said that they had been fooled before, and only "ragged" him about it. Some of them went so far as to put on slickers and march around in mock pantomime, as though protecting themselves from the rain.

But the prediction came true, as even weather forecasts will do sometimes if you wait long enough and claim everything like the chairman of a political campaign committee on the eve of the election. That ribald crowd got the ducking of their lives, while Noah and his family went on a long cruise. He was shut in that ark for a year, during which time it became necessary for him to abstain from the use of all spirituous, vinous, or intoxicating drinks of any kind, unless he had the 54

cellars of his cruiser well stocked, unknown to the revenue officers. But you cannot change the nature of a man simply by putting him into a place for awhile where he cannot indulge his natural propensities. He will come back big when the restraints are removed, unless you protect him from his weakness and help him to reform. The fact is that Noah was a good sailor, but a poor prohibitionist. He never did believe in a bone-dry law except as a distinct emergency. He was willing to endure a long dry spell while in the Ark, because he was scared anyway, but when he struck the slopes of Ararat and felt the solid earth under his feet again, he went on one notorious bender which came near getting the whole family in dutch. 

This tendency to drink is one of the prime weaknesses of the genus homo. It has caused more sorrow, poverty, and crime than any other one thing. 

The problems of intemperance are more decidedly complex than can be computed by the most mathematical mind. Like the measles, this curse has remained uncured, breaking out afresh in each generation, and preying most heavily upon those who are weakest.

This country of ours has recently tried a most interesting experiment in the handling of this vexatious question. Our National Congress has said, backed by a great wave of public opinion, 55

that the liquor traffic is an evil which can and ought to be combatted for the good of mankind, and has decreed certain proscriptions upon the traffic which practically destroy it as a legal proposition. This action of Congress has been ratified by an overwhelming majority of the American people, who gave a magnificent response in getting prohibition established.

On account of our position on this question, our nation is just now in the crucible of international interest, and, in many cases, ridicule. There is a tremendous crusade on, as everybody knows, to smash into smithereens our eighteenth amendment to the Federal Constitution, and turn the country back over into the hands of the brewers. The whiskey gang all over the world is acutely concerned about the facts of prohibition in America, and its members are doing their best to assist in defeating it. No other crusade in history has ever been so completely organized as this one, and so desperately determined to put over any measure as the slaughter of this legislation in the United States. 

It is no news to anybody to say that this propaganda against our dry laws is on in earnest, but it needs to be reiterated again and again so that our people shall not drift into an attitude of indifference. The wets are undertaking to defeat the will of the majority which they profess to revere so 56

passionately, as one of the cardinal principles of our democracy, thus hoping to give their beloved booze a return engagement. It is not so much personal liberty they are fighting for, as the legal right to debauch the nation in the name of fair play and in defiance of the expressed will of the vast majority of our citizens who have already expressed their convictions against intoxicants in no uncertain way, and by the largest majority that has ever been recorded for any measure in the history of the nation. When a whole people speak out against this traffic and condemn it almost unanimously, it ought to be a serious offense for anybody to attack the Constitution of the United

States thus amended by the will of the electorate, and demand its repeal. In Russia, if the populace attempted to do a thing like that, off would go their heads, but, in this country, we allow our parlor bolshevists to romp around over our Constitution, breaking down respect for that immortal instrument, and then exult over the alleged failure of a law which they have openly and defiantly opposed, charging its failure to its own inherent weakness.

But they are engaged in a losing fight. I have no disposition to prolong the acrimonious features of the recent Presidential campaign in the United

It ought to be perfectly apparent by this time that any gentleman in politics in America in the future who thinks he can stampede the nation into electing him to the White House on a wet ticket, or even a moist one, has too little sense to know what to do in the office if he could be elected. The only way he will ever live in a White House is to paint his own shack. And no party in the future is going to court oblivion or commit suicide by declaring in favor of alcoholism, as a party issue, and heading its ticket with a man whose breath would break up a hobo convention. We haven't reached, at so much cost, the position we occupy today on this question, wilfully to discard it now at the behest of the organized opposition parading itself under the cloak of personal liberty, who ostensibly contend for principle but would really exploit their country and inebriate its citizens for personal gain. I do not speak of citizens in all parties who, during the recent campaign, supported the designated ticket through considerations of party loyalty, but of the unfriendly, un-American element among us who are opposed to our temperance legislation upon grounds which are inimicable to the distinctive principles and institutions of our American Commonwealth.

Really, the gravest thing about the situation at present is the danger that some friends of the measure may be deceived by the adroit argument of the propagandist, and remain quiet while the whiskey element corrals public sentiment in favor of the modification of the present law. But when this justly famous scientist and great citizen leaves his laboratory, where his investigations entitle him to speak with authority, and takes a fling at subjects which he has never studied at all but has treated all his life with characteristic prejudice and gruff inhospitality, he need not be surprised to meet with the disapproval and criticism of people who are informed on these self-same subjects, and to be allowed by them to amble along toward the close of the day bearing infinitely less of their respect than his scientific attainments, on their merits, would entitle him to receive. 

We may as well admit it, that most people, even strong giants in some respects, are infants in other particulars. Not all babies wear the regulation uniform of the nursery. Some of them are "dyked" out in tuxedos and top hats, but they haven't quit having colic yet. I have sat up with them and listened to them bawl until I wished the sacred Nile river was flowing conveniently near, and that it was part of our present religious practice to drown such brats in 77

its merciful depths, or to throw them to the crocodiles. 

When it comes to religious intelligence, many an old sun-bonnet sister from the rear woods, who never saw a test tube nor even heard that there is such a thing as a kilowatt, can beat Brother Edison to a frazzle in her conception of Deity. Not that ignorance of science is an aid to a better understanding of religion, but neither is a knowledge of science, within itself, a qualification for discovering the Supreme Being. We have been startlingly inconsistent in our thinking in this realm. A person's understanding of God (if he has previously discovered Him) is tremendously enriched and enlarged by a genuine acquaintance with physical and material forces, but his discovery and apprehension of Deity depend upon a personal quality which may function accurately without regard to his comprehension of material forces. The true scientist is in a position to know more of God than any other man, if he have the devout spirit of inquiry, because he is brought daily into closest contact with Jehovah, and is put in possession of secrets and facts upon which to base an intelligent understanding of God; but, if he have not that spirit, he may search the world over and the ages through without being able to find God anywhere.

Mr. Edison's position, therefore, is like that of 78

a blind man standing in the glare of the mid-day sun and saying: "Light means nothing at all to me"; or like a deaf man moving around in a universe that is athrill with the most exquisite harmonies, and saying: "There isn't any such thing as sound." Instead of speculating around about religion and God and spiritualism, and expatiating about immortality and the weight of the soul and other like subjects, our esteemed wizard ought frankly to admit his limitations of knowledge in regard to such matters, and quit disturbing the minds of fickle people once a year by broadcasting his unbeliefs.

The most deplorable feature connected with these periodic tempests in a tea pot is the psychological reaction which is precipitated in America because of them. People get an idea that, because a man is a shining success in one line of endeavor, he is, therefore, entitled to speak with authority upon all subjects he may choose to discuss. But that doesn't follow by any manner of means. A person may be a philosopher in one realm and an ignoramus in another. So when he goes into eruption, every little seismograph in the country begins to tango, and we turn white with fear lest the house of faith shall utterly perish in the impending cataclysm. The Ark of the Covenant is on the move again, and is receiving rough treat- 79

ment enroute; so we reach out our hands to steady it and keep it from falling. But we need not be unduly alarmed, nor should we be apprehensive concerning the final fate of that Ark. Sometimes it is a greater offense to attempt to steady it when it strikes a rough place in the road than to allow it to regain its own equilibrium. It certainly will not rock for long under the assault of any critic, and for us to exercise undue concern about its ultimate fate is to publish the fact of our own lack of confidence in its ability to ride triumphantly the rough ways of unbelief.

Some of these days, we fellows who have been brought up on asa fœtida and free grace are going to chip in and buy a booby to be awarded to the champion unbeliever of the age. The rules of the game are to be simple, but clearly defined. 

The contestants must be men and women of prominence in some realm as far remote from religion as possible, and the condition upon which the prize is to be given is that the winner must have devoted all of his time and study to some other subject entirely different from religion. If he says anything that has a single bit of sense in it, he will be ruled out of the contest. The one who talks the longest and says the least will be the winner. 80

The divorce record of America has become quite smelly of late. We are getting ourselves classed with Russia as home wreckers, for in no other two nations in the world do the people become disgusted with each other more quickly and with less provocation than we do here and there.

swept from their feet by the fascinations of physical attraction and led to consummate a relation impossible to maintain afterward upon the high plane of a genuine spiritual union, the horror of a mismated, loveless alliance would inevitably rob the participants of that measure of happiness which they had anticipated so eagerly, and blast their lives with unending despair. It is far better to make that discovery before the vows are spoken than afterward, and the way to make it is to study one person in particular and not so many of them in general. Nobody ever did justice by this courtship business, and put all that he had into it, while driving a Ford at forty miles an hour, even though he might have been driving with only one hand. 

I am glad to see that the parsons are taking this view of the situation and are endeavoring to slow down the momentum with which the average marriage is arranged, as a precaution against domestic dismemberment later on, but even then, many marriages which seemed in the beginning to be as nearly perfect and well-advised as human foresight could pre-arrange, later on, for one reason or another, turn out to be unhappy and go upon the rocks. What is proposed in such extreme cases? Right here is where the state is asked to step in and tell them that their only hope for the annulment of an unhappy and unprofitable marital 104

contract is for one or both of the parties to become immoral.

Divorce for the one cause of infidelity alone is plead by most Christian churches as a sufficient basis upon which to deal with this whole troublesome question. This cause, among others, has also been recognized by practically all civilized commonwealths as being a justifiable ground for divorce, and perhaps but few people would challenge its validity as a remedial measure designed to protect the innocent parties in case they desire to invoke it, thus releasing them from the thralldom of uncongenial and repulsive associations.

In addition to this ground, however, most civilized states have found it desirable from their standpoint to impose a few other grounds for the annulment of the marriage relation, under aggravated and extreme conditions. In most cases this has been done in full recognition of, and with a sincere desire to preserve, the sanctities of that relation upon which rests the security of the nations. 

But, in many instances, entirely too much latitude has been allowed and too much freedom given, to satisfy enlightened public conscience on the subject, and a reaction has set in for the reform of our whole divorce machinery. States should be made to understand that they must not lend themselves to the despoliation of homes by breaking 105

them up upon the slightest pretext and turning them over to the mercies of a sensuous minority of people who would scrap that institution for their own selfish pleasure or gratification. Yet, would it not be well for all persons concerned to recognize the gravity of the situation, and enter sympathetically with the state into an honest effort to solve the difficulties involved in a brave but honest way, so that society as a whole might become the beneficiary of the joint wisdom of church and state?

And after we shall have reached an agreement as to the best way to handle a question which is increasingly menacing to society as a whole, we should charge the courts with a sane and strict administration of this matter under penalty of censure or removal in cases of a lax handling of it, or complicity with the enemies of our domestic peace and security, and thus avoid radicalism in reform as well as radicalism in practice. 

These remarks must not be interpreted in any sense of the word as being a plea for divorce. I regret that there is even one ground which must be recognized as a justification for it. I make no plea for laxity, but for sanity. Marriage is designed to serve the individual and to conserve the race, and no marriage is contributing its influence to those ends which does not involve a spiritual union of the persons concerned, as well as physical, 106

whether consciously recognized as such or not. In view of the fact, however, that the end cannot always be foretold with certainty from the beginning, even when the best of intentions prevail on both sides, sufficient authority must be delegated to our courts to adjudicate difficulties, and even to annul marriages which threaten to disrupt the high ends they were designed to fill. In an important sense, every marriage is an experiment, but most of them turn out well under the management of men and women of sense who practice mutual forbearance and affection toward each other. It is the exceptional case that needs attention lest the institution of marriage as a whole be imperiled. 

It is thinkable, therefore, that there should be some cases in which constitutional differences between husband and wife are so radically antagonistic as to make harmony and tolerance practically, at least humanly, impossible, do what they may to correct the defect. The question is, would it be better in such cases to coerce a continuance of a plainly unsuccessful marriage in order, theoretically, to protect a single home, than frankly to recognize the mistake, perhaps innocently made, and correct it for the sake of all homes? If Jesus ever put an institution ahead of an individual, I never heard of it. And if you pull Paul and Moses 107

into the discussion and qualify them as expert witnesses, I do not question their inspirational right to speak, but I do have some little suspicion that neither of them, in common practice, would be entitled to a blind following on the part of the matrimonially inclined.

I am discussing the question, in this phase of it, from the standpoint of the state, not of the church. The latter may hold a different opinion and be entirely consistent in doing so. A civil contract that has been made by a state can be annulled, for cause, by the same agency, and the state must pass upon the cause. The church, however, may not accept the validity of the cause as prescribed by the state and may take her own position and maintain her own course, so long as her position does not violate the law of the state. That is true of the present situation with reference to the divorce question. The church acts upon her understanding of a higher law, and is bound by convictions and standards arising from moral concepts with which the state is not primarily concerned. So far as her attitude to the state in the matter under discussion is concerned, while doing nothing to interfere with the functions of the state within the realm of its jurisdiction, she imposes upon herself a stricter observance of a law which she regards as coming from a higher source than 108

that of the state, to the author of which law she is amenable for her acts, and for whose administration she is accountable to the Head of the Church. 

Many Protestant ministers, for example, are not allowed by sanction of their respective churches, to perform the marriage ceremony for divorced persons, except in the case of the innocent party, for the one scriptural cause only. But the state acts otherwise. The situation is one which calls for the exercise of great wisdom and forbearance toward each other in working out an ultimate solution which may be acceptable to the enlightened conscience of the race as a whole. But until we devise some means of working the natural depravity out of men and women, there are going to arise some domestic situations which demand outside attention in the interest of preserving the institution of marriage, and without which relief some alleged homes will continue to resemble the place that Sherman said war is-a locality where powder undergoes a sudden and violent chemical change.

That there are thousands of people in this country who secretly believe in trial marriages, and some writers who openly and boldly advocate them, cannot be denied. All right thinking people regard these views as a menace to society. Still, there is another considerable group who have per- 109

sonally been victimized by intolerable marriages and who would willingly offer their lives in martyrdom to maintain the permanency of the marriage contract "till death us do part," if it would do any good, but who are praying every day that death may soon do his bit and give them a happy release. And we may talk about this thing until we are blue in the countenance over the general cussedness of anybody who may, in desperation, apply to the courts for a divorce, but that isn't going to solve our problem. A marriage which ought not to have been made ought not to be perpetuated, and one that ought not to be perpetuated ought to be annulled, and it will be-either by the courts or the Krupps.

Suppose we preachers get up some brand new sermons dealing with the general proposition of people living honorably and uprightly with each other in all the relations of life, with a view to creating public sentiment in favor of higher standards all along the line. The divorce evil is only one manifestation (though a serious one) of a defective social order that needs toning up at every point. Public opinion can do vastly more to find a solution of this vexatious question than can the Legislature. The Legislature can help by standing by and furnishing the proper machinery for carrying out the ideals of the people, whereby a general 110

reform may be wrought by the forces of righteousness working subjectively every day in the year, whereas the Legislature meets only once in two years. Just think what a predicament this country would be in sometime if we should wake up one morning and find out that Newton was in error about the law of gravity, and no Legislature in session to correct the mistake. 111

Perhaps everybody, friend and foe, will concede that he is a man of considerable ability, certainly of strong personality and indomitable will-power. He is also a perfect egotist and an essential autocrat. He is strong and stern and patriotic, and these qualities appeal with tremendous force to the Italian temperament. Italy loves a hero and doesn't always stop to consider how he became such a character. Her people are temperamental and enthusiastic, and their devotion to one of their own countrymen who has attained distinction is beautiful. In this respect, they are the exact counterpart of our American people who are entirely too much disposed to "crown" anybody with a brickbat who sticks his head above the crowd. 113

America has not been content to leave her great men and women free from the impertinent familiarity which breeds contempt. We just naturally like to get something on a high-up, and if we can pull him down to the level of the average run of people, we think we have done a nifty stunt. We are not in the habit of canonizing anybody over here even the dead. Doubtless Washington had his faults and Woodrow Wilson his flaws, but it is seriously to be doubted whether Rupert Hughes and Mary Allen Hulburt have made any constructive contribution to the good of the nation by featuring their weaknesses before a morbid public who needs nothing more roundly than to pin a little more faith in the essential goodness of their leaders. We need more, not less, of filial respect and gratitude in our national character. 

We could never duplicate the senseless ancestorworship which obtains in some countries of the world, but we can, at least, and should, worship occasionally at the shrine of our departed great ones without peeping under their shrouds for scandal. In some countries they hardly bury a great man at all, but hark him about over the land as a sort of national relic to be worshipped; but, in

But Italy is not entirely deceived concerning the character of Mussolini. He makes no attempt to conceal his autocratic propensities; rather, he boasts of them, imposing his will upon the people without respect for the views of his subjects or the opinions of other nations. The thing he forgets, however, is that the world doesn't love an autocrat any more, and Italy is no exception to the rule. The thing they admire is the virility of this impressive and spectacular man who performs in the spotlight of publicity, and in whom they feel a sense of proprietorship. They are wondering how long he will be able to bluff the rest of the world and get away with it. When he weakens, they will give him the hook. 

Recall that, almost overnight, this man's name loomed big in newspaper headlines, just a few years ago, following the great war. He had imperialistic aspirations all along. The World War gave him his chance to put them into concrete shape. He had a newspaper of his own in Milan and knew the value of publicity. The government didn't suit him. He thought it weak and vacillating in its internal policies and international contacts. 

He felt that a strong hand should be at the helm 115

of state to guard against unjust discrimination in behalf of his people, and he believed that his was the hand that could do it. And so, at the chosen moment, with a clatter and a flourish, this reincarnation of the Cæsars foisted himself out of comparative obscurity and suddenly appeared in one grand coup d'état in the streets of the Eternal City, and announced to the astonished populace that he had appointed himself as their lord and master. Rome had seen many a conqueror riding through her boulevards at the head of the representatives of vanquished states almost beyond number, but not until now had there arisen one who put his iron heel upon the necks of his own subjects, and been acclaimed by them for doing so. 

Chaperoned through the streets by thousands of black-shirted peasants, ready to tear down or build up their empire according to the turn of chance, he hurriedly appeared in government circles, shoved the king aside, seized the reins of state, and declared his ultimatum of force against all who opposed his policies. The moment was ripe for a "bloodless revolution." Italy was practically helpless to resist the invader on account of her connection with the World War, only recently concluded. Her resources were depleted and there was no fight left in her. The other nations were too preoccupied with their own internal problems, 116

growing out of the same war, to protest the admission into their circles of a man who embodied in his character the very ideals that humanity had all but effaced herself trying to destroy. With the whole world shell-shocked, men peered through their half-blinded eyes and saw one Kaiser go out and another come in; and the one who came in was the enforced ally of the nations who fought to destroy the one who went out.

Such is the mockery of war-the irony, the deceptive, fraudulent treachery of it. It settles nothing. It is an outlet for all the bickerings and strifes and animosities of a selfish and degenerate humanity. Were we not told in swelling words that the last sordid military jamboree was a "war to end war"? That democracy was at stake, and that it had to be defended and extended? How many of the objectives which were urged upon the people as a justification for the enforced draft which carried thousands of our citizens into the maelstrom of war, on foreign soil, where their lives were sacrificed in wanton disregard for their conscientious objections to such a war under such conditions, have been made good in the light of later developments? Democracy is a whale of a war slogan, but it is treated about like a double stepson in times of peace.

Down in Italy, the Pope said that the Virgin Mary won it. France is sure that she turned the trick which brought ultimate victory. Belgium knows that if she hadn't resisted the invaders in the beginning the allies would have lost, which isn't far from the truth. Great Britain accredited victory to her superior prowess and generalship. Germany lay low and said but little but there is an impression over there that America had something to do with the way the war ended. 

But it makes no difference which nation waves the flag, nobody won the war unless it has vindicated the principles of democracy, for which we were supposed to be fighting, and promoted peace and good-will among all the nations involved; and he would be a brave man who should make a claim like that for this war. True, we have penned up the Kaiser and sent various petty kings and emperors into exile. CHAPTER IX Why Are Trustees? 

EARLY every institution, educational and otherwise, that I know anything about, is cluttered up with a lot of trustees who meet annually, sit through a bombardment of executive rehearsal of the year's activities, rubber stamp all recommendations without inquiry or discussion, collect their expenses, and then solemnly adjourn for another twelve months. After that, they go home shaking their heads over the sad fate of Alma Mater who has been transformed from a foster mother into a newfangled educational incubator that is capable of turning out young collegians by the hundreds, representing all breeds and strains, and all wearing the same kind of feathers whether they be bantams or Rhode Island reds, and with no remaining visible means of distinguishing between eds and co-eds, students and professors, coaches and deans. That is democratization with a bang. And, just think, we did it, perhaps, by staying off the job and allowing somebody from somewhere to equip our schools with methods and machinery, 130

which standardize the curriculum, but submerge the individuality of the student.

We are graduating so many students today that we cannot stop long enough to read their names before voting to confer their degrees. We pass them all up, without difference or distinction, as qualifying for some academic honor, which is granted by the authorities chiefly on condition that they have paid all their college bills in full. 

I am for hearing read the names of candidates for degrees, before these same candidates "strip up" in their caps and gowns, after which the Lord only knows who they are, or whether they have "majored" in boxing, wrestling, football, or just some secondary thing like history or mathematics. After they have spent four years of their valuable life, and all of their dad's money, for a quarter of a yard of mutton hide and the smile of the president of the institution upon presentation of the same, it does seem that we ought to pause long enough in our alleged deliberations, to check up on the graduates and see whether they are all there. For my part, I am glad that I graduated far enough back to retain my name; nobody has ever heard of it since, but it still clings to me. They didn't give me any medals, but they did allow me to keep my name. 

But, why are trustees? That is the great and all 131

absorbing question of the day, and what are they expected to do, if anything, and why? Did anybody ever hear of their originating anything since the foundation of the world? I'll bet that if the

Temple of Solomon was managed by a board of trustees, the old patriarch turned a handspring when he heard that they had all been captured and carried into the wilderness by the Amalekites. 

Can you imagine what would happen today if the proceedings of that honorable and ornamental body should be interrupted by some heck of a hang-dog member who might rise up in his sleep and ask a question or two, or venture a timid suggestion as to where the ashes from the furnace should be dumped? And, if this same member should wax bold and launch into a discussion of some policy or project that might be up for endorsement, would it be necessary to pass the ammonia around in order to quiet the excitement of the hour?

These questions are suggested to my mind by the rather desultory memory of some years of service on several such boards who were pushed for members when I was admitted, and also by the threadbare condition of my nether sartorial outfit which has been overworked in certain portions of its geographical areas by my sitting around listening to academic orders and advice, 132

until I have worn out more trousers material than any one man ought to be expected to donate to Christian education, without being allowed by the Lay Activities Committee to charge the expense of it to his tithe. 

If anybody should think that the remarks which shall be made herein are inspired by an unsanctified grouch on our part against education, he has miscued an honest motive. Education is a hobby of mine, and my qualification for writing on this subject is based upon experience. I taught an "old field" school for a year and a half, and later, spent five years getting through college. The professors liked me so well that they persuaded me to spend an extra year with them.

I devoutly hope that my venture in this realm may not terminate as did that of another educational enthusiast who used to go around over the country making speeches and exhorting the people to patronize the schools. He never let pass an opportunity to get off his spiel on every possible occasion, and his remarks were very much of a one cylinder affair. In fact, when you had heard him once, you knew what he would say the next time, and the next. Naturally, he became a nuisance whom everybody wanted to avoid. One day, he was haranguing a much bored crowd, on his favorite subject, and, reaching his climax, he ex- 133

claimed in stentorian tones: "Oh, that I had the wings of a dove, for then I would fly from housetop to housetop, and cry 'Education, Education, Education,'" whereupon an old alumnus of hilltop academy, sitting near the rear of the house, broke up the meeting with the response: "Yaas, an' you wouldn't fly very fur 'til somebody'd shoot you down fur a turkey buzzard."

Education is one of the live questions of the day, and is growing more so all the while. In this field, as everywhere else—perhaps more than anywhere else—recent years have witnessed almost a complete change of front. In most respects, these changes are a vast improvement over the past. A serious effort is being made to co-ordinate these schools with the needs of their greatly expanding clientele, by the imposition of standards of education and ideals of life which shall meet the demands of the day. It is a tremendous task, and one which calls for the co-operation of our entire citizenship. 

But here again, as in other realms, there are developing certain tendencies which give concern to all unbiased thinkers on the subject. Perhaps it will be conceded that every institution in the world, whether educational or industrial, is to be judged ultimately by its product. Judged by this standard, our schools must stand or fall accord- 134

ing to the character and usefulness of its students and graduates. Would it be lèse majesté for a friend of education to ask the question: Do our schools in their management, ideals, student supervision, personnel of teaching force, etc., give the young people of the day their best chance to build a satisfactory foundation upon which to base a life of unselfish service to humanity?

Certainly there could be no blanket application of this question to the schools of the nation as a whole, nor is there any sinister application of it to any particular school. But only, and if, it applies anywhere, does the question have force. I suppose that a man can mess himself up more writing on this subject than any other under the sun, unless it be talking about his kin folks. One trouble arises from the fact that people become opinionated within their own fields of labor, and intolerant of outside suggestion, and school people are human beings like the rest of us. They usually do not crave any advice from anybody who does not write a string of titles after his name, or wear phylacteries instead of overalls. This is the kind of intolerance that used to snip off the heads of honest dissenters, and is, in fact, the kind that many scholars accuse other people of having. 

I subscribe to the old-fashioned theory that the school exists mainly for the sake of the pupil, 135

not the teacher, the vicinity in which it is located, the textbook commission, nor anyone else. These all share, of course, in the benefits of the system, but they are not paramount except as they contribute to the one main objective, namely, the education of the individual student. Therefore, everything connected with the school should be held in trust to that end. 

I am wondering whether we are more engrossed just at this time in building up and maintaining a great material equipment, the operation of a system, the adjustment of certain standardization problems, etc., than we are in the end which these are designed to serve. Perhaps some of this cannot be avoided temporarily, when the services of everybody, from the president of an institution to the janitor, is required to set in order the amazing resources which are being placed at our disposal for purposes of expansion. 

But it would be unfortunate, indeed, if we should be unable speedily to come out from under the dominance of such a material ideal, and get back to the real task of all educational endeavor, which, as we have seen, is the development of each boy and girl along lines of constitutional aptitude, and the bringing of every dollar of the state's resources or the church's wealth, as the case may be, along with all the scholarship and character 136

of our vast host of teachers, to the supreme task of constituting each student the most worthy and valuable citizen it is possible for him or her to become under the fostering care of genuine education. 

If we ever forget the object of all this superior equipment, and give our attention to maintaining schools for their own sake, thinking more of the character of the building and the care of the grounds, than of the thousands of students who pass in and out each year, and deifying a few benefactors while allowing the future citizens of the nation to be victimized by a materialistic order of education, it will be a day of reckoning for us. 

It may not come-it should not, certainly-but there is always something in the ambitions of men which makes it entirely possible for us, perhaps unwittingly, under the stimulus of unprecedented wealth and power, to build up an educational oligarchy which shall feel mighty independent and "biggity," and one that shall dream of position instead of service. Prosperity is a wonderful thing, but it does have the unhappy faculty of expanding that portion of one's anatomy where one's brains are supposed to reside.

I have been looking around a good deal lately, in a quiet way, to see what has become of Mark Hopkins, and have discovered that the scamp has 137

made kindling wood out of his log, and that he is now sitting behind a great big polished desk, wearing a goatee and conducting his classes under the personal supervision of some Association of 

Colleges, instead of exercising a little freedom of his own based upon the needs and temperaments of the actual students coming under his tutelage. 

Uncle Hopkins used to establish contacts with his pupils upon the classic contour of an unendowed log, and send them out into the world on fire with his own ideals and personality. Now he calls the roll and grades papers and fumbles with standards and rubs the heads of his students against the walls of some impressive material plant, which has been donated to the cause of education by some great benefactor, and then sends them out thinking that he has done a fine job by them when he has tattooed the outlines of the institution upon their hides, instead of saturating their souls with his own personality. Well, if that is education, the illiterates should call a convention and celebrate their ignorance. As the rustic said, who sat and looked at an educated Chinaman: "If he's created in the image of God, then I'm the son of a monkey," which he probably was.

Education is very much of a personal proposition, and aims and methods should be operated constantly with that in mind. Every student should 138

have contacts with his teachers, and such supervision of his personal habits as may be desirable, for the full development of his expanding personality during the period of his high school and college training. In universities for more mature men and women, this personal contact with individual teachers is not so vital to the welfare of the students as in the cases of younger and more immature students in their earlier experiences in school. If education has to do with the development of the student as well as the impartation of facts, which all must concede that it does, then it is absolutely unthinkable that, in any sane administration, young boys and girls should be given absolute control of their own conduct and habits immediately upon their arrival in college, and left to shift for themselves without careful counsel and oversight being given them by their teachers. 

And yet, this is persistently being done by institutions, ostensibly to develop self-reliance in their students, but which, in actual experience, reacts to their immense hurt. 

The youth of America are going away from home to school at a much earlier age than heretofore. Many of these have not yet completely formed their life habits, and are not competent to weigh matters and control their own conduct wisely, out in that strange environment. If the 139

ban of necessary discipline to which they have been subjected at home, should be entirely removed, and they left to their own resources and inclinations, their position is most perilous. Many such a student has gone away from home to college and remained a few years, only to return with a warped soul, a poisoned mind, and alien views of life, to break, instead of comfort, the hearts of those whose very sacrifices made it possible for him to attend school-the victim of an abominable heresy in academic circles, that immature boys and girls are capable of self-government and should be granted all freedom to do as they please while in college, just so they are present when the class roll is called and do not bring reproach upon the institution by any gross misconduct on their part. That is far more latitude than the Constitution of the United States grants to its citizens. If it be said that many of these go to college not having had any discipline at home before they left for school, I fear that we shall have to admit the charge, though the principle is not altered. In that case, the parents ought to be shot at sunrise on the twenty-first day of June, for the reason that the sun rises earlier that morning than on any other day in the year.

The church has been in the field of education from the beginning. In spite of this fact, there are 140

a few people left who advertise their ignorance by claiming that she is an intruder in that realm. 

It was inevitable that the state should accept responsibility, sooner or later, for the education of her citizens, which she has finally done in a very large and most commendable way; and he is a poor sport who doesn't rejoice in the enlarging life and usefulness of the state institutions. On the other hand, he is no sport at all who doesn't concede the right of the church to be active also in the educational field, and who refuses to rejoice in the success of her institutions. The fact is that there is no reasonable conflict between the two, and both may, and should, operate side by side in the comradeship of a mutual ministry. 

One of the most thrilling chapters that has ever been written in the educational life of the nation is that which is being indited today by the schools and universities under control of the religious denominations, and, while it would take a detective, many times, to discover the difference between "Christian education" and the other sort, if there be any other kind, it is true that some people prefer to have the church label upon their culture, and are willing to pay extra for that distinction and advantage.

It is apparent to anybody who can discern the times, that more money is being placed at the 141

disposal of church schools, by men who have it to give in large quantities, than they are devoting to any other field of philanthropy. This indicates the faith they have in the church's ability to educate the youth of the land. This money, too, is given voluntarily, without any semblance of coercion. 

That which goes to the state to improve her system of education is taken from the people by orderly process of law, which they cannot resist, if they would. Much of this money is given freely, no doubt, but it is required. But that which goes to church institutions is contributed out of the private holdings of the donors, after the state has exacted the portion required for the maintenance of her schools. Therefore, the money which is made available for education under distinct church control might have been withheld, had the donors so elected, but, having been given voluntarily, it represents the real interest of capitalists in this phase of education, and is a complimentary expression of their confidence in the competency of the church to function efficiently in this field, and of their endorsement of the character of the service that is rendered the public by these church owned institutions.

We have some notable instances of this in the East and the South, not to speak of other sections of the country. Apart from the construction and 142

endowment of various denominational secondary schools and junior colleges, several benefactors have made notable contributions to institutions of higher learning, as well as to various charitable and eleemosynary institutions, that challenge the praise and wonderment of all men. James Buchanan Duke comes along and turns loose a sum of money for Christian education, which staggers the credulity of the continent and challenges the admiration of the whole world. This is the princeliest, the most massive benefaction of its kind, that has ever been bestowed by a single person upon any institution within time, and the act signalizes him as one of the world's foremost men of vision. We need build him no other monument. 

Men may hate him or they may bless him, but his place in history is secure. The thing which should concern us now is, how are we to show up in our administration of the trust which his generosity imparts to us? By the very necessities of the situation, he makes us the stewards of his vast resources now invested in humanity. The Lord pity us if that great foundation should fall into the hands of pigmies to manage.

Then, Mr. Asa G. Candler, for no other reason than that of loyalty to his church and faith in her ability to handle wisely a great educational trust, 143

devoted a number of millions to Emory University, in Atlanta. This money has been expended mostly in the acquisition of grounds, the erection of buildings, the creation of an endowment, and providing, in a general way, the physical equipment for the construction of a great University, which the church herself must enlarge and operate at her own expense. By this man's unselfish generosity, along with some others who have stood with him, the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, is beginning to put in action in a tremendously effective way, an institution whose influence is to reach out through all her connectional territory and serve, according to the broad principles and traditions of Methodism, the generations of her youth, equipping them for leadership in every field of honorable endeavor.

All honor to these, and others of like spirit (and there are many), who are making possible, through their benefactions, the carrying out of a high educational program within the church, which is imperative to the maintenance of the church herself as an intelligent and enlightened force for righteousness in the nation, and, in a larger way, to the realization of the nation's ideals of citizenship and upright character. The 

Methodist contingent is not peculiar in this field, 144

by any means, for all the denominations have had a similar record, with variations. 

There is one other stage which must be reached in our attitude to church institutions before the greatest good may be derived by the greatest number. We have been content to sit in the bleachers and cheer the few men who have been furnishing the money. That is commendable, but leaves much to be desired. I do not know of anything more wholesome which could happen to this Southland of ours, than for all our church people to loosen up and invest some of their hard earned savings in these schools which have been made possible by the generosity of a few outstanding benefactors. We are called upon to contribute to every kind of campaign, for the relief of every kind of ailment from corns to cross-eyes, and we are generally game and willing to ante up for a worthy cause. But because of the multiplicity of such periodic and spasmodic calls, we often feel bled white, so that we can do nothing, in a financial way, for our own institutions. And then, too, many of us just don't give a "hang." We say: "Let the rich men do it," while we sit around in the sorry attitude of professional alms-takers. 

But no institution can command the honest respect and loyal support of a people unless, and 145

until, they themselves are tied to it by virtue of the investment of some of their means in behalf of its support. It is great to have rich friends and benefactors, but a school is a joint enterprise between these and the people who are to be served. The large gift builds the house; it is the smaller gift of the humbler person that provides the interest which insures success. 146

CHAPTER X Say It by Telephone 

OR fifty years we have been saying "hello" over the telephone, as well as a lot of other things which it might not be well to reveal. 

Recently the telephone companies have been celebrating the half century anniversary of their existence, and, in the spirit of good fellowship, they have invited the world and his wife to make merry with them over the achievements they have wrought within that limited period.

The telephone certainly is at least fifty years old, because some of us have been waiting almost that long to have one of the things installed in our office.

This harmless looking device has developed a vocabulary all its own, also a new set of manners, among its patrons. Many a person is the soul of courtesy and gentility in the ordinary personal contacts of life, who becomes a barking hyena when he picks up the receiver. He is transformed into a riveting machine the moment he hears the buzz of the bell. The best thing that could happen to these telephone degenerates who are always 147

bawling out central and scattering sulphur all over the premises every time some small thing goes wrong, would be to banish them to some out-ofthe-way desert island where there are no modern conveniences, and give them a chance to listen to the bull bats awhile, and try out the methods of communication which were practiced by their ancestors when the latter were occupying apartments in the cocoanut trees and palm bushes of Madagascar.

It is a little trying, though, for a fact, upon the sensibilities of a high-strung gentleman to waltz out into the hall to answer the phone, at 2:00 A.M. and thirty-two Fahrenheit, only to be informed that it is the wrong number. When our phone rang some time ago at that unearthly hour, and the dairyman inquired of us if we were "about ready to do the milking," we wished that Mr. Alexander 

Bell had turned his genius in some other direction.

But when, a little later the same night, the bell rang again and we were interrogated as to whether we were "the sanitary department," our rage knew no bounds and we spoke right out and gave an exhibition of linguistic legerdemain which put that worthy department in bad with the inquirer for several weeks to come.

But I was thinking of this newfangled way of staging a formal social function every time one 148

puts in a call for central these days. It is proving a real embarrassment to everybody who has not attended finishing school or made a call upon royalty. The custom is threatening to destroy the virility of the nation by putting our great unwashed population to studying etiquette instead of making a living. As a result of it, about two generations from now, we are all going to be glorious social flips and cake eaters. One cannot help wondering what future complications await us under the reign of television and various kinds of monkeying with our aural appendages.

This innovation was borne in upon us unexpectedly one morning, when, calling central in our usual gruff manner and giving the number desired, we were met by the response: "Thank you." The suddenness of it came near sweeping us off our feet. Manners had become so nearly extinct in nature, along with charity and brotherly love, that we were wholly unprepared for such consideration, and the shock of it still lingers. It seems so strange and unnecessary, as the one and only exception to the almost universal rule of mutual disregard for the feelings and rights of other people, that we are having difficulty in adjusting ourselves to the new order of things in this realm. 

The day before, as I rode uptown in my car wondering whether it would last until the final 149

installment was paid, I had been all but knocked into my neighbor's back lot, by some cute looking sheik wearing a bungalow mustache and riding in a sport model, who bored his way through traffic and had to swipe my front fender in order to save his own worthless life, and when I opened social amenities with him, he told me that I was a "hound" and several other varieties of the canine family. And now, to have a great corporation thank me for my putting them to some trouble to serve me, was an experience which caused a flurry in the region of my pericardium. "Ting-a-ling-a-ling. Hello-0-0-0, is this Mrs. 

Wampus Astorbilt of the Sylph Reducing Emporium? Yes. Well, this is central speaking. A

Mr. John Doe is calling-has been calling all the morning for you. I have looked up his social rating and find that he is not listed in our local four hundred, but I am told upon reliable authority that he once picked up the Prince of Wales when the latter fell off his horse while chasing a rabbit, and thus his connection with royalty is established. 

However, I dared not connect him with your number until after submitting these facts to you for your approval. His voice? Oh, it is horridsounds like a short circuit from a high voltage battery-but he says that he is a lawyer and that he must talk with you about the all-e-money of 150

your latest divorced husband-you know, the one prior to the one you murdered recently. Shall I connect you, madam? Very well, then, 'thank you,' 'thank you, oh, so very much.' Ting-a-ling-aling. Hello-0-0-0, Mr. Doe, deposit seventy-five cents for three minutes, please. Thank you. Go ahead." 

That is far different from the small town call we used to make when, after giving the number desired, we were plunged into silence, and, so far as we knew anything to the contrary, the operator went off downtown shopping and left us hanging on the wire. This pending method is far better, only the decision of the defendant company to have all operators put on their sweetest, carolling notes and talk soft to a customer who is steaming his request to headquarters for a certain number, is apt to upset all the habits and traditions of the telephone using clans, and they will be compelled to receive expert coaching from some source.

Whether to take off one's hat and smile back at the operator, or to stand in silent dignity like a headwaiter receiving a five-cent tip-these are necessary details which must be settled speedily by some commission from Paris or New York, else we may be in for more confusion than that which broke loose at Babel when the "Unknown

Tongues Society" held its convention there in the 151

days of reconstruction, following the big freshet. 

Is the customer to respond at all, and, if so, what shall he say? I have personally tried out such stock phrases as "don't mention it," "you're welcome," and "so long, Geraldine," and none of them seem to work out very well in actual practice. 

It is right hard for people nowadays to be polite to the folks they see, much less for them to "take on" much over the ones who are invisible, and if this corporation is determined to reinstate the days of chivalry here in the South at so much per call, it is strictly up to the management of the same to provide a suitable and acceptable code of behavior for the use of its patrons without placing them under the stress of having to work it out for themselves. No customer of the telephone has the time to spend an hour or so each morning before making his calls for the day, thinking up a lot of cute things to say back to the girl when she hands him a coquettish "thank-you-oo" in response to his innocent request for a number. Neither can he afford to jeopardize his reputation for courtesy with the company or the telephone-using public, by depending upon the inspiration of the moment to furnish him a suitable reply, upon which is to hinge his classification in the blue book as savage, civilized, or prosperous. 

I am for the telephone companies strong. They 152

have struggled for a half century with a situation which has presented almost insuperable difficulties from the beginning, and they have brought the service to a point of efficiency which is the marvel of all who understand the ground that has been covered. The service they render is usually courteous and satisfactory, which is more than can be said of some other public utilities, including politicians in off election years. It is only in co-operation with the people served that the best results are to be had. This is always true of all corporations dealing with the public weal. There is a mutual responsibility between owner and patron, and ine best interests of all concerned are attained only by co-operative assistance all along the line, and a sympathetic attitude among all the persons involved. 

Then, too, these telephone companies are called upon to deal with more original human cussedness than the average citizen faintly imagines to be inherent in men and women who claim superiority over the inhabitants of the zoo, and they must smile and keep sweet, at least until their competitors have been driven out of the field, after which time the Golden Rule loses its jurisdiction, and the public catches Hail Columbia. Anybody, though, is entitled to a gold medal who can calmly listen to a frank rendering of what the 153

people think about him, and yet keep his feet on the ground. 

But the telephone folks should not get chesty and conclude that they are the only ones in existence who have made any progress in the gentilities within the last fifty years. Some of the rest of us have been sifting along at a fair rate of speed, on our own hook, during that time. Which calls to my mind an occasion which took place down in the old home community in the earlier days, when contacts with the outside world were limited. The leading event of the year was the protracted meeting in the neighborhood church, which always came off in August about a month after the crops were "laid by." Everybody was there from everywhere, reflecting a variety of physiognomy which betrayed a tendency toward inter-racial marriage, minus any marked degree of respect for eugenics, selective preference, or choice of partners, and displaying a diversity of glad rags ranging from seersucker suits and "fried shirts" worn by the men, to merry widow hats, mutton leg sleeves, and bustles affected by the female members of the aggregation. That was back in the days when ladies wore enough clothes to cover a bustle.

It was a big, good-natured, primitive, perspiring, God-fearing crowd, with no concern about 154

evolution, and without a suspicion that anybody should ever arise to take away the "bad place" or banish their orthodox "booger man," which were the community's chief stock in trade to keep errant youngsters in the straight and narrow way. 

In those days, there were no naughty "September 

Morns," nor November Eves, either, to disturb the glorious virtues of farm life, but Cupid was there in person, keeping a battery of quivers flying through the air all day long.

This scribe was also very much present, with his major interests centered upon the culinary marvels which experience had taught him reposed in the dinner baskets lined up along the hillside, waiting to be spread on the ground by thrifty housewives, in the presence of an eager, onlooking crowd of natives whose appetites were unimpaired by late hours and fancy victuals. I doubt whether there was a man in the whole county who even knew what pepsin is, or who had ever taken a dose of soda for indigestion, even after a big bait of chitterlings, sauerkraut and dumplings, or snitz pies for breakfast. Finally, the word was given for everybody to "come up," and, after the parson had said grace, which sounded to juvenile ears like static-mere interference-we fell to with all the pent up enthusiasm which had been generated by hours of anticipation, aided by the grilling 155

experience of having to wait for thirty minutes after the usual time for eating, on account of the mourners in the church holding up the festivities by their unexpected tardiness in "coming through."

In the midst of the feast, with competition still going strong, and while my appetite was yet far from being appeased, my mother, who was sitting on the opposite side of the table, requested me to hand her a drink of water from the bucket nearby. 

There was no time to waste in the face of the rapidly vanishing delicacies spread before us, and so the maternal request was hurriedly complied with, but, in my haste, I inadvertently "straddled" the bucket to economize time, and was subjected to the humiliation of being called down publicly. 

Thus the stars in their courses combined to ruin the day for me. I had become the innocent victim of an inexcusable breach of decorum which was so generally observed and commented upon by my neighbors as to bring out all kinds of uncomplimentary remarks involving the unpromising outlook for my future career. I was informed that there is such a thing in the world as manners, and that I didn't have any to speak of, which weighed heavily upon my conscience for days to come, and marked me as the black sheep of the community when manners were under consideration. 

I have played the stellar part in many a plebeian 156

situation since that time, and have managed thus far to get through in some fashion with the social amenities of those numerous functions in life to which I have been invited. By devious ways and through many perils by sea and land, I have now acquired sufficient polish to have my shoes shined every Saturday night, and to attend a Freshman banquet without blowing my coffee or wearing my napkin in my shirt bosom. In the meantime, I have kept my eyes and ears open to find out, if possible, what this thing called etiquette is all about anyway, and have discovered that it is just as well as not for one to pretend to be well-behaved if he is going out into company much, whether he lives the part at home or not. It is perfectly right and proper for one to be natural and comfortable while eating breakfast in the kitchen in the morning, but when the town decides to set a swell banquet in the evening for the Count of Hoboken, who probably hasn't a change of socks to his name if the truth were told, it is all right again for the committee on arrangements to make us demonstrate, in advance, our eligibility for a place of honor there by our skill in negotiating our way through the mazes of a surplus variety of table utensils, and our ability to carve our steak without putting our foot upon the same.

Into this rural scene of long ago, there fits a 157

little story, which, although not particularly germane, will not be hard to get away from, and its telling may be permitted by my readers since the incidents given occurred the very next day after the bucket episode. We used to have a man down there by the name of Nelson Slough (pronounced as in wow), who served the county as sheriff for a long number of years, and made a good one, I am told. He invariably closed his official documents and letters with the subscription: "Respectfully submitted, N. Slough, Sheriff of Cabarrus

County." He happened to be campaigning for reelection that summer and was making as many county gatherings as possible in the interest of his candidacy.

As fate would have it, he precipitated himself upon that protracted meeting, and, of course, was there for dinner when it was again spread under the trees. When everything was in readiness to begin, the master of ceremonies unexpectedly and unaccountably singled out Mr. Slough and requested him to ask the blessing. This was a new experience for the high sheriff. He could hang a man without batting an eye, but to address the

Lord in language suitable for an occasion like this promised to be a knockout in the first round. But, although somewhat dazed, he realized that his political fortunes might be at stake, and he pitched 158

in with what show of courage he could command under the circumstances.

At first, he negotiated a pass at repeating first one and then another of the models he had heard used on such occasions in the past, but, while he had no particular difficulty in recalling the opening phrases, he never seemed able to edge in the amen which he saw from afar. He rattled on indefinitely until things were becoming painfully embarrassing, when he suddenly remembered the

Lord's Prayer, which he knew did have a stopping place, and he pounced upon that as the device which was to save the day, and, perhaps, the election.

But he hadn't proceeded far with that until his memory failed him again as he traversed the unfamiliar phraseology of that great old prayer, and he had to shift gear again while pulling a steep grade. Finally, in desperation, and realizing that his success at the polls in November might depend upon his ability to terminate the proceedings at once, he paused a moment, as if weighing the consequences of his act, and then, evidently deciding that the end justified the means, he swiftly glided into port by repeating the word's which were so familiar to him, closing his blessing as he did his legal documents, with the oft repeated phrase: "Respectfully submitted, O Lord 159

God, N. Slough, Sheriff of Cabarrus County."

When the votes were counted that fall, he had an impressive majority.

It must not be remotely surmised, from the stray remarks contained in this article, that the writer intends to discount, in any measure, the practice of courtesy in the transactions that people have with each other, whether social, professional, business, or what not. There isn't as much of that commodity today, especially of a spontaneous, unstudied, unprofessional nature, as there was formerly when the currents of life activity followed more leisurely lines. Gentle manners used to be looked upon with high favor; now they arouse suspicion. Whenever anybody gets to acting nice around us, we wonder what he has to sell, or what he is fixing to put over on us, and we brace ourselves against him. Yet, after all, there isn't an attitude which is due to win more quickly and genuinely than one of consideration and courtesy. Business has learned this and is putting the principle into practice in spots. If one is going to be robbed, even, he had rather have it done by a gentleman than a thug, who would probably beat him over the head with some hard substance, and leave him with his toes turned up in the highway. 

The telephone companies are right. Relieve the bumps, soften the irritations of life, and send the 160

people on their way with lighter hearts. Incidentally, it gets business for the man or the corporation that practices such a policy. 

I have wished, for instance, that the people whom I owe would call their collectors together once a month and teach them how to deal courteously and gently with permanent delinquents. It would be so much less trying on our respective nerves. Besides, it might cause one to dig up a quarter that he didn't even know he had salvaged from his last transaction with the filling station, and pay it on his account. The only time I have ever enjoyed having some repairs made on my automobile was several weeks ago, when the service man was so unexpectedly "white" that he influenced me to claim that there was much more the matter with the car than there actually was. 

I insisted that he do something to it all over, so taken was I with his affable manner and charming spirit, and felt that I was doing myself a favor by allowing him to tinker with it. Of course, my generous impulses suffered a sudden collapse a little later on, when he presented his bill, but the experience was enjoyable while it lasted, and profitable to him. 

That sort of thing works wherever it is tried out. For instance, policemen are thought sometimes to be rather stern in their habits, and in- 161

clined to have their own way about things, but there is one motor cop in North Carolina who can be courteous, while extracting the coin from a hapless driver. F some of our southern states have suffered any sad decline in their spirits recently, on account of the too rigid enforcement of the Volstead act, they have made up for that deficiency by staging a large controversial jag over the comparative sizes of some of their leading cities, neither one of which, by the way, is big enough yet, if they would quit ringing their traffic signals, to confuse a blind pedestrian on his way to the corner grocery.

When that ungodly Census Bureau in Washington got on the air some time ago, and broadcast to this whole upstanding universe just how many folks, in its unanimous opinion, reside at present within the various municipalities of these several states, the incident started something akin to that famous assault and battery episode listed in the classical encyclopedia under the general head of Kilkenny cats. Instantly, the tocsin of war sounded throughout the land, and, from the various cotton fields and real estate dependencies of these rival towns, there poured forth an army of braves, 164

I have attended many discussions in my time, participated in by high school debating clubs, on the subject: "Who was the greater general- 

Washington or Napoleon?" and have seen the gore of the contestants run ruddy beneath their respective feet as they battled heroically in favor of these two celebrities. Besides, isn't once in a decade often enough to bring up this unpleasant subject which is more loaded with municipal rivalry than darkey-town back-alley bones are filled with the potentialities of a riot call to police headquarters?

Why snoop down upon us right here between decades, and catch us napping, with many of our regular population residing temporarily in certain state institutions, with the governor out of town and no way of assembling the pardoning board until he gets back?

The stork is a dandy old bird, but no town can be made great simply by the frequency of his visits. 

When he is influenced by the city council to pick up any old thing and drop it down in our "mongst," in order to swell our population, he has played a scurvy trick on us. Like individuals, they are striving for advantage over their neighbors, while bumming along with a very ordinary population for whose general improvement 166

This whole discussion reminds me of something which took place on Christmas Eve several years ago, in a splendid little city which found itself willing to spare a few of its population without going into deep mourning for them. The city in question, like most of the others in this section. of the country, has an ordinance on its books prohibiting the popping of firecrackers and other explosives within the corporate limits of the town. 

More especially do the officials of said town recommend that the use of dynamite, T.N.T. and other such funny-bone ticklers be limited to people's own backyards, and not pressed into service too much at public social functions, particularly when the officials themselves are invited to attend the party.

CHAPTER XII Baying the Movie Stars 

SHORT while ago, feeling in the mood to set myself up to something and finding my conscience at a low ebb, I drifted into the movies. I hate to make this confession right out in public, lest somebody may think I am hopeless, but, really, I am not an escaped convict from Sing Sing, nor am I a candidate for a place in the Fed eral Penitentiary in Atlanta or Leavenworth. 

Some of our modern censors of human conduct have doped it out that moving pictures embrace about all the iniquity of the seven deadly sins, plus a liberal sprinkling of gratuitous deviltry thrown in for good measure. It is marvelous to me how a person with an inflated conscience and an abbreviated judgment can personally practice gluttony, gossip, intolerance, buffoonery, halitosis and a lot of other things distasteful and offensive to others, all the while thinking himself to be a perfect paragon of virtue, while, if somebody else takes a drink of "pop" out of a bottle, or sings in a vested choir at church, or goes to see an occasional picture at the close of a hard day's work, he is 178

regarded by the aforementioned critic as the chief of sinners, and worthy to have the tower of Siloam fall upon him.

I am no movie fan; but neither am I a movie hammer. Like every other useful institution, the moving-picture business lends itself to great abuse when in the hands of designing promoters who are willing to exploit the public in the interest of their own financial gain. That is what the sane portion of our population is trying right now to avoid. We want to swipe this industry, which is so full of potential good, from a gang of money sharks, and use it for educational and entertainment purposes rather than to feed the lower instincts of the race through the use of lewd and coarse pictures, in order that a few folks may make money at the expense of the many.

There is a growing conviction in this country that Hollywood is about the rottenest hole on the continent. So many of the so-called screen artists have shown up badly under the spotlight which their very profession has turned upon them, that it is getting time the nation paused and demanded a cleaning out of the whole pestiferous gang before they wreck one of the best and most potent institutions for good that has ever been invented by man. Many of these people have been lifted suddenly out of the obscurity of back alleys and 179

elevated to positions of great wealth and publicity, and they have been unable to meet the test of substantial character. They have carried their underworld instincts into the mansions which their fabulous and unreasonable incomes have built, and their conduct is presenting a sorry spectacle to the rest of the world, who naturally wish them well but want them to behave themselves. Some of them have good looks, and proceed to show all that they have. Others know very well how to capitalize human emotions, and proceed to cash in on them. Most of them are showered with attentions and money, the latter of which they proceed to spend on midnight frolics and bath-tub parties until decent people are scandalized by such conduct. Hod carriers, lounge lizards, resort mashers, and ballroom sheiks, suddenly made millionaires overnight, find themselves the victims of every form of excess, and are too weak to resist any of it.

It would be very unfair, however, to bring a wholesale charge against the whole colony of picture makers, because some people appearing in that rôle are undoubtedly clean and representative of our best ideals. They are honestly striving to serve the public in a legitimate way, and are seeking to give the people wholesome entertainment and instruction. As such, they are entitled to the 180

respect and esteem of everybody who has the good of the nation at heart, and should be permitted to enter, with others of the various professions and vocations, into the fellowship of a common purpose to realize life's full meaning and to prepare the highway over which the multitudes may walk in their effort to attain it for themselves. 

It is not an easy matter for a person who is suffering from chronic mental inertia, or who is the victim of a closed or prejudiced mind, to draw the distinction between the institution itself, and the individuals who play on the movie stage. Yet that is exactly what we must do, in justice to ourselves and the future of moviedom. Individuals vary in their observance of law, moral and civil; institutions hold to inflexible principle as the needle seeks out the pole, regardless of the conduct of individuals who may or may not play their parts in harmony with that principle.

I hold no brief for anybody in the movie business-not even a pass to the performances. But I attend whenever I have time, and want to go, which is infrequent. If there is any pontifical mercy to be extended to me on account of my gross worldliness in so doing, I am now ready to bare my bosom in humility to receive the full force of it without anybody having to strap me in the chair. 

Some take calomel when they feel grouchy, I go to 181

the movies instead. In doing so, I have to swallow a lot of vulgarity and salacious suggestions nearly every time, thrown in, I suppose, as a concession to the rough element, all of which is uncalled for, neither desirable nor funny. The very few pictures I have seen recently have been spoiled by that sort of thing, which has rendered them utterly unacceptable to anybody with a particle of refinement, and thoroughly unsuited for anybody to see, especially children and grown-ups.

Then I beat it around to the management and protest those objectionable features, only to be told that such parts of the pictures complained of are just as objectionable to themselves as to the patrons; that the pictures are dished out by great subsidized corporations who are in control, and that these films must be taken by them, along with the better ones, as a condition upon which they may receive any films at all, so that they can stay in the business. The whole thing goes back to the producers, and the claim that they are furnishing what the public wants, which mostly ain't so. 

With the superficial thinker, it is an easy matter to charge local managers with the irregularities of performers, and the cupidity of producers, but it is very unjust to them to do so. The private life of any screen artist is not the affair of the management of the local playhouse. The boys who need 182

straightening out are these subsidized, foreignized, hoboized corporations who fight outside censorship and flout advice from every quarter, until, some day, they are going to wake up and find out that there is a punch in public sentiment when it becomes aroused, sufficient to knock them out of business, and turn this mighty industry over into hands which are capable of directing it in the interest of the public weal. The Federal Government could easily write into our flexible Constitution, backed by the sentiment of the nation, another amendment designed to protect the morals of our citizenship from the vultures of moviedom, and to preserve the institution itself from vandalism and iniquity. 

Charlie Chaplin used to be one of the big screams of the screen. Ever since Greensboro College printed in their catalog several years ago that

A "good cry" is thought by many people to be an exceedingly virtuous thing. It is supposed to absolve our misconduct and wash away all our troubles. Movie actors know this, so they have proceeded to overwork this weakness of humanity.

Nor could it be concealed that the boy was equally fond of his dog. They were inseparable.

Everybody who knows anything at all understands perfectly well that there is a peculiar constitutional affinity existing between a boy and a dog, on general principles, and when that affinity narrows down to a single pair, the result is a devotion between them as beautiful and transfiguring as anything that was ever written in the classics descriptive of romantic friendships which ripened into love when Knighthood was in flower. This affinity they have inherited from their respective families based upon long centuries of association together in the chase and elsewhere as their fancy or mutual interests may have carried them. Especially is this feeling intensified in the case of a boy who lives his life in the open, and who possesses but few playmates, spending much of his time. alone. I have an idea that any person who wilfully interferes with the intimate companionship and 208

Furthermore, whenever a boy is proposing to part with his dog, however onery the latter may be, even for gain, something has gone wrong in the constellation of Canis. You may put it down as a fact that, when such a thing happens, the explanation is to be found in one of two alternative reasons: either some dire financial necessity has arisen which calls for desperate attention, or else the family supply of pups has been greatly overstocked by some unexpected maternal indiscretion which calls for heroic measures to relieve the congestion.

Now they come along with stump pullers and modern machinery doing the work of a score of men who are laid off the job, until they have become so flabby that they find it difficult to walk a half mile after the morning mail without having to stop several times to rest on the way. 

No, I would not re-enact for present-day practice the program of a third of a century ago, but I have a hunch, when it comes to boys that they are about the most unvarying, as well as the greatest, institution in the land. They are the same under Republican or Democratic rule, and are not affected at all by the tariff. If solitude fired the soul of Lincoln and gave his untutored mind the formula by which a whole race of people was to be freed, and a nation preserved; if the challenge of humanity reached the inner consciousness of lads like Thomas Jefferson, in the sequestered homes of the day in which they lived, and dictated to them the exalted tenets of an immortal document like the Declaration of American Indepen- 218

dence; I still feel that the kind of discipline a young chap needs today, in order to fit him for life's stern task in later days, is the kind he can best secure close to the heart of nature, our mother and best teacher, rather than in the artificial environment of walled in kindergartens and primary schools which, at most, can only reveal life at its second best. 

Do not call my boy of the mountains-the same with the pup-an underprivileged boy. He may miss much that others receive, and cry out of the bitterness of his soul against all the gods that be for having made his lot in life, as he thinks, so hard. But by the very concomitants of his situation, he has been saved from a far worse fate than that which he now deplores. How unfortunate for any boy to have every wish immediately satisfied, without holding anything in reserve for future realization. There is no unfortunate, struggling derelict hoboing the highways of misfortune who is one-half so much to be pitied as is the pampered pet of some great fortune, whose only distinction inheres in what he owns, and whose character is perpetually being sacrificed upon the high altars of Mammon. 

I do not fear that my boy of the open spaces will know too little, but that others may know too much. The hills are his teachers and the blue of 219

the firmament is the pattern of his life. The stars are his alphabet, imparting to him in the nighttime, while others frolic, the mighty secrets of a thousand dynasties of successful human achievement. And some day he will inherit a real kingdom, and come to his coronation with glad acclaim. Daniel faced all the discouragements of exile, but struggled through them to success. And when he took his seat by the side of Darius, a hundred generations of Hebrew life sat sponsor to the king of Babylon, and all the Patriarchs lived anew in the person of their distinguished descendant.

If I were asked to select the man who will be inaugurated president of this country in 1965, I would pick the boy who now roams the hills with his dog by day and goes to bed with the chickens by night, rather than the young fellow who, in this year of grace, is making sixty miles an hour over the highways in his Dad's limousine by day, and who has never learned yet that night was made to sleep in. The former might not know all about the intricacies of political economy, but he would bring to his office a type of rugged manhood which is the last sure arbitrament of any enduring commonwealth. 220

HIFT the scenery, clear the stage, adjust the STH lights for the new act. Christmas has gone again into the oblivion of the years, and, with it, have gone the players and the plot. I would not prolong the entertainment, nor call the actors back, pleasing though their parts may have been. Life is not all play, nor is religion all worship. Another day has dawned, with its challenge to saner, better living. But before the parting finally takes place, there are certain reflections which come to me in the aftermath, concerning which I will feel much better if I get them out of my system.

They were telling us in a Standard Training 

School for Sunday School teachers, sometime ago, that there isn't any Santa Claus any more. Well, I nearly cried my eyes out when I heard that until 

I learned better, for fear he had been hit by an automobile, or run over and killed by a Saturday night holiday crowd in Woolworth's. Later, I 221

learned that that Sunday School faculty was only trying to discourage the Ananias tendencies in us and to keep us in the straight and narrow way. 

The shock was terrible, though, until their motive was discovered. It was almost as trying on our nerves, while it lasted, as was the campaign put on a little while before by the same crowd, against red chairs and other things the children like, being installed in Sunday School departments for fear the bright colors might lead the babies to commit bloody murder. 

But why pick on Santa Claus instead of real estate advertisements and other forms of modern fiction? Is it fair to knock good old Saint Nick out of juvenile fairyland and leave so many other goblins running loose to deceive and mislead our youth? My opinion is that if we never give our children any better reason than that for calling us liars, we are headed straight in for a celestial halo. 

No, it isn't Santa Claus who is dead, it is God.

We have killed him again in the twentieth century. 

Pilate crucified Him; America commercialized

Him; and both methods of execution are equally effective. The only difference is that our method of doing it is a little more humane and a little less brutal and revolting. We have become "civilized" and have discarded the Cross in favor of covetous- 222

ness, and substituted greed in place of the garrote. 

The secular interests of this country, and all other lands, as for that, have said to us: "Hand over your Christ. We will pay you thirty, yes, a million pieces of silver for him, and you may keep your delusion to boot." And when we have protested such treatment of Him as that, they have said: "His blood be upon us and upon our children." 

So we have trafficked with them in the market place, while the rabble have wagged their heads at Him in their mad rush for gain or pleasure, just as that other holiday crowd in Jerusalem did so many years ago. The result of our agreement with the world is that we sit in the supineness of sheer physical inertia and clutch to our bosoms only the memory of that perfect life, without even the redeeming desire to go out and commit suicide, as did that other Judas when he found that he had actually betrayed his Lord. say "we" in the sense that the whole fabric of society is involved, with the full understanding, however, that there are very many individual exceptions. It is a known fact, though, that Christmas has become a Mecca for business instead of a shrine for worship. The sole interest that thousands of people have in the event at all is the chance it gives them to fatten their bank accounts, just as the money changers in the temple dese- 223

crated a holy ordinance with their avarice and cupidity, and got driven out of the house of prayer for their offense. All the sharks in creation patrol these waters for big game. Under the guise of paying homage to the only wise God, they have been chiefly interested in paying dividends to themselves.

A few years ago, Japan suffered high nervous tension for weeks for fear that her imbecile emperor would die before the holiday trade could be consummated, and the merchants, thereby, lose a lot of money. Their interest in the condition of their ruler, theoretically, was a national, ancestral reverence for the ancient line of god-men who had governed their empire from time immemorial, but, in actuality, it was an insatiable desire to profit materially by an occasion which they neither endorsed nor respected, but which they were perfectly willing to acknowledge and observe if it could be made financially profitable to them. It is a curious fact that when the old fellow did finally pass away, the announcement of his demise was sent out to the press on December 24th, but dated the 25th, for release, so anxious were they to hold on to him until the last firecracker had been sold.

Great Britain came near having a similar experience with the approach of the holiday season two winters ago, although her observance of 224

Christmas is characterized by a Christian concept of the occasion, underlying its attendant commercial aspects. In common with all nations where the religion of Jesus is practiced, her people recognize a deep spiritual significance in the event, which is not conceded by pagan countries. And yet, in the midst of the national anxiety occasioned by the serious and prolonged illness of her king, there came to the surface repeatedly, the implied, and sometimes expressed, concern of merchants and tradesmen that the commercial interests of the empire would be seriously curtailed by the illness and anticipated death of the royal patient. It is ground for considerable speculation as to what America would do if some high functionary should threaten to kick off unexpectedly in the midst of the Christmas shopping season. My guess is that the passing of the greatest citizen of this generation would not halt the procession over here longer than it would take for the traffic signal to change from red to green.

Be it known by these presents that I would not return to the puritanical Christmas of other days and put everybody to playing harps and fondling crucifixes as their major and only appropriate activities of the day. Christmas is not directly and exclusively a religious season by divine proclamation, but unless there should be a spontaneous 225

adoration accorded by the Christian world to its

Redeemer and Lord, in connection with its celebration, the observance of the day is an impertinent mockery, and its inception and perpetuation has no justification. He would be a poor economist and a poor Christian, indeed, who should deny to business the privilege of catering to the demands of the people growing out of a legitimate observance of the occasion, by way of serving their higher needs, or even their ordinary needs, but when the dominant thought of the nation is one of personal profit instead of personal surrender to the Supreme Being whom we are supposed to honor, our religious interests are submerged beneath a sordid materialism which will surely react against us and bring spiritual destitution to us in the midst of our affluence and prosperity. 

George Bernard Shaw was shooting off his mouth a little while ago about this very thing.

He would have a law enacted abolishing Christmas because, he says, it has become an "unmitigated nuisance." I do not quote him approvingly. 

If that gentleman should find out that the rest of mankind believe that two and two make four, he would probably rise up and assert that there is a flaw in the multiplication table. He is dead set against nearly everything that other people approve and find necessary to their development 226

and peace of mind. At heart, he would perhaps abolish, if he could, the life that furnishes us 

Christmas. For my part, I would exalt and conserve that life, but swat the way in which we celebrate His nativity, protesting against the manner in which we stage the most sordid, disgraceful, and supremely selfish celebration that has ever been devised in the name of the Christian religion. 

Again, I speak in the large. There are many devout and earnest souls who discern the essence of the season, and who find the joy of His companionship as they gather their children and families about the hearthstone and the table and rejoice in the sunlight of His smile. Thousands of good deeds and generous acts are performed by individuals and institutions at this golden season of the year, all of which are gratefully recognized by this writer, but the sacred note of the occasion has been too much muffled in these days by materialism, until it scarcely can be heard at all, while the gentleman who still wears horns and a tail, in spite of Paris and evolution, yells at us through a megaphone from every housetop and skyscraper and street level in Christendom, throwing the masses into a panic of excitement, distorting the purpose of Christmas, destroying its simplicity, and putting the whole occasion upon a basis of extravagance which drains the resources of the 227

land almost to the point of dishonesty, and makes for social excesses which mock the purposes of the day.

Right here I enter the confessional and plead guilty to the grave charge of extravagance myself. 

Nearly everybody has a few dear, close friends, and we cannot do too much to show them how much we love them. So we send out Christmas cards. I myself sent out five cards at a total outlay of fifty cents, exclusive of postage and tuberculosis seals, and received three neckties in return, and several boxes of cigarettes, although I do not smoke. I figure that I am about even on that deal, and can start the New Year with a clear conscience, having seen my duty and done it right nobly. 

I have learned to begin my Christmas shopping about eight o'clock on Christmas Eve. In that way, I avoid the early rush, also any back chat from the clerks, because by that time they are so exhausted that they cannot interfere with you by making suggestions, or otherwise influencing your selections by offering leading questions. Then, too, the stock has been raided by the early fiends, and most of the inferior articles are already sold, leaving one in a position to buy the best bargains at greatly reduced prices. 

While knocking around, and being knocked 228

around, in a big store on Christmas Eve, my attention was drawn to a gentleman who had put off the inevitable shopping expedition until the last minute. He was darting from one counter to another in evident search of a suitable present for some absent member of his family, whose other name might be easily guessed by heads of households. Finally he came to a beautiful fur coat attractively displayed upon a rack. He was seized with a sudden inspiration, and perhaps saw himself in prospect walking home with a gift for his wife which would compensate for all the derelictions of the year, and prove to her satisfaction that he was, after all, a good sport. Quickly I saw him turn the price tag over. It was tragical to witness his surprise and disgust, for on it he read the extravagant figures $395. Wham, went another peace conference. The saleslady peered into his lowering face and said in her sweetest tones of womanly persuasion: "We'll sell that cheap tonight, if you want a real bargain-$350 will buy it just for today." But he had gone. A few minutes later he went out with a martyr look on his face, carrying a percolator done up in fancy holiday paper. What took place behind the scenes, I do not know, but am too well versed in human nature not to venture a guess.

Easter is another church festival whose spir- 229

itual significance is being greatly modified in the public mind by the modern methods of its observance. It has become the conventional time to change wardrobes, and to display an endless variety of glad rags. The Easter procession reminds me of Irish potatoes-there are so many ways of fixing them, but they are just Irish potatoes, after all. We couldn't well get along without them, but how monotonous it would be to eat potatoes all our lives served just one way-say in the jacket, or stewed, or mashed. It adds to our enjoyment of the menu to have it changed occasionally. 

That is one of the chief reasons, with many folks, why Easter is so welcome-it furnishes us an opportunity to improve our looks, or, at least, to change them. Not that the human subject is altered by outside ornamentation, but neither is a plank fence changed by a coat of whitewash. It just looks better and is better. And why not perk up a little in looks, if we can? There are some people who seem to think that every attempt to embellish oneself is of the devil, though, when you come to think of it, of all the charges ever brought against that eminent gentleman, nobody has ever accused him of being good-looking. It is no sin to look good. If it were, I know a lot of folks who ought to be in purgatory; and then, too, on the same basis, there are a lot of others who couldn't 230

miss heaven, if they tried. Slouchiness is no virtue.

It is often more iniquitous than mild vanity, for the latter does not take into consideration the feelings of others. If it is good sense to brighten up one's dwelling or to paint up the old car in the spring of the year, why should it be thought a mortal offense to devote some attention to the appearance of our bodies.

However, it doesn't take a fortune nor require the whole family income to be sacrificed upon the altar of extravagance, in order to do that, as so many people seem to think. Wearing clothes has several objectives, as, for instance, to cover the body, to minister to its health and comfort, and to add to its attractiveness. The two first objectives are almost entirely eliminated now, but the last one is having the run of its life, and is taking all the resources of the country to do it, which doesn't say much for our type of pulchritude to begin with.

Formerly people wore thick fabrics and heavy wraps during the winter, and at Easter discarded them for the lighter and more blithesome patterns of the spring season. Today, we wear almost exactly nothing, winter or summer, and when spring comes we have nothing we can "leave off." We just change the tilt of the hat, the color of the tie, the length of the skirt, and say to the clerk as we 231

walk out of the store: "Charge it." Then we all hit it out for the boulevards to see and be seen. 

But there are many things besides human pulchritude revealed to the onlookers, by the costumes of spring. For one thing, paterfamilias sees stars when the bills come in. It just seems to him that the less material the "creation" contains, the more they charge for it. But perhaps, after all, the show is worth the admission.

The trouble with most of us is that we haven't the courage to be saving nor the honesty to live within our means. If the same shoe sells on one corner of the street for twelve dollars and on the other for eight dollars, it is all but immoral to pay the higher price for it, if one's income is limited, just to satisfy his vanity by having his foot measured by the same scale which calculated the pedal dimensions of old Gotrox of the brownstone front. 

We can face the bullets of a regiment of soldiers, and walk unblanched into imminent danger, if necessary, but to go up against a department store of lingerie and furs and gowns and cosmetics and haberdashery of every kind without surrendering to their extravagant appeal, is more than we can resist.

It is at this point that Easter appeals to the vanity of human nature, and finds its proper observance dissipated in the minds of many people by 232

the extravagant observance of the customs attendant upon its celebration. At one and the same time, it ministers to a legitimate desire on the part of the people, albeit that desire may be abnormally developed, but finds, in doing so, that it meets its greatest competition in the realm of human vanity.

America is a nation of holidays. She has so many of them that they all look alike to her. Their relative significance is not easily discerned by people who care but little for any of them. But there is a difference between a holiday and a holyday. Let us not lose the substantial qualities of the latter in the froth and frivolity with which custom has surrounded their celebration. Every holiday carries a message. Some do not get it, but it is not the fault of the day. Its significance and meaning are conditioned upon the discernment and moral response of those who observe it. 233
