NEW YORK CHATTEL MORTGAGES INTRODUCTION PURPOSE AND SCOPE 

Flexible Security. The chattel mortgage is one of the oldest and most familiar security devices employed by bankers and other lenders. It is perhaps one of the most flexible in use, for unlike conditional sales contracts and some other forms of security it is not generally subject to cumbersome procedures. However, there is much room for improvement in our laws relating to chattel mortgages. 

Practical Aspects. What follows is an attempt to treat the more important practical aspects of New York's chattel mortgage laws with some emphasis on farm details. Obviously, little more than generalities can be discussed for practically every “rule" has its many exceptions. Specific, confusing questions are for attorneys. 

What is a Chattel Mortgage? It is a present transfer of title from the owner of goods and chattels to the lender, the mortgagee. It is more than a mere security or lien. Actual ownership passes to the mortgagee and can only return to the former owner, the mortgagor, when he pays in full his obligation, usually set forth in a so-called chattel mortgage note. 

Scope of Discussion. For purposes of simplification three forms will be discussed: first, the common, "standard" form; second, a farm and crop form; third, a so-called "$1500.00 mortgage" form. In addition, a few "miscellaneous mortgages" will be mentioned, and federal farm mortgages will be compared with New York instruments.

I. Common, "Standard" Form

GENERAL 

Caution; Description of Property. There are a number of practical points concerning this form. There is no "easy way out" in handling chattel mortgages the most cautious approach is the only safe one. The property must be fully described - catch-all phrases mean absolutely nothing; it must be so listed that it is "readily identifiable". For example, motor, serial, cabinet and cattle ear tag numbers should be used. 

Ownership; Possession. It is obvious that the party who is mortgaging the goods must be their owner; indeed, there is a warranty to this effect in practically all forms. Although title is actually transferred from the mortgagor to the mortgagee, by implication of law, and usually by agreement of the parties, the mortgagor retains possession. This implication may be found in the wording of the mortgage itself which states, for example, the location of the chattels ordinarily at the mortgagor's residence or place of business, and the right of the mortgagee to enter upon the premises of the mortgagor and take possession of the mortgaged property in case of default. 

Blanks; Witnesses. May blanks be filled in after the borrower has signed and departed? As a practical matter, it is desirable to have everything filled in before the instrument is executed. But nevertheless the law permits subsequent filling-in, if it is by agreement between the parties or is consistent with the nature of the transaction. Although, strictly speaking, witnesses are not required except upon a "crop mortgage" provided for by a new statute, the only safe procedure is to have every chattel mortgage acknowledged.

Stockholders' Consent; Delivery. In addition, a corporation chattel mortgage must generally have the consent of two-thirds of the stockholders. This consent may be given either in writing or by vote at a stockholders' meeting. A certificate thereof is filed with the mortgage. 

A copy of the mortgage need not be delivered to the borrower, except in the case of a "$1500.00 mortgage.'

FILING 

Necessity. Filing is perhaps one of the most important aspects of this subject. All things being equal, a mortgage is valid as between the parties, but to be protected against third persons filing is a vital necessity. However, if the lender, the mortgagee, takes possession of the mortgaged chattels and continues to keep them until payment in full, filing is unnecessary. Moreover, if a third person knows about the mortgage, has what in law is called "actual notice", filing is not required for protection against such an individual. There are certain exceptions to the filing requirements which cannot be treated, but any question on this point should be resolved in favor of filing. 

Procedure. What must be filed? The original mortgage or a "true" copy, with stockholders' consent if necessary. And the law says it must be filed within a reasonable time. This means, practically, at once. Many lenders are becoming inexcusably lax in this respect. The original or "true" copy must be filed in the proper office in the city or town where the

mortgagor actually resides at the time of execution-not necessarily the residence stated in the instrument; a corporation or association mortgage in the proper office in the city or town of the principal place of business; if there is more than one mortgagor or it is a partnership mortgage, the mortgage or a "certified" copy must be filed in the proper office in the city or town where each mortgagor or partner actually lives when the mortgage is executed.

New York City. If the mortgaged goods are in New York City at the time of execution, the mortgage or a "true" copy must be filed in the proper office in the county where the mortgagor""alleges” in the instrument that he resides and also in the proper office in the county where the property is located.

Residence Change. What of removal of residence after filing and prior to renewal? There is nothing in the law respecting this point and it may perhaps be safely ignored until the time of refiling. 

RENEWAL 

Period. Until September 1, 1943, chattel mortgages had to be refiled annually-within 30 days prior to the anniversary date. By a new amendment, a chattel mortgage must be refiled not later than 3 years from the date of the original filing, and annually thereafter. The refiling must take place within 30 days next preceding the expiration of the 3-year term and each subsequent annual term. 

Statutory Recommendation. There is a question concerning the possible retroactive effect of the new amendment which calls for statutory clarification by the Legislature. In the meantime, however, to be on the safe side, it is perhaps the best procedure to utilize the provisions of the new refiling amendment only in respect to mortgages executed and filed on or after September 1, 1943.

Refiling-No Change of Residence. Where should the chattel mort gage be refiled? If the mortgagor still actually resides where the original 

Aling took place, either a renewal statement, or a "true" copy of the mortgage with its endorsements and a statement as to the mortgagee's interest (the balance due) must be filed.

Renewal Statement. The common form of renewal statement requires a description of the chattel mortgage, the names of the parties, the time and place the mortgage was filed, and the interest of the mortgagee or his successor. Respecting the statement of the mortgagee's interest, ordinarily the unpaid balance is all that would be required. But where there is an agreement to furnish future advances, or revolving credit, it would seem advisable to state this fact and the agreed amount, in addition to the balance actually due, when describing the interest of the mortgagee or his successor. Of course, clauses respecting future advances or revolving credit would also be included in the mortgage itself. 

Refiling - Residence Changed; Statutory Recommendation. But if the mortgagor has moved to another town or city, the refiling must take place in the proper office at the new residence. A renewal statement cannot be filed. Rather, a "true" copy with the additions mentioned must be used. 

However, if the mortgagor is not a resident of the State, the refiling must take place in the proper office in the city or town where the mortgaged

chattels were actually located at the time of the execution of the mortgage —and it seems a mere renewal statement is insufficient. The statute is not so clear as it might be respecting refiling requirements when the mortgagor is a non-resident. Here again statutory clarification is indicated. 

New York City. When the mortgaged chattels were located in New 

York City at the time of the execution of the mortgage, a copy of the mortgage and its endorsements with a statement as to the mortgagee's or his successor's interest is required to be filed in the same office or offices where the original mortgage or copy was filed at the time of its execution, in order to renew the mortgage. Special provisions relating to the mortgagor's residence or location of the mortgaged goods in the borough of the Bronx are to be found in Section 235 of the Lien Law. 

Failure to Renew-Remedy. It is idle to take extreme caution in executing and filing chattel mortgages, if renewals are not promptly and properly filed. Too often "slips", wholly unnecessary, occur in actual prac tice. But what can be done, if one is made? It seems the only safe method is to take a new mortgage. Of course rights of third persons may have intervened. That is the penalty of sloppy office procedure. Incidentally, nothing prevents taking a new mortgage for the same debt. It seems, moreover, that new notes, as well as renewals, or a consolidation of notes, will not necessarily wipe out the mortgage providing it can be shown they are merely another form of the original obligation which the mortgage secures.

PRIORITIES; PREFERENCES 

In General. One of the most practical aspects of this subject is that of priorities. On one side is the lender, the first mortgagee; on the other are subsequent mortgagees and purchasers, and creditors "armed with the process of law." It is a complicated matter and its pitfalls can best be avoided by prompt filing and refiling. Generally speaking, the "rules" respecting priorities apply to both conditional sales contracts and chattel mortgages; the terms may be used interchangeably for this purpose. 

No Original Filing. If the first mortgage is unfiled, a subsequent second mortgage is prior if it is taken in good faith, for a fair consideration, and without knowledge ("actual notice") of the first mortgage. As strange as it may seem, in such a case the second mortgage is prior whether it is thereafter filed or not; and, furthermore, even if the first mortgage is later filed before a filing of the second mortgage, the second mortgage is still prior. Of course, the second mortgage should be filed as a protection against other parties.

No Refilling. What if there is no refiling of a properly filed first mortgage? Included would also be a defective or delayed refiling which is generally the same as none at all. This question is the most troublesome for it is here most problems arise. The second mortgage is prior if it is taken in good faith, with no "actual notice" (knowledge) of the first mortgage, and for a fair consideration. 

Warning. But one vital caution: for the second mortgage to be prior there is the added requirement that it be executed after the refiling date of the first mortgage has expired. Moreover, if the first mortgagee obtains possession of the mortgaged property before the execution of the second mortgage, the holder of the later mortgage is “out”. 10

Common Law Liens. There are certain common law liens" which have priority over all other rights since the lienor has improved or preserved the property, thus conferring definite benefits. Examples are a garageman, agister and bailee of animals, veterinarian, artisan (except work unnecessary for repair or preservation), and an innkeeper; a warehouseman's lien is subordinate unless there has been no filing of the mortgage. 

Bankruptcy. In bankruptcy proceedings, of both the more usual and the reorganization type, the chattel mortgagee is generally protected. 

He may, however, be stayed from foreclosing his mortgage in order to protect the assets of the debtor; but he must not be prejudiced by any delay. Where the sale of mortgaged property does not cover expenses, they may come out of the general fund, if any. Finally, there is no need to refile after bankruptcy proceedings begin. But refiling is necessary when there is a receivership. 

Levy and Sale. By a new amendment, a levy upon and sale of the interest of a judgment debtor in mortgaged property are legalized. The purchaser at the sale steps into the mortgagor's shoes. 

Military Preferences. Military preferences should be taken into consideration. The new federal amendment of October 6, 1942 to the Soldiers' and Sailors' Relief Act applies although the State law lags. An action is necessary for repossession. In chattel mortgage foreclosure proceedings a stay may be granted if justice and equity require; here an action is obviously necessary. However, after induction into the armed services the mortgagor, the borrower, may consent to repossession and sale, abandoning any rights under the Act. In a recent case the seller was permitted by the Court to recover his automobile because of its deterioration, upon condition he pay the buyer the difference between the balance due and its fair value.

DISCHARGE; ASSIGNMENT; SALE 

Discharge. Discharges and assignments of chattel mortgages are provided for in detail by the Lien Law. At the request of the mortgagor, the mortgagee must sign and acknowledge a certificate of payment when the obligation is paid in full. This satisfaction or discharge may then be filed in the proper office where the clerk is required to cancel the mortgage. 

Assignment. Particularly respecting assignments the law must be strictly followed; the provisions of Section 232-a of the Lien Law must be obeyed to the letter. Briefly it states an assignment must be in writing and acknowledged; the names of the parties must appear, a description of the instrument assigned, the date of its filing, and the property claimed under the mortgage. To be effective against third persons the assignment must be filed (prior to the filing of a satisfaction) in the office where the mortgage was filed at the time of its execution. 

Sale. Unless there is a provision to the contrary in the chattel mortgage, the mortgagor may sell, again mortgage, or transfer the mortgaged property (prior to any default in his obligation), for the benefit of the mortgagee, applying the proceeds on the mortgage debt. His successor receives his rights in the property, ordinarily subject to the mortgage until the obligation it secures is paid in full, but does not assume the debt unless he agrees to do so. However, generally there may be no sale nor disposition of the mortgaged property for the mortgagor's own benefit-it is said 11

to be fraudulent and voidable by creditors. Moreover, there are criminal penalties for secreting or disposing of the mortgaged property if it is to defraud the mortgagee.

DEFAULT 

Rights; Cutting Off. The next important problem is that of default. 

The so-called "$1500.00 mortgages" are not included in this part of the discussion. Upon default the mortgagee has the right to retake by peaceable means the mortgaged property which ordinarily the mortgagor has retained. Legal title vests in the lender upon default-the mortgagor has only an "equitable right" to redeem the goods. But this right may be cut off by a sale or foreclosure which also lays the basis for a deficiency judg ment. Lapse of time may also bury the borrower's right of redemption. 

Sale. While there is generally no obligation upon the mortgagee to sell the retaken mortgaged property, as a practical matter it should be sold to cut off the borrower's rights. Unless there is a provision to the contrary in the mortgage, a sale need not be public nor notice given. However, it is advisable to give the mortgagor notice of the sale. It must be held within a reasonable time and fairly and honestly conducted; the mortgagee may purchase the property but there must be no fraud. The sale proceeds are first applied against the expenses of retaking, storing and selling, secondly against the debt and interest. 

Deficiency; Surplus. Any deficiency may be obtained in an action. 

No accounting by the mortgagee is necessary unless there is a surplus from the sale. This may be obtained by the mortgagor, in an action if necessary, or by a creditor or any person "armed with the process of the law." Cutting off the borrower's rights by a foreclosure action is generally too slow and cumbersome. A sale is the speedier and more familiar remedy. 12

II. 

Farm and Crop Form

DISTINGUISHING FEATURES 

Exceptions Listed. With three major exceptions, the "farm and crop chattel mortgage" is generally similar in all respects to the common, "standard" form. These three features are: first, the continuing security clause for future advances; second, coverage; third, use of the property. 

FUTURE ADVANCES 

Suggested Clause. The courts have recognized the usefulness of employing the same chattel mortgage as security for future advances. A common clause states the mortgagor shall ". . . also pay to the mortgagee such sums as may be advanced by the mortgagee at its option to the mortgagor within a period of one year from the date of the execution of this mortgage, such additional advances not exceeding in the aggregate the sum of. ...dollars. As a practical matter this is a way to line up future business, and, of course, could be used in the “standard” form for other than farm loans. Statutory Recommendation. Rights of intervening parties and other legal questions are sometimes raised in connection with future advances. 

They can best be laid to rest, as well as the matter of revolving credit, by clear statutory authorization in place of court decisions. 11 

COVERAGE 

Rule. The question of "coverage" leads right into the barnyard. 

Centuries ago it was stated, “A man cannot grant or charge that which he hath not.' In other words, a tractor now owned can be mortgaged; a tractor to be owned a year from now cannot. Immediately upon promulgating this ancient rule, the Courts began to make exceptions. "Potential Property." What of wool, dairy products, livestock increase, fruit, grass, wine, plants, nursery stock? These problems introduced the concept of "potential property". In all of the cases mentioned the basis for growth exists and is owned by the mortgagor. Therefore, a chattel mortgage is permissable. But it should be emphasized that the instrument should expressly cover such increases of potential property. "Natural", "Industrial" Crops. With the dilemma of crops another distinction was given legal birth. "Natural" crops such as alfalfa were distinguished from "industrial" crops like wheat which must be sown. 

A mortgage on "natural" crops is always possible. On the other hand, until the adoption of a new statute, a mortgage of "industrial" crops to be grown in the future was not possible; a mortgage on “industrial” crops actually grown or growing is proper. "Crop Mortgage" Statute. The new "crop mortgage" statute, section 

230-b of the Lien Law, permits any person, association, partnership or corporation engaged in the business of farming, crop production, market gardening, or the raising, breeding, fattening or marketing of poultry or other domesticated birds or fowl to contract to purchase seeds, fertilizers, feed and other such materials as he or it may require for such business, or to borrow money for such purpose, and to secure payment of the pur- 13

chase price or of the borrowed money by a chattel mortgage upon the resulting crops, whether grown, growing, or to be grown within one year from the giving of the chattel mortgage. "Crops" are defined as all crops of the soil, whether annual or perennial, and include fruits, berries, poultry, other domesticated birds and fowl and any increases or accretions thereof. 

After-Acquired Property. This raises the question of after-acquired property-the tractor to be owned next year. This problem is one of the most important in the realm of farm chattel mortgages. With the exception of crops to be grown within a year from the giving of a chattel mortgage, a mortgage of after-acquired property is "legally" void. However, such a provision is enforceable "in equity". The lender may seize the property when the mortgagor becomes its owner, or he may enforce his rights by an action in equity-obviously cumbersome procedures. Moreover, the lender's rights are only enforceable against subsequent mortgagees and purchasers with notice of the mortgage; they are never enforceable against creditors.

Statutory Recommendations. In this connection, recommendations for statutory correction are brought to mind. The uncertainties and difficulties surrounding the use of future property for present credit call for clarification. Moreover, years ago Chief Justice Stone called attention to that aspect of the after-acquired property issue dealing with priorities; his remarks also seem applicable to the favoritism sometimes shown casual creditors in other priority questions. In effect the then Dean of the Columbia Law School stated, "It is a curious legal incongruity that a diligent creditor who has attempted to make himself secure by a chattel mortgage is given less consideration than a casual creditor. One who in good faith advances money on a chattel should be in no worse position than a casual creditor who has not.'

Tenant-Mortgagor. In connection with crop mortgages two incidental matters deserve attention. First, it appears that since adoption of the new "crop" statute the mortgagor need not have at the time of executing the mortgage a present interest in the land where the mortgaged crops are to be grown within the one-year period. Secondly, where there is a mortgage by a farm tenant a careful check should be made to discover whether he has a right to remove the crops intended to be mortgaged, and further, whether the owner has recorded as a chattel mortgage a lease with a clause providing that any crops grown shall be security for the payment of rent.

Changes in Form. Accessions or changes in form, and confusion or mixture of chattels, should both be distinguished from "after-acquired property". Accessions may be covered by a chattel mortgage, particularly where material harm would result if the property were restored to its original form. They may be compared, rather loosely, to "potential property" rather than to "after-acquired property". However, superior liens of third persons are often a factor here.

Mixture. Where there is a confusion or mixture of mortgaged chattels with non-mortgaged property, an attempt to unravel and separate them should be made. If it is not possible to do this, in the event of a sale, for example, property of the same kind and amount as that covered by the mortgage could be sold. But if the property is not of the same type it might be held to be an "accessory" and subject to the mortgage. However, except where the mixed property is identifiable, the mortgagee is "out" if he permits the confusion of the mortgaged goods. 14

USE OF PROPERTY 

Rule Criticized. The third distinguishing feature of the "farm or crop mortgage" is the exception it makes to the legal prohibition against the mortgagor's selling or disposing of the mortgaged chattels for his own benefit-an out-moded rule, according to Chief Justice Stone, which some other states have discarded. "Crop Mortgage" Exception. Only a partial relaxation of the New

York doctrine may be found in the new "crop statute" where the borrower has the right to sell, not exchange, any part of the mortgaged crops if the proceeds of the sale are applied on the mortgage debt or subject to the lien of the mortgage, or are permitted to be used for the purpose of paying the expense of cultivating, harvesting, preparing for market, processing, marketing or otherwise preserving or rendering merchantable or salable the remaining portion of the crops covered by the mortgage. 15

III. 

"$1500 Mortgage” Form

GENERAL 

New Statute. Since January 1, 1942, where a chattel mortgage is given to secure the unpaid purchase price of goods, wares or merchandise amounting to fifteen hundred dollars or less, sold for any use other than a commercial or business use, a number of technical statutory requirements must be strictly obeyed. This new statute modifies the law, as heretofore stated, respecting this particular type of chattel mortgage. 

Farm Exception?-Statutory Recommendation. Moreover, there is a question whether the new law is applicable to farm loans. In the "crop mortgage statute, farming is referred to as a "business". Certainly farm loans would seem to come within the commercial and business exception of the law. However, the Legislature should amend the "$1500.00 Mortgage" statute by specifically excluding agricultural loans.

FORM 

Size of Type; Heading. There are certain definite requirements respecting the form of this kind of mortgage. The printed portion of any "$1500.00 Mortgage" must be in at least 8 point type, and at its top and also just above the signature blank must be printed or written in a size equal to at least 10 point bold type the words "Chattel Mortgage”.

Details Listed. Every mortgage of this kind must also contain: (a) The name of the mortgagee and the mortgagor. (b) The principal place of business of the mortgagee and the residence of the mortgagor. (c) The date of sale. (d) A description of the goods, wares or merchandise sold with the exception that the serial number or numbers thereof or similar information may be inserted in the mortgagee's copy of such mortgage after the date of its execution. (e) A statement of the transaction, itemized in figures in the following categories, in form substantially as follows: 

Cash price 

Credit service charge 

Insurance premium (if any) 

Other charges, if any (itemized) 

Total purchase price 

Down payment: (a) Cash (b) Allowances 

Other credits 

Balance payable in installments...

It should be noted that modifications may be made in the above statutory statement of transaction-so long as the form is "substantially" the same. 

For example, Regulation W, individual methods, and other reasons may dictate immaterial changes. 16

Regulation W-Statement of Transaction. Incidentally, the familiar "Statement of Transaction" required by Regulation W´of the Federal 

Reserve Board and the form requirements of the new statute are similar with but two exceptions: Two additions are necessary to conform to the 

Regulation: (1) Under "Allowances" should be included a description and amount of any trade-in; (2) the unpaid cash (deferred balance), which is the "Cash price" less the "Down payment," should be inserted just before the "Balance payable in installments".

Payments; Credit Charge. The amount of each payment succeeding the down payment, the due date or period thereof, and the place where payments are to be made are also required in the instrument by the State Law. The "Credit Service Charge" may be set forth in dollars or, if such be the case, may be stated as a simple interest charge on the unpaid balances payable in installments.

Insurance. If a separate charge is made for any insurance, or if the cost of insurance on the mortgaged goods is included, there must be a statement in the mortgage as to the insurance premium, the type of coverage, and whether the mortgagor or the mortgagee is to procure the insurance. 

To avoid the statute's requirement of sending the mortgagor within 25 days a separate statement, the "Credit Service Charge" and the amount of the insurance premium should be separately set forth in the instrument. 

In any case, copies of the policies must be mailed to the borrower within 25 days if the insurance is procured by the mortgagee. "Chattel Mortgage As Additional Security". Whenever to secure the purchase price of goods, wares or merchandise sold for $1500.00 or less. for a non-commercial use a mortgage is given upon property other than that sold, or whenever to secure the payment for services performed or materials furnished for a price of $1500.00 or less for a non-business use a mortgage is given upon property other than the materials furnished, a separate instrument must be used to mortgage such additional property. 

Additional Security- Special Provisions. All the provisions of the new "$1500.00 Mortgage" statute apply except that "Chattel Mortgage

As Additional Security" should appear in the same size as "Chattel Mortgage" at the top of the instrument and below the signature space. It would seem that the "Chattel Mortgage As Additional Security" should be executed separately and in addition to the chattel mortgage or conditional sales contract, if any, given in connection with the sale of the goods, wares and merchandise, or the performance of services and furnishing of materials. 

An executed copy 

Copy to be Delivered; Statutory Recommendation. of a $1500.00 "Chattel Mortgage" or a "Chattel Mortgage As Additional

Security" must be delivered to the mortgagee at the time of its execution. 

This requirement means exactly what it says, and a statement in the mortgage that a copy has been received by the borrower may be rebutted by evidence that the contrary was the fact. There have been many questions concerning this provision. As a practical matter it is often most difficult to hand the borrower a copy of the mortgage at the time of execution. 

In view of the fact that the new statute permits subsequent filling-in on the mortgagee's copy of serial numbers or similar information, and also allows a 25-day period for sending policies of insurance if procured by the mortgagee (as well as for sending a statement separating the amounts 17

of the insurance premium and the credit service charge when not so separated in the mortgage), it would seem advisable to clarify the 'delivery of a copy provision" by permitting the mortgagee to send a copy of the instrument within the 25-day period.

Certain Penalties. Failure to comply with the statute's requirements as to form, delivery of a copy of the mortgage, policies of insurance, and a statement separating the amounts of the insurance premium and the credit service charge when not so separated in the instrument, gives the mortgagor the right to recover from the lender an amount equal to the "Credit Service Charge" or 10% of the cash price if the "Credit Service

Charge" is unspecified in the mortgage or supplementary statment. 

Statement of Account. A complete statement of his account must be given free of charge, annually, to the mortgagor if he demands it. A charge of 25c each may be made for additional copies. DEFAULT Right to Redeem. Along with the requirements as to form, the new statutory provisions relating to the procedure in cases of default on obligations secured by "$1500.00 Mortgages" are vitally important. For the first time a mortgagor is given a "legal", as opposed to an "equitable", right of redemption. Under the new statute, however, there is still an opportunity to cut off any right of redemption in the defaulting mortgagor, both by the usual method of a sale (or foreclosure) and also by serving a “notice of intention to retake.” "Notice of Intention to Retake"-40-20 Days. A "notice of intention to retake" may be served, personally or by registered mail not less than 20 nor more than 40 days prior to the intended retaking. This notice should state the default, the period at the end of which the goods will be retaken, and briefly and clearly set forth the mortgagor's rights in the event of a retaking. 

Right to Redeem Cut Off by Notice. If a proper "notice of intention to retake" is correctly served and the mortgagor does not perform the obligations in which he has defaulted before the day set for retaking, the mortgagee may retake the goods without any right of redemption in the mortgagor. Although it is thus then unnecessary to hold a sale to cut off any right of redemption, yet for practical reasons and also to lay the basis for a deficiency judgment one should be held. However, the sale price is only prima facie and not conclusive evidence of value in a later action for a deficiency. Unless the goods can be retaken without a breach of the peace, legal process must be utilized. 

No Notice-Hold 10 Days for Redemption. But if no "notice of intention to retake" is served, the mortgagor has a legal right of redemption in the event of a retaking on default. Before selling the retaken goods to cut off a right of redemption, the mortgagee must hold them for 10 days, unless they are perishable. During this ten-day period the mortgagor may redeem the goods, become entitled to take possession of them, and continue in the performance of the terms of the mortgage as if no default had occurred, if: (1) he pays or tenders the amout due, with interest, under the mortgage at the time of retaking; or, (2) performs or tenders performance of such other condition as may be named in the mortgage as precedent to the satisfaction of the mortgage; or, (3) performs or tenders 18

performance of any other promise for the breach of which the goods were retaken; AND also pays the expenses of retaking, keeping and storage. 

If the mortgagee fails to observe the mortgagor's right of redemption, the mortgagor may recover his actual damages, but in no case less than onefourth of all payments made, with interest. "5 Day Notice" for Expenses; Demand. Furthermore, where no "notice of intention to retake" is served, the mortgagee is not entitled to any expenses for retaking, keeping and storage if the mortgagor redeems within the 10-day period, unless at least 5 days before the retaking a notice stating the default is mailed to the mortgagor's last known address as shown by the mortgagee's records. In addition, the mortgagor may serve personally or by registered mail a written demand on the mortgagee for a written statement as to the sum due under the mortgage and the expenses of retaking, keeping and storage. If the mortgagee ignores such a demand, he must forfeit $10.00 and be liable for all damages suffered thereby. 

Deficiency Allowed. If the proceeds of a sale do not cover expenses of the sale, retaking, keeping and storage, and the balance due, any deficiency may be recovered by the mortgagee. In any action brought for a deficiency, the mortgagor may have determined not only the reasonableness of the "expenses", but also the reasonable value of the goods at the time of the sale the sale price or the determined value, whichever is higher, is credited on the debt. 

Surrender of Goods 80% or more Paid. However, if an amount equal to 80% or more of the purchase price has been paid and the mortgagor surrenders the goods in ordinary condition, free from malicious damage, at the request of the mortgagee and without legal proceedings, the following must be done: The mortgagee within 5 days after receiving the goods at his place of business must elect: (1) to retain the goods and release the mortgagor from further obligation under the mortgage, or (2) to return the goods at the mortgagee's expense and be limited to an action to recover the balance due. 

No Waiver of Rights; Recission Allowed. The mortgagor cannot waive his rights under the new statute before or at the time of making the contract; no confession of judgment may be entered on any statement executed prior to default. However, it may be agreed that in case of a default the mortgagee may rescind the original transaction either as to all or any part of the goods for which a specific price was fixed in the mortgage. If it is so agreed, the mortgagee may retake without observing the requirements outlined in the statute. However, the mortgagee must credit the full purchase price of the retaken goods to cancel any indebtedness of the mortgagor to the mortgagee, and on demand must pay the mortgagor any surplus not required for this purpose. 19

IV. 

Miscellaneous Mortgages GENERAL 

Motor Vehicle Statement; Lease. Although there are various types of chattel mortgages other than those discussed in the foregoing pages, only a few can be mentioned and two treated in slight detail. Section 230-c of the Lien Law provides for the filing by motor vehicle dealers of a mortgage statement in lieu of filing mortgages on a number of automobiles. 

A lease may be filed as a chattel mortgage where, for example, hay and crops grown by the lessee are security for the payment of rent. 

Real Property Mortgages Also Covering Chattels. Every mortgage transferring both real property as well as goods and chattels as security should not only be recorded as a real estate mortgage, but should also be filed as a chattel mortgage. The only exceptions are corporations securing the payment of bonds issued by the mortgagor-corporation or by any other corporation; if properly recorded as a real property mortgage, it is unnecessary to file it as a chattel encumbrance. 

Fixtures. If a chattel mortgage is given on property subsequently affixed to realty, it is a question of the intention of the parties whether it becomes a part of the real property. Therefore such intention should be clearly stated in the chattel or realty mortgage. However, it is sometimes held that, if the chattel is so annexed to realty as to render it irremovable without material injury to or destruction of the chattel or the structure to which it is attached, it then becomes part of the real property. This exception has been the cause of prolific and confusing court decisions. 

CHATTEL MORTGAGES ON STOCKS OF MERCHANDISE 

Statutory Rules. A chattel mortgage on a stock of merchandise in bulk or any part thereof, or upon merchandise and fixtures pertaining to the conduct of the mortgagor's business, is void as against creditors unless: (1) at least 5 days before execution the mortgagor makes a detailed inventory showing the quantity and cost price of each article; (2) the mortgagee demands and receives a written list of the names and addresses of the creditors, specifying the amount owing each, and certified by the mortgagor under oath to be a full, accurate and complete list; (3) at least 5 days before execution the mortgagee notifies personally or by registered mail every named creditor, or those of whom he has knowledge, respecting the proposed mortgage, its terms and conditions. 

Limitations. A chattel mortgage of this type is effective on existing stocks but not replenishments, and the parties cannot agree to permit the borrower to sell the mortgaged property for his own benefit since it would be void as against creditors.

Floating Lien on Shifting Stocks. However, the law relating to chattel mortgages on stocks of merchandise does not apply to agreements creating liens on merchandise or its proceeds for securing repayment of loans or advances made upon such security if Section 45 of the Personal Property Law is obeyed. This section, allows a floating lien on a shifting stock of merchandise, including after-acquired property, valid as against creditors, if: (1) a sign with the name of the lienor designated as such is placed conspicuously at the main entrance where the goods are kept; (2) a notice 20

of lien specifying the names of the parties, character of the goods, and the period during which advances may be made is filed as a chattel mortgage in the proper office in the city or town where the goods are located and also where the lienor has his principal place of business. 

Statutory Recommendation. It can be seen that compliance with Section 45 of the Personal Property Law might preclude application of the "rules" relating to chattel mortgages that "after-acquired property" cannot be "legally" covered nor the property sold for the mortgagor's own benefit-"rules" limiting the usefulness of chattel mortgages on stocks of merchandise. However, it has been said by Chief Justice Stone that the provisions of Section 45 are "cumbersome and onerous and the privileges afforded by the statute have not been extensively used." He suggested that since the rights of general creditors are properly protected by an adequate recording system there is a need for more comprehensive, clear and flexible provision under New York Law for a general lien or floating charges on shifting stock of merchandise. 

FEDERAL FARM MORTGAGES 

Special Legislation Obtained. In 1934, federal farm lending agencies stole a march on the chartered banks and other lenders in New York State. They obtained special chattel mortgage legislation giving them the advantages of a streamlined, modern law. In effect, they said, "New York bankers and other lenders may be satisfied with an antiquated, cumbersome law replete with incongruities, ambiguities, impossible provisions, or none at all, but we cannot lend money under such a statute. We want a law of our own." And they got it! 

10 Advantages Listed. After seven years New Yorkers began to awaken, and then only partially. In only two respects New York law has been modified, and even then but to a limited extent. The advantages of the "federal" chattel mortgage law are many: 

1. The federal agencies have to refile only every three years, whereas other lenders formerly had to refile every year. Effective September 1st of this year, New Yorkers must refile three years after the original filing, and annually thereafter. The federal advantages are obvious. In addition, it was pointed out that a question as to the retroactive effect of the new statutory amendment calls for legislative clarification. 

2. Refiling by the federal agencies may be within 60 days of the anniversary date; 30 days is the period for New Yorkers. 

3. The federals can refile at the place of original filing, while the banks and other lenders have to follow the borrower. 

4. New York law is vague as to removal of the mortgaged property or of the mortgagor prior to the refiling date. The "federal statute” clarifies the matter. 

5. After-acquired property is legally subject to the federal agencies' "liens." The difficulties under New York law providing only equitable rights and none against casual creditors-are many. The only exception is the new crop mortgage law, and that is but a small advantage compared with the strength of a "federal mortgage.' 21

6. A New York "crop mortgage" must be witnessed, a federal “lien" need not be. 

7. A slight federal advantage is present in the schedule of filing fees. 

8. The "federal statute" clarifies the vital question of future advances; 

New York Law is silent except for court decisions. 

9. While the New York statute applicable to crops permits only a sale of authorized crops by the mortgagor for certain purposes, the federal agencies' mortgagors may not only sell but also exchange. Moreover mortgaged feed stuffs may be used and consumed under the "federal law." 

10. There are other advantages, including an extremely simplified method of assignment. 

Judicial "Realism" or "Humor"? In this connection, it is pertinent to quote a masterpiece of judicial "realism" or "humor." In upholding the legal validity of the after-acquired property clause in a federal farm mortgage as against a creditor, the learned judge stated: [This lending] ". . . . is part of an attempt by the State of 

New York to assist the Federal Government and its said sovereign agencies in their effort to aid the needy and those unable to obtain aid elsewhere.. ??

The United States Supreme Court has held in the cases above cited that in such activities as in the case at bar and others, the Government is exercising a Governmental function and is, therefore, not going into business in competition with private business. This purpose to aid those who cannot get aid from private agencies is made plain in the statute itself."

Unsatisfactory Dual System. One of the major legal questions facing farm and other credit today is, "How to use future property for present credit?" This and other legal problems have been solved by the federal agencies. At present New York has a dual system of statutes: one set, streamlined and up-to-date, for the use of the federal farm loaning agencies; a second, comparing none-too favorably with the former, to be employed by bankers and others outside the favored federal group. 

Conclusion; Statutory Recommendations. The situation in this State is another concrete indication of a trend recognized by every thoughtful individual. What is true here may very likely prove the fact elsewhere. 

The lesson is plain. New York must modernize, in every detail, its chattel mortgage laws to afford its own residents at least equal footing in this respect with competing federal farm loaning agencies. Indeed, improvements along the lines suggested in the foregoing pages, as well as those contained in the "federal statute", should be adopted by the Legislature. 22

In any such event, the mortgagee shall have full authority to enter upon the premises where said property may be, take possession thereof, remove the property and sell the same at public or private sale, without notice of sale which is hereby expressly waived by mortgagor, and apply the proceeds, after deducting all expenses of the sale, taking of possession, and the keeping of said property and all charges connected therewith, in payment of the debt secured hereby; the mortgagee may purchase at such sale in the same manner as a person not interested therein; or in any such event the mortgagee may foreclose this mortgage according to law. If the property fails to satisfy said debt, interest, charges and expenses, the mortgagor covenants and agrees to pay the deficiency and all costs and expenses, including reasonable attorney's fees incurred by the holder of this mortgage in any legal proceeding arising from or connected with this mortgage or the debt hereby secured.
