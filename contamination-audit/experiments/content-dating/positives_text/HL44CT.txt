RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE COUNCIL (1) THE JUDICIARY (a) Discipline and Removal of Judges: The 

Oregon Constitution should be amended to provide that a judge of any court may be censured, suspended or removed from office for cause by the 

Supreme Court. See Sections 8 through 12. 

(b) Judicial Salary and Retirement Benefits: 

Supreme Court, circuit, tax and district court salaries and retirement benefits should be increased. There should be a reduction in the disparity existing between salaries of judges of the circuit courts and judges of the district courts. See Sections 13 through 16. 

(2) COURT STRUCTURE AND ADMINISTRATION (a) Additional Judges for Western Oregon 

Districts 

(i) The Second Judicial District: An addi tional judgeship should be created in the Second 

Judicial District (Lane County). See Sections 18 and 

19. 

(ii) The Fourth Judicial District: An addi tional judgeship should be created in the Fourth 

Judicial District (Multnomah County). See Sections 

20 and 21. 

(b) Redistricting Judicial Districts in Eastern 

Oregon 

(i) The Sixth, Eighth and Tenth Judicial Districts: The Sixth (Morrow and Umatilla Counties), 

Eighth (Baker County), and Tenth (Union and Wallowa Counties) Judicial Districts should be combined into one judicial district. Three judges should be elected in the combined district, one to reside in either Morrow or Umatilla County, one to reside in Baker County, and one to reside in either Union or Wallowa County. See Sections 23 and 24. 

(ii) The Seventh Judicial District: Sherman 

County should be detached from the present Eleventh Judicial District (Sherman, Gilliam, Wheeler, and Grant Counties) and be made a part of the 

Seventh Judicial District (Hood River and Wasco 

Counties). See Sections 25 and 26. 

(iii) The Thirteenth and Fourteenth Judicial 

Districts: The Thirteenth (Klamath County) and

Fourteenth (Lake County) Judicial Districts should be combined into one judicial district. Two of the judges should reside in Klamath County and one of the judges should reside in Lake County. See 

Sections 27 and 28. 

(iv) The Eleventh and Eighteenth Judicial Districts: The Eleventh (Gilliam, Wheeler, and 

Grant Counties) and the Eighteenth (Jefferson,

Crook and Deschutes Counties) Judicial Districts should be combined into one judicial district. One of the judges of the combined district should reside in Gilliam, Wheeler, or Grant County and one of the judges should reside in Jefferson, Deschutes or 

Crook County. See Sections 29 and 30. 

(c) Justice of the Peace Courts: The number of justices of the peace in Oregon should be reduced as rapidly as possible with the objective of ultimately abolishing the office. In aid of this objective, a statute should be enacted abolishing, at the end of the terms of the present incumbents, all such courts which have territorial boundaries within 25 miles of the courthouse in any county in which a district court has been created. See Sections 31 through 35. 

(d) Transfer of Juvenile and Probate Jurisdiction from County Courts: The jurisdiction of county courts in juvenile and probate matters should be terminated and transferred as promptly as practicable to the appropriate district court or circuit court. 

State aid should be provided for service staffs to assist circuit courts in the solution and disposition of family-related matters. See Sections 36 through 39. 

(e) The District Courts: (1) District courts should by statute be made courts of record with all proceedings in such courts recorded by electronic devices or otherwise as may be the most practical. Trials de novo (a retrying of the entire case as if it had not been tried before) on appeal from district court to circuit court should be eliminated. Instead, the circuit court should review only the record of the case as tried in the district court; (2) the circuit courts should by statute be given discretionary authority to transfer cases to the district courts when the case is filed in a circuit court and the amount claimed is $1,000 or less; (3) the present practice of temporarily assigning district court judges to serve in districts other than their own where workloads warrant should be expanded; (4) the present practice of assigning district court judges to serve as circuit court judges pro tempore where workloads warrant should be expanded; (5) the present practice of district court judges holding court in places within their districts in addition to the county seat should be expanded. See Sections 

40 through 43. 

(3) DEFENSE OF INDIGENTS: 

The Oregon statutes dealing with the appointment of counsel for defense of indigents should be revised to provide: (1) for the appointment of counsel in probation revocation proceedings; (2) that an appointment of counsel shall continue through all stages of proceedings, including appellate proceedings; (3) that the appointing court have authority

to terminate appointments and to make substitutions; (4) that courts be authorized to appoint counsel at any stage of a proceeding when it is shown that a defendant is unable to pay retained counsel. 

(4) USE OF ELECTRONIC RECORDING DEVICES 

Legislation should be enacted authorizing use of electronic or mechanical devices for reporting court proceedings within the discretion of the trial judge.

The Supreme Court should be authorized to establish standards for the making and preservation of electronic records. See Sections 51 through 55. (5) BAIL 

(a) Pretrial Release Methods: The provisions of the statute which now provides for release of accused persons on their personal recognizance should be widely publicized by the Oregon State Bar in order that attorneys and judges may be made more aware of such provisions. Experimental programs should be instituted where possible, to investigate persons applying for release under the statute so that information can be quickly developed to aid courts in determining whether to grant such requests. See 

Sections 56 through 61. (b) Bail for Certain Persons Accused of Felonies:

The statute should be amended to provide that a defendant arrested in a county other than where the charge was brought shall be admitted to bail by a magistrate of the county where arrested in the same manner as if the defendant was brought before a magistrate in the county where the charge was brought. See Sections 62 and 63.

I. Introduction: The Oregon Judicial Council and Its Operation (A) The Need for a Judicial Council. 

(§ 1) The Judicial Council of Oregon was created by action of the 1965 Legislative Assembly. Legislative action came in response to recommendations from the Oregon State Bar, members of the judiciary and non-lawyers interested in the administration of justice, who had for a number of years suggested the desirability of such a council. Similar agencies had been created in more than thirty other states. The advocates of the Judicial Council proposed a permanent public agency to study the court system in Oregon, compare the Oregon system with the systems existing in other states, receive recommendations from the public, and make recommendations to the judiciary, the Legislative Assembly and to the Governor for improvements in the Oregon judicial system. The Council as proposed and created is designed to have a membership more broadly representative of all segments of the population than was available through any then existing organization. The 1965 legislature determined that the Council should exist initially only until July 1, 1967, leaving to the discretion of the 1967 legislature the question of whether the Council should be then continued in existence for an indefinite period. 

(B) The Statute Creating the Council. 

(§ 2) The Act creating the Oregon Judicial Coun cil appears as Chapter 494, Oregon Laws 1965. Its provisions have been codified as Sections 1.610 to 

1.710, inclusive, of Oregon Revised Statutes. The text of the act is attached to this report as Appendix A. 

(C) Membership and Officers of the Council. 

(83) A broad range of view is embodied in the membership of the Council. The five public members, appointed by the Governor, include the follow ing: Robert W. Chandler, Editor of The Bulletin, 

Bend, representing commerce and industry; E. A.

George V. Guy, Portland State College, Portland, representing education; John G. Kegg, Portland, representing labor; and Donald I. McNamara, Chief of Police, Portland, representing other professions generally.

The Council members representing the Oregon

Appointed to represent the judiciary are Dean

Ex officio members are William M. McAllister, Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Oregon, Thomas R. Mahoney, Chairman of the Oregon Senate Judiciary Committee, John R. Dellenback, Chairman of the Oregon House of Representatives Ju diciary Committee, and Robert Y. Thornton, Attorney General of the State of Oregon.

The statute creating the Council designates Chief Justice McAllister as chairman of the Council. Representative Dellenback was elected vice chairman by the Council membership. 

(D) Organization and Procedures. 

(§ 4) The large range of vital topics available for Council discussion dictated the need for a plan for organization of the manpower of the Council. 

The early meetings of the Council were devoted to a compilation of a selective list of topics of immediate concern and of long range import which were deemed most urgent for the Council's attention. In order to complete the necessary study of these topics, the membership was divided into four committees.

Topics selected for study were assigned as the responsibility of a particular committee.

The gathering of the study materials and research in the assigned topics was performed at the direction of the four committees. Based on its studies, each committee then made specific recommendations to the full Council membership. These committee recommendations formed the basis of discussion of the Council, and the final Council recommendations, contained in this report, evolved out of the committee work and recommendations. 

(E) Public Hearings. 

(§ 5) All meetings of the Council were open to the public. Representatives of the press and members of the public attended meetings of the Council and its committees.

In addition, pursuant to Section 1.700 of the 

Oregon Revised Statutes, members of the public were specifically invited to submit any complaint, observation or recommendation concerning the administration of justice in the courts in sessions held at four regular Council meetings. Invitation to these sessions was carried in the public press. 

The response to the open sessions was disappointing. Few persons appeared at the four sessions, and, of these, some were disgruntled litigants who had no constructive suggestion for improving the courts for the benefit of the public in general. However, the Council feels that the existence of an open forum which will entertain freely the complaints of citizens is in the public interest. The Council proposes to continue holding these open sessions.

Belt, an attorney. Mrs. Frances Robertson serves as secretary to the Council.

In addition to its regular staff, the Council has received research assistance from Frank R. Lacy, professor of law, University of Oregon, and George

Portland, who served as interim executive secretary until a permanent executive secretary could be found, and John R. McCullough, administrative assistant to the Chief Justice, who freely made available to the Council data on the courts compiled by his office. 

(G) Nature of the Council's Work. 

(§ 7) Some of the recommendations of the Coun cil may be controversial. Although a high degree of agreement was achieved among the Council membership for these recommendations, agreement was by no means unanimous. The Council anticipates reaction to its programs and recognizes the need for constructive discussion if desirable improvements in the Oregon court system are to be realized. With this in mind the Council presents its recommendations set out and discussed hereafter in this report.

II. The Judiciary (A) Discipline and Removal of Judges 1. (§ 8) Recommendation: The Council recommends that Article VII of the Oregon Constitution be amended to provide that a judge of any court may be censured, suspended or removed from office for cause by the Supreme Court. 

2. (§ 9) The Situation in Oregon. The judiciary in 

Oregon enjoys the justified high regard of the public. 

Therefore, the time is especially ripe to review in a dispassionate atmosphere the methods for dealing with judicial misconduct with a view to avoiding possible future difficulties.

Article VII, the Judicial Article, of the Oregon 

Constitution is not entirely clear as to the authority granted for the removal of judges. One thing is clear, at least: judges are subject to the recall pro vision in Article II, Section 18. 

In 1910 Article VII, Section 6 was added by amendment and contained substantially the lan guage of original Article VII, Section 19. Section 6 

"Public officers shall not be impeached, but incompetency, corruption, malfeasance, or delinquency in office may be tried in the same manner as criminal offenses, and judgment may be given of dismissal from office, and such further punishment as may have been prescribed by law."

This section of amended Article VII would seem by implication to abridge the impeachment system for Supreme Court judges contained in original 

Article VII, Section 20 which reads as follows: 

"The Governor may remove from office a 

Judge of the Supreme Court, or Prosecuting

Attorney upon the joint resolution of the Legislative Assembly, in which two-thirds of the members elected to each house shall concur, for incompetency, corruption, malfeasance, or delinquency in office, or other sufficient cause stated in such resolution."

Since Section 6 provides a method of removal (trial as at criminal law) and clearly supersedes the original Section 19, it is arguable that Section 6 was intended to supersede Section 20 of the original Article VII. This is so, it is argued, because Section 

20 was not re-enacted or included in an amended version. 

On the other hand, the framers of the Constitution in 1859 may have envisioned a separate role for Section 20 which would indicate that Section 20 is not superseded. There is some evidence that the original draftsmen feared that Supreme Court judges and prosecuting attorneys might by reason of their duties and functions be too close to proceedings under Section 19 for their own effective removal. 

It can be inferred, therefore, that Section 20 was intended to operate independently of original Section 19 and that the incorporation of original Section 19 into amended Section 6 did not repeal or supersede original Section 20.

Whatever the present legal relationship between Section 6 and Section 20 may be, certain facts are apparent. In no instance has a Supreme Court judge been removed from office by impeachment under 

Section 20. Nor has any judge apparently ever been removed from office under Section 6 of Article VII. 

Removal through impeachment, recall or criminal trial leaves much to be desired. In the first place all methods are costly and cumbersome. 

The impeachment process is in fact so cumbersome that it is, practically speaking, an absurdity, especially in our modern times when the Legislative Assembly barely can cope with its primary function of law making. Even as early as 1819 Thomas Jefferson criticized the device as not even worthy of serving as a "scarecrow."

Bitter experiences in Florida are illustrative of the inefficiency of the impeachment system. Florida undertook two trials of judges by impeachment, one in 1957 and the other in 1963. The legislature had to meet in special session for each trial, because it was far too busy to take time during its regular legislative session. The trials cost about a quarter of a million dollars, which equalled over half the annual budget of the Florida Supreme Court. The two judges were acquitted although it is reported that they were guilty of misconduct. However, many of the legislators voting on the question of removal, the only punishment available, apparently felt that the punishment was too harsh to fit the misconduct proved.

More serious than the lack of practically operable removal procedures is the non-existence of authority to discipline short of removal. In many instances it might be highly beneficial to the public interest and the administration of justice to bring an errant judge up short with appropriate disciplinary measures such as reprimands or temporary suspension from office. But in Oregon, at the present, the judge who develops bad habits such as tardiness, laziness, overindulgence in alcohol and the like cannot effectively be disciplined with a view to correction. 

3. (§ 10) The California Plan. Several states have enacted discipline and removal plans which depart radically from the traditional approach of impeachment. These states include California, Texas, Illinois,

§ 11 

Louisiana, Alabama, and New Jersey. In at least three other states, Colorado, Florida and New Mexico, proposals have been made recently to depart from the old concept of impeachment. 

The history of the movement for better discipline provisions resulting in the California plan is fairly recent. The New York Citizens' Committee on the 

Courts, a public group with important standing, advocated successfully in 1949 a Court on the Judiciary for New York. By 1959 sentiment to deal with the discipline and removal problems ripened in the form of a national conference in Chicago sponsored by the American Bar Association, the Institute of Judicial Administration and the American Judicature Society. This conference recommended many of the features later adopted in California and elsewhere which serve as the basis for programs in states currently studying the problem. 

California in 1960 adopted the first comprehensive new approach to judicial discipline and removal. 

The California plan consists basically of a commission composed of five judges, two lawyers and two laymen. The commission, called the Commission on 

Judicial Qualifications, is appointed partially by the California Supreme Court, partially by the board of governors of the California State Bar Association and partially by the Governor with the consent of the California Senate.

The commission entertains complaints from any person. The complaints are privileged and are kept confidential by the commission to protect the judiciary from undue harrassment and unjustified allegations. 

The commission may (1) recommend to the Supreme Court involuntary retirement for "disability seriously interfering with the performance of his duties, which is or is likely to become, of a permanent character"; (2) recommend to the Supreme

Court removal for "wilful misconduct in office or wilful and persistent failure to perform his duties or habitual intemperance"; (3) recommend dis- (3) recommend discipline, which is not specifically delineated in the 

California constitutional provision. The practice in

California is to communicate directly with judges about results of disciplinary investigations and suggest corrections.

The commission in California proceeds generall as follows: complaints are received and confidential investigations are made; a hearing by three special masters may be ordered; recommendations may be made to the Supreme Court for removal or retirement based on the investigations and hearings. The 

Supreme Court is charged with reviewing the law and fact of the hearing and may permit additional evidence. The Supreme Court makes final disposition of the matter. 

4. (§ 11) Experience under the California Plan. 

Figures for the period 1960-64 in California show the beneficial effects of the discipline and removal plan. 

During that four-year period the commission received 344 complaints against the 1,000 sitting judges.

Most of the complaints were dismissed as groundless. The commission directly caused the voluntary resignation or retirement of 26 judges without formal removal proceedings. This was usually accomplished by approaching the judge informally through his family or otherwise when it was obvious to the commission that the judge must be removed. This informal method, besides being effective, has minimized harmful publicity and unnecessary disgrace of the judges affected. Only one case is known to have been carried to the California Supreme Court, and the Supreme Court on review of the evidence declined to remove the judge.

In other cases questionable activity was found which did not warrant removal in the opinion of the commission. Misconduct included unjudicial participation in a case, old fashioned laziness and faulty courtroom demeanor. The offending judges were given a simple notice by the commission to mend their ways and this notice was enough to bring about notable improvement in all situations. 

The commission in California seems to realize the sensitive position it occupies as evidenced in its 

1963 report in which it said: "The Commission is mindful that it is exercising a significant and sensitive attribute of state sovereignty. By operating carefully and unobtrusively a practical contribution to jurisprudence is being forged. It has now been demonstrated that an independent commission, possessing the authority to investigate and hold hearings, can act for the maintenance of judicial fitness without infringing on the essential prerogatives of the judicial branch."

The California commission operates in strict confidentiality, thus preserving the integrity of the good judge against unfounded attacks. At the same time the confidential nature of the proceedings encourages the unethical judge to resign without public disgrace which might unfairly reflect on the entire bench.

The comments of a California judge appearing in the July, 1965 Trial Judge's Journal are interesting: "Judge Wrath was peevish to the point of terrorizing all who came before him, appellate reviewers repeatedly denounced his abusive and prejudicial misconduct, all to no avail . . . "Inoffensive, but ineffective, was Judge Dormant, who dozed through most hearings. 

Countless files on submitted matters accumu-

$15 lated dust in his closet. His disinterest in case and calendar created backlogs which caused colleagues at the bench and counsel before the bar to fume and fret . 

"The infirmities of advancing years and successive disabling attacks had reduced Judge Fourscore to a pitiable caricature of his former self, literally unable to comprehend, let alone concentrate · · · "Judge Elsewhere was able, but not often in court. His many investments and private employments required most of his time and attention, despite reiterated pleas by his brethren on the bench that he bear more of a judicial hand. . . "The foregoing are fictional, but founded in fact. They illustrate some of the problems -uncommon, yet intolerable-which in California now receive scrutiny by our Commission on Judicial Qualifications. As soon as reports of misbehavior, unusual procrastination, inexcusable discourtesy, vindictiveness, absenteeism, disability, etc., are called to the

Commission's attention they are reviewed to see if further consideration is warranted. The judge in question may receive a letter setting forth the practice, impropriety or incapacity charged, and requesting an explanation. A preliminary examination may be conducted, with a formal hearing possibly to follow. A recommendation for the removal or retirement of the judge can be submitted to our Supreme Court. Strict confidentiality applies until the time such a recommendation is so filed . "How have California judges reacted to the omnipresence of such a supervisory authority? For us the system is no longer a novelty, but a proven fact of judicial life. 

Extensive probings evoke no indications of misgiving or regret. Contrariwise, the Commission's existence keeps us mindful that we are ministers of the law, not masters of it...' 

5. (§ 12) Specific Recommendations for Oregon.

The present system in Oregon for removal of judges in deserving cases is completely inadequate. An effective system for discipline of judges short of removal is non-existent. To correct both of these inadequacies, the Council proposes the adoption of an amendment to the Oregon Constitution. The text of the amendment is set out in Exhibit 1. To implement this provision, the draft of a statute is also set out (Exhibit 2) for the study of the legislature, which is encouraged to consider its provisions in connection with the constitutional amendment. The major features of the enabling amendment and the suggested statute are as follows. 

Commission Membership. The proposed statute authorizes the Commission on Judicial Fitness. The statute provides that it be composed of three judges appointed by the Supreme Court, three lawyers appointed by the Board of Governors of the Oregon State Bar, and three laymen appointed by the Governor. The Governor's appointments are subject to confirmation by the Senate.

Chairmanship, terms and salary: The implementing statute provides that the chairman of the Commission is elected by the other Commission members. 

All serve for terms of six years without compensation except for expenses incurred. 

Complaint: The statute provides that complaints may be made to the Commission by "any person." 

The form for the complaint is not specified but it is presumed any written complaint would be acceptable. All complaints are kept confidential. 

Causes for discipline or removal: The amendment provides that a judge of any court may be censured, suspended or removed from office "for conduct that brings the judicial office into disrepute, including but not limited to: (a) Wilful misconduct in judicial office, occurring not more than six years prior to the commencement of his current term; or (b) failure to perform judicial duties; or (c) habitual intemperance."

Procedures: The implementing statute provides that the complaints received shall be investigated by the Commission as considered necessary or that the Commission may request the Supreme Court to appoint a panel of three persons to take evidence and report to the Commission. The Commission is charged with making recommendations for censure, suspension or removal to the Supreme Court. The 

Supreme Court may accept or reject the Commission recommendations and may censure, suspend or remove as it sees fit.

3. (15) Importance of Judicial Compensation. 

Salary is not the only factor that attracts the best lawyers to the judiciary. Factors such as an in- clination to the judicial temperament, the prestige of being a judge, and others, are equally important. 

There is, however, no substitute for a proper judiciary salary. All too frequently, here in Oregon, the first choices for appointment to vacancies in the judiciary decline on the grounds that they cannot afford to make the financial sacrifice involved in leaving the practice of law. Sitting judges are tempted to resign to return to the more lucrative private practice. All this works to the detriment of the administration of justice in Oregon. 

Retirement benefits also need the attention of the Oregon Legislative Assembly. Like salaries, retirement benefits are not as high or as beneficial as they should be. Since the judicial salary levels in

Oregon will not usually permit the development by the individual judge of appropriate personal investments against future contingencies, a sound and generous retirement plan is as important as proper salaries. 

4. (§ 16) Comment on Recommendations: The 

Council makes no dollar amount increase proposals for judicial salaries or retirement plans but urges improvement in both. The Council emphasizes the need to narrow the salary gap between district court judges and circuit judges. The need for a larger salary increase is more marked at the district court level where it is hardest to attract the best qualified younger men.

(C) Justice of the Peace Courts 1. (§ 31) Recommendation: The Council acclaims the gradual reduction of numbers of justices of the peace in Oregon and recommends that all such offices be abolished which have territory of their districts within 25 miles of the county courthouse in any county in which a district court is located. 

2. (§ 32) A Brief History of the Office of Justice of the Peace. When the office of justice of the peace was first created in Oregon there was a great need for local courts because of the severe travel difficulties encountered in that early era. Thus, from the very outset of the creation of the state, justices of the peace existed in every hamlet and village. This situation was changed in 1891 by creation of the system, still generally in effect, whereby the county judge, or the county commissioners, are empowered to establish justice of the peace offices in the county This power is directly limited by statutes which (1) prohibit the office of justice of the peace to be created in certain county seats and, (2) automatically eliminate certain existing offices of justice of the peace where a district court is created or where the number of judges of an existing district court is increased.

The number of justices of the peace is continuing to decline at a steady rate as shown in Figure 9. In 

1957, 92 justices of the peace were in office. The figure in 1966 has declined to 70.

Of the seventy in existence in 1966, 32, or almost half, hold office in eastern Oregon although the population of that district is far below half of the total in the state.

The explanation of this phenomenon is found in the traditions of the eastern part of the state. Because it is largely agricultural and sparsely populated, not many circuit and district judgeships exist. This scarcity of judges has enabled the old justice of the peace system to flourish with more vigor than in the more urbanized part of the state west of the Cascades. It is believed, however, that the justice of peace is no more necessary to the administration of justice in eastern Oregon than he is in western Oregon. The map in Figure 10 shows the present locations of justices of the peace in Oregon. 

3. (§ 33) Present Functions of Justices of the 

Peace. Justices of the peace have limited jurisdiction. In criminal affairs this includes traffic offenses and some other minor criminal offenses. Civil jurisdiction extends to most matters where the amount claimed does not exceed $250. The justice of the peace, like judges in Oregon, sets bail and conducts preliminary hearings in felony cases.

The first part of the function, consisting of jurisdiction over delineated criminal and civil cases, is largely judicial in character. In cases which reach trial, the justice of the peace should, but most often does not, have detailed knowledge of procedural rules, rules of evidence, the substantive law of the case and much other technical knowledge obtainable only through formal legal training. Yet only nine of the present 70 justices of the peace are lawyers. 

The position presently held by one of these nine lawyers is being abolished at the end of 1966. 

The other functions, setting and taking bail and conducting preliminary hearings, are largely ministerial in nature. In a sense much of the criminal matter heard by justices of the peace is ministerial in nature, because most cases are heard on a plea of guilty. In these cases the justice is required only to know how to enter a forfeiture of bail or how to accept the guilty plea and enter judgment. Any public officer of a county or locality, with a minimum of basic training, would be able to perform these ministerial duties in place of the justice of the peace. 

4. (§ 34) Basic Weaknesses of the Justice of the 

Peace System. Justices of the peace are occasionally called upon to hold civil and criminal trials. Yet, as has been mentioned, only nine of Oregon's 70 justices of the peace are lawyers. The procedural and substantive fields of the law are far too difficult to leave to someone without formal training. This is particularly true when an accused felon is taken before a magistrate to be advised of his constitutional rights. Yet it is unlikely that lawyers will ever be attracted in large numbers to serve as justices of the peace.

§ 35 trial held in justice of the peace court is subject to complete retrial (trial de novo) in a circuit court. This means that litigants, who thought they were getting some inexpensive justice, may be put to the unexpected expense of trying the suit twice. It is even sometimes difficult to obtain counsel for a trial in justice of the peace court. The work in properly preparing and trying the case is not much easier in a justice court than in a district or circuit court. As a result the attorney may have to charge a fee that might be too high to warrant his employment by a litigant with a very modest claim in justice court.

The salary paid justices of the peace, though low in general, is very uneconomical for the county which must foot the bill. The salary, no matter how small, is often far beyond the actual value of service rendered for the reason that many justice of the peace courts simply do not have much business. 

A hundred years ago each community needed a justice of the peace because it usually was not possible to travel conveniently to the presence of a judge. The circuit riding judge was not the entire answer either in the old days, because it was often weeks before he could get around to a particular locality. As a result a local judicial officer was necessary and the office of justice of the peace was the result. In modern times, however, most citizens have access to a circuit or district court in minutes over excellent roads and no longer need the services of a justice of the peace.

The Council is not unmindful of the need for an officer on the spot who is authorized to accept bail, take pleas of guilty, conduct preliminary hearings in felony cases and perform other ministerial tasks. 

It is the belief of the Council that these functions can be performed by a non-judicial officer. Overall, the Council feels that the justice of the peace system is no longer adequate. 

5. (835) Comment on Recommendation. The proposal by the Council to eliminate justice of the peace districts where they are within 25 miles of the county courthouse in a county in which a district court has been created will result in the abolition of approximately half of the existing offices. No undue hardship will result in requiring persons residing within 25 miles or thereabouts of the county seat to appear before the district judge. 

(D) Transfer of Juvenile and Probate Juris diction from County Courts 

1. (§36) Recommendation: The jurisdiction of county courts in juvenile and probate matters should be terminated and transferred as promptly as practicable to the appropriate district court or circuit court. State aid should be provided for service staffs to assist circuit courts in the solution and disposition of family-related matters. 

2. (§37) The Present Situation in Oregon. The county judge from the beginning primarily has been the chief administrative officer of the county and not a judicial officer. In the early days of the state's history, he was incidentally given jurisdiction in probate and some other limited civil matters. The need for local judges was the main reason for this investiture of judicial functions. Travel in the vast expanse of Oregon was very limited. Thus, the travel limitations imposed before the age of the automobile and good roads dictated the early structure of Oregon's courts.

In 1907, the county court almost everywhere was given jurisdiction in juvenile matters. The reason was basically the same-limited travel ability and the need for convenient courts.

Since 1907, however, there has been a steady decline in the number of county courts with juvenile jurisdiction. At present, as seen in Figure 11, only 

17 county courts still retain jurisdiction in juvenile matters.

In 1910 the Constitution of Oregon was amended removing the constitutional basis for county court probate jurisdiction. Accordingly, the number of county courts that have probate jurisdiction has slowly, but steadily, declined. 

Today, as seen in Figure 11, only 15 county courts retain jurisdiction over probate matters. 

3. (§ 38) Present Circumstances Warranting 

Transfer. The change in methods of transportation since the beginnings of the court system has resulted in entirely different circumstances with respect to the need for local courts. The ease and speed of travel today assures persons with need of court services that a court is nearby in travel time. As a result the justification based on local convenience disappears for the vesting of county judges with judicial functions.

In the early days of the state's existence there were only a few circuit court judges and none of the present day district court judges. This was another reason why county courts were given judicial powers. The availability today of numerous circuit and district court judges, who all must be lawyers, is further reason for withdrawing judicial functions from the county court.

A serious conflict of interest situation also exists with respect to county courts. The county judge in his capacity as county administrator has extensive, if not exclusive, control over county fiscal affairs. 

The county judge, when faced with the question of whether to place a juvenile offender on probation at county expense or whether to send him to the custody of a state institution at state expense often in the past has been prejudiced by his desire to keep county expenses down without reference to the needs of the individual in question.

With respect to the supervision and adjudication by the county judge of probate affairs, the criticism is all too true that non-lawyer county judges, by far in the majority, defer to the opinion of the attorneys practicing before them. As a result, lawyers frequently "run" the probate division of the county court, a situation which reflects unfavorably on both bench and bar.

The imposition of the additional duties in probate and juvenile jurisdiction can be shared by the district and circuit courts. It is believed that the added probate duties are not onerous and will of themselves cause no undue hardship in any circuit or district court. Where a district court exists in a county it is the recommendation of the Council that it be assigned the transferred duties. Otherwise, the circuit court will entertain all duties under the jurisdiction transferred from the county courts. 

4. (§ 39) Court Staff Services in Family Matters. 

The Oregon Council on Crime and Delinquency has recently concluded a survey on the treatment by the courts of family problems. The Oregon Judicial 

Council agrees that it is desirable to have juvenile matters come before circuit and district courts rather than county courts.

The Judicial Council concurs also with the conclusions of the Oregon Council on Crime and Delinquency with regard to court staff services to assist judges in handling family-related problems. To make such staffs effective it will be necessary to obtain greater numbers of trained personnel. This in turn will require the availability of larger financial resources. The Judicial Council, therefore, recommends that a system of state aid, both financial and professional, be instituted under which counties would receive a large portion of the cost of the court staff services to assist in disposition of the familyrelated matters coming before the trial courts of Oregon. 

(E) The District Courts 1. (§40) Recommendations: The Council makes the following recommendations with regard to the operation and jurisdiction of the district courts: (1) District courts should by statute be made courts of record with all proceedings in such courts recorded by electronic devices or otherwise as may be the most practical. Trials de novo (a retrying of the entire case as if it had not been tried before) on appeal from district court to circuit court should be eliminated. Instead the circuit court should review only the record of the case as tried in the district court; (2) The circuit courts should be given discretionary authority to transfer cases to the district courts when the case is filed in a circuit court and the amount claimed is $1,000 or less; (3) The present practice of temporarily assigning district court judges to serve in districts other than their own where workloads warrant should be expanded; (4) The present practice of assigning district court judges to serve as circuit court judges pro tempore where workloads warrant should be expanded; (5) The present practice of district court judges holding court in places within their districts in addition to the county seat should be expanded.

The judges of the district courts are all lawyers and the level of competence generally is high. Because of the capabilities of the district judges, litigants ought not to be forced to the expense of retrying the entire case when the decision of the district court is appealed to the circuit court. The purpose of review is served more efficiently and less expensively if the circuit court is required only to review the record made in the district court.

Since the district court presently has no system for transcribing what takes place at a trial or hearing, a system of record keeping must be instituted making the district court a court of record. There are a number of electronic recording devices presently available for court use. A more detailed discussion of records based on electronic devices is included elsewhere in this report. The Council recommends that the most promising of these devices, or any other suitable, economical recording system, be employed in the district courts so that a record will be available for appeal to the circuit court. 

A recent survey by the Council of the district, circuit and Supreme Court judges of Oregon indicates that judges decidedly favor converting district courts into courts of record. Fifty of the 71 judges replying to the question favored elimination of the trial de novo on appeal from the district court to the circuit court, and 47 of the 70 replying to the question favored making the district courts courts of record.

A by-product of the elimination of the trial de novo will be the saving of time in the circuit courts that now is consumed in re-trying the cases appealed from the district court. Consequently expense to litigants will be lowered and a saving will be realized in circuit court trial time which can be devoted to the hearing of the rising number of cases filed originally in circuit courts. 

3. (§ 42) Removal of Certain Cases to the District 

Court. The circuit and district courts have concurrent original jurisdiction in civil matters where the amount claimed does not exceed $2,500. To put more flexibility in this arrangement the Council recommends that the circuit court be empowered within its discretion to transfer to a district court any case filed originally in a circuit court where the amount claimed does not exceed $1,000.

The district courts were originally designed to handle the less serious civil and criminal cases, thus taking some of the burden from the circuit courts. 

It seems advisable to further this original design by allowing the circuit courts to free themselves of the smaller cases which otherwise might tend to clog circuit court dockets. However, the amount involved is not always indicative of the serious effects on other persons not before the court. Thus, it is deemed advisable to put the transfer authority on a discretionary basis so that the circuit courts may continue to hear cases involving major issues though the money amount involved be small. 

4. (§43) Assignment of District Judges and Places of Sitting. The Oregon court system on a lim ited basis presently employs district judges through assignment as judges in districts other than their own and as circuit judges pro tempore. Some district judges follow the practice of holding court in their districts in places other than the county seat. These

practices are favored by the Council which recommends that the practices be expanded. 

The general capability of the present district court judges is conducive to their assignment to try circuit court cases. In fact in 1965 nine district court judges tried 57 cases while sitting as circuit court judges pro tempore. Undoubtedly many more cases were assigned to these circuit judges pro tempore which were settled without trial. 

Use of district court judges in the circuit courts has at least three advantages. First, it helps to reduce case loads for the circuit judges. Second, it utilizes the spare time of district court judges in areas where their case loads may not be consuming all the judges' time at a given moment. 

Third, serving on a higher court in more difficult cases enables the district judge to expand his professional capacity and improve his judicial techniques. This makes him a better judge in his own court.

The practice of having district judges sit in districts other than their own permits a flexibility in the district court system beneficial to the administration of justice. Figures for 1965 show that 14 of the 

21 district courts had a loss of currency in civil cases (more cases filed than terminated). This loss of currency was not serious, however, in any of the district courts, but a system of intra district court assignment of district judges would tend to reverse this loss of currency trend.

The practice in district courts of having the judge sit in places in his district in addition to the county seat makes for convenience to litigants. The Council approves this practice and recommends that all district judges follow this procedure where feasible.

IV. Defense of Indigents A. (§44) Recommendations: The Council makes the following recommendations with respect to the defense of indigent accused persons: (1) The Council recommends a statute be enacted dealing with the appointment of counsel for defense of indigents: that the statute include probation revocation as a ground for appointment of counsel; that the appointment shall continue through all stages; that the court be authorized to substitute one appointed counsel for another and be authorized to terminate appointment; that the court be authorized to appoint counsel after proceedings are commenced if the defendant is found to be unable to pay retained counsel. (2) A statute should be enacted permitting a single county or a group of counties jointly to create the office of public defender for representing indigent accused persons in the trial courts of the county or counties.

B. (§45) The Situation in General. The subject of defense of indigents has come under close scrutiny by the U. S. Supreme Court. Although the case law affecting indigents' defense dates back more years, the case of Gideon v. Wainwright, decided in 1962, has given the subject its modern look. The process of defining what constitutes fair treatment in the defense of indigents is still in evolution, but some of the main principles have emerged and are reviewed here briefly. 

Prior to the 1962 decision in Gideon v. Wainwright, the U. S. Supreme Court had taken the view that a defendant was denied due process only if, under the circumstances of the case, failure to provide counsel at the trial denied him "fundamental fairness". The Gideon case enlarged the rights of a defendant at the trial stage to insure him right to have counsel at his trial. The famous case of Escobedo v. Illinois, decided in 1964, again enlarged the rights of the indigent, this time at the pretrial stage. 

Realizing that adequate representation at the trial is useless if conviction has been assured during the pretrial stage, the U. S. Supreme Court in the Escobedo decision held that "every" accused person is not only entitled to representation in a criminal case but that he is entitled to be advised of his right to remain silent or of his right to consult with an attorney before he is questioned by police about the commis- Ision of a crime. This holding has been further delineated in Miranda v. State of Arizona. 

C. (8 46) The Situation in Oregon. The Gideon vs. Wainwright case did not have the impact in

Oregon that it did in many other states. Oregon has for many years operated under statutes calling for the appointment of counsel in all felony cases where requested by an indigent. The Escobedo case seems to indicate that indigents accused of misdemeanors also are entitled to court-appointed counsel. Only in a very few counties in Oregon is this practice, an informal one without express statutory authority, generally followed in the trial courts. Thus, the 

Escobedo and Miranda decisions, and the decisions expected to flow from them in the near future with respect to misdemeanants, will probably have a major impact in Oregon not only as to the kind of case in which counsel must be appointed but also as to the time when counsel must be appointed. 

The practice in Oregon has been to appoint counsel when the accused is brought before a magistrate upon arrest on a charge of having committed a felony. Under the Escobedo and Miranda decisions this now will be too late, certainly in felony cases and probably in some kinds of misdemeanor cases, as well, as the doctrine becomes applicable to misdemeanor cases. However, the Council is continuing to study this complex situation and has no recommendations on the subject at this time. 

D. (§47) Who Is an Indigent. In order to be entitled to court-appointed, publicly-paid counsel the accused must be indigent. What constitutes indigency is not defined in the Oregon statutes, so that courts must determine indigency on their own motion. The procedures leave much to be desired. 

The question of what constitutes indigency is a major problem since it has been estimated, based on surveys of several counties, that between sixty and seventy-five percent of all accused persons in Oregon are indigent. It is the recommendation of the Council, therefore, that statutory directives be established to aid courts uniformly to determine the question of indigency. The problem remains one for further study, however, and the Council will make additional recommendations at a later time.

E. (§ 48) Payment of Court-Appointed Counsel. 

The fees paid to court-appointed counsel for representing indigents come from the county treasuries. 

The rates established by law are as follows: $25 for misdemeanor cases if the defendant pleads guilty, and if he pleads not guilty, $50 per each day of trial with a limit of two days payment; $35 for felony cases if the defendant pleads guilty, and, if he pleads not guilty, $75 per day of trial, limited to three days. The amounts presently paid are being studied by the Council and a future recommendation will be made on the subject.

In addition to counsel fees the law also directs that the appointed attorney shall be reimbursed for expenses "or such portion thereof as may be approved by the court." It is in the area of the payment

§ 31 of expenses that the most serious difficulty is encountered. The result of one survey of Oregon judges indicates that the defense counsel cannot count on adequate expense reimbursement. As a result, lawyers appointed to represent indigents understandably are reluctant to advance expense money to pay expert witnesses and to cover vital investigations. 

On the other hand, the prosecution usually has adequate resources in preparing its case. 

F. (849) The Cost of Defense of Indigents. The 

Council presently has underway a survey of the costs in other states of plans for the defense of indigents accused of misdemeanors. This latter study is being made in view of the recent U.S. Supreme Court decisions which indicate that at least some kinds of misdemeanants who are indigent are going to be entitled to counsel. When these studies are complete the 

Council will have specific recommendations to make.

G. (§ 50) The County Public Defender Plan. The 

Oregon State Bar is proposing a plan for establishment of a county public defender program in which the Council concurs in principle. The State Bar plan for establishment of the office is entirely permissive, so that a county wishing to retain the appointed counsel system may do so.

The economic feasibility of a public defender system in a particular county seems to depend largely on the size of the county. In three states, Minnesota, California, and Connecticut, studies indicate that in the larger counties where public defender systems exist, the cost per indigent case is less than the cost per case in counties where counsel is appointed on a case-by-case basis. Multnomah county would probably be of sufficient size to realize savings by instituting the county public defender plan. Lane and Marion counties might also have a large enough incidence of indigent cases to warrant the plan. 

The smaller Oregon counties would probably not find it feasible to adopt the plan individually. Therefore, it is the opinion of the Council that the statute authorizing the plan should allow more than one county jointly to create the office of public defender to serve all the contracting counties. The cost of the plan thus prorated would probably encourage many smaller counties to establish the office.

The Council concludes that a county public defender system, where instituted by action of the counties, will improve the system of defense of indigents perhaps without increasing the burden on the taxpayer and will insure fairer trials for indigents.

V. Use of Electronic Recording Devices A. (§51) Recommendation: The Council recom mends enactment of legislation authorizing use of electronic or mechanical devices for reporting court proceedings within the discretion of the trial judge and further recommends that the Supreme Court be authorized to establish standards for the making and preservation of electronic records.

B. (§ 52) Use of Electronic Recording Devices 

Elsewhere. The use of electronic devices for recording trial and other court proceedings is not widespread, but there is a growing interest in them. 

Alaska is the most notable example of a jurisdiction making use of electronic equipment. It is used exclusively in all courts in that state. Tennessee, Indiana and Virginia have authorized use of electronic devices and the use in the latter state is now widespread in the more serious criminal offense proceedings. Hawaii is also expanding the use of electronic recording in its courts.

C. (§ 53) The Alaska Experience. The court sys tem of the new state government of Alaska began functioning in 1960. Since there were not enough shorthand court reporters in the state to man the court system, the courts were equipped with electronic recorders operated by persons trained specially for this work. 

Careful preparations were made for the opening of the new court system. Recording units were installed in all courtrooms, repair technicians were trained, detailed instructions for operation of equipment and logging techniques were distributed, and rules were adopted making trial judges responsible for the adequacy of the record.

The use of the electronic devices in Alaska has apparently been successful. In a manual published by the State of Alaska explaining the techniques to be employed in connection with the system, a number of advantages in the electronic system found to exist are pointed out. Among them are the following: (1) the consequences of a continuing, acute shortage of available personnel trained in court reporting are avoided; (2) counsel and court have opportunity to satisfy themselves as to what transpired at a hearing without the limitation inherent in manual reporting. In other words, proceedings can be replayed just as they sounded. Enunciation, tone and pitch of voice, manner and speed of response and other peculiarities of oral testimony which are apt to leave their mark on the jurors' minds are preserved permanently; (3) effective control by the courts over time and cost elements involved in preparing transcripts for records on appeal is achieved. 

The centralization of recording and transcription functions under a single authority enables such control which is not possible with court reporters. 

D. (854) The Multnomah County Experimental 

Study. About two years ago the Oregon State Bar

Committee on Court Reporters began planning a controlled experiment to test the efficiency of electronic equipment for use in making records of trials against the shorthand court reporter system. The 

Council joined the Bar, the Association of Oregon Counties and Multnomah County as a sponsoring agency for this study which was conducted in the courtroom of Charles S. Crookham, circuit judge of 

Multnomah County.

The judge arranged for the trial and hearing of 11 cases and matters during a two week period in May, 1966. Ampex recording equipment was installed in the courtroom. Multnomah County employed personnel for auditing of the transcripts. 

Careful comparisons were made between the projected cost of the equipment and operation of an electronically based system and the cost of the operation of the present shorthand court reporter system. 

Also compared were the relative speed and accuracy of the two methods and the effect each system had on the preparation of transcripts for appeal.

Detailed conclusions and observations of the Oregon State Bar Committee, based on the study, indicate that the electronic recording method has great promise for alleviating what is anticipated to be an ever increasing shortage of court reporters. The Bar 

Committee concluded in general that the electronic method should be authorized by statute on a discretionary basis. In this the Council concurs. 

E. ($55) Reasons for Recommendation. Oregon, like many other states, is experiencing a growing shortage of competent manual court reporters. In addition, the Council has recommended that district courts be made courts of record and that the proceedings in these courts be recorded so that the trial de novo on appeal from the district court to the circuit court can be eliminated. It is doubtful that the supply of qualified court reporters will increase sufficiently to meet the anticipated increase in normal demands. If the Legislature agrees with the Council recommendation to make district courts courts of record, the needs, created for transcripts of proceedings in the district courts, will become critical. 

Therefore, the Council recommends that the use of electronic devices, the feasibility of which has been demonstrated in the recent experiments conducted in Multnomah County, be made discretionary with the trial judge so that the devices may be employed not only in the district courts, if no reporter is available, but also in the circuit courts should the need arise.

VI. Bail 

(A) Pretrial Release Methods 1. (§ 56) Recommendations: The Council recom mends that the statute, enacted in 1965, providing for release of accused persons on their own personal recognizance be given wide publicity by the Oregon State Bar so that attorneys and judges alike will be afforded a new awareness of it and the advantages available through its use. The Council further recommends that experimental programs manned by volunteers be instituted where possible in the state for the purpose of screening the backgrounds of persons applying for release on personal recognizance with a view toward assisting the courts in granting such requests as soon as is possible to those qualified. 

2. (§ 57) Basic Concepts. The simple and funda mental function of bail is one of the basic concepts of law which is most often obscured by emotion, both on the part of the public and the bench. Basically bail is a device to allow persons accused of crimes to be free prior to trial to help prepare their defense. Inherent in this concept is that the bail be no more in amount than that which will insure the appearance of the accused at time of trial. All too often in practice, although it is not to be assumed as true in Oregon, bail is used as a tool of punishment of the accused. The public loudly demands security and retribution against accused offenders and out of this demand arises the attitude of many courts that certain kinds of accused persons should be kept in jail by setting high bail which the court is sure an accused cannot raise. The idea, among others, is to keep the accused from committing any more crimes prior to trial. 

Abuses in fixing bail give rise to possible violations of three basic constitutional rights of accused persons. The U. S. and Oregon Constitutions prohibit bail in excessive amounts. Thus, a court setting bail at a high figure for the unavowed but transparent purpose of keeping an accused in jail is vulnerable on the excessive bail concept. 

A second constitutional guarantee affecting bail is the equal protection clause of the U. S. Constitution. All persons in the same group-in this case persons accused of a crime-must be treated in the same general manner with respect to bail. To make pretrial freedom turn on the accused's financial ability is a substantial denial of equal protection of the laws.

Yet a third constitutional concept affecting bail is the due process clause of the U. S. Constitution. 

Surveys of bail systems in various parts of the country indicate that the incidence of conviction at trial is noticeably higher for accused persons who have failed to make bail and have remained in jail from the time of arrest to the time of trial. If this is true, and there are a few indications that it is true in Oregon as will be discussed later in this portion of the report, it is a substantial denial of due process of law to impose this kind of bail-connected handicap on accused persons. (§ 58) Abuses in the Bail System. Judges tend to follow set patterns for fixing bail rather than viewing each applicant on his individual merit. The result is that bail is often imposed in automatic amounts even where the indications are excellent that the accused would certainly appear for trial on a much reduced amount of bail. In many cases the automatically imposed bail is too high for the accused to meet, or, if he meets it through employment of a bail bondsman, the accused suffers the economic punishment of having to pay ten percent of the face amount of the bail as a fee to obtain the bondsman's services. 

Studies made outside of Oregon indicate that courts sometimes use bail to prod an accused person to pursue a course of action in his case against his own interest. For instance a judge might threaten an increase in amount of bail if the accused insists on asking for a justified continuance of his case. 

Although there is little known difficulty at the present time in Oregon, the bail bond business elsewhere is continually plagued by charges of corruption and collusion between bondsmen and police officers. It is a practice in some places, though apparently not in Oregon, for some bondsmen to get the inside track for writing bonds in an area by paying the right police officer a commission on each bond written by the bondsman. In another abuse, some bondsmen insist that before they put up bond the accused must employ a specified attorney. The attorney may "kick back" part of his fee to the bondsman. What has happened elsewhere could easily happen in Oregon.

Another objection to the bail bond system is its cost to the accused person. The criminal in the organized crime syndicate's scope of operation usually gets bail money quickly from his masters in the organization. But the indigent, or near-indigent accused person, not a part of an organized crime operation, must stay in jail or must find in his restricted means the ten percent fee to pay a bondsman. This fee is often taken from the marginal budget on which the accused is trying to raise a family and quite often works a crushing hardship on his family.

Another deleterious effect resulting from unrealistic bail setting is the imprisonment of persons who eventually will be released after trial. The place

§ 59 of this detention is invariably the common jail. The net result is an unforgettable and degrading experience for too many people. 

By abusing the basic concepts of bail and setting bail too high in general, courts unwittingly contribute to a large public expense. High, unrealistic bail results directly in overcrowding of the jails.

In Chicago where a new, liberal release system has been in effect since 1964, a dramatic drop in the city prison inmate population has occurred, traceable to the new system. A sizeable saving in tax dollars has been realized as a result.

Of vital importance to the preparation of an accused's defense is his freedom before the trial to aid in the preparation of his defense. But if the accused is detained for failure to post an unreasonably high bail, he cannot contribute money or labor to his defense and, since he is deprived of gainful employment, frequently cannot afford to employ an attorney of his own choice. He is unable to help obtain witnesses or evidence in his defense, and the pretrial detention often adversely affects his demeanor and attitude in the courtroom or on the witness stand. In conclusion it can be fairly stated that available data indicates that freed defendants enjoy a considerable advantage over detained defendants at the time of the trial. 

4. (59) Solutions to the Bail Problem Outside 

Oregon. At least four promising release procedures exist in addition to the traditional bail system for freeing accused persons prior to trial. One is the personal recognizance method. Pursuant to this method (which also exists in Oregon) the accused is allowed to go free on his own word that he will be present at the appointed time for his trial. 

To work properly, however, release on personal recognizance presupposes that the court has available or determines a complete set of facts regarding each recognizance applicant. In various places this function is performed by probation officers, prosecuting attorneys, public defenders, law student volunteers, or court staff investigators. In many jurisdictions the statutes provide for release on personal bond but the courts, for lack of fact-finding apparatus, are reluctant to use this system. Where in use, however, such as in New York City, the figures show a lower percentage of failures to appear for trial under the personal recognizance release system than under the regular bail bond release system. 

A second method of release in operation in Illinois as a variation of the bail bond release procedure is the system in which the accused deposits cash or securities totaling ten percent of the face amount of the bail. After his trial is over, the accused gets back all but ten percent of his original deposit. For example, if bail is set at $1,000, the accused would be permitted to avoid detention by depositing $100 in cash or securities with the court. 

After completion of the trial, the court would refund $90 to the accused. As a result the accused who appears for trial is better off by $90 than he would have been had he paid a ten percent, or $100 fee to a bail bondsman. This cash bail system was originated in Illinois where it has operated with notable success since 1963. A study in Illinois after one year of operation of the new system shows that the defaulting percentage is lower than the percentage of defaults on regular bail bonds.

A third way of avoiding detention before trial is the system known as summons in lieu of arrest, and a variation, summons after arrest. Pursuant to the summons in lieu of arrest, the accused, instead of having his case start with arrest, receives a summons to appear and defend against a specific charge at a given time. In many of the lesser offenses where sight arrests are not involved there is nothing to be gained by an arrest. Experience elsewhere with this system of summons in lieu of arrest has been encouraging. Cooperation by accused persons has been excellent. If the summons fails to bring the accused into court, it becomes appropriate at that point to institute the regular arrest procedure. In case the accused is arrested, it is possible at the time he is brought into the police station or before a magistrate to release him with a summons to appear for trial at a certain time and place. No bail bond or personal recognizance is involved. 

A fourth way to avoid the posting of bail bonds is to release the accused to the custody of a third person who is made responsible for the appearance of the accused at trial. The third party might be the accused's attorney (as is the current practice in 

Tulsa, Oklahoma), his employer, school or labor union. In Tulsa the attorney to be eligible for the program must certify that to his personal knowledge the client who is to be released is not a previously convicted felon or has not, within the previous six months, been convicted of a crime of moral turpitude. If the attorney fails to produce his client in court, the attorney is penalized by having his name removed from the court's approved participating lawyer list.

The federal system has very recently undergone a re-examination of its pretrial release procedures. 

Pursuant to a statute recently enacted as a result of this study, persons accused of federal crimes may be released (1) on their own recognizance, (2) to the custody of a third person without bail, or (3) on a cash bail deposit with a refund after trial (the Illinois system). The federal system in effect combines most of the new methods and avoids the disadvantages of the old bail bond system.

One of the best results produced by the newer

§ 35 methods of pretrial release is the elimination of the need for bail bondsmen. It cannot be denied that many bail bondsmen in the past have performed a necessary and beneficial function in an honest fashion. However, the new ways for pretrial release seem to be working well and inexpensively for defendants and obviate further need for bail bondsmen.

In studying the pretrial release situation in Oregon, the major concerns of the Council have been (1) whether defendants are spending too much time in jail prior to trial because of their inability to meet bail requirements, and (2) the effect of incarceration on the defendant's fate in the proceedings.

Criminal defendants are presumed to be innocent until proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.

The bail survey has indicated: 

1. The granting of release on personal recognizance should be expanded.

The period of the Council's investigation occurred prior to the effectiveness in August, 1965 of the new Oregon statute on recognizance. This statute, however, did not significantly alter the traditional discretionary power of the court in the matter of release upon recognizance, except as to require a statement and assurance of appearance by the defendant, waiver of extradition and the imposition of a penalty for non-appearance. Authorities have said that the 1965 statute has had no appreciable impact on the incidence of release on recognizance. 

Nor is it believed that, in the absence of the use of personnel required for proper investigation of applications for release on recognizance, the new law will increase significantly, if at all, the granting of release on personal recognizance.

The survey reflects an unexpectedly high occurrence of the setting of no bail. This is believed to be largely attributable to such factors as quick guilty pleas at or near the time of arraignment, holding of persons for extradition purposes, the defendant having been held for another charge and under other bail, the absence of Oregon statutory authority for the setting of felony bail for persons detained in counties other than where the charge is filed, and some instances of insanity commitment.

The granting of release on personal recognizance has been practiced in virtually all courts under study. The incidence of this type of release has, however, been too low in the City Court of Portland, only granted in 10 of 453 cases (2 percent). In circuit courts, recognizance was granted in only seven percent of cases and in district courts in only five percent of the cases. 

2. The inability of accused persons to meet bail requirements indicates that the amounts of bail imposed are often too high.

The amount of bail imposed by charge appears to vary considerably between courts and within individual courts. Recommendations of district attorneys as to bail amounts are normally based upon the nature of the offense, the criminal history of the defendant, when known, and other factors affecting the probability of his appearance. Judges often tend to follow these recommendations, but often entertain motions for reduction in bail, which are but rarely opposed by prosecutors' offices. 

The ability of persons to meet bail seems to depend largely on the dollar amount of the bond or cash required. In certain low categories a very high percentage of alleged offenders post bail, while as to more serious offenses the requirement seems impossible for a higher percentage of prisoners. 

It was surprising to find that, with the exception of the Marion and Multnomah county circuit courts (5 and 8 cases, respectively), all requests in all courts for reduced bail were granted. The ability to post reduced bail was high in most courts. The Council encourages a review of present practices for the setting of original bail, and suggests that bail may be set more realistically with the guidance of the personnel proposed to be employed by the courts for "recog" investigations. 

The percentage of defendants able to post bail in the "$300 and less" category is nearly 50 percent in Portland city court, varies from 25 to 90 percent in district courts and varies from 25 to 100 percent in circuit courts. Strangely, a larger percentage of persons are able to post bail between $300 and $1000 in Portland city court than are able to meet bail requirements below $300. And in district and circuit courts a substantial percentage of persons are similarly able to post bail of $1000 or less. Bail seldom exceeds $2000 in the courts studied, but bail of over $2000 is apparently more difficult to meet than in lesser amounts. 

3. The time spent in jail between arrest and final disposition of the case should be reduced.

The argument is occasionally heard that some criminal defendants should be held in jail prior to trial to prevent the commission of another crime. Unfortunately, no acceptable method existed during this survey for discovering whether accused persons released on bail committed other crimes while out on bail. It is submitted, however, that this is not a proper ground either for the existence of the bail system or for the requirement of excessive bail. 

In the Portland city court, 405 of 455 defendants spent less than 10 days in jail (88 percent). This figure is 80 percent in district courts and 75 percent in circuit courts, the latter court alone, of course, reflecting incarceration of felons. 

In district courts, 31 persons later found not guilty served time in jail. Only four such cases occurred in Portland city court and 33 cases were found in circuit courts.

A very few cases of prolonged incarceration prior to trial are reflected in the statistics. To remove the

§ 37 2 possibility of error, these cases will be verified before a final analysis of the bail survey is prepared. 

4. The effect of jail time on success upon trial is a difficult subject for objective assessment. 

If a man is to properly assist in his own defense, by employing counsel, searching for witnesses, etc., and at the same time improve his chances on trial by appearing in court in neat attire, properly groomed and with some degree of morale and composure, he should be on release prior to the trial date, in the absence of very persuasive evidence that he will not produce himself when required by the court. The effect of continuing in his employment and preserving the integrity of his home during this period are other factors of a sociological nature militating in favor of the pretrial release of accused persons who are reasonably responsible members of the community.

In all courts, 79 percent of those making bond were convicted; 86 percent of those never released were convicted on trial. Many cases, of course, ended in pleas of guilty.

There appears to be some inverse relationship between length of detention and success on trial. Other factors contributing to the ability of accused persons to secure bail are, however, involved in this situation.

Habitual criminals tend to have lesser ties to the community, by way of intact families, financial assets, steady employment and regular residence, and for these reasons are less able to meet requirements exacted by bondsmen. Thus, this type of accused person probably spends more time in detention and probably enjoys less success on trial, due to the prejudice of juries against him for his prior convictions when these are brought out on trial. His sentences also are larger for the same reason. 

It is notable that indigent defendants in district and city courts are not entitled to assigned counsel. 

Obviously, they are not able to present their own cases for trial and pleas for reduced bail or probationary sentences as well as defendants in felony cases, who are almost always represented in court. 

The result is that the misdemeanor defendants in district court probably spend more jail time, fare less well on trial and suffer greater sentences than would occur with an appointive counsel program for misdemeanors. 

6. (61) Comment on Recommendations. The work of the Council on the subject of pretrial release in Oregon is not yet complete. The preliminary observations set out above, based on surveys in the courts mentioned, will be re-examined and expanded. In addition to the present recommendations for greater use of the personal recognizance statute, the Council anticipates that it will, during the next biennium, complete its study of bail in Oregon and make further specific suggestions for improvement. (B) Bail for Certain Persons Accused of Felonies 

1. (§ 62) Recommendation: The Council recom mends that the statute be amended to provide that a defendant arrested in a county other than where the charge was brought shall be admitted to bail by a magistrate of the county where arrested in the same manner as if the defendant was brought before a magistrate in the county where the charge was brought. 

2. (§ 63) Reasons for Recommendation. The Ore gon statute now provides that a misdemeanant is entitled to have a hearing on bail before any magistrate no matter where the misdemeanant is arrested. 

No such recourse is available to the person accused of a felony, with the result that no reduction in bail requirement may be granted by a magistrate in a jurisdiction other than that in which the defendant is charged, nor can bail be set by a magistrate outside the district where the charge is filed, where none has been set in the district where charged. 

Consequently, persons accused of felonies who are arrested and detained in distant jurisdictions in the state may suffer detention for a considerable period, either because no bail has been set by the court of origin, or because the judge of the district where arrest occurs has no power to establish bail or to entertain a reduction of the bail required.

Under present statutory law, the result is that a person accused of a felony arrested in the district in which the charge arises is in a much more favorable position than the person accused of a felony arrested outside the district in which the charge is made. The former is eligible for bail as of right but the latter has no right to bail until he is returned to the district in which charged. This could very well be held to constitute a violation of the equal protection of the laws doctrine and the due process of law guarantee.

CHAPTER VII Subjects for Continuing and Future Study by the Council 

The 1965 statute which created the Judicial Council gave it broad authority to study, review, and make recommendations on the state's court system.

It was obvious to the Council that it would be impossible to study the entire subject of judicial administration in Oregon during the current biennium. 

Several subjects were picked for initial study; others were scheduled for later investigation.

At this time, the Council has two categories of work before it. The first is composed of subjects on which study has not yet been completed, and on which further work is needed before the Council will be ready to make definite recommendations.

The second consists of items which require initial study. 

Included in the first group are: 

1. The need to determine if improvements can be made in the system for the selection, retention, and tenure of the state's judges, at all court levels. 

2. Completion of the study on bail and pretrial release programs. 

3. Basic recommendations on the Oregon system for defense of indigents accused of all classes of crime, whether felonies or misdemeanors. The Council also intends to examine the need for legal aid for the indigent in Oregon, to determine if the needs are being met adequately or if improvements are required. 

4. One of the major problems in appealing a case to a higher court is the preparation of the transcript of the original trial. The Council wishes to examine methods of expediting transcripts. Included in this examination will be further study of electronic court reporting. 

5. The Council is commanded by statute to conduct public hearings on the administration of justice in Oregon. Means will be sought to make these hearings more meaningful and to expand their usefulness to the citizenry, to the court system, and to the Council.

Council action has not been taken on several subjects which are docketed for immediate attention. 

These include: 

1. A study of the workload of the Oregon Supreme Court. The number of cases appealed to the 

Court increased by nearly 50 per cent from 1960 to 1965. The workload on individual members of the 

Court has increased substantially. There are a number of possible solutions to the problems created by the courts increasing case load. The Council will examine them and make recommendations to the 

Court and the next Legislature. 

2. Improving administrative procedures in the court system. The Council feels it needs to study the procedures used in all levels of other court systems, to see what improvement can be made in Oregon.

Some procedure also must be devised to establish clear responsibility for initiating changes in court rules, and for making them more uniform throughout the state. 

3. The system for selecting the presiding judge in multi-judge districts is not uniform in Oregon.

The Council wishes to examine various methods used in Oregon and other states; it expects to have a recommendation on the subject for the next Legislature. 

4. Operating procedures at the trial court level vary considerably in Oregon. In the interest of litigants, the Council feels more uniformity is desirable and that some procedures might be improved. It will make recommendations on the subject to the courts during the next year. 

5. The Council feels more effective utilization can be made of our District Courts. It is considering proposals to widen boundaries past county lines in some instances, to make more use of District Courts outside the county seats, and to make greater use of 

District Judges as Circuit Judges pro tem. 

6. The Council has gone on record as favoring the abolition of Justice Courts. The Legislature has favored moves in this direction in past years. In some areas of the state, distance will require the part-time services of some judicial officer, to serve in and as a part of the state's court system. The 

Council expects to complete a study of systems used in other states and by the Federal courts and prepare a recommendation on this subject for the next Legislature's consideration. 

7. All past Oregon studies of judicial administration have recommended the transfer of judicial functions from the County Court to Circuit or District Court. The Council expects to recommend a program for the balance of the State to the next Legislature. 

8. The Council will study the possible integration of the functions of Municipal Courts into the state's court system. 

9. Some students have suggested a broader use of referees and arbitration to reduce expense to litigants and relieve the courts of some of their work. 

The Council expects to study these suggestions, to see which might be applicable in Oregon. 

10. Suggestions have been made for some possible reforms in criminal procedures, and for changes and improvements in the jury system and grand jury system. The Council will start its studies on these matters in 1966.

(1) The manner of providing audio recording devices and other equipment and supplies required to report trial court proceedings as authorized by paragraph (c) of subsection (3) of ORS 8.340 and equipment and supplies necessary for a monitor-reporter to prepare transcripts of such reports shall be the manner provided for furnishing other supplies and equipment to the court. (2) The clerk of the court shall furnish clerical assistance to the monitor-reporter, when so directed by the judge of the court for which the reporter is appointed, when such assistance is required in the preparation of transcripts in accordance with ORS 8.350. (3) When a transcript ordered for the benefit of a party to the proceedings is prepared and certified by or under the direction of a monitorreporter, the transcript fees provided by ORS 21.470 shall be paid to the treasury of the county to be used for general county purposes. 

Section 7. ORS 1.006 is amended to read: 

1.006. To facilitate his exercise of administrative authority and super vision under ORS 1.002, the Chief Justice shall: 

(1) Conduct studies, make recommendations and issue orders and directions concerning the administrative methods and activities, dockets, business and facilities of the courts of the state, other than the Supreme Court and municipal courts, and of the judges, clerks and other personnel of such courts. (2) Conduct studies, make recommendations and issue orders and directions concerning minimum standards for the use and operation of audio recording devices to report trial court proceedings as provided by

ORS 8.340 and other statutes, for the transcribing of tapes or records made by such devices, and for the custody and preservation of such tapes or records. [(2)] (3) Require necessary or appropriate reports from the judges, clerks and other personnel of such courts. [(3)] (4) Collect, compile and publish statistical and other data relating to such courts and make reports on the business and condition of such courts. [(4)] (5) Take such other action as may be necessary or appropriate in the furtherance of the administration of justice. 

Section 8. ORS 8.360 is amended to read: 

8.360. (1) The report of the official reporter, when transcribed and certified to as being a correct transcript of the notes, tapes or audio records of the testimony, exceptions taken, charge of the judge, and other proceedings in the matter, shall be prima facie a correct statement thereof, and may thereafter be read in evidence as the deposition of a witness in the cases mentioned in ORS [45.170] 45.270. (2) When the official reporter in any cause has ceased to be the official reporter of that court, any transcript by him made therefrom, or made by a competent person under direction of the court, and duly certified to by him, under oath, as a full, true and complete transcript of said notes, tapes or audio records, shall have the same force and effect as though certified in the same manner by the official reporter.
