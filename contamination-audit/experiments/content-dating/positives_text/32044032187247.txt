PREFACE When the 1961 edition of the Circuit Court Directory and 

Manual was published, the court was at the very beginning of its existence and the public and bar knew little about the court. That edition was, accordingly, designed to provide the public and the bar with a handy compendium of information about the structure and functions of the court. 

That there was need for such a Directory and Manual is demonstrated by the fact that approximately 9,000 copies of the first edition were distributed to members of the bar and interested persons and agencies. The second edition, published in 1968, met with the same enthusiastic response and approximately 10,000 copies of that edition were distributed.

The Executive Committee has decided to print this third edition, which revises the second edition and provides current information about the court.

HISTORY AND ORGANIZATION OF THE CIRCUIT COURT 

5 

On January 1, 1961, the Circuit Court of Connecticut came into being pursuant to legislation entitled "An Act Concern ing the Creation of a Circuit Court to Replace the Trial 

Justice and Municipal Courts." The court is a single state court with statewide jurisdiction; the business of the court, however, is transacted on a circuit basis, the state having been divided into eighteen circuits, each circuit consisting of one or more towns and complete with court facilities, clerk's office and necessary personnel. The court is composed of forty-four judges appointed for four-year terms, each of whom must be a member of the bar of the state and devote full time to the duties of his office. Not more than one-half of the number of the judges first appointed to the court could be of the same political party. Bipartisan division has continued as a tradition, so that each new appointee has been a member of the same political party as his predecessor. The court is a court of record and has a seal.' It has limited civil and criminal jurisdiction. There is a right to a trial by jury in appropriate cases. On the trial of any civil action to a jury, the trial shall be by a jury of six.10 In any criminal trial in the Circuit Court, such trial shall be by a jury of six.¹¹ 

Provision is made for small claims sessions of the court and for the maintenance of domestic relations bureaus.12 An appellate session of the Circuit Court existed¹³ until September 1, 1971, but appeals taken thereafter shall be heard by the

JURISDICTION 1. Civil Jurisdiction. The Circuit Court has "jurisdiction of all civil matters for legal or equitable relief, or both, including actions of summary process and paternity proceedings, but not including those actions which by statute are triable only by the Superior Court or the Court of Common Pleas or by a judge of one of said courts, wherein the amount, legal interest or property in demand does not exceed in value seven thousand five hundred dollars, not including interest, costs and attorney's fees."16

The Circuit Court has exclusive jurisdiction of actions of summary process irrespective of the value of the premises involved." It also has jurisdiction of ejectment actions irrespective of the value of the premises provided the amount of any money damages claimed is within the jurisdictional limit of the Circuit Court.18

Exclusive jurisdiction of paternity actions is vested in the 

Circuit Court, irrespective of the amount involved.¹º

Within its jurisdictional limit the Circuit Court has jurisdiction of foreclosure actions, the amount of the matter in demand being the amount of the debt secured by the mortgage or lien as described in the mortgage or certificate of lien.20

3. Criminal Jurisdiction. The Circuit Court has criminal jurisdiction of all crimes and all violations of ordinances, regulations and bylaws of any town, city, borough, district or other municipal corporation or authority which are punishable by a fine of not more than five thousand dollars or imprisonment for not more than five years or both, except that in prosecutions under § 14-267 of the General Statutes (operating overweight commercial vehicles) the court may impose penalties as provided in that section in excess of its maximum jurisdictional limit.29

Any accused when arraigned before the Circuit Court, shall be advised of his rights and allowed a reasonable opportunity to consult counsel. The court shall continue, modify or set conditions of release, in bailable offenses, unless it finds custody to be necessary to provide reasonable assurance of the appearance of the accused in court, upon the first of the following conditions of release found sufficient to provide such assurance: (1) Upon his execution of a written promise to appear, (2) Upon his execution of a bond without surety in no greater amount than necessary, (3) Upon his execution of a bond with surety in no greater amount than necessary, 27 Conn. VENUE 

Criminal actions shall be brought to the court in the circuit where the crime was alleged to have been committed. 

Civil actions generally, except as otherwise prescribed by statute, shall be brought to the court in the circuit wherein lies the town where the plaintiff or the defendant dwells; if neither is an inhabitant of the state the action shall be brought

to the circuit where the defendant is at the commencement of the action or if the defendant is not within the state, where the estate attached is situated." Where all plaintiffs and defendants are nonresidents, venue shall be in the circuit where the injury occurred or the subject matter of the litigation is located or where the defendant is served.50 Actions by a foreign corporation shall be returned to the court in the circuit where the defendant or one of the defendants resides; any corporation incorporated under the laws of the United States and located in any town in the state may sue before the same courts in which any inhabitant of such town may sue. 51 Civil actions wherein title to land is to be tried and determined and civil actions for trespass to land shall be brought and tried in the circuit where the land lies,52 and if the land consists of an entire tract or farm situated in two or more circuits the action may be brought in either circuit.53 

No action shall fail because of improper venue but shall be transferred to the appropriate circuit except that such action may be heard in the circuit where brought on agreement of the parties and on the consent of the judge holding court in such circuit.54

Any action may be transferred from one circuit to another circuit by order of the court on its own motion or on motion of any of the parties or by agreement of the parties.55

JURY SESSIONS 

In criminal cases there is no right to a jury trial where the maximum penalty for the crime charged is a fine of fifty dollars or a jail sentence of thirty days, or both.63

In civil actions there is no right to a jury trial where the amount, legal interest or property in demand does not exceed two hundred and fifty dollars, or in summary process cases in which money damages are not claimed.** 64 In all other criminal and civil cases any party may demand a trial by jury of issues which are triable of right by a jury. 

In civil actions a jury is waived unless requested by a party; in criminal proceedings the judge advises the accused of his right to a jury trial at the time of plea and if the accused does not then request a jury trial it is waived.65 In any criminal trial by a jury in the Circuit Court, such trial shall be by a jury of six.66

The chief judge makes the orders and the assignments for the maintenance of jury sessions as are necessary for the adjudication of cases claimed for jury trial and such sessions at such central locations as the facilitation of court business, the convenience of parties and counsel and the efficient use of judicial personnel requires. He shall establish continuous civil jury sessions at specific locations throughout the state to serve, at such locations, one or more circuits or portions thereof. No juror shall be impaneled in a new case during any session of court after he has been in attendance four court weeks.67

APPEALS 

1. Appeals. Appeals from any final judgment or action of the Circuit Court, except small claims matters which are not appealable, appeals under sections 52-542 and 53-36b of the General Statutes and where otherwise specified by general statutes, shall be taken to an Appellate Session of the Court of Common Pleas within fourteen days after the entry of judgment or of the findings of fact and conclusions of law, whichever is later, and shall be by way of review of errors of law." 

The practice, procedure and rules on appeals from the Circuit Court to the Appellate Session of the Court of Common Pleas are set forth in the Practice Book.72 They conform, as nearly as may be, to the rules of practice governing appeals to the Supreme Court.73

SMALL CLAIMS 

Provision is made for the prompt hearing and determination of claims for money damages not in excess of seven hundred and fifty dollars including attorneys' fees and other costs, by a simple, inexpensive and informal procedure, the services of an attorney are permissible but not obligatory.** 

Low cost, no lawyers in a vast majority of the cases, no formal pleadings, informal hearings, no legal rules of evidence, no juries, no formal findings and the determination of the issues by the judge with common sense but in accordance with the rules of law are the basic characteristics of small claims procedure. A person having a small claim is not required to use the small claims procedure but may at his option pursue his claim as in a regular civil action. Such small claims procedure shall be applicable to all actions, except actions of libel and slander, claiming money damages not in excess of seven hundred and fifty dollars, and to no other actions.97 Provision is made for the institution of small claims actions by attorneys by service as in civil actions⁹ as well as the commencement of actions by attorneys or other persons without writ or requirement of pleading other than a written or oral statement to the clerk.99 Venue is the same as in ordinary civil actions.100

TRAFFIC OFFENSES 

Traffic offenses are classified as "moving" and "nonmoving.' A moving traffic offense means a violation of a statute, ordinance or regulation relating to the operation or use of a motor vehicle or to the use of streets and highways by pedestrians or by the operation of any vehicle; a nonmoving offense means any parking or other violation of a statute, ordinance or regulation involving a vehicle not in motion.107

Generally traffic offenses are handled in a manner different from other criminal matters; instead of an Information, a 

Uniform Summons/Complaint is used. This form contains five parts: (1) the complaint, (2) a copy of the complaint for the Department of Motor Vehicles, (3) a copy of the complaint for the police record, (4) a copy of the complaint for the arresting officer, and (5) the summons. It also contains a statement of the promise by the person arrested to appear in court at the time and place specified to be signed by him in cases where he is released without bond.¹
