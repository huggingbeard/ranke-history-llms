In a small community on a remote island of the Hawaiian group, amid tropical flowers and swaying palms, suddenly occurs a brutal murder. Virtually the only supporter of law and order within call is the big, native Hawaiian cop, Komako. This new detective that Max 

Long has created will inevitably be compared with Charlie Chan or Mr. Moto, but Komako, who is not nearly so ingenuous as he lets people think he is, can stand on his own two feet and will, we believe, take his place with the popular fiction detectives. Komako is called into the case when lovely Isabel Becquist is found dead from a blow on her head. And Isabel is not the only victim of the slayer's savagely relentless activities. Another, and still another murder is committed-and always there is the menace of the great blowhole that spouts like a geyser just off shore and that can churn any body fed to it into indistinguishable small pieces. The colorful setting, the sharp characterization and the swift pace of this thriller combine to introduce the reader to a new and original mystery novel author.

We lived on the western edge of one of the remote islands of the Hawaiian group. Our bit of paradise, though in ages past swept by lava flows from the volcano far inland, was now enchanting with verdure because of the decomposed lava soil resulting from those bygone flows. We were about

as isolated as it would be possible to be in this modern world of swift communication, miles off the main highway which circled the island. The narrow dirt road from the nearby plantation straggled down off the cliffs to come to an end at our little bay. 

Seldom did strangers find their way down that winding road. The Hawaiian fisherfolk, who had a village at the left of the bay where outrigger canoes lazed high on the sand, used the road. And down it, at intervals, came the white people from the plantation to bathe. They were six in number, and together with the five of us boarding at Mrs. 

Hoapili's house on the beach, made up what we called the "Lazy Waters Club."

The house had once been the "summer palace" of a Hawaiian queen. It was large and square, built of coral blocks, with wide comfortable verandasalways called lanais in the Islands-extending around it on both floors, giving it a hospitable, gracious air. It lay in a deep walled garden between the cliffs and the beach, with ancient coconut palms slanting high over all. A place so quaint, so otherworldly, so sweet with the scent of jasmine and gardenias and all the Island flowers, that it lulled one into the belief that human beings might be like that, too.

It was Saturday, and the time for our usual swim before the dinner and dance at the house. Some of the plantation members of our group had arrived and we trooped out in bathing togs across the deep lanais and through the fragrant garden. Outside the wall which separates the garden from the beach, we stopped from long habit to deposit towels-and my spectacles and pipe-on the chosen water-worn lava rock safely back from the waves. The lowering sun cast long weaving shadows from the overhead fronds as we crossed the beach, and the peace and beauty of that isolated and primitive nook still held. 

Then came the first minor explosion, which I record for the reason that it was the first outward evidence of the jealousy which had been seething around us-apparent to all the others, as I afterward learned, but unsuspected by me. It played an important part in our bewildered investigation of the murder which-I know now-must have been at that moment already plotted.

Marge Temple, overly plump, fortyish, and hitherto patient with her husband's friendship for our little Reno widow, seized his arm and cried: "No sitting it out with Isabel this time. You're going to help me through the waves!"

Marge always had to be escorted out to the

smooth water beyond the breakers which was our playground.

Dr. Temple frowned impatiently. “Don't be childish! I'm not going in right now. There's something I want to talk over with Isabel."

I had never thought Doc was the type to be chasing women. He had a coldly professional manner and seldom warmed up. I could see that Isabel might easily be attracted by his austere good looks, for he had a fine muscular body and there was distinction in his regular features, wide-spaced gray eyes and pointed brown beard. Up till now, I had supposed his attentions to Isabel had been forced by the young lady herself.

But Marge evidently thought otherwise, for her small mouth tightened and she gave Doc a deadly look from her slightly protruding blue eyes, then transferred the glare to Isabel. 

Isabel Becquist had been one of Mrs. Hoapili's paying guests for only about three months. Time enough, however, to have snaked herself into our Eden and caused more damage than I had any idea of, until later. She was pretty, if you like them very petite, dark-haired and blue-eyed, with a penchant for scarlet fingernails. She was dressed this time in something fragile and pink, with a sun hat, for she

never swam with us.

One of the lazy swells rose, green and transparent, towering slowly to make a marching wall. The rest of us went through the next wave and came to the surface swimming on to quiet water. 

Looking back I saw Isabel, quite unruffled, enticingly patting the sand beside her as she smiled up at Doc. 

Then I became uneasy, not so much over Marge's continued hysterics, but because of the strange expression in Tom Luther's eyes as, near me, he watched those two on the beach. Tom would be jealous, I supposed in disgust, since he was one of Isabel's conspicuous conquests. Tom Luther was the big shot among us, manager of the plantation.
