PRESIDENT SAYS U.S. AND ALLIES MUST BE STRONG BECAUSE THE USSR, IS STRONG. President 

Truman, delivering his State of the Union message before a joint session of the Congress on January 9, said that "until the Soviet Union accepts a sound disarmament proposal, and joins in peaceful settlements, we have no choice except to build up our defenses." Pointing out. that the Soviet Union has continued to increase its armed might, he said that "the free nations may have to maintain for years the larger military forces needed to deter aggression." 

Mr. Truman declared that the threat of war is still very real, and that if the US "had to try to stand alone against a Soviet-dominated world, it would destroy the life we know and the ideals we hold dear", for "our allies are essential to us, just as we are essential to them." (Department of State Bulletin, Jan. 21, p. 79.)

MR. TRUMAN CITES PRESENT WAR THREAT. 

In a special message to the Congress on January 14, President Truman stated: "The communist aggression in Korea has demonstrated to the world beyond any doubt the true intentions of the Soviet leaders, and their willingness to use armed attack, if necessary, to accomplish their objectives. The Korean invasion has made it eminently plain that the entire free world lives in the constant danger of further communist aggression. United Nations successes in Korea have been great, but the threat of a third world war is still very real and very menacing. He stated that our vast preparedness program to meet this threat involves raising and equipping promptly sufficient armed forces to resist aggression if it should come and assuring an adequate and uninterrupted supply of critical materials. Pointing out that most of the world's natural rubber is produced in 

Southeast Asia, where Communist subversion and sabotage are now a serious problem, and that Communist invasion of these areas is an ever-present possibility, he recommended that the US Government retain ownership and continue operation of synthetic rubber plants for two more years. (White House press release, Jan. 14.) 

President Truman's MR. TRUMAN REJECTS ISOLATION IN A COMMUNIST-THREATENED WORLD. 

message to Congress transmitting the budget for the fiscal year 1953, read in the House of Representatives on January 21, noted that the single objective of this Nation's vast rearmament program is "the building of defensive forces and a mobilization base which will serve as a deterrent to Communist aggression and which can, if the deterrent fails, give us and our allies the strength to defeat the aggression." Referring to "the areas most vulnerable to aggression", in Europe, in the Middle East, and in Asia, he stressed that "to deter aggression and provide for our own security require not only building up our own strength but also building strength in these areas." "We cannot, as two world wars have proved, isolate ourselves from threats to other free men", he stated. "This Nation cannot stand by and see free peoples overrun. In the end, that could only mean isolation of this country in a world organized against it by Soviet masters." (To be published in the Bulletin, Feb. 4, p. 179.)

SECURITY COUNCIL CONSULTATIONS: MR. COHEN DISCUSSES SOVIET PROPOSAL. In the debate in the General Assembly on the Soviet proposal for Security Council discussions to relieve international tension, Mr. Cohen stated on January 12: "My government has always been prepared to discuss, in the Security Council and other appropriate forums measures to reduce international tension. The people of the United States are eager to see a reduction in those tensions which threaten their peace and security. But we do not wish to see high-level conferences convened as a sounding board for harangues which may aggravate and not reduce the dangers of war. We are for solemn and serious discussions where all parties in good faith seek honest agreement. Whenever the members of the Security Council believe that tensions can be reduced by the holding of periodic meetings we believe such meetings should be held. 

We have joined with Brazil, France and the United Kingdom in an amendment to the Soviet proposal to this effect and we hope that the proposal as so amended will be adopted. We hope that the Soviet Union will cooperate with other members of the Security Council to make possible and fruitful the holding of periodic meetings in accordance with this resolution and the Charter. But we all know that the Security Council cammot function effectively as long as the Soviet Union makes no effort to reach unanimity with the other members and exercises without restraint its power of veto. No government and no people would welcome a change in the Soviet attitude more than the government and people of the United States." (US Delegation to the General Assembly, press release 1384, Jan. 12.)

DEFINITION OF AGGRESSION: 

MR. MAKTOS OPPOSES SOVIET PROPOSAL. 

US Representative John 

Maktos, speaking before the General Assembly's Committee VI on January 10 with respect to a Soviet resolution to define, aggression, pointed out that the definition of aggression is not in the interest of peace, that "the evil purposes of States cannot be controlled by a mechani cal devine", and that it is "preferable to leave the term undefined and to permit the requisite organs of the United Nations to pass upon the aggressive nature of acts on a caseby-case basis." He added: "It has been the consistent opinion of the United States that, because of the very nature of the question, it would not be possible to arrive at a satisfactory definition of aggression. Because of the dynamic nature of present world events, any enumeration of acts which constitute aggression must of necessity be incomplete." (Bulletin, Jan. 28, p. 131.)

GENERAL PRESIDENT TRUMAN REPORTS ON GRANTING OF MORE MUTUAL SECURITY FUNDS TO MEET DEFENSE PLANS OF FIVE COUNTRIES. -In identical letters of February 5 to the Chairmen of the Senate Committees on Foreign Relations and Armed Services and the House Committees on Foreign Affairs and Armed Services, President Truman reported that additional resources would be made available, under the Mutual Security Act, to France, Greece, Turkey, the UK, and Yugoslavia during this fiscal year, to permit them to meet their present and projected defense plans. Stressing that in the interest of US defense objectives in Europe these countries should not have to cut their defense programs, he said (1) "the strength of French military forces is a key element in the effective defense of the free world, both in deterring the threat of Soviet aggression in Europe and in stemming active Communist aggression in

1. GENERAL MESSRS. TRUMAN AND ACHESON APPEAL FOR SUPPORT OF M. S.P. AS ANSWER TO SOVIET THREAT. - 

President Truman urged Congress, in a special message of March 6, to authorize $7.9 billion for military and defense aid and for economic and technical aid in the coming fiscal year to strengthen other free nations in their defense against the threat of aggressive Soviet imperialism. "The problems of American survival would be multiplied to an incalculable extent if we had to face the Soviet threat without the support and assistance of others, ho said, and he continued: "We are joined with other countries in the patient and systematic building in the free world of enough military strength to deter external communist aggression; and of economic and political and moral strength to remove internal threats of communist subversion and point the way toward democratic progress." (Bulletin, Mar. 17, p. 403.) 

On the night of March 6, Mr. Truman stressed to his radio and television audience the urgency and seriousness of the danger of communist aggression. Referring to the sum of $600 million requested for economic and technical assistance to the underdeveloped countries, he noted that in those countries the "communist makes his bid for power not as a conqueror, but in the guise of a friend offering an end to the torments of famine and disease." Explaining that "stomach communism cannot be halted with weapons of war", he said we must meet the challenge with more appropriate means, and the Mutual Security Program proposes to do that through the Point Four approach. (White House press release, Mar. 6.) 

The President transmitted to Congress on March 7 the first report on the Mutual security Program (dated December 1951). The report described the many different actions taken to help reach the objective of building in the free world sufficient military and other strength to assure peace by deterring, or, if necessary, by repelling any further Communist aggression. (Office of the Director for Mutual Security press release, March 7. published in the Bulletin, Mar. 24, p. 471.)

On March 13, at the opening of public hearings on the President's request for mutualsecurity funds before a joint Congressional committee, Secretary of State Dean Ache son stated: "The American people have clearly rejected both isolationism and preventive war as self-defeating courses of action. ... 

"What the Mutual Security Program represents is our share of the total effort which is now going on, to reduce the weakness which stands as a tempta tion to aggression, and to build instead a strong and confident structure of peace. ... "In every land, to a greater or lesser degree, the struggle against communist imperialism must be fought on two or more fronts at the same time. No nation and no people can be truly safe until the free world as a whole possesses enough military strength to protect itself against a treacherous attack. And it is equally true that no nation can be secure unless the political, economic and moral foundations of its society are sound and solid." (Bulletin, Mar. 24, p. 463.)

MR. MARSHALL ANALYZES U.S. FOREIGN POLICY IN RELATION TO THE U.S.S.R. In an article published by the Department during March, Charles B. Marshall, a member of the Department's 

Policy Planning Staff, outlined five main elements of the world situation affecting our foreign policy in the recent past and the present and four general lines of our endeavor in these circumstances: (1) Complex historic changes, notably two World Wars, have left bipolar world of power, the poles being the US and the USSR; (2) the USSR is in the grip of tyrannous rulers who achieved power by conspiracy and require tension and conflict within and at the periphery so as to hold onto their power; (3) a climate of intimidation and fear in many countries has resulted from the circumstance that the USSR has great military forces and that these forces are deployed along a huge span bearing on northern and central Europe, the Mediterranean area, the Middle East, southeast Asia, and Japan; (4) the dislocation of economic patterns in consequence of World War II have created situations affording special opportunities for Soviet communism; and (5) the weakening of old restraints in Africa, the 

Middle East, and east Asia have created opportunities for the USSR, alert as it is to the quest for advantage in the troubles of others. Our foreign policy, in these circumstances, has been directed toward (1) developing situations of strength so as to relieve the senso of anxiety--and with it the Kremlin's power of intimidation--among the nations disposed to go along with us; (2) insuring that the areas where the crisis of politics is sharpe st-the areas of contest, such as southeast Asia, the Middle East, and the Arab areas--shall not be lost; (3) exercising leadership in working toward the ideas of responsibility and peaceful adjustment in contra-distinction to the Soviet pattern of turmoil and conflict; and (4) steer ing away from the tragedy of another world war. (Bulletin, Mar. 17, p. 415.)

PAMPHLET CALLS U.S.S.R.OBSTACLE TO FREEDOM AND SECURITY. - The Soviet Union is the most disturbing obstacle to freedom and security at present, according to an 80-page pamphlet, Our Foreign Policy 1952, issued by the Department in March. As a result of the aggressive tyranny of the USSR, which has seized control of its neighbor countries and reduced them satellite puppets, the free nations of the world have been forced to unite for protection against Soviet aggression, and have told the Soviet rulers that they "will not allow them to capture more and more countries", it stated. JAPAN: MR. ACHESON COMMENTS ON RISK OF SOVIET OR RED CHINESE MILITARY ACTION. In a letter of February 5 (published by the Department during March), Secretary Acheson replied to Senator Alexander Wiley's question, asked in connection with Congressional hearings on the Japanese peace treaty, as to whether the USSR or Communist China might attempt to send armed forces into Japan in pursuance of alleged "belligerent" rights within the terms of the treaty. 

Secretary Acheson said that in his judgment the risk was neither diminished nor increased by purely legalistic considerations", but that "in such matters the leaders of the Soviet Union and of other nations dominated by the Soviet Union act externally not because of the presence or absence of legalistic rights, but because of their own determination of what is in their over-all interest." Citing the Potsdam surrender terms and the San Francisco Peace Treaty, he said that "any effort of any single power hereafter to assert independent belligerent rights of occupation would not be an exercise of belligerent rights but a violation of the Potsdam surrender terms by which the Allied Nations, including the Soviet Union, are bound." GERMANY: U.N. ELECTIONS COMMISSION CONTRASTS U.S. AND U.S.S.R. ATTITUDES. - 

The UN Commission to Investigate Conditions for Free Elections in Germany announced on August 5 its intention to adjourn because of the failure of the USSR to cooperate in the execution of its mandate, and "little prospect" of any cooperation in the near future. It was pointed out that the Commission had been able to secure by the end of April 1952 every assurance of cooperation from the Allied High Commissioner and from the authorities of both the Federal Republic of Germany and the western sectors of Berlin, but that despite repeated attempts to establish reciprocal contact, the Soviet authorities had ignored the Commission. In its final report of August 11, submitted to the 

UN Secretary-General, the Commission noted that (1) the US, Great Britain, and France preferred action through the UN Commission but were prepared "to consider any other practical and precise proposals for an impartial commission of investigation which the Soviet Government may wish to put forward, on the one condition that they are likely to promote the early holding of free elections throughout Germany" and (2) the USSR, objecting to the competence of the UN to concern itself with the German question and rejecting investigation by the present Commission, proposed instead an investigation by a commission formed by the four powers occupying Germany. (Bulletin, Aug. 18 and 25, pp. 245 and 298.)
