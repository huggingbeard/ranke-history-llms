PREFACE 

In reply to some letters expressing doubts of the soundness of the conclusions reached in the treatises of the writer entitled "Monoply vs. 

Freedom" and "More Money for Everyman,”* or making enquiries as to the method of application of the economic changes proposed, some general observations are submitted. For a more complete analysis than is now made the reader is referred to those treatises. 

But before getting to the work in hand it is desired to refer to some pertinent remarks contained in a recent book by Lawrence Dennis. "Never," he says, "have economic affairs been so under the domination of so-called practical statesmen and practical business men than in the post war period and the following period of crisis. There has been lacking philosophy, spiritual leadership, true and fearless thinking. Courageous facing of facts has been outlawed as a form of dangerous radicalism. The truth, readily ascertainable, in important matters, has been shunned as an evil and dreaded as an encroachment on vested rights and vested interests, which are considered more important than human rights and human welfare. And for the want of a courageous application of the truth to practical affairs the economic injustices and miseries continue unabated. No adequate steps have been taken to eliminate the intolerable condition of unemployment in the midst of unlimited productive capacity."

While the author of these pertinent quotations from "Is Capitalism Doomed?" presents a treatment of his own for the economic disorder quite different from the treatment of the present writer, which he doubtless would refuse to accept, nevertheless what has been quoted serves as a fitting preface to the following observations. *Roland Swain Company, Philadelphia, Publishers, 1933. LAND AND RENT 

Of the doubts and enquiries received virtually none question the conclusion that the economic rent of land is unearned. The law of rent as formulated by Ricardo is generally accepted. Briefly, rent is the excess of the price of a given product over its cost of production on intra-marginal land. The illustration of this principle given in the pamphlet is not denied, namely, on land No. 1 the cost of obtaining a given amount of produce is $50, on land No. 2 it is $60, and on land 

No. 3 it is $70. If the demand for the produce of the land is such that it takes the yields of all three tracts to supply it, the market price is $70, for however the cost of production on different tracts may vary, in the market equal products, however obtained, command an equal price which is determined by the marginal cost, that is to say, by the cost on the poorest tracts in use. It follows that the cultivators of land No. 1 and land No. 2 reap a profit of $20 and $10 respectively. This profit is termed rent. It is provided by the purchasers of the produce who give $70 in exchange for what normally costs only $50 and $60 to produce on lands Nos. 1 and 2. It is thus clear that economic rent is unearned by the recipients of it.

But some of the enquiries are directed to the method of rectifying the injustice of permitting the holders of intra-marginal land to receive the unearned rent. It is asked how the amount of the rent can be judged and how it can be taken for social use without doing an injustice to those who now receive it.

Rent is determined by competition. If land which yields a profit, or rent, is offered for lease, competition obliges the lessee to pay for its use a charge practically equal to the rent. If for a given amount of produce the cost on land No. 1 is $50 and on land No. 2 it is $60, a tenant can afford to pay a rent for the lease of land No. 1 based on the difference in costs, namely, at the rate of approximately $10 in the illustration, for a given amount of produce. The costs and advantages of various tracts are roughly approximated by trial and error, as recorded by local estimates and prices. Where land is used for industrial and commercial pursuits the principle is the same. In the case of a mine the rent, or profit, is the differential in the cost of production under inferior mines. 

In some large copper mines the cost is said to be six cents per pound, whereas in others it is ten cents or even considerably more, varying with the cost of labor and supplies. If the demand for copper requires the working of the less productive mines, the price of copper will rise to cover the cost these mines and the differential is the rent. In the case cited the lesser would be obliged, by competition, to pay a rent approximately equal to four cents a pound on copper mined. The data as to costs and differentials would be determined by access to the books of the operating corporation or individual.

II 

There are two ways of taking economic rent for social use by taxation. In one way, a tax would be levied upon the value of the land equal to the rent, or advantage, that the owners of intra-marginal land, whether [4]

of agricultural, mineral, or commercial character, or of residential location, now derive, either as occupants and producers themselves, or as lessors. The machinery now used in our fiscal system, for the assessment of land values and the collection of the tax that takes only a part of the rent, could with but little additional expense, be strengthened and used. 

To prevent unavoidable inaccuracies in the assessment, provision could be made for each owner of land to be his own assessor and to levy the tax on his assessment, with the understanding, in order to prevent undervaluation that, if a bona fide offer for the property was made at a price exceeding his assessment by more than 10 or 15 percent, the owner would either raise his assessment to within a reasonable amount of the offered price, or sell.

As land is not a product of labor, its value cannot be determined by marginal cost or effort, nor by marginal utility, since it is not in course of consumption and its utility is only in a potential state. Recourse must be had to the method of capitalization by "comparison of land with money or capital-goods on the basis of the one property which they have in common, namely, the power of bringing an income." This method shows that the value of land equals the rent capitalized at the current rate of interest, plus the rate of taxation (not here considering the increments of value). If the yearly rent of a tract of land is $1200, the rate of interest 5 percent, and the rate of taxation 3 percent, the market value is the rent capitalized at 5+ 3, that is, 8 percent or $15,000. Of this amount $450 are taken in taxes and $750 are received by the owner, which represents an investment returning a net income of 5 percent. 

If the annual increments of value are included the result is somewhat different. In the illustration, supposing the annual increment is $300 and the rent $1200, the value of the land would be $1500 capitalized by the rate of interest 5 and the rate of taxation 3, that is 8 percent or $18,750. The excess of this value over the value in the first case, namely, $3750 is termed "speculative land value," as it cannot be accounted for by the rent alone, but is due to an expected increase of value in the future.

If, therefore, the full 100 percent of the rent was taken in taxation, there would be nothing left for the owner, and the land would have no market value as an investment. Those who now hold land merely as an investment for the rent, and not for use, could no longer afford to hold it and consequently such land would be thrown open to those who would use it. While land under such conditions would have no investment value, it would have a value for use, and those who wish to produce on it would naturally be the owners of it. The improvements on the land would be exempt from taxation, which would tend to facilitate greater and better improvements. It is estimated that the full taxation of economic rent of land (and of public utilities) would provide ample revenue for an economical administration of the State, and that personal property, as well as improvements on the land, would no longer need to be taxed.

The taxation of the rent cannot be shifted onto the consumer, as nearly all other taxes can, for it does not decrease the supply of land, or increase the price of the produce, as rent is an effect, and not a cause, [5]

of price. Nor has it any tendency to check production, unless the tax exceeds the rent. The tendency is rather for an increase in production by destroying speculative holdings in land and thus raising the margin of cultivation and lowering the cost.

III 

Another way of absorbing economic rent for social use is through a reform of the money system, which has been advocated in these papers. 

If reforms were instituted which resulted in an adequate volume of money, and both money and capital-goods lost their power to command pure interest, the demand for intra-marginal land, by reason of it still earning a surplus income, would increase, through competition, until the value of land rose to a point where the tax of 3 percent would absorb the total rent, namely, in the illustration given above, to $40,000. On that amount the 3 percent tax would be equal to the $1200 rent. At any price less than $40,000 an investor could obtain an income and would accordingly be willing to purchase. This method of collection of the tax would result in preserving to present owners of intra-marginal land what is approximately the equivalent of thirty-three years rent; that is to say, if the value of the land in the illustration rose to, or nearly to, $40,000 and the owners could dispose of it at approximately that price they would have what is the equivalent of about $1200 a year for thirtythree years. Or, the valuation could readily be reduced by increasing the rate of taxation. For instance, if the rate was made 8 percent, the value of the land would be $15,000, the equivalent of about twelve and one-half years' rent.

IV 

As it stands now, the right of land ownership is clearly a remnant of the feudal ages which enabled the landowner to obtain from the community an income which he did not earn. The exclusive possession of land, however, is necessary for its cultivation and improvement. And to effect this security of tenure while still depriving the landowner of the right to get something for nothing, the remedy applied through the abolition of pure interest on money and capital is preferred by some students of the subject. It is also preferred by them on the ground that many innocent purchasers now hold land on faith in the continuance of the present system of land ownership which gives them the rent, and which has long been upheld by society. To tax away without any consideration, the total rent which they now enjoy, is not only a hardship and injustice to them, but a confiscation of what they paid for the land. 

Conversely it is unjust and a hardship to all others who finally pay the rent in the additional price of the goods they consume, to withhold from them the compensating advantage which they would receive through the remission of other taxes which they now also pay and which would be unnecessary when the land tax is inaugurated. 

Put in another way society should no longer be deprived of a large aggregate amount of income which it creates. 

If the rent was taken in the manner advocated by Henry George and his followers, which has been indicated above, and in the absence of a reform of the money question, some believe, and the present writer is [6]

one, that the tax should be applied and increased gradually until it absorbed the whole rent. This, like the other method, would be in the nature of a temporary compromise. But it should be borne in mind that the appropriation to private use of the economic rent of land is “a bold, bare, enormous wrong" that must be rectified. Nevertheless, while compromise is never admissible in thought, it is often desirable in action. The serious plight of agriculture is due to a complex of circumstances. In the last few decades there have been alternating periods of under-production and over-production of staple crops. It is difficult, owing to weather uncertainties and lack of co-operation among producers, to adjust supply to demand. Only a few years ago the fear was expressed that there would be insufficient wheat grown for domestic use, with a rise in living costs. But later the abnormal demand coming on a short world supply due to the War greatly reducing the number of wheat growers, resulted in the price of wheat advancing to over $2.85 per bushel. This stimulated production and farmers in the United States went heavily into debt for the purchase of land in order to increase their output. The rent of intra-marginal land advanced with the high price for wheat, which led to a rise in land values which was accelerated by the speculative purchases. There was about a 70 percent increase in farm land values and a 200 percent increase in farm debts during the War. But in 1931 there appears to be a 375 percent increase in debt and a decrease of farm values as compared with 1910. The rise in rent and the interest on the debt which averaged from 6 to 14 percent in different localities, together with greatly increased taxation (some 250 percent increase since the War), made too heavy a burden for the farmers to carry, particularly those farmers producing on marginal land. This burden was emphasized by the resulting over-production of wheat, and the decline in its price commencing in 1920 and continuing until it reached 40 cents in 1933. A large part of this so-called over-production is, however, really under-consumption, for the millions of unemployed and only partially employed, both here and abroad, were without adequate purchasing power, which greatly reduced both the domestic and foreign demand.

Farming is primarily a way of life and only secondarily an industry or business. "The farmer's problem is not how to make money, but how to live happily on the soil." But farmers, as a rule, abandoned these homely but sound principles and made a business of their vocation in the endeavor to reap profits and get rich. They joined producers in large manufacturing industries, and financial interests, in the mad rush for profits, most of which, as is argued elsewhere in this paper, are unearned. 

Too much debt, increased by interest charges, has been their undoing. It is stated that the total of farm mortgage debt is so great, about nine and one-half billion dollars, that since the War the farmers have had to bear an increase of 300 percent in their total interest charges. 

The permanent remedies for the plight of the farmers are three. 

First, the taxation of economic rent and the remission of taxes on their improvements and personality. These changes in taxation would have [7]

the result of transferring many thousands of farms, now the property of absentee owners, to occupiers and users. They would destroy speculation in agricultural land and would raise the margin of cultivation, giving the farmers a better living. They would tend to bring the operation of all farms into the hands of owners. Secondly, the elimination of pure interest which would relieve farmers of an artificial burden on the money they legitimately might have to borrow. No class of borrowers can stand in the long run the burden of interest, much of which becomes, in time, compounded. Thirdly, changes in the tariff so as to remove unfair discrimination against agriculture. The import duties on manufactured products accomplished the purpose for which they were levied, namely, a rise of domestic prices. But whatever import duties there may have been levied, from time to time, on agricultural products, they were unavailing for a rise in domestic prices because of an export surplus of agricultural products, the price for which is fixed in the Liverpool market, which fixes also the price in the domestic market. The farmers were obliged to pay high prices for what they bought and to accept low prices for what they sold. It is evident that government subsidies to one group of producers must be paid for by other groups of producers, and the farmers have always been the losers. Freedom of international trade would be beneficial to the farmers as tending to an equilibrium of prices for agricultural and manufactured products. 

If these burdens of rent, interest and tariff discrimination are not removed the status of agriculture and farmers will decline from one of independent proprietors, the backbone of American life, to a class of peasant tillers of the soil. It was said by Madison that "the freeholders of the country would be the safest depositories of Republican liberty"; but they have been largely destroyed by monopoly, and Jefferson's free agrarian democracy on which he counted so much has not been perpetuated.

We have dwelt at some length on agricultural problems because of their fundamental importance. There are some nine million engaged in farming, making, with their families, over thirty million people living on the soil, and their purchasing power is necessary for prosperity. For the production of one industry supports the production of another and so on among all the different industries. In the post-war years the prices of agricultural products suffered a decline much greater than the decline of prices for the goods that the farmer buys. The United States Department of Agriculture stated in November, 1931, that the index of prices paid by farmers (on a five-year basis, 1919-24-100) declined from 206 in 1920 to 126 in October, 1931, about 40 percent, while the index of all farm prices declined in the same period from 205 to 68, over 66 percent. 

The buying power of the farmers was reduced to 54 percent of what it was in 1920. The consequence was a severe decline in consumers' demand for manufactured goods, resulting in the closing down of plants and unemployment. The great lack of balance between agricultural and manufactured prices was a contributing cause of the depression, and so long as this disequilibrium lasts it will retard recovery. [8]

VI The concentration in the ownership of land in all its aspects continues unabated. The number of home owners has been steadily declining both as a percentage of the total families and in actual number. It declined from eleven and one-half millions of owners in 1919 to about nine and three-fourths millions in 1930. Concurrently there has been a steady gain in the number of families renting homes, increasing from nine and one-fourth millions to about fifteen millions in the same period. 

More than 50 percent of all farms in the United States are possessed by absentee owners, and leased to tenant farmers. The number of operating farm tenants is increasing and the number of operating owners is decreasing. The raw materials of industry are in greater concentrated monopoly control by combinations of capital, especially the most important, such as coal, iron ore, copper, lead, oil, timber, etc. The valuable and strategic natural sources of water power are monopolized. The land and raw materials, the sources of power, the farms and the homes, are falling more and more into the possession of absentee owners. 

Thousands of idle workers, many of whom, if land was free, would settle on and cultivate it, diminishing poverty and relieving the congestion in large cities, are deprived from doing so by monopoly control of the land. What a travesty of our so-called American freedom and fairness is this paradox of the monopoly of land and millions in want and poverty! 

Our landed domain is wide with millions of acres only sparsely, and not intensively, cultivated. While all "free" land has been taken up, immense tracts would again be thrown open to new producers if monopoly was eliminated. There is enough food supplying land and ample stores of the important raw materials of manufacture, in the United States, to support and supply the needs of a population many times greater than the present one. And yet there is the most abject poverty and want. "Interlink'd, food-yielding lands! 

Land of coal and iron! land of gold! land of cotton, sugar, rice! 

Land of wheat, beef, pork! land of wool and hemp! land of the apple and the grape!

Land of the pastoral plains, the grass-land of the world! land of those sweet-air'd interminable plateaus!

Land of the herd, the garden, the healthy house of adobe! 

Land of the ocean shores! land of sierras and peaks! 

Land of boatmen and sailors! fishermen's land! 

Inextricable lands! the clutch'd together!" [9]

MONEY AND INTEREST 

The definition of money used here is the definition of Francis A. 

Walker, that money is anything that does the money work. The function of money is to serve as a medium of exchange. For no other purpose would it have been necessary to create it. 

Money is essentially a credit. The kind of money that does over 

90 percent of the work of a medium of exchange in industry and commerce is credit-money, or deposit currency, that is to say, a paper credit instrument. It comes into being through so-called loans by the banks, the borrowers supplying the security by a pledge of their wealth. The discounted proceeds of the loans are placed as deposits to the credit of the borrowers who draw checks on them as needed. It is thus wealth monetized, or coined, it may be said, into means of payment. Wealth circulates itself through the principle of credit which is gratuitous and performs a valuable service to society. For every credit there must be a correlative debt. The debtors of the money system are the borrowers, the real issuers of deposit currency, who are the possessors, but only part owners, of the wealth they have pledged; and the creditors of the money system are the holders of money who have a claim on the debtors, and who are part owners, but not possessors, of the wealth pledged to the extent of the money they hold. 

How far can this principle of credit be safely extended? The only economic limitation is the amount of wealth that can be pledged. That amount naturally limits the total of assured credit-money that can be issued. No individual can borrow more, in commercial loans, than he can secure by a pledge of his wealth and credit. But the total of commercial loans, and therefore the total of credit-money, can safely be increased so long as it is amply secured by wealth taken at a good margin. of its value. And as the total of credit-money of all kinds in circulation is much less than the total of all wealth, the issue of credit-money can be greatly expanded. The amount of deposit currency is constantly changing, deposits rising and falling with the expansion and contraction of loans. It is normally self-liquidating. The exceptions are the inability of some borrowers to pay their debts, when the pledged security is sold. 

In actual practice, however, there are three limitations imposed on the expansion of credit-money. Two of these are legal and one is psychological.

Taking the last one first, it may be said that it is due to a widespread belief in the quantity theory of money, according to which the value of money is in inverse ratio to its quantity; as the quantity is increased the value falls and prices rise; as it is decreased the value rises and prices fall. The fear of inflation of prices, through a belief in this theory, tends to restrict the expansion of the volume of money. In the little treatise on Money the quantity theory is shown, it is hoped, to be fallacious where gold is the standard of value. Thus in this psychological case the supply of money is restricted through fear based on ignorance. 

At present it is the desire of those in power to bring about inflation by [ 10 ]

raising the price level to that of 1926, and to do so the gold standard has been abandoned, at least temporarily, with the probability that the gold content of the dollar may be lowered. This seems to be an admission that it is the value of gold, and not the quantity of money, that governs the price level.

The legal limitations on the expansion of credit-money are more serious. They are due partly to fear and ignorance, and partly, if not greatly, to the conscious establishment of a legal basis for maintenance of the present social order, a prominent economic characteristic of which is the persistent exploitation by the holders of privilege. 

One of these limitations is the exclusive right given to the banks for the issue of credit-money, or monetized credit. All others are prohibited from doing so by a federal tax of ten percent on note circulation and by some states making it a penal offense. The banks thus have a monopoly control over the supply of money and, as they are organized for profit, their self-interest induces them to charge a monopoly price, or interest, on money loans. The rate of interest charged is fixed, not by competition, but by agreement. It is uniformly 6 percent for commercial loans, and from 8 to 14 percent for agricultural loans at the many thouands of banks throughout the country, but often less, and sometimes more than 6 percent at large city banks. The interest on money is due to an inadequate supply of money. Those who must have money in their industry and trade, owing to the great extension of the principle of division of labor, bid for it against each other, and every one remembers times when, owing to the scarcity of money, interest rose through the competition of borrowers to prohibitive rates, restricting and paralyzing business and enterprise. If the supply was elastic, if it increased by competition as the demand increased, the price for issuing credit-money would tend to fall to the cost, and interest to nil, that is to say, the item of pure interest which is that part of gross interest over the legitimate cost.

The service of the banks consists essentially in an exchange of their well known and widely available credits for the unknown, unavailable, but equally good credits of the borrowers. The cost for performing this service, consisting of labor and superintendence in making the loans, and insurance of risk, is very small, probably not over one-half of one percent in normal times. The tendency is universal when competition is free for the price of a freely reproducible good to equal the marginal cost of production. Credit-money is freely reproducible so long as ample security is pledged with provision for redemption, and the price (or interest) for issuing it tends, with free competition, to equal the marginal cost which consists of labor and insurance. It must be remembered that the banks do not lend capital. The borrowers pay interest, not for capital, but for monetized credit which is the only kind of credit that will freely circulate. The credit that the banks lend (it really is not a loan but an exchange) is not taken out of an existing fund which has been accumulated by saving, or on which labor has been expended, and which has a value in itself. It is freely manufactured at little or no cost by the banks and payable as money. It is not wealth in itself. It is the representative of wealth owned and pledged by the borrowers. [ 11 ]

It will be noted that the borrowers, in supplying the capital security for loans, are in reality both borrowers and lenders. They create both the demand and the supply, at least the substance of the supply. If it were practicable and safe for them to issue their own medium of exchange on their own wealth, if it would freely circulate, the process of issuing it would cost them virtually nothing. Interest would not exist. But in the present state of intelligence and social morality the consensus of opinion requires that credit-money, or the medium of exchange, shall be issued by an independent agency. But it does not follow because of this that there must be a monopoly of the issue. 

II 

There are three concrete forms of capital that are capable of bringing a return to the owner, namely, land, capital-goods and money. There is no evidence of an abstract, mobile fund of capital in addition to these concrete forms, as is contended by J. B. Clark. Any such abstract fund, if the term is used for the sake of discussion, must needs be made up of some of the concrete forms, for at any one moment of time there can only be one, or more, of the three forms named, however they may change by purchase and sale or transfer.

That capital-goods, that is to say, the means of production and things in course of production, are in great abundance is evident on every side. 

They are for sale in every market, with idle plant and equipment and idle workers capable of producing great additional amounts. Large cash expenditures are constantly being incurred in advertising and salesmanship to market surplus goods not only here but abroad. The most convincing proof of the superabundance of existing capital-goods is the universal willingness of their owners to give them up for money. In selling things for money, a mere credit-instrument, "the producers part with present goods in exchange for a right to obtain goods in the future, and for this virtual loan of material wealth the holder of the right receives no interest. The total amount of actual wealth so loaned without interest by the community equals the volume of all money in use." (The amount of money in use at present is about 25 or 30 billions of dollars. In 1929 it was 70 billions.) The supply of capital-goods being greater than the demand, there is no sound economic reason why they should command interest or profit. 

But it is evident that the supply of money is inadequate, and equally evident that interest is paid for the use of money and not for the use of capital. With money borrowers buy capital-goods. As these goods bring an income to the user, it would naturally be expected that interest would be paid to those who have parted with them, if interest, as is generally contended, is paid for the use of that form of capital. But interest is not paid to them, but to the banks. What have the banks done to earn it?

Suppose that A, a manufacturer of yarns, wants to enlarge his plant by obtaining new spinning frames costing $10,000. B, a banker, has $10,000 to lend out, and C is a maker of spinning frames. A borrows from B the $10,000 and pays it to C for the frames. Before the transaction B as creditor of the money system held a claim against the community amounting to $10,000; A owned a factory worth $40,000; while [12]

C had a stock of frames. After the transaction B has a claim against 

A to the amount of $10,000, A is in possession of a factory worth $50,000, of which he owns only four-fifths, the other one-fifth being owned by B to secure his claim. While C, instead of his frames, has now merely a claim against the community amounting to $10,000. It must furthermore be observed that from now on A is under obligation to pay interest on $10,000 to B the lender.

For what is this interest paid? It is not paid for a loan of capitalgoods, for the borrower A did not get the frames from the lender B. 

It is well known that the frames as active capital to the manufacturer A yield returns. But he received these capital-goods from C, the maker of frames, and not from the lender B. No one but C has given up actual products of labor in the transaction. But he does not receive interest, nor does he expect it. He is not only willing but anxious to give up his frames which are real capital for money, a mere credit instrument, because in the course of specialized production the frames are a finished product, have become idle capital to him, and the money that he received for them possesses a certain special faculty, the faculty of enabling him to select in the market the particular things needed by him to make new frames. In whatever way that this transaction is considered, it is clear that interest is not paid for capital-goods, but for money and the advantage afforded by money.

Under the system of division of labor, or specialized industry, each group of producers completes only one part of the final finished product. 

One raises the cotton, another spins it into yarn, a third weaves the yarn into cloth, a fourth makes the spinning frames, a fifth makes the looms. 

In this method of unconscious co-operation a much greater final finished product is obtained than would be the case if one group alone carried out all the separate operations.

Each group assembles the raw materials and supplies necessary for its own operation, and to this group these are active capital. The specialized operation of this group is to advance the production one stage further and the finished product of this group is idle capital to it. Its business is to continue this one step in the co-operative effort by buying the raw materials and selling the product, that is to say, the converting of active capital into idle capital. The capital-goods, namely, the plant, equipment, etc., necessary for this conversion of active into idle capital, are ample for full and continued production. But for the conversion of the idle capital into active capital, that is to say, for the disposal of the finished product and the acquirement of fresh supplies and raw materials so that the productive process may be continued for the group in question, money is necessary, and owing to the inadequate volume of money, this latter conversion is impeded and hampered. In other words, the process of production outruns the process of exchange. It is through production that goods are advanced from the active to the idle state, and through exchange that they are turned from an idle to an active state. From this it is at once apparent that with the process of production going on, and with the process of exchange held back through the arbitrary limitation of the means of exchange, the conversion of active capital into idle capital takes place more freely than the conversion of idle into active [13]

capital. This accounts for the desire of the group to give capital which is idle to it in exchange for money, because money enables it to acquire what is active capital to it. It is for this faculty of money that the group is willing to pay interest, for without money the productive operation cannot be continued and the specialization of industry would be brought to an end. (The last two arguments are summarized from the work of an able economic thinker.)

These illustrations give a good example of the efficiency of money. 

Without money the specialization of industry would be impossible. All business as it is at present would come to a standstill. The development of industry would be impossible where the method of exchange is so primitive and would be without any of the benefits of applied science. 

Proceeding from a system of simple barter, each instalment of money would naturally be used in the channels of production and exchange where it is most needed and most effective. With each successive instalment, or increase in the volume of money, the efficiency would be less than that of the preceding one, and so on to the final efficiency of money, which is nil when the volume of money is ample to supply the effective demand for it. Interest would be eliminated. For it is clear that the interest bearing power of money is related to the final efficiency of money. 

And not only is this so, but it is equally clear that the final efficiency of capital is related to the final efficiency of money. For if the volume of money were adequate, and producers could readily borrow it, without a charge for pure interest, they could readily obtain a yearly output exceeding what they can now obtain, and the amount of capital would correspondingly be increased. "Just as the available supply of any class of commodities is used for the most important requirements, as far as the supply goes, so the available volume of the medium of exchange would be applied, as far as it would reach, to those uses through which the most advantageous specialization of work is attainable. As the final utility of any class of goods is determined by the least urgent of the needs that can be supplied from the quantity of goods available, so is the final efficiency of money determined by the least remunerative of all the industrial developments which can be effected by means of the available supply of money. The final efficiency of money, then, is equivalent to the benefit afforded by the last of the several instalments added to the volume of money." 

Every impediment to the issue of an adequate volume of money is at the same time an impediment to production. A full specialization of the processes of production are impossible, for specialization requires that the goods, while being advanced toward maturity, be transferred from group to group and subjected to repeated rearrangement. This explains the existing demand for money. It follows "that if the total demand for money is not supplied, competition will tend to put a premium on its use.

Interest is accordingly paid on money because of its inadequacy to meet the demand of those who need it for the exchange of their products or services, and this competition raises the rate of interest as high as the market will bear, indeed, so high as to exert a destructive effect upon the market."

It is clear as the volume of money grows more inadequate and the rate of interest rises, that production is restricted and curtailed. If the [14]

rate continues to rise, production will continue to contract, and in the end, will collapse in panic and depression. Conversely, as the volume of money becomes more adequate and the rate of interest falls, production increases, and if the rate continues to fall, production will increase until the final efficiency of money is nil and the volume of production and trade is at the maximum with all available workers employed. 

III 

The second legal limitation of the issue of assured credit-money is the requirement of a gold reserve for its redemption in order to give stability to its value and consequently stability to the price level. The available amount of monetary gold thus limits the maximum amount of credit-money that can be issued. The present requirement of 40 percent of gold by the federal reserve banks on their note liabilities limits their issue to two and one-half times their fund of gold, and indirectly limits the total amount of credit-money. The growing inadequacy of the production of gold to meet the increasing demand for monetary reserves, so that the necessary expansion of the volume of credit-money may safely be made, must be overcome. Ways of accomplishing this are discussed in the treatise on Money, with the presentation of a plan for the enlargement of the redemption fund by the inclusion of silver with gold, at the option of the government. Other possible changes which would make feasible a larger volume of credit-money are a smaller percentage of gold reserve; a smaller gold content of the dollar; and delayed redemption so as to give time to obtain the necessary redemption fund of gold. 

IV 

It is asked by what method the volume of money can be so adequately increased that interest will be eliminated. Brief references to ways of accomplishing this are made in both of the treatises of the writer, to which the reader is referred. But remarks on the subject may be somewhat expanded.

There are two distinct kinds of credit-money in circulation. One kind that does over 90 percent of the mediation of exchange is, as we have already noted, deposit currency which is circulated by checks. The other kind is currency notes which pass from hand to hand. The latter consist, chiefly, of national bank notes, federal reserve bank notes, gold and silver certificates, etc.

The currency notes, to speak of them first, are furnished by the government without interest to both the reserve and national banks on deposit with it of security as designated by law. The range of securities that is now acceptable should be broadened to include other kinds so that the volume of notes may readily be increased. As the people look to the government as the only agency for the guarantee of currency notes, the government requires an adequate pledge of security, and so long as security is furnished and deposited with the government, the issue of these notes can safely be expanded, provided provision is made for their redeemability in the standard commodity. Power of the banks to increase the assured issue of these notes by deposit of security with the government should be granted, so that their volume may be ample, especially [15]

for payment of demand deposits when called for. Prompt payment to depositors tends to alleviate and limit the severity of runs on banks in times of panic. The notes paid out to depositors are in turn redeemable in gold, but generally the notes, which are guaranteed by the government, allay the fears of depositors generated by panic, and gold is rarely demanded.

In regard to deposit-currency, changes could readily be made in the law by increasing the power and functions of the national banks. The discount rate for bank loans, now determined by the banks through the monopoly they hold, could be subjected to competition. For assuring freedom of competition no needless impediment should be placed upon the incorporation of new competing national banks, nor should the right of issue be subjected to taxation of any kind. As the essential function of the banks is the conversion of ordinary credit into money, that is to say, into monetized or mobilized credit, there is no sound reason why the capital of the banks should not consist of valid credit instead of money as at present; or why they should not be organized on the basis. of stock issued, not against cash, but against valid forms of credit, such as bonds of federal, state, or municipal governments, and recorded liens on improved real estate which, in turn, could be handed over to the government as security for the currency notes issued to the banks for their working funds. The incorporation of the banks on a basis of valid credit instead of cash would tend to diminish the desire for profits and to facilitate operation at cost. These remarks about national banks apply with equal force to the reserve banks, so that they could readily grant re-discounts, at cost, to the member banks on deposit with them of ample security. If these changes were adopted in practise, and the redemption fund increased as provided in the plan submitted in the treatise on Money, there is no sound reason why the volume of credit-money should not be ample to meet the effective demand and interest be eliminated. 

This would be in accordance with common justice, "for money is a medium of exchange that was not invented and patented, but developed by experience extending through centuries. By all rules of justice the cost of using one's wealth through the principle of credit as a medium of exchange should not exceed the competitive cost of the services which make this medium available to its users, and under rational management this cost would be almost negligible." 

There never has been a safer currency "than that based on sound business debts coupled with strict insurance of its redeemability, and this principle should of course be maintained rigidly, so as to safeguard the value of the currency." "In banking as in other branches of business," says Prof. Seager, "competition is a force which compels a sharing of profits with the whole community. The more fully the banker is permitted to utilize his credit either in the form of deposit accounts or bank notes, the lower will be the rate of interest which he can afford to take for his services, and competition may be relied upon to force him to accept this lower rate. The guiding principle in connection with bank regulations should be to grant the fullest liberty that is compatible with a reasonable degree of security. Little fear need be entertained lest in the long run this liberty will not be used to advance the general good." [16]

In order to avoid losses to depositors, such as have occurred in disastrous amounts in recent years, through the failure of thousands of banks, provision should be made for the insurance of deposits. Ample safeguards now exist for the insurance of every bank note. But for the much larger volume of deposit-currency only partial insurance exists. 

For the plan generally advocated of a system of mutual insurance among the banks there are serious objections. It tends to create carelessness, inefficiency and speculation in the irresponsible and weaker banks at the expense of the efficient and responsible banks on which the losses fall. 

Insurance by joint stock companies organized for the purpose, or by existing companies, is far preferable. The rate of deposit insurance for the strongest and best managed banks would be negligible, the rate rising as the financial condition of the banks was impaired. This would be an added inducement for sound banking, especially if the rates of insurance of the various banks were published, as they should be, as a guide to depositors for the safeguarding of their money. 

There is a growing agitation for branch banking in which a few large banks will control hundreds of small banks. It would result in greater monopoly of banking, putting it in still fewer hands than it is in now, and giving greater control over the destinies of production and trade. Complete control of credit would gradually center in the big banks with the power of manipulating the quantity and velocity of creditmoney, and through this power of regulating the rate of interest. It has been too much the modern function of big banks, not to supply credit to help little business, but to control big business. Such a further concentration of banking would be a still greater menace to the liberties of the individual and thousands of small business interests. 

Instead of further consolidation, there should be greater competition, in banking. It has been the historic function of the small banks to furnish credit to "small business men and home owners in every village and town in the country." With the insurance of deposits referred to these small banks would be made safe for depositors.

But the method of freeing the issue of credit money from monopoly control, preferred by true libertarians, is the voluntary setting up of mutual banks for service and not for profit. Lovers of economic freedom prefer voluntary associations to those formed by government, as experience shows that the latter always either result in some form of monopoly, or give an advantage to powerful interests, and are also more costly in their operation. Surely such is the case of government formed banks. With the repeal of the present 10 percent tax on circulation, producers and merchants could readily arrange for the establishment of mutual banks to provide the medium of exchange they need without a charge for interest, that is to say, the item of pure interest over the cost of the service rendered by the banks. 

The members of mutual banks, when they need money, would follow practically the same procedure that borrowers do today with national and state banks. They would file with the mutual bank a mortgage lien on their improved property, taken at a safe percentage of its value, or other sound security, and the bank would place the amount borrowed to their credit on its books against which they could draw at pleasure. [17]

Members would agree to accept checks drawn by other members in all payments at par in lieu of gold coin. The operations of the banks would be confined strictly to commercial loans for purposes of production and exchange. In case of default by a member in the liquidation of his loan, when it becomes due, the pledged security is sold for payment in gold. 

By this means the value, or purchasing power, of the credit-money is kept equal to the value of gold, the measure or standard of value. 

Let us suppose that A is a farmer in need of new farm equipment costing $1000. He pledges for a loan of that amount with the mutual bank a mortgage note secured on his property at say not more than 50 percent of its assessed value. With the proceeds passed to his credit he pays B for the equipment. B pays C for the raw material, and D for the labor needed in the manufacture of the equipment. C and D pay 

E and others for what they need, and so on down to Z, who as a wholesale dealer in farm produce pays it over to A for purchases made from him. A discharges his debt at the mutual bank and gets a release of his mortgage lien. Who in these transactions has supplied capital and to whom should interest be paid? Under freedom without interest these transactions are similar in principle to the transactions every day under monopoly with a monopoly charge for interest. Here we have a fair example of the difference between freedom in an important economic process, and political control of that process.

There is no sound economic reason why the supply of assured creditmoney should not be adequate to meet the effective demand for it. The reasons are legal, and are uneconomic, unnecessary, and unjust. With an adequate volume of money and the elimination of interest, a persistent profit on capital would also be eliminated. For no one would be able to lend money at interest when it can be obtained from the bank without interest. And no one would be able to sell capital-goods at a persistent profit when, with interest eliminated, their price is adjusted through competition to marginal cost which includes only labor and services.

But it is feared that, with the elimination of interest, it would be impossible for any one to save enough for his old age and for the protection of his family out of his capital alone, but, it is said, if the capital can be put out at compound interest it is a splendid thing for such people. 

It would, of course, be reassuring to know, when one stops work, that one could live on the invested income from savings without impairment of the principal, which could then be handed down to one's heirs. But if all did this whence would the interest come? It must be paid out of the proceeds of labor. Therefore it would obviously be impossible for all to live on interest without work. In order that some may do so, others must work all the more. How can they, too, save for their old age? In a free economic system one can save only out of what one has produced; and live, in one's old age, only out of what one has saved.

There is no other way in which one can live without work, save by charity, or pensions. Unless, indeed, there is monopoly of money, and the issue of money, and monopoly of land. Then it becomes a matter [18]

of course for some to consume without producing, to live in idleness on what others produce.

Suppose that a worker after forty years has saved enough, if invested, for his widow to live on the interest, without spending the principal. 

Another worker leaves his widow nothing. It follows that the latter is one of many who are obliged to support the former. Our sympathy is with the victim and her class,-virtually the whole class of workers who, under our present economic system, must work not only to support themselves but, in addition, to support thousands of idlers through the higher prices they are obliged to pay, as consumers, for the goods and services they need. "It is self evident," as has been well said, "that the human race cannot continue to consume more than is produced. It follows that no one individual can consume more than he produces, unless some one else produces more than he consumes. Yet it is taken as a matter of course that wealth, once acquired, returns a continuous income. Suppose a man to work until he is fifty years old and to produce wealth, all told, amounting to $40,000. Suppose that during this time he consumes the equivalent of $20,000 and, on retiring, invests his savings of $20,000 at the rate of 5 percent. Suppose, furthermore, that the yearly income from his investment, amounting to $1000 supplies his subsequent needs, and that he dies when seventy-five years old leaving the principal of $20,000 to his heirs. Summing up, this man will have produced wealth to the amount of $40,000, and after using up $20,000 before he retires and $25,000 after that, a total of $45,000, there is still left $20,000 to be shared among his heirs. Existing economic conditions have enabled him to acquire $25,000 more than he produced." How did this come about?

That capital has no creative power and therefore cannot add value to itself has been demonstrated in these various papers. The inevitable conclusion is that the $25,000 received by him in excess of what he has produced represents wealth produced by others who, by existing economic conditions, were deprived of it. 

It is somewhere said by Kant that the test of the morality of a principle or course of action is whether it can be made universal, or words to that effect. The principle on which you act, to be moral, must be capable of adapting itself to a universal application. Can interest on money survive that test? Can it be made universal? If it is right for one to live on interest without work, it should be right for all to do so. 

But to suppose such a thing is a reductio ad absurdum. If, indeed, it were possible, a few multi-millionaires could confer a great boon on humanity. By investing five billion dollars at 6 percent compound interest, the principal will have grown, in 100 years, to an amount which, if kept invested at 6 percent, would be sufficient to give yearly to every man, woman and child at present in the United States the sum of $750; equal for a family of four to $3000 without work. 

It all comes down to a question of the validity of interest. Interest is either justifiable or it is not. "If interest is due to the working of natural economic forces, if it is an inevitable sequence of free competition," then it is just and valid. "But if interest arises through an arbitrary interference with the working of natural economic forces, if it [19]

is a sequence of some distortion of the industrial and commercial order of things," then interest is unjust and invalid, and the only remedy is obviously the removal of the disturbing influence. 

One of the standard conventional theories of interest is the "Productivity Theory" which holds that interest is due to the greater productivity rendered by the use of capital. 

It is true that by the use of capital, that is to say, labor saving devices and tools, the productive capacity of labor is increased. But when subjected to strict analysis this greater productivity fails to justify the power of capital to yield profitable returns. Let us suppose that tailors can make by hand two coats in a day. A new machine is invented which enables ten coats to be made in the same time. So long as the inventor holds a patent on the invention he is enabled to make and rent out the machines and obtain for them a profit proportionate to the benefit the machines bestow. But after the patent expires, others will compete in making the machines and tailors will now buy the machines instead of renting them. The manufacture of the machines will continue until the profit on the capital invested in them equals the current rate of capital returns in other lines of endeavor. This current rate we have found equals the current rate of interest on money. If, then, the interest on money is eliminated, competition in the making of the machines would continue until the rate of profit is also eliminated. The result would be a cheapening of the produce of the new machines which would be reaped by consumers.

Other conventional theories to account for interest likewise yield to strict analysis. One after the other has in turn been abandoned by many economists. The theory that has now, perhaps, the greatest vogue among professional economists is the "Positive Theory" of capital advanced by Böhm-Bawerk. It is an outgrowth of the negative abstinence theory of 

Senior. Its presentation generally takes the form that immediate payments are preferred to future payments; that men desire present goods to future goods; and that the providing of these present goods yields an interest to those who abstain and lend their savings. This view overlooks the fact that many prefer future goods to present goods, as is evident from insurance, from investments, from deposits in saving funds and other provident ways, especially from the acquirement of labor saving devices and tools as aids to greater production. There are abundant facts that show that those who produce before spending exceed those who spend before producing, that is to say, that the supply of savings exceeds the demand for them. 

In this theory the "element of time," time-value, is frequently mentioned as a factor in the law of distribution. But however this doctrine of abstinence and time is viewed, "when brought to its logical conclusion it fails to show how, under free competition in a community capable of producing more than sufficient to satisfy the immediate needs, the difference between the present and future valuation of wealth, which is claimed to determine the rate of interest, can in the average exceed the rate of risk and deterioration." For a brilliant refutation of the "Positive Theory" the reader is referred to a work by Hugo Bilgram entitled "The Cause of Business Depressions" (Lippincott, 1914) which is too long to be given here. [20]

An economist of once great popularity based his belief in interest on the value of time. It may be instructive to give some space to his argument. "Suppose a natural spring free to all, and that Hodge carries a pail of water from it to a place where he can build a fire and boil the water. Having hung a kettle and poured the water into it, and arranged the fuel and started the fire, he has by his labor set natural forces at work in a certain direction; and they are at work for him alone, because without his previous labor they would not be at work in that direction at all. Now he may go to sleep, or run off and play, or amuse himself in any way that he pleases; and when an hour-a period of time-shall have elapsed, he will have, instead of a pail of cold water, a pot of boiling water. Is there no difference in value between that boiling water and the cold water of an hour before? Would he exchange the pot of boiling water for a pail of cold water, even though the cold water were in the pot and the fire started? 

Of course not, and no one would expect him to. And yet between the time when the fire is started and the time when the water boils he does no work. To what, then, is that difference in value due? Is it not clearly due to the element of time? Why does Hodge demand more than a pail of cold water for the pot of boiling water if it is not that the ultimate object of his original labor—the making of tea, for example—is nearer complete than it was an hour before, and that an even exchange of boiling water for cold water would delay him an hour, to which he will not submit unless he is paid for it? And why is Podge willing to give more than a pail of cold water for the pot of boiling water, if it is not that it gives him the benefit of an hour's time in production, and thus increases his productive power very much as greater skill would? And if 

Podge gives to Hodge more than a pail of cold water for the pot of boiling water, does Podge lose anything that he had, or Hodge gain anything that he had not? No. The effect of the transaction is a transfer for a consideration of the advantage in point of time that Hodge had, to Podge who had it not, as if a skilful compositor should, if he could, sell his skill to a less skilful member of the craft."

The consideration referred to is, of course, interest. Let us see how a keen thinker answers this argument. "The illustration is vitiated from beginning to end by the neglect of the most important question involved in it, namely, whether Hodge's idleness during the hour required for the boiling of the water is a matter of choice or of necessity. It was necessary to leave this out in order to give time the credit of boiling the water. Let us not leave it out, and see what will come of it. If Hodge's idleness is a matter of necessity, it is equivalent, from the economic standpoint, to labor, and counts as labor in the price of the boiling water. A storekeeper may spend only five hours in waiting on his customers, but, as he has to spend another five hours in waiting for them, he gets paid by them for ten hours' labor. His five hours' idleness counts as labor, because, to accommodate his customers, he has to give up what he could produce in those five hours if he could labor in them. Likewise, if Hodge, when boiling the water for Podge, is obliged to spend an hour in idleness, he will charge Podge for the hour in the price which he sets on the boiling water. But it is Hodge himself, this disposi- [21]

tion of himself, and not the abstraction, time, that gives the water its exchangeable value. The abstraction, time, is as truly at work when Hodge is bringing the water from the spring and starting the fire as when he is asleep waiting for the water to boil; yet......would not dream of attributing the value of the water after it had been brought from the spring to the element of time. He would say that it was due entirely to the labor of Hodge. Properly speaking, time does not work at all, but, if the phrase is to be insisted on in economic discussion, it can be admitted only with such qualification as the following: the services of time are venal only when rendered through human forces; when rendered exclusively through the forces of nature, they are gratuitous.

That time does not give the boiling water any exchangeable value becomes still more evident when we start from the hypothesis that Hodge's idleness, instead of being a matter of necessity, is a matter of choice. In that case, if Hodge chooses to be idle, and still tries, in selling the boiling water to Podge, to charge him for his unnecessary idleness, the enterprising Dodge will step up and offer boiling water to Podge at a price lower than Hodge's, knowing that he can afford to do so by performing some productive labor while waiting for the water to boil, instead of loafing like Hodge. The effect of this will be that Hodge himself will go to work productively, and then will offer Podge a better bargain than Dodge has proposed, and so competition between Hodge and Dodge will go on until the price of the boiling water to Podge shall fall to the value of the labor expended by either Hodge or Dodge in bringing the water from the spring and starting the fire. Here, then, the exchangeable value of the boiling water which was said to be due to time has disappeared, and yet it takes just as much time to boil the water as it did in the first place.' 

Some get into difficulty in discussing the question of the increase of capital "simply because they continually lose sight of the fact that competition lowers prices to the cost of production and thereby distributes this so-called product of capital among the whole people." "The banker, who invests little or no capital of his own, and, therefore, lends none to his customers, since the security which they furnish him constitutes the capital upon which he operates, is forced, in the absence of the money monopoly, to reduce the price of his services to labor cost, which the statistics of the banking business show to be much less than one percent. 

As this fraction of one percent represents simply the banker's wages and incidental expenses, and is not a payment for the use of capital, the element of interest disappears from his transaction.”

But, to return to our original comparison, if interest is due to an inadequate volume of money, and if the power of capital to return a profit is due to interest on money, for both of which there is abundant evidence, then interest is unjust and invalid. We suggest that the reader again study the critique of conventional interest theories in the appendix of the work on Money. He will find that they all assume, directly or indirectly, a scarcity of capital. "In the first place it is not true that there is a scarcity of capital-goods, the fact being that such goods can be obtained in abundance without the payment of interest in exchange for mere evidences of debt, provided only that these evidences are acceptable as a medium of exchange. In the second place, the lending of money is not a lending of goods, but merely an exchange of a credit which is [22]

qualified by law or convention to circulate as money for a credit which is not so qualified."

If, after the elimination of monopoly control in the issue of money, and the elimination of all other impediments to free competition in the production and exchange of wealth, interest should still remain, it would be evidence of a relative scarcity of capital-goods. Interest on capitalgoods would then be necessary and unavoidable until the supply of capital-goods equaled the demand. But it would not follow that interest on money would still exist, for money being a credit instrument there is no reason why the supply of money should not equal the effective demand. 

Very few seem to realize the great economic disequilibrium and social harm that interest on money and capital causes. It results, through the very inequitable distribution of income, in the wealth of the country becoming concentrated more and more in a comparatively few hands. It is to these few that producers, needing to expand their operations_and requiring working capital, must turn for financial accommodation. They and the banks are the chief investors and lenders of money. As indebtedness grows, they own, or come to possess liens on, virtually all the property in the country. In many cases the physical improvements for which the debt was contracted have become obsolete or worn out, without the debt having been extinguished. Sinking fund requirements are generally inadequate for retirement of the debt, which at maturity is renewed for another term of years. 

To a considerable extent the interest on this growing indebtedness is not expended for consumption goods by the financial class, but reloaned. Thus much of the interest is compounded. This so greatly increases the total indebtedness that a point is reached beyond which it cannot continue to grow, and the industrial class is depleted of money by the interest flow being no longer returned through loans to the channels of trade. Instead, money accumulates in the hands of the financial class and the amount in circulation becomes insufficient for the liquidation of loans, and the burden of debt can no longer be borne. The only relief is repudiation of debts, bankruptcy, forced liquidation and business depression with unemployment and poverty. 

That is precisely the condition in which the country is in today. 

We have gone through a period when the returns to combinations of capital were unprecedentedly large. The rent of land, the interest on money, and the profits on capital were never so great as they were in the war and post-war years up to 1930. A large part of these returns to capital was reloaned or invested in additional plants and equipment when they were not needed. The indebtedness of the industrial class to the financial class grew, with the compounding of interest, to an unparalleled total. It is greater, according to the best authorities, than the total wealth of the country at deflated prices, and is a constant menace. It retards the resumption of productive activities. It cannot be paid at deflated prices, and at present the government is attempting, by questionable methods and at great risk of failure, to alleviate debtors by the confiscation of creditors through inflation of prices. It would have been wiser and safer to follow the method of nature, by arranging for the reduction of debts, voluntarily by agreements with creditors, or by polit- [ 23 ]

ical pressure, to a level where they were secured; or, as to some of the debts, by foreclosure or bankruptcy proceedings.

VI The harm, both individual and social, that interest, profit and rent do is incalculable. Nevertheless they are stoutly defended as necessary for the maintenance of the present social order. Individuals will not save, it is contended, unless they are rewarded by interest. Such a contention is unreasonable, for one of the objects in saving is for protection in old age or in case of disability, and the lower the interest rate is, the greater naturally will be the amount saved. It is an insult to men's intelligence to say that they will not save unless they obtain interest. But a greater motive, perhaps, for saving is the acquirement of tools and labor-saving devices in order to take advantage of the indirect method of production, which not only saves labor but yields a greater product. The underlying desire of men to gratify their needs with the least exertion prompts them to save and acquire those auxiliaries of labor even if they lack the power of returning a revenue. They would be forced by competition to adopt the most productive method. 

Entrepreneurs must have extraordinary inducements in the shape of large profits, it is contended, or they will not engage in productive enterprises. They are not satisfied with the ordinary and universal reward that comes from production in a free competitive market which is measured by their contribution to production. Profit is the motive today of business men. But if pure interest on money and capital were eliminated there would be no other recourse for them but to continue production and gain what they could in the way of larger wages of superintendence by the application of their ability to reduction of costs below their competitors through new processes and better management. Entrepreneurs in new enterprises would still reap the pioneer's profits until they were eliminated by competition.

But not content with ordinary returns, and to make it possible for extraordinary and unearned returns in rent, interest, and profits, it is necessary to maintain legal privilege which denies freedom of competition. This enables entrepreneurs and the financial class to amass unearned wealth; and to consume more than they produce at the expense of others who consume less than they produce. If it were not for privilege these social parasites would have no place in our economy. Those who receive something for nothing are oblivious, as a rule, of the deplorable consequences flowing from it in the poverty of thousands, and the terrifying fear of poverty that haunts hundreds of thousands more. For if our analysis is sound the one is the correlative of the other. Wealth has been the only distinction of many who are the so-called pillars and saviours of society. The greatest legal robbers are said to be the greatest saviours! Their conspicuous waste has added to their repute. They form an exclusive class of themselves, an "aristocracy of money-bags,' to use Carlyle's expression. Their interests and sympathies are in antagonism to those of the workers. The true aristocracy is not one of wealth or birth, but one of character, intellectuality, achievement, service, with a philosophy, an ideal, a sense of justice. وو [24]

The interest of big business, as well as of those who receive unearned income, is diametrically opposed to the interest of society. The former seek maximum returns for minimum service, while the latter seeks maximum service for minimum returns. It is evident that both cannot thrive together. Unfortunately, and most disturbing, the government, which is supposed to represent society, is in co-operation, and allied, with big business and privileged groups.

VII 

It is asked whether with monopoly abolished "production would increase as rapidly as technological improvements thus keeping the rate of employment quite steady." This enquiry seems to overlook the fact that, with monopoly in existence, the volume of production is the smallest and the number of the unemployed the greatest that have ever been experienced. In the last few years, with the growth of technological improvements, unemployment has steadily increased. 

There are four economic factors necessary for production, namely, labor, land, money, and capital. With the monopoly of land destroyed by taxation of economic rent the margin of cultivation and production would be raised. Land and raw materials now held out of use for speculation and the prevention of competition by others would be accessible to labor and capital. With the monopoly of money also destroyed by an adequate volume of money interest would be eliminated, the marginal cost of production would be lowered, and money would be more accessible to producers. With interest on money eliminated capital would be in greater supply and would no longer bring a return to its owner. Labor, which has been discriminated against by monopoly, would then come into its own.

The way would be cleared of all artificial impediments to a marked increase in production. Thousands who now are debarred would join the ranks of independent producers. Thousands more who now are living on invested incomes would be obliged to become producers. Many thousands of employees who now compete with each other for work would become employers competing with each other for workers. With the marginal cost of production lowered prices of commodities would decline. 

Thus from two influences, greater demand for workers and lower prices, the wages of workers would rise, which would increase the demand for consumption goods. These are some of the logical consequences of the abolition of monopoly. Are they too great to be reasonably expected? 

The cry of "overproduction" has been raised as a ready, but false, excuse for thinking. Even at the zenith of our productive energies in the post-war years, millions of unemployed were walking the streets, huge annual charitable contributions were being made, and millions more, although at work, were unable to buy more than the bare necessities of life. What seemed to be over-production was acute under-consumption. 

It is the workers of various degrees of ability, both of hand and brain, the engineer, the chemist, the technician, the inventor, the executive, the manager, as well as the manual worker, who make the technological improvements and carry on production. They would still be the producers, stimulated and encouraged to greater efforts by receiving, [25]

in addition to their normal pay at present, what the idle capitalists and investors now receive, which is estimated at over 40 percent of the national income. Instead of the advantages of virtually all the technological improvements going to the holders of land and capital, as they do today under monopoly, they would go, under freedom, to the workers. 

Every one would of necessity produce in one way or another, for he who works not neither shall he eat,-save through charity. 

With monopoly abolished, the owners of land, together with the stockholding owners of industrial plants and equipment, the means of transportation, and the agencies of finance, will be in far greater numbers than at present. The new owners will be the workers, and industry will be conducted by them, and not, as at present, by the representatives of the absentee owners on whom Veblen pours out his wrath. As the new owners, in the absence of monopoly, will be unable to realize more in price than the legitimate labor cost of their products, they will be vitally interested in keeping the volume of production as great as possible and prices down, so as to keep their wages up; quite the converse of the efforts of the present absentee owners whose self-interest is in keeping wages down and prices up, so that they may enjoy profits. And to this end they curtail the volume of production below the needs of the community and cause unemployment and poverty. 

To quote from the pamphlet, "with the abolition of monopoly, money and capital would be more plentiful, land and raw materials more readily obtainable and, consequently, the cost of production would be lower. 

New energies would be released and new hopes realized. New employers in large number would engage in production. Wages would rise. Both production and consumption would be greater. For production would no longer be hampered and restricted, as it is now, by government regulation and interference necessary because of monopoly." 

As is well known there is monopoly control of the supplies of the chief raw materials of industry; there is concentration of control by the great industrial, banking, and transportation corporations, a veritable moneyed oligarchy over great stretches of the industrial field, an imperium in imperio. The thesis that we maintain is that these secondary monopolies and quasi-monopolies are the logical consequences, accumulating over many years, of the primary basic monopolies of money and land, and that if it were not for the latter the former could not have developed. 

With the control of industry so concentrated, there is little or no elasticity in economic processes. Supply does not readily respond to changing demand. There is but little tendency to a balance of prices for various products necessary for continuous industrial operation. When technological improvements resulted in the displacement of several millions of workers in 1922-1929, there was no corresponding adjustment of prices to the lower costs and no shortening of working hours, so that full employment could have been maintained and consumers' demand increased to absorb the greater output. Monopoly and the denial of competition prevented this adjustment. The consequence was a crisis. If monopoly had never existed, the stockholding owners would have been thousands of workers, instead of a few absentee idlers, and competition would have brought about a new adjustment [ 26 ]

of supply and demand. As machines tended to displace workers, the hours of labor would have been reduced instead of the number of workers. If as much could be produced, with the aid of machines, in four hours as was produced, without the machines, in eight hours, it would have been to the self-interest of the workers to cut the working time in half and each would have received as much as before. Or some of the workers would have become engaged in supplying new wants. 

The result would have been either the same wages for less hours of work, or higher wages for the same hours of work. Is it not clear that it was privilege giving capitalism the power to exploit that was the ultimate cause of the crisis?

VIII 

The acute depression is the result, largely, of "unbearable disequilibria." There is great lack of balance in prices, some prices having fallen far more than others. When that is the case the economic organism cannot function, for in our system of division of labor, the price of the product of one industry or group of producers supports the price of the products of other industries or groups of producers. A conspicuous example of great disequilibrium of prices is seen in the prices for agricultural and manufactured products. The decline in the former was much greater than in the latter. Agricultural prices fairly collapsed, wheat falling from $2.85 to 40 cents. The result was an almost negligible purchasing power by agricultural producers, which eliminated a large percentage of consumers' demand for manufactured goods, forcing the closing down of plants.

There was, furthermore, a great disequilibrium in the distribution of the national income. In the post-war years 1922-1929 over 42 percent of the national income was paid to capital, which left too small a purchasing power to the great body of workers for wages and salaries to enable them to absorb consumption goods on the market. The capitalists reloaned a considerable part of their income in the investment in new capital-goods, and in financing heavy exports of goods. But it was soon apparent that large amounts of unsold consumption goods were accumulating on the market, and plants had to be closed down and workers discharged. The level of wholesale prices, in the period named, changed but little and remained high. But the cost of production per unit, owing to increased mechanization of industry, had materially declined. The result was abnormally large profits for monopolistic combinations of capital, industrial, banking, and public utilities. When prices rise faster than purchasing power, there is the inevitable result of catastrophe and depression. Owing to the improper and unjust distribution of income there was too much saved and too little spent. If the distribution had been equitable consumption would have been greater, consumers' goods would have been absorbed, and production continued. 

These disequilibria, and others that could be named, were due, primarily, to monopoly which destroyed free and fair competition. The results were, first, the power by favored and powerful interests of fixing prices for some important goods considerably above the cost of production, and curtailing the supply of goods below the needs of the community, [ 27 ]

and second, the very improper and inequitable distribution of income and wealth.

There are two ways of restoring equilibrium in the economic system. 

Not only the ideal and just way, but the normal American way, is by the repeal of all laws that create privilege and monopoly. With privilege abolished production and consumption would tend to be in equilibrium, just as supply would tend to equal demand, for with freedom of competition market prices are constantly tending to bring about an equilibrium and so to preserve the economic system of free exchange. With freedom of competition "the supply of one good in the market constitutes the demand for another good of equal value, and it follows that the total of all supply and the total of all demand are always equal. 

Every demand involves an equal supply, just as every supply involves an equal demand." Moreover with privilege and monopoly abolished the inequitable distribution of wealth would end, and every one would receive in the measure of his personal contribution to production. Cecil 

Rhodes harped on the theory that small things, not big ones, produce the great changes in history. The doing away with interest on money is a small thing, considered in itself, yet it hath a leverage that could change the destiny of humanity.

Unfortunately, the powers that be have taken the other way, involving a partial revolution in the economic system. As one of the ablest of economists said in a recent letter to the writer they "have taken the wrong fork in the road for enduring human welfare." They have made no adequate analysis to determine the true cause of the economic disorder, but are pursuing a policy of merely treating effects, in spite of the fact that the sine qua non of a scientific cure is a treatment of causes. They are introducing a system of planned industry under regulation and coercion by the government, necessitating the fixing of the volume of production, wages, hours of work, and prices, which is an impossible task. It retains privilege, the very cause of the crisis. It perpetuates and guarantees unearned incomes. It endeavors to restore some semblance of a balance in prices by coercion. It is a kind of state-capitalism based on privilege, retaining monopolistic and unfair intra-marginal advantages enjoyed by large capitalistic combinations. 

It is said that the "new economics" is necessary to save our institutions from unlimited greed. But, as we have seen, greed feeds on monopoly and the power to get something for nothing. The way to exterminate greed is to eliminate monopoly, not to preserve it and endeavor to regulate and control the disequilibria that result from it. 

Instead of freedom, we have coercion!

In the daily press the head of the enforcement at Washington of the new industrial planning was quoted as saying in a speech in Boston, "We are still a land of limitless resources. We are still a people of boundless energy. There is no sense in our starving in a land of plenty......The trouble is plainly and simply that producers have not received enough for their output to enable them to buy it back and keep our farms and factories going." But why did producers receive less than they produced? And why did non-producers and idlers get what producers failed to get? These questions he did not answer. Is it [28]

possible that those in high authority, in spite of the want and misery caused by the crisis, are going to permit the continuance of laws that caused the crisis, that give some the power to exploit others, to consume without producing? Is it possible that they dare not abolish special privileges? What valid objection can they find to restoring equality of economic opportunity and equality of human rights? 

IX 

It has been truly said by some one that "the classic purposes of the State, liberty, equality, the career open to the talents, social justice, need a thorough analysis to determine why they leave no real meaning or truth in the modern democratic State." Why is it that every one of these cherished concepts, that are the foundation of our system of free enterprise, has failed?

Only a few obscure and despised students of economics fearlessly make a searching fundamental analysis and boldly advocate freedom. 

They demand the repeal of all laws creating special privileges. With privileges abolished, with rent interest and profits would go tariffs, subsidies, intra-marginal advantages, political perquisites, economic sinecures, all of which "Liberty grinds beneath her heel." They demand equality of economic opportunity and equality of human rights. For only by economic freedom and equality of rights can a civilization that is worth having be preserved. But the trend of the times is away from freedom, and while we shall not see it in our time, we have the satisfaction of knowing that the economic and social malady is the consequence of its denial.

In an industrial regime especially the conflict between different classes of society is for the national income. Each class strives to obtain more than it contributes. The formation of political parties is fundamentally for that purpose. The party in power, representing the dominant class, enacts the laws which control the distribution of income. 

The greatest happiness and prosperity of the greatest number results from a discontinuance of the exploitation by the dominant class. This, in turn, is the result of a greater intelligence by the masses on economic questions; of a balance of power, so to speak; of a demand that every one must have a fair and equal chance, an equal economic opportunity, so that the distribution of income will be in accordance with each one's contribution to production. For all that is valuable in human society depends upon the opportunity for development of the individual. Just how this opportunity is denied, it has been the purpose of these discourses to make clear. The great menace to human society is that the ignorance and apathy and fear of the masses will persist and permit the continuance of a violation of economic law, and a denial of freedom, with the consequent exploitation of the proceeds of their production that will degrade them to the status of a proletarian class. The definition of economics that we like best is the following: "The subject of economics comprises those social relations which arise in the course of the co-operative efforts of men to satisfy their needs and [29]

gratify their desires." While the discourse in these various papers treats of abstract theoretical economics, it has been the endeavor, by fundamental analysis, to restate the economic laws and tendencies through the observance of which production and exchange may be carried on in agreement with sound economic theory, and the social relations of men be made more harmonious and just. There is no other way to make them 

So. In the last sentence of a well known work, a prominent economist speaking of economists in general, says: "It is his confident expectation that men will grow better as the conditions of their economic life become pleasanter; and his belief that they can grow better in no other way is what gives its chief interest to his subject."
