GEORGE WASHINGTON IN SOMERVILLE 

The visits of Washington to that part of Charlestown, now comprising the territory of Somerville, are established by records made by him in his own handwriting, and are supported by the testimony of others who were present and saw him. His visits fall into two groups, those made in 1775, during the investment of Boston by the Continental army, and those made in 1789, during his tour of New England as first President of the United States. Similarly the first group falls naturally into two parts, visits to fortifications already existing when he arrived, and visits to fortifications that he caused to be erected after his arrival. The known facts relating to his presence in Somerville during these periods will be presented in that order.

FORTS BUILT PRIOR TO WASHINGTON'S ARRIVAL 

On July 10, 1775, Washington wrote from Cambridge, Massachusetts, to John Hancock, President of the Contin ental Congress, as follows: 

"I arrived safe at this place on the third instant . . .

Upon my arrival, I immediately visited the several posts occupied by our troops. . . on our side we have thrown up intrenchments on Winter and Prospect Hills... the enemy's camp in full view, at a distance of a little more than a mile. . . ."

Of the thousands who saw Washington there must have been a goodly number who noted that event in their diaries (since that was a day of diaries), or who wrote home about it. It appears, however, that these diarists were more interested in events that were occurring than they were in personalities. It must be remembered that Washington had yet to become a famous man. Undoubtedly, to the New England countrymen, such local heroes as Putnam were of much greater interest than the comparatively unknown Virginian. [3]

Nevertheless, two of these diarists are definitely helpful. 

Paul Lunt, a soldier from Newburyport, kept a journal of his experiences that has been preserved. In camp on Prospect 

Hill he writes as follows: "Sunday, July 2nd, 1775. General Washington came into camp. "Monday, 3rd (July) Turned out early in the morning, got in readiness to be reviewed by the general. New orders given out by General Washington." 

Noah Chapin, Jr., of Somers, Connecticut, Ensign in Willes' company, Spencer's (2nd Conn.) regiment, kept a diary, the unpublished original of which is in the Connecticut State 

Library. He says: 

"1775. July 2 this day about 11 o'c (lock) Genrel Washington & Genrel Lee with several other gentlemen arrived at Cambridge and in the afternoon they Road out to the line of forts at Prospect Hill in Charlestown." 

It will be noted that both these men set the date of Washington's arrival a day earlier than he himself dates it. Apparently Washington made a mistake in the date when writing about it a week later. Historians are in agreement that July 2 is correct. The clause of his letter essential to our inquiry is this: "I immediately visited the several posts occupied by our troops." Ensign Chapin, in his diary entry quoted above, confirms this visit on the afternoon of July 2. 

We are therefore warranted in accepting the date, July 2, as the one upon which Washington made his visit of inspection to "the several posts occupied by our troops." 

In General Orders, Headquarters, Cambridge, July 16, 1775, Washington issued the following order: "It was with much surprise and concern that the General, in passing along the New Hampshire lines yesterday, observed most wanton, mischevous, and unprofitable abuse of property, in the destruction of many valuable trees, which were standing along the side of the road, out of the way of our works or guns; he therefore orders that an effective stop be put to such practices for the [4]

future, or severe punishment will fall upon the transgressors of this order."

In General Orders Washington was accustomed to refer to himself as "the General", instead of using the personal pronoun.

Since the New Hampshire troops were stationed on Winter Hill, to which they retired after the battle of Bunker Hill, this order definitely places Washington there on July 15, 1775. The Winter Hill fort was one of the strongest "of the several posts occupied by our troops" that Washington says he inspected on July 2.

We have now clearly established the presence of Washington upon certain dates at Prospect and Winter hills. We can go further. In addition to the military posts already mentioned we can identify four others as then in existence, and therefore among the "several posts" Washington says he inspected. The hills of Somerville on the tops and on the 

Boston-ward slopes were covered with minor outworks, and numerous trenches constructed for the purpose of communication between major fortifications, and of obstructing the approach of assaulting troops, all of which Washington must have viewed and some of which he must have traversed. Disregarding these secondary works, let us consider the four remaining posts under occupation by the Continental troops on the day of his inspection.

They were: the French redoubt, the White House redoubt, the Ten Hills Farm redoubt and a redoubt at the foot of 

Prospect Hill known as Fort No. 3. 

The French redoubt was situated on the northwest peak of Prospect Hill, now Central Hill. The rear of the redoubt abutted upon the present School Street, already in 1775 an established highway connecting Somerville Avenue and Broadway. The street and the location of the redoubt are clearly shown on Pelham's military map of 1775.* Trumbull's sketch, though smaller and less detailed, is in general 

[5]

agreement. The summit of the hill must have been fully occupied by the redoubt and its outworks. The trenches connecting the redoubt with the Citadel on the other peak of the hill survived until recent years. The site of the main work is now occupied by the City Hall and the High School. 

The location of the White House redoubt has been the subject of discussion. It is possible, however, to locate it within narrow limits. In 1824, only a generation after the Revolution, John Finch made a study of revolutionary fortifications, the results of which he published in the American Journal of Science, Vol. 8, p. 341. He visited the remains of the Somerville forts and made a careful examination of them. Of the redoubt situated between Winter and Prospect hills, he says, it "has been completely carried away and a quarry has been opened on the spot. In the general orders, issued at 

Cambridge, guards were directed to be stationed at White House redoubt, and this I believe was the post intended. General Lee is said to have had his headquarters in a farm house immediately in the rear of this redoubt."

In the diaries of Continental soldiers there are numerous references to standing guard at the White House redoubt. 

The quarry operations, noted by Finch, long ago leveled the ledge, and succeeding building activities eventually obliterated its remains. Fortunately there are men still living who can remember the old quarry pits, and are definitely able to locate them within the quadrangle bounded by Bonair and 

Walnut streets and Wellington avenue. Moreover, Pelham's map, though lacking modern street locations, confirms the identity of this site.

The headquarters of General Lee, referred to by Finch, is, of course, the Oliver Tufts house still standing on Sycamore street. This would be not far back of the redoubt, and its elevation and isolation would make it a landmark. If it could be shown that this house was painted white, as was so often the case, the source of the name of the redoubt would become obvious. [Continued on Page 11] [6]

SOMERVILLE SECTION OF PELHAM'S MAP 

Pelham's map, a facsimile of which is in the possession of the Somerville Public 

Library, was made in 1775 by Henry Pelham, a civil engineer, by direction of British Headquarters in Boston. It gives topographical, military and civil features of Boston and environs, roughly including the territory from Alewife brook on the north to Squantum Neck on the south, and from Chelsea on the east to Brookline meeting house on the west.

The military works of the Somerville sector are clearly shown. The map is 38 by 27 inches in size. Only a few copies have survived, and it is a collector's rarity. 

This reproduction is from a tracing and omits the numerous gun ranges, and such minor features as trees, fences and contour shadings. [E 7]

SOMERVILLE SECTION OF TRUMBULL'S SKETCH 

Trumbull's General Sketch of the lines at Charlestown, Prospect Hill and Roxbury has never been published entire. In manuscript original it is preserved in the Papers of the Continental Congress, Letters of George Washington, No. 152, Vol. 1, Folios 74-75. 

The Somerville Public Library has a photo stat copy. In general this map did for Washington what Pelham's map did for the British, though on a smaller scale and with little detail of terrain and civil features. It is an interesting and valuable contemporary source for the study of the siege of Boston, and confirms the conclusions of this investigation. 

John Trumbull, the draftsman, was for a short time on Washington's personal staff. [10]

The two remaining important posts in the line of forts that made up the Somerville sector of siegeworks were Fort No. 3, and Ten Hills Farm redoubt, both of which are shown by Pelham.

Fort No. 3 was situated at the foot of Prospect Hill in the area south of, and near, Union Square. It commanded Prospect street and Miller's creek.

Ten Hills Farm redoubt was some distance below the big bend of the Mystic river, opposite the junction of School street with Broadway. It commanded the river approach and effectively prevented a flank attack by British warships. 

Cannon fire from this redoubt caused the withdrawal of 

British floating batteries.

FORTS BUILT SUBSEQUENT TO WASHINGTON'S ARRIVAL 

We come finally to Ploughed Hill and Cobble Hill. Neither can be included among those that Washington said he visited on July 2, since they had not then been fortified. Ploughed 

Hill was nearest of all to the British lines, and was frequently under fire. The advanced trenches of the British were pushed out from Charlestown to the foot of the hill, and some apprehension was felt that they might seize it. In anticipation of such a move, and pending its final occupation by the American army, a strong picket guard of Colonials was maintained on the hill at night. The fort on this hill was hastily thrown up on the night of August 26, 1775, by a force of a thousand or more men with tools, supported by three thousand troops, all under the command of General Lee. There are numerous contemporary records in general agreement as to these essential facts. Unfortunately for our purpose no mention is made of Washington. With Lee, a trained soldier, in charge, Washington's presence was ob viously unnecessary. No record has been found that offers evidence, either direct or inferential, of the presence of Washington at Ploughed Hill at any time. We can only guess that he may have been there, and probably was there at some time to inspect the fort. It would be only a guess,. [11]

however, for there is not the slightest warrant for accepting it as historically established. 

Under date of November 27, 1775, Washington wrote to

Richard Henry Lee from camp at Cambridge as follows: 

"Dear Sir: Your favor of the 13th, with the enclosure, for which I thank you, came to this place on Wednesday evening; (Nov. 22,) part of which, that is, the night, I was engaged with a party of men throwing up a work upon a hill called Cobble Hill, which, in case we should ever be supplied with such things as we want, may prove useful to us, and could not be delayed, as the earth is getting as hard as a rock.”

Cobble Hill, also known as *Miller's Hill, afterward the site of the McLean Asylum, has been completely leveled, and is now a railroad yard. Its location was within the roughly 

U-shaped enclosure made by the tracks of the Boston & 

Lowell, the Fitchburg and the Grand Junction railroads.

This is the scene of one of the personal activities of Washington.

WASHINGTON'S SECOND VISIT TO SOMERVILLE 

We now come to the question of Washington's presence in Somerville during his New England tour in 1789. 

On Thursday, Oct. 29, 1789, Washington made the following entry in his diary: "Left Boston about 8 o'clock. Passed over the Bridge at

Charlestown, and went to see that at Malden, but proceded to the college at Cambridge, attended by the Vice- 

President, (John Adams) Mr. Bowdoin, and a great number of gentlemen." 

And further: "After leaving Cambridge, at the distance of four miles, we passed through Mystick." 

*Archibald Robertson, Lieutenant-General of the Royal Engineers, who joined Gates in Boston on November 9, 1775, writes as follows in his Private Journal, "20th November or there about the Rebels began their Work on Miller's hill within 1400 Yds. of the Citadel and 1000 from our Post at the Mill. As they had advanced their work so far the first Night and occupied the Summit of the hill so well it was thought not proper to fire upon them considering the then state of our works and the Camp exposed to insult." [12]

Washington made no mention of the country roads through. which he had to pass in going to Cambridge. But for a part of the way through Somerville we know exactly how he went. 

There was only one way. He must have gone across Charlestown Neck to the present Sullivan Square, and traversed Washington street at least as far as the junction of Prospect street. At this point he had the option of turning into Prospect street, and going past the house of Ralph Inman, the Tory, through the present Inman Square, and thence to the college. What is more likely, he could continue along Washington street through Union Square and Kirkland Street. 

Both routes were established arteries of travel, and both are shown on Pelham's map. The very name of Washington 

Street suggests that it was the route he followed. As far as 

Prospect Street the evidence is clear. From that point the route is open to conjecture, with the probabilities strongly favoring Union Square and Kirkland Street. 

On the trip from Cambridge to Mystick (Medford) Washington was obliged to traverse Somerville territory again, but he had the choice of several routes. There is no evidence as to which one he actually did follow. The most direct would have been via Massachusetts and College avenues to 

Main Street, Medford.

To recapitulate; it may be regarded as established by contemporary evidence that Washington was present in Somerville at the following points on the dates named. 

1. The Citadel on Prospect Hill, July 2, 1775. 

2. The French redoubt on the northwest peak of Prospect Hill, now known as Central Hill, July 2, 1775. 

3. The fort on Winter Hill at the junction of Broadway and Main Streets, July 2 and 15, 1775. 

4. The White House redoubt within the quadrangle bounded by Walnut and Bonair streets and Welling. ton Avenue, July 2, 1775. 

5. Ten Hills Farm redoubt on Temple's farm, July 2, 1775. [13]

6. Fort No. 3 in the angle between the present Union 

Square and Prospect Street, July 2, 1775. 

7. Cobble Hill in the area enclosed by the tracks of the 

Boston & Lowell, the Fitchburg and the Grand Junction railroads, Nov. 22, 1775. 

8. Along Washington Street from Sullivan Square to 

Prospect Street, Oct. 29, 1789.

HISTORIC HOUSES 

Tradition also has it that Washington frequented or visited certain houses within Somerville territory. He must have; that he did not, would be almost inconceivable. Several of his generals were quartered here. He must have stopped at their headquarters on his tours of inspection for purposes of consultation, shelter, rest and refreshment. Even if these reasons did not compel such visits, the most ordinary dictates of courtesy to his staff, and considerations of the mili tary morale of the leaders, would make such measures a paramount necessity. Unfortunately there appears to be no contemporary record of such an act, nor even a reference from which a fair inference may be drawn. Historical confirmation awaits the discovery of evidence as yet unknown. 

Traditions, only, persist. They have their place and their value in the common lore of the times, but they are not history; only its shadow. In the picture of the past they provide the dim background against which recorded facts stand out as the features of the historical landscape. 

The historic houses of Somerville have ample reason for preservation and for marking on the basis of the known facts about them, which need no embroidery. The venerable associations of the ancient battle ground upon which later generations dwell nobly serve to enrich the peaceful activities of the present day. [14]
