PREFACE 

The material in this book has been arranged so that it may be used with any chemistry text. It is the first attempt which has been made to correlate major understandings, scientific methods of thinking, and character traits. The author has used other methods, but finds that this plan reduces failures. The plan retains the good features of the daily assignment and at the same time gives the pupil an over-view of the entire unit. By organizing his own material and budgeting the work, the pupil is given a sense of direction and is stimulated to develop systematic study habits. The work has been disorganized in some units to enable the pupil to fit the pieces together and see the whole pattern. This is not too difficult for high school pupils. Before preparing lectures or using the book, teachers are urged to read Downing "An Introduction to the Teaching of Science" 1934, University of 

Chicago Press.

The experiments have been written to stimulate the pupil to think scientifically. In the author's experience, pupils rarely read the objective of an experiment. The "cook book" chemist of hazy ideas is too frequently found in beginning classes. Writing his own objective trains the pupil to read through the experiment. 

This is one of the few attempts which have been made to train pupils directly, through experimentation and class exercises, consciously to use the scientific method.

The author wishes to thank R. W. Abbott for painstaking checking, R. D. Shouse, and W. Sawyer for helpful criticism, and M. Herring for suggestions and secretarial work. The diagrams were prepared by a pupil, Mary Jane Beggs. 

On request to the author, lectures will be mailed. September 3, 1935. E.M.J.L.

INTRODUCTION 

One morning Becquerel, a French scientist, walked into a dark room which he used for developing X-ray and photographic plates. By accident he had placed a piece of uranium ore upon a sensitive photographic plate which had been lying on a table. On removing the ore, he was surprised to find that the plate had been changed exactly where the ore had rested. Becquerel could not understand how this could have happened. His scientific curiosity was aroused. Why had the plate been changed? With all the precision of a trained investigator, he set about the job of discovering the cause for such an effect. 

Sample after sample of uranium ore was tested. In every case a spot was left on the plate. What was in the ore to produce such an effect? Becquerel, his interest now at a high pitch, analyzed the ore and found a mineral which was much more powerful than its uranium content could account for. 

He said that to affect a photographic plate, there must be another element with power many times greater than that of uranium itself. This element was obtained from the mineral pitchblende. Becquerel needed help. This work had possibilities. 

He searched his laboratories for a student who would be worthy of such a task. He chose. Marie Curie, a Polish girl. Marie was eager for an opportunity to do this experimental work. Marie worked for days over tons of ore and steaming cauldrons. 

Then, in all humbleness, came the great report to the world of the discovery of radium. Imagine the joy that came to Becquerel and to Marie!

TRAITS OF A SCIENTIST 

The field of chemistry is what it is today largely because of the character of the people who have worked in it and made the discoveries. Certain traits mark the scientist.

One trait you will study in the first unit is joy in making discoveries. 

Berzelius, a Swedish chemist, always felt a great deal of pleasure on making a new observation. With beaming eyes, he would call to his students, "Well, boys, I have found something interesting."

This same Berzelius, as a boy, wrote, "One day I was making fuming nitric acid and noticed some gas escaping. I collected it over water in bottles to find out what gas it was. I suspected oxygen, and seldom have I had a moment of such pure and heartfelt joy as when the glowing splint placed in the gas burst into flame and lighted up my dark laboratory."* 

Many of the great chemists started in some dingy room performing experiments from some old chemistry book bought second hand. This is significant because a pupil never becomes a master of the subject until, by doing it, he has made the work of the great masters his own. "Pin pricks" of knowledge and "close-up" attitudes should be avoided if pupils wish to keep their perspective. If you should attend a movie and see only "closeups", you would soon grow tired of the picture. Pupils often perform experiments and work exercises, but never take the time to see how the bit of knowledge works into an understanding of the whole fabric of the unit. This leads to a feeling of frustration or boredom. Watch for a "close-up" attitude. Feel the joy and satisfaction that comes from having made a discovery for yourself. 

In the second unit, the emphasis will be on another trait, namely, respectfulmindedness. In the course of his work, a scientist must purposefully observe and appreciate the unfolding of life about him. When the chemist Moseley, who first opened up the work on the inner structure of the atom, gave up his work to enter the World War, his letters to his mother were full of cheer even though he was suffering from the hardships of the campaign in the Dardanelles. In the third unit, you will learn that a scientist perseveres. courage and hope are qualities that drive a scientist doggedly on. 

Marie Curie is one you will enjoy reading in "Crucibles" by Jaffe. and indomitable will of this woman is one of the strong, beautiful milestones along the road of chemical progress. Years after the great discovery of radium, Marie 

Curie remarked, "We lived in a preoccupation as complete as that of a dream. 

When the cold was so intense that they could no longer continue their work, she would brew some tea and draw closer to the cast-iron stove. The bitter winter of 1896 came and found the mad couple still laboring in their hangar."* 

Marie broke under the terrific strain. She was in bed three months with pneumonia. Pierre, her husband, was broken with fatigue at the end of each day's work. In spite of this, the search went on. They worked for hours over their boiling cauldrons. Scientists see things through and persevere. 

In your fourth unit, you will see that a true scientist is honest. Pope has written, "An honest man's the noblest work of God." Dishonesty is just our desire to escape from reality, by putting honest thoughts from the mind. A fine botanist refused to allow a laboratory partner to walk through a raging snow storm for two miles to take a simple reading for him. The botanist went himself, because it would have been dishonest for a student to record the laboratory findings for him. Dishonesty is the result of a lack of self-reliance. The scientist must rely entirely on himself for results. 

No one else can do his thinking for him.

Consecration to work is a mark of the scientist which you will study in the fifth unit. The Englishman Cavendish, one of the richest men of his time, never owned but one suit of clothes at a time. He could have entered politics and have led the normal life of a nobleman. He preferred, however, to live as a recluse, and could be found among his flasks and tubes. Some of the great scientists of the world have kept young at heart by wonder- Scientific curiosity is a valuable scientific trait that you will study in Unit Six. Dr. Park, for years the health commissioner of New York City, went to the platform at a great scientific meeting and faced his audience to make an address. A murmur swept over the audience. He was an old young man. His face was radiant in spite of his advanced years. Someone said, "There is a grand old man. He has never grown old."

There is a secret in keeping young. Part of the secret is cultivating the company of young children and youth. As a child, the chemist Langmuir asked many questions. Clerk-Maxwell, at three years of age, kept asking, "Show me how it does," "Why does water turn to ice?" "Why does water boil in a kettle?" "Why does rain fall?" Children have a natural and intense curiosity. They wonder and keep young at heart.

In Unit Seven you will learn that the scientific approach demands unselfishness. Some of the kindest men have been scientists. The great scientists have always been willing to lay down their lives for humanity. Consider the courage and absolute disregard for themselves marking the men who have paved the way by using themselves as guinea pigs in the great fight against disease. Many of these men were chemists, not doctors. Those who became doctors were first, in most cases, trained chemists. Many chemists have pursued the investigation of fluorine to lose their lives, their lungs horribly affected by the gas. Many of these men and women stuck doggedly to their posts even though injuries had occurred. True scientists are unselfish.

During your study of Unit Eight on Organic Chemistry, there should be ample opportunity for you to investigate, at first hand, the joy of working, and of getting your hands dirty. Few chemists escape the experience.

Modern Ideas of Atomic Structure 

In your work on the first unit, you learned that the atom was composed of a nucleus of positive and negative charges and planetary negatrons. Most of your reference books and your text refer to electrons instead of negatrons. 

We shall use the newer word, negatron. It is probably safe to say that things in the universe are energy. Atoms can no longer be thought of as being made of protons, negatrons, neutrons, positrons, and deutrons. Neutrons of mass two, neutrinos, and negative protons may also be present but of this scientists are not sure. 

The weight of the atom is concentrated in a relatively small space near the center of the atom. The negatrons in the nucleus are called binding negatrons. 

When the nucleus is bombarded, neutrons are released. 

The exact composition of the

64. neutron is still problematical and not positively known. The nucleus also contains protons.

The planetary negatrons traveling around the nucleus on orbits are no longer single units. The planetary negatron is composed of a positively charged body or positron and a negatively charged body or electron. This may also be true of the negatrons in the nucleus. 

Read the Lewis-Langmuir Theory in the text. 

Certain combinations of negatrons and protons are stable. Some atomic combinations are so unstable that they have never been isolated; thus there are varying degrees of stability. 

List three essential points in the Lewis-Langmuir Theory: 1. 2. 3. 

Atoms of the same element have the same number of and

The nuclear units (possibly neutrons) may vary in mass, however, giving rise to isotopes. Atoms of two different elements will not have the same number of protons and negatrons in both atomic systems. The greatest number of negatrons on the first shell outside the nucleus is two. The greatest number on the second layer is eight. For limits of subsequent shells, see the Lewis-Langmuir 

Theory. Atoms become stable by sharing or gaining 

Chemists used to think that the negatrons corresponded with the corners of a cube. The nucleus was inside the cube. Now scientists know that the negatrons pair or group themselves.

This takes place even though they bear the same charge, because the motion of the negatrons and the nucleus sets up magnetic fields. The magnetic fields are responsible for the grouping.

When two or more atoms unite, they form molecules. The outer negatron layer of the atom is called the valence layer. The negatrons shared or gained in a chemical reaction are called negatrons. The rest of the atom including planetary negatrons and the nucleus is called the "kernal".

HYDROGEN ION CONCENTRATION* 

The growing industrial importance of hydrogen ion concentration necessitates a presentation of pH, its meaning and uses, to a class in beginning chemistry. You will recall from your past work that a strong acid was one that had the most water in it. Such an acid is highly dissociated or broken up so that hydrogen protons or hydrogen ions are present in the solution. The stronger the acid the more electrically charged protons of hydrogen there will be in the solution. This is also true of bases, except that bases associate protons. If sodium hydroxide is diluted, hydroxyl ions will be formed, and the sodium hydroxide will be stronger. Usually in nature and in industry there are acids and bases together in substances so that there are hydrogen and hydroxyl ions present when substances are in solution. Study carefully Experiment 21. You will recall that you first added acid to the flask and then put in a drop or so of an indicator. Very carefully you added alkalie to the flask until a faint pink color was observed. 

This was the "end point." If the experiment was performed with care, you found that at this faint color change, you had added just as much N/10 acid as alkalie. This neutral point is called pH7. At this point there are just as many hydrogen as hydroxyl ions in the solution. As the numbers go down from seven the hydrogen ions increase in concentration over the hydroxyl ions. 

Certain indicators used in the series show solutions to be acid. If the pH goes above seven, indicators or dyes used show a basic reaction, because there are more hydroxyl ions present.

APPENDIX LABORATORY MANAGEMENT 

The experienced teacher knows how to order supplies and is familiar with the countless other details of laboratory management. This section has been added for practice teachers.

Tables of physical constants have been omitted, because most chemistry texts are well supplied with them. Manuals of physical and chemical constants with a host of valuable data may be purchased for a very small price. Slightly antiquated editions are often offered for sale at bargain prices. These manuals are quite satisfactory for high school use.

Teachers employed for the first time are advised to consult the requisition list of supplies from the previous year as a guide. In making out requisitions, it is advisable to indicate in your specifications the size and quality of equipment to be used. Catalogues may be obtained free of charge from the various supply houses. 

Glancing through these catalogues is a valuable experience. You will learn the names of various types of apparatus that you have probably not worked with before.
