But Mr. Roosevelt objects to destructive criticism. And if he refers to those who have been weighed in the balances and found wanting, and who still offer no solution for our problem except the suggestion of a return to the fossilized formulas that brought us where we are, he may be justified. And however sharp my criticism of present or past policies may be, it is accompanied by what I am perfectly convinced is a practicable plan for restoration and maintenance of prosperity. 

Unfortunately the merits of this plan are not so "self-evident" as with those of the Townsend variety. However it is neither a Chinese Puzzle nor an Einstein

I would suggest, in this connection, that the reader give repeated consideration to this question, as containing the key to the whole problem: What would be the effect, and why, if someone would point out a way by which every unemployed person could produce one ounce of gold per week by honest labor? EXPOSITION 

The subject of money is a tough one; and this exposition is intended only as a skeleton outline. It would have been easy to render each separate point clear by means of extended explanation and illustration; but the process would have defeated its own purpose by leaving the reader "unable to see the town for the houses". The character of money might be compared to the architectural character of a building: a correct conclusion as to architectural character may be reached promptly by viewing a building as a whole; while it may be slow and difficult, though it certainly is possible, to reach the same conclusion by means of calculations made only from the size and location of each separate piece of material in the building. And only common sense and common knowledge are necessary for arriving at a correct general conclusion concerning this plan; but it is an intricate and difficult matter to build up the premises that lead to that conclusion. Accordingly, I wrote this plan, and stated its general effects, in perhaps two hours; while I have worked for about two years in preparing this exposition; yet an analysis of the cause and cure of depression was compulsory to me in a presentation of the plan. 

Therefore I purpose to show, in the briefest space that admits of clearness, that our economic breakdown lies in our currency system rather than in some other part of our economic machinery; that the inability of gold or gold base currency to maintain prosperity lies in its inherent nature, and is not any accidental thing; that no other medium of exchange that has been used or generally considered is capable of maintaining prosperity; that the plan set forth in this pamphlet will permanently solve our difficulty; and that the application of that plan will not create evils comparable with those that it will correct. 

We are told that this depression is due to over-production—that there is too much food, while millions of our able bodied people are unable to earn enough money to avoid hunger; that there is too much clothing, while millions of our industrious people are unable to earn enough money to avoid raggedness; that our utilities are overexpanded, while millions of our best people are unable to earn enough money to obtain their services. If that is sense then I belong beneath a slab on which is inscribed the epitaph, "He Outlived His Faculties".

MANY MILLIONS OF US COULD AND WOULD USE MORE FOOD, CLOTHING, SERVICES, ETC., IF WE HAD THE MONEY TO BUY THEM. 

AND WE COULD AND WOULD EARN THE MONEY TO BUY THEM BY PRODUCING THEIR EQUIVALENT IN VALUE IN OUR OWN OCCUPA- TIONAL LINE IF WE COULD OBTAIN CONSTRUCTIVE WORK. AND ORDINARY ECONOMIC MOTIVES WILL FURNISH THE WORK IF THE MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE FUNCTIONS PROPERLY. Men, facilities, and resources that are now idle would be producing useful goods, and exchanging them for the useful products of other men, facilities, and resources that are now idle, as they did in the most prosperous times, except for paralysis of the medium of exchange. 

By way of illustration: if Jones can produce a large surplus of food, but no clothing nor fuel; and if Brown can produce a large surplus of clothing, but no food nor fuel; and if Smith can produce a large surplus of fuel, but no food nor clothing, then all will have ample food, clothing, and fuel as long as they have an effective medium of exchange. But if they are barred from barter, as we are in the modern economic world; and if their medium of exchange fails to function, then, regardless of the cause of that failure, Jones must go ragged and cold, or consume his reserves of clothing and fuel, though he may be able to produce food for a city; Brown must go hungry and cold, or consume his reserves of food and fuel, though he may be able to produce clothing for an army; and Smith must go hungry and ragged, or consume his reserves of food and clothing, though he may be able to produce fuel for a navy. 

Herein lies the difference between poverty and depression: if Jones, Brown, and

Smith lose out on production they are headed for poverty; but if they lose out on mutual exchange they are in the grip of depression-they can produce their usual surplus, but they can reap no benefit from it. 

Now it certainly is possible to have unbalanced production—too much of some things in comparison with others, or rather, too little of the others. But when men undertake to tell us that there is an over-production of useful commodities in general they are either lunatics or criminals. They are simply telling us that the richer we become the poorer we are. And when they tell us that the right way to balance production is deliberately to reduce production of the items on the heavy side they are little better. DID THEY EVER THINK OF STEPPING-UP PRODUCTION OF THE ITEMS ON THE LIGHT SIDE? If we can "cure the evil of want in the midst of plenty by destroying the plenty" then I would suggest that we proceed to cure the social evil by hanging all the women. If there is any such thing as a plan in deliberately reducing production it is a plan equivalent to that of cremating a sick man in order to rid him of disease germs. 

But the only important unbalance of this depression has been the unbalance of commodities in general with money. Otherwise let someone tell us what commodities held their own in money price. What then? Simply this, that we must have either a superfluity of commodities in general, or an inadequate money system. To assume that we can have a superfluity of commodities in general while millions of our people stand in desperate need of them solely because of their inability to obtain useful employment at profitable compensation is conclusive evidence of mental unbalance; so we must have an inadequate money system-a medium of exchange incapable of fulfilling its purpose.

In the utter confusion of thought that has prevailed concerning the subject various other causes have been assigned for this depression. BUT NOT ONE OF THEM 

EXPLAINS THAT APPARENTLY IMPOSSIBLE CONTRADICTION-CAPI- TAL SEEKING INVESTMENT, FACILITIES SEEKING OPERATION, AND RESOURCES SEEKING USE, SIDE BY SIDE WITH MANY MILLIONS OF DESPERATELY NEEDY MEN SEEKING WORK. 

The poor are inclined to ascribe the depression to the deliberate machinations of the rich. Bunk. Depression injures the rich along with the poor. Capital, facilities, and resources have endured enforced idleness as surely as men have. 

The rich are inclined to ascribe the depression to the extravagance of the poor during prosperity. More bunk. Few of us got so pot-gutted during prosperity as to render us unfit for work; and lack of profitable work, not lack of wealth, is what constitutes depression.

Some ascribe the depression to deliberate tie-up by the banks. Still more bunk. 

Generally speaking, depression brings grief, not benefit, to banks; and bankers are partly human-they have no desire to cut their own throats. 

Many ascribe the depression to the World War. Why not ascribe it to Napoleon? 

War could produce poverty; but it is forced idleness, not poverty, that ails us. 

Recently the depression has been traced to debts. It may yet be traced to sunspots. Debt is a bad thing; but how can it force us into idleness when we have ample capital, facilities, resources, and men? 

If an automobile collides with a building, solely because of the lack of brakes on the automobile, we do not place the blame on, nor state the cause of the accident to be, the existence of the building, although that particular accident could not have occurred if the building had not existed. This depression could not have occurred if there had been no debt, and probably not if there had been no war; but neither debt nor war were the cause of the depression. Which one of these assigned causes could have produced this depression if gold had been producible by honest labor at the rate of an ounce per week per man; or if the plan set forth on page two had been in operation?

THE SOLE CAUSE OF THIS DEPRESSION WAS A FORM OF MONEY INCAPABLE OF MEETING THE MONEY-DEMAND. GIVE US A SOUND MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE, THE PRODUCTION OF WHICH DEPENDS ONLY ON CONSTRUCTIVE EFFORT, AND THE PROSPERITY OF 1929 WILL BE EXCEEDED WITHIN A YEAR; AND IT WILL BE PERMANENT. 

It is well understood that money is a standard and official medium of exchange. 

Business cannot be efficiently transacted by means of barter. We cannot profitably trade wheat for automobiles, nor cotton for furniture. Money must perform the functions of a medium of exchange for these and all other commodities. ALL MOD- 

ERN BUSINESS AND PROSPERITY IS BASED ON THE SALE OF THE PRODUCTS OF EACH MAN OR UNIT OF MEN FOR MONEY, AND THEIR PURCHASE OF THE PRODUCTS OF OTHER MEN OR UNITS OF MEN WITH MONEY. 

The functioning of money depends solely on the confidence of those accepting it that its full buying power will continue. And consequently any money, however unsound, will function while confidence holds. Cardboard money would do it. But since perfect confidence could hardly be created, and could not possibly be sustained, in regard to unsound money, it is absolutely essential to permanent prosperity that money be given the soundest and most stable form that is humanly possible. 

The soundest and most stable form of money conceivable is either a properly adapted commodity, the actual intrinsic value of which is the amount for which it is accepted as money; or, an authoritative certificate of the ownership of such commodity. Our gold coins were an example of the one form, and our gold certificates were an example of the other form. Any other form of money involves the factor of economic ability, as well as that of integrity; whereas this form reduces all uncertainty concerning money to the single factor of integrity of government; and human limitations forestall any advance beyond that. 

Gold was originally a commodity like any other commodity; but it was a commodity outstanding in the qualities of being valuable, durable, transportable, and regular in quantity, to a greater degree than any other generally known and distributed commodity; so it naturally became generally accepted for the purpose to which it was so admirably adapted-a medium of exchange for all other commodities, or money.

For the limited transactions of early times gold itself was well suited to the functions of money. But increasing transactions rendered the actual transfer of the metal inconvenient and undesirable; so the expedient of paper money was adopted; the fundamental idea being to issue certificates in exchange for gold, and store the gold, and redeem the certificates by delivering the gold in exchange for them when demanded.

Paper money is much more convenient than gold, and is perfectly satisfactory in its functions as a substitute for the metal, as long as it is backed by a one hundred per cent reserve. But if paper money is backed by a one hundred per cent gold reserve then it necessarily follows that, whatever convenience the paper may carry over the metal, it cannot add anything to the volume of it. 

AND FOR MANY DECADES THE RATE OF PRODUCTION OF GOLD HAS BEEN MORE AND MORE RAPIDLY FALLING BEHIND THE RATE OF INCREASE IN BUSINESS TRANSACTIONS REQUIRING THE USE OF MONEY, WITH THE RESULT THAT GOLD, OR PAPER WITH ONE HUN- DRED PER CENT GOLD RESERVE, CANNOT EVEN APPROXIMATELY MEET THE REQUIREMENTS OF BUSINESS FOR A MEDIUM OF EX- 

CHANGE. (I have stated that business transactions requiring the use of money have increased enormously faster than the supply of gold has increased, and not that commodities have increased thus. As an illustration: I probably eat less bread than my

grandfather ate; so the per capita consumption of bread may have declined. But how about the business transactions requiring the use of money that are necessary to get the bread to my table? My grandfather probably grew his wheat on homesteaded land; he paid for his labor by exchange labor; his wheat was ground at the mill for toll; his bread was baked by his family's labor, by heat from wood secured by his labor. For other ingredients he probably paid an infinitesimal amount of money. Is that practicable now? The farmer's investment and expense are such that he must have money for his wheat; and the transporting agency, the milling concern, the wholesale company, the baker, and the retailer are under the same necessity of receiving money. This factor of increased specialization can scarcely be overestimated in comparing the present with the past. We have simply outgrown our money system. But it is idle to talk of returning to an economy in which an individual produces what he consumes, for every general step in that direction is a step backward from prosperity and high living standards.)

Subterfuges, such as the reduction of the gold reserve for paper money, were long ago devised to overcome the inadequacy of gold as a medium of exchange. But the principal thing resorted to has been the substitute for money, credit. 

Our past periods of prosperity have admittedly existed in spite of the money inadequacy of which we complain; and credit, acting as a substitute for money, has rendered this possible. Credit can act as a medium of exchange while general confidence in it is maintained; but it is not and cannot be a sound and stable medium of exchange; for it depends largely on the economic ability of millions of individuals, and on several other uncertain factors, instead of on the single point of integrity of government.

When confidence in credit weakens people instinctively turn to money itself, or to fixed money values, as being the safest security against loss that may arise from forced liquidation or from price decline. Money being utterly inadequate to meet the demand for confidence-producing security, it promptly obtains an entirely artificial value in relation to commodities, forcing commodities down in money price without respect to their intrinsic values. The fall in commodity prices fans the flame of economic conflagration by bankrupting those who are caught with money liabilities and commodity assets, and by rendering general business unprofitable and general investment unsafe. When the process has largely paralyzed the activity of money, and practically destroyed the credit that had served as an overgrown substitute for money, stagnation inevitably prevails. 

By 1929 credit in its various forms had been expanded until it had produced extravagant expenditures, fallacious investments, and wild-cat speculation in nearly all lines. A national frenzy had been built up, causing constructive effort to be held in general contempt; while gambling and graft were elevated to the dignity of a profession; and those who were satisfied with moderate and constructive success were generally considered about as brilliant as a swine. At last the unsoundness of the structure began to be seen. It is not fully seen even yet, for it is only dimly recognized that a moral debacle contains economic dynamite. But enough was seen to cause confidence to waver: losses, inaction, and unemployment resulted; while prices went downward and credit tightened. That was sufficient to cause confidence to collapse: then liquidation or liquidity became the order of the day. Money, or some form of fixed money value, was the only thing that people retained full confidence in; and they naturally turned to it, and held to it, as being the safest security against loss. Credit virtually ceased to exist. Bankruptcy, unemployment, and stagnation became dominant. THE DREAMS OF 1929 HAD BECOME NIGHTMARES BY 1933. 

But all this was the natural result of an inadequate and inflexible medium of exchange; and any effort on the part of government to coerce people into spending, or banks into lending, is economically unsound, and is absolutely unjustifiable. Without proper assurance of safety money cannot sensibly be expected to be put into action; and without reasonable hope of profit any attempt to "scare" it into action is likely to do more harm than good. The innumerable links of the profit system

form a chain that automatically functions as long as the system proceeds on a profit basis; but it can neither be persuaded nor forced to function on a profitless basis; for every link in the chain instantly crumples and refuses to act without the profit motive. Nothing less than the most absolute regimentation and the most grinding tyranny conceivable could overcome this human factor in economics. 

Perhaps it is easier for us to perceive how gold or gold base money fails to maintain its effectiveness as a medium of exchange by considering some suppositional illustrations. 

Suppose we should make radium our sole money. There is admittedly great value in radium; but its total quantity is not more than a few ounces, and it is not practically producible. It is easy to see that this small quantity might fall into a hoarded or inactive condition. And it is easy to see that it would make no difference whether held in inaction by one man or by many, nor whether held in inaction for vicious purposes or for reasonable purposes, as it would be the simple fact of hoarding or inaction that would keep the radium out of circulation, without regard either to the method or the motive. And it is easy to see that business would then be thrown completely onto barter and credit, which manifestly cannot maintain modern civilization. 

Again: suppose that human hair had possessed a high intrinsic value, and had acquired the prerogative of being the sole medium of exchange. Suppose our grandfathers had possessed just enough surplus human hair for fairly satisfactory transaction of their business. Our supply of human hair would have increased over their supply, but the increase would have been vastly less than the increase in business transactions. Consequently we should have been forced into a vast expansion of credit, payable in human hair; and into a vast increase in the velocity of circulation of the human hair. Then when confidence in credit weakened the demand for human hair would have jumped; for every man would have been inclined to grab and to hold human hair, as being the only thing with which he could meet his obligations, and on which he could not suffer price decline. The same thing that increased demand would have stagnated circulation. Now human hair, like gold, is producible; but with impossible slowness. And men would soon have been shearing their women, and applying hair-growing nostrums to their own heads, in a vain effort to meet their desperate need for human hair.

What we manifestly must have is a money that is both sound and dynamic-a money that represents a full intrinsic value; and at the same time is producible by constructive effort to an extent that will forestall the possibility of serious shortage or inaction of it. In other words, we must have a money such as gold would be if it were producible by honest labor at the rate of an ounce per week per man. (This is only intended as a practical illustration. To be an exact illustration gold would have to be increasingly producible-tallying with general production progress.) 

But gold does not meet these requirements. It is abundantly sound, but it is fatally inadequate. This will hardly be denied concerning gold money in metallic form. We admit that its effective quantity as a medium of exchange can be increased by reducing the gold reserve for paper currency; but every step in this direction reduces the soundness of the currency; and the ultimate possibilities of the process are very limited. We may lessen debt burden, and obtain some temporary relief, by reducing the gold content of the dollar, if we issue currency against the profit; but that process adds nothing to the ultimate volume of our medium of exchange, and leaves the basic cause of our trouble untouched; and it is in every moral sense an action in bankruptcy.

Of course there is a possible method of forcing gold to perform the functions of a medium of exchange efficiently—a method that was boldly proclaimed by Mr. Hoover during a former administration, and that was kept amazingly clear of by Mr. Hoover

during his own administration. I refer to the proposal to guarantee reasonable income to all who are willing and able to earn it, by offering unlimited constructive governmental employment, with means provided by properly calculated taxation. This method would have no effect on the volume of money, but it would forcibly increase its constructive activity perhaps sufficiently to counteract its shortage. I formerly believed in this method, but I have instinctively doubted its practicality all through this depression. Yet, if the gold worshippers prefer this method to any other, and if they will translate that preference into suitable action, then I have little quarrel with them. But suitable action in this line does not comport with Hoover's inaction; and it does not comport any better with Roosevelt's R. A. & H. G. wage scale, nor with his boondoggling projects, nor with his mounting deficits.

It is frequently suggested that shortage of a medium of exchange can be overcome by printing fiat money. But the advocates of fiat money seem to overlook the fact that the only fixed value that can be given to money by governmental decree is the power to pay debts. The purchasing power of money is due ultimately to confidence on the part of those accepting it that its purchasing power will continue unimpaired. 

If that confidence could be sustained corn cobs would make perfectly good money.

But that confidence is not subject to regulation by law. That degree of confidence in money that is necessary for what we call prosperity is best secured by the assurance that the money is redeemable in full intrinsic value. 

Probably it would be possible to devise a technically fiat money that would be fully sound and efficient. But its method of issue would have to be fixedly such that it would automatically expand with the expansion of sound demand for money, and automatically contract with the contraction of that demand. Fiat money cannot but be dangerous if issued arbitrarily even by the wisest and best of men; and ruinous if issued thus at the whim of popular clamour.

And assuredly fiat money can be made to serve half-way the purposes of a medium of exchange; for there is a point at which the natural resistance to questionable money breaks down under the compulsion to transact business. But this factor can operate only when we are in that very condition which we wish to avoid, stagnation of constructive business.

FULL PROSPERITY IS ABSOLUTELY IMPOSSIBLE UNDER ANY MONEY SYSTEM THAT CREATES RESISTANCE TO CONSTRUCTIVE 

TRANSACTIONS. And the result is the same whether the resistance originates with the consumer or buyer, as is natural in case of money shortage; or whether it originates with the producer or seller, as is natural in case of questionable money. 

BUT A MONEY SYSTEM IS POSSIBLE THAT WILL ELIMINATE BOTH TYPES OF RESISTANCE-A SYSTEM THAT WILL ELIMINATE INADE- QUACY WITHOUT ENDANGERING SOUNDNESS—THE PLAN SET FORTH ON PAGE TWO. 

A gold dollar is valuable to anybody but a goldsmith only because it is exchangeable for a dollar's worth of the commodities we need. The value of money, as money, lies wholly in the commodity purchasing power; and nothing is more certain than that paper is as good for a medium of exchange as gold as long as we are assured that it will give us the same commodity purchasing power as gold. The usefulness of gold base for paper money is limited to the single point of sustaining confidence in the soundness, that is, the continued commodity purchasing power, of the paper money. 

Manifestly it can add nothing to that confidence above what would be produced if the commodities themselves could be used as the base; for in that case even the fluctuations of the gold would be eliminated, and the general purchasing power of the paper would be exact and perpetual. 

Gold, as we use it, is simply a convenient link between the paper and the commodities. It is the inadequate volume and the non-expanding nature of this link that 10

causes our trouble. Commodities are reasonably plentiful and may be easily increased, and paper is easily printed; but this link of gold has our money system, and consequently our business, bottle-necked.

Now if all commodities possessed the necessary attributes of a money base—if every form of wealth could be stored and held as a guarantee for paper money—the solution of the difficulty would be apparent; for admittedly gold as a wealth could not possess an advantage over general wealth itself, except for its liquidity; and the liquidity would be given to the general wealth by the paper. 

But most commodities and values in the final form in which we use them are utterly lacking in one or more of the essential qualities of a money base. Some are not transportable; some are not measureable; and few are sufficiently durable. However a sufficiently large number of commodities, in a comparatively raw state, possess the necessary attributes of a money base so that a list may be selected that will constitute in itself a substantially balanced wealth. 

MONEY ISSUED AGAINST THAT LIST OF COMMODITIES WOULD OPERATE ON EXACTLY THE SAME PRINCIPLE, AND WITH EXACTLY THE SAME SOUNDNESS, AS OUR GOLD CERTIFICATES DID. BUT IT WOULD POSSESS THE ALL-IMPORTANT ADVANTAGE OF INCREASING IN ACTIVITY IN PROPORTION TO THE INCREASE IN SOUND PRODUC- TION, AND OF EXPANDING IN VOLUME IN PROPORTION TO THE INCREASE IN SOUND ACCUMULATION. 

This would result in the specific effects listed on page three of this paper. 

We can certainly depend on the age-old economic factors of common sense and self-interest to give us balanced production when balanced production is absolutely assured of a profitable market, and when unbalanced production lacks that assurance. 

And unless the depression has turned men into something else beside men we can depend on them producing at general capacity speed when they are absolutely assured of a profitable market.

The purchasing power of the dollar in terms of general basic commodities would be permanently stabilized. If Mr. Roosevelt honestly "seeks the kind of a dollar which a generation hence will have the same purchasing power as the dollar we hope to attain in the near future" this is his kind of dollar. The uncertainties due to unstable employment would be reduced to a minimum by the best possible form of insurance—the elimination of the risk. Long-time investments, improvements, and expansion would be relieved of the uncertainties injected by boom and depression irregularities, and placed on a basis approaching an actuarial science. 

And a proper premium would be placed on productive efficiency. I admit that it is better to pay ten men a certain sum to dig a ditch by hand than to pay one man onefifth of that sum to dig it by machinery if the other nine must starve. But either of these dispositions of the machine problem is senseless. We must use and improve the machine; and turn those displaced by it to useful production in other lines. The currency suggested would do this automatically, and without arbitrary regimentation; with no harm except change in occupation. That may not be pleasant, but there is no remedy for it when it becomes necessary. Means can be and should be devised to soften the blow; but it cannot be avoided-it is useless for the buggy maker to mourn because his occupation has been killed by the automobile-but it cannot be considered disastrous when one can turn to something else. 

Stated briefly, the effects of this currency on business would be exactly the same as for the government to buy all surplus of balanced production at a fixed and profitable price. But the effect on the government would be very different; for the one thing would cost the government practically nothing, while the other would probably bankrupt it.

Stated differently, but in an equally understandable way, the effects of this currency on business would be the same as raising the price of gold and making unlim- 11

ited purchases would have been if all who were unemployed could have turned with reasonable profit to gold production at the increased price. 

Perhaps a superficial glance at the stated effects of this plan may leave the impression that it is fantastic. But a careful consideration will convince even those who are not economists that the plan is neither a sure-fire nostrum for the calamities of mankind; nor a lift-yourself-by-your-bootstraps proposition. It will not eliminate crime, nor ignorance, nor selfishness; and it will not produce wealth without work. 

BUT IT WILL PROMPTLY AND PERMANENTLY CURE ECONOMIC- ALLY ENFORced idleneSS. IT WILL ENABLE CAPITAL, FACILITIES, RESOURCES, AND MEN TO OBTAIN PROFITABLE AND CONTINUED WORK-AND THAT SPANS THE WHOLE GULF BETWEEN DEPRESSION AND PROSPERITY. 

Now full employment under modern methods and normal conditions must admittedly result either in fabulous accumulation of wealth, or in luxurious consumption of wealth. And I think it is unnecessary to prove that greater wealth and more abundant life are desirable.

Of course this plan does not completely forestall the possibility of a major disaster such as a radical climatic change, or a devastating war, holding even capacity production below necessary consumption. But it would go farther than any other conceivable plan toward the elimination of that danger; for the accumulations of a few normal years would serve to stabilize the dollar absolutely, and to meet the emergency needs completely, in any disaster for which our experience offers a parallel. And if such a disaster were unparalleled then we might become poor; but as long as we held together as a nation we should all be constructively busy in repairing the damage, instead of vainly searching for work; and we should have the satisfaction of knowing that our condition had been produced by causes beyond our control, and not by asinine stupidity. (Mr. Roosevelt has lately indicated that he opposes any program that tends to shipping of our soil fertility to other lands with inadequate compensation. Good! 

But when a man can see this highly important but highly subtle point, why can he not see the superiority of a balanced physical wealth over a metal that may lose most of its value under emergencies that are even now portentous?) 

This plan is undoubtedly subject to several objections, some specious and some valid, which are worthy of attention. But I am convinced that none of them are serious.

A specious objection to the plan is that the accumulated commodities would only constitute an ever-growing and useless surplus. The statement of this objection is exactly equivalent to saying that the nation would become too rich. The balanced list of commodities suggested would constitute real wealth. How then could we get too much of them? These commodities would absolutely constitute a more stable and a more perfect form of wealth than gold-and who feared the accumulation of too much gold? I think it is self-evident that the very statement of this objection would be an outstanding evidence of the merits of the plan, if the premise on which it is based were correct. But I do not think that premise-that the accumulation of the commodities would continue uninterruptedly-is correct. I think it is quite certain that under the stimulus of full prosperity consumption would rapidly approach, and might even overtake, production; for there is no limit to possible consumption. 

Another specious objection to the plan is that it would not assure balanced production of anything except the commodities listed; and that this would leave many raw materials, and most finished products, subject to unbalanced production. But under normal competition this condition will take care of itself; not perfectly of course, but as nearly so as profit motives can produce calculations for. Some may ask, Then why did it not take care of itself in the past? I answer, Simply because of our straight-jacketed money set-up. Whatever unbalance of production existed at the beginning of this depression would probably have been corrected before now by the 12

producers changing vocation, or type of product, if they had not run into the snag of nothing to change to. Gold would have kept us reasonably near to balanced production if its production could have been sufficiently accelerated by a slight rise in its value. If, as men became unemployed in other lines, they could have turned with only slightly less profit to the production of gold there could have been neither serious unbalance nor serious depression.

A valid objection to the plan is that however wisely a commodity list might be chosen there would exist a possibility of some of the items losing their value through the discovery of substitutes, or the improvement of the commodity. But the objection is of little practical importance; for there could be no sensible danger of any considerable portion of the items taking such a course in relation to items not included in the list; and of course any transfer of value from one item to another among those included in the list would have no effect whatever. Serious danger from this source would be less than the danger of gold itself going wild through the discovery of an unprecedented amount of it, or of a cheap process of obtaining it. 

Another valid objection to the plan is the cost of physical storage of commodities. (Cost of deterioration practically resolves itself into cost of storage; for proper care practically eliminates it in regard to most commodities; while the occasional exchange of old crops for new practically eliminates it for others.) Now I am not interested in mathematical "demonstrations" that such costs would consume everything in a few years, for several reasons, the most patent of which is that the same kind of cost is chargeable against gold; and I have never known a mathematical nit-wit to waste his time figuring that gold storage costs would consume us, even in a million years. 

Considering the case practically, it must be kept in mind that only the surplus of production over consumption of the commodities would be stored; and that might be much less than we suppose; for most of the increase in national wealth must naturally go into other things than money base, whether that be gold or commodities. And if the accumulations should grow to prodigious proportions it could be only because of unparalleled prosperity, and unparalleled accumulation of wealth; and if any man thinks storage costs would be too high a price to pay for that he is mentally hopeless. 

It may be objected that the plan involves commodity price-fixing and price-inflation. But the price-fixing would apply only to the integrated list of commodities; leaving the relative price of the individual items practically subject to the law of supply and demand. And the price-inflation would be only the amount necessary to restore pre-depression parity with money and debts; and surely that is what we have long needed, provided that the price is frozen at that point, and has been reached by sound means. Ordinary money inflation is as dangerous as it is represented to be, but that is because it is neither fully sound nor fully controllable. And inflation of commodity prices by means of deliberately produced scarcity is the work of a criminal or a madman.

It may be objected that under the plan no listed commodity could be released from storage without release of a proportionate amount of all other listed commodities, however sharp the need for the single commodity. There might be danger in this if the list were necessarily inflexible, in case of an emergency due to a shortage of one or of a few of the commodities listed. But even then it would be far less dangerous than deliberately reducing production, for it could not cause more than expensive difficulty, while that deliberately invites disaster. But the list need not be absolutely inflexible. It certainly should not be tampered with for light reasons after being established; but it could be changed in case of emergency, without harm and with positive benefit. By way of illustration: suppose crude oil were on the list, with a hundred million barrels in storage as currency base; suppose a war arose requiring unexpected quantities of crude oil; suppose the price advanced to double the normal price, with the supply still inadequate to meet the necessity. Then Congress could remove crude oil from the currency base list; release the amount in storage; and replace it on the list with some other commodity in sufficient quantity to equal the value of the crude oil released at the time of its removal from the list. Neither the government nor the currency would suffer any loss from the transaction; and the 13

exorbitant rise in the price of the item would be checked. (Would not this help appreciably to curtail war profits, and at the same time to strengthen national defences; and do it without tyrannous regimentation?)

Any objection to the plan on the ground that it is radical must be answered by inquiring as to the meaning of the term. The term radical may mean drastic, or it may mean socialistic. And while even the Chief of the New Deal might call the concept of the plan drastic; yet even the shade of Warren Harding would not call it socialistic. And for our natural and very reasonable hesitancy in adopting drastic change I think it is sufficient motive that the disease itself is drastic, if not fatal; and that no less drastic remedy can effect a permanent cure. 

It is not claimed that this plan would be highly effective for a nation that necessarily depends for a prosperous standard of living on a heavy surplus production of a few items, and the exchange of that heavy surplus for foreign goods; or in other words, for a nation that is incapable of a reasonable approach to balanced production. 

For such a situation automatically throws the calculation of values very largely onto gold, or foreign currencies, regardless of the internal money system. But the United 

States is not thus situated. 

I am not attempting, in this paper, to discuss the details of practical application of the plan. Determination of the specific items to be included in the list; the method of integration of items to be followed; the details of changing systems; and many similar matters, would present problems for solution. But they would require only the application of that degree of intelligence and common sense that is necessary for the success of any important undertaking. And it is my purpose here to present merely an understandable plan, not a draft of a law. 

I wish to point out specifically that in commending profit motives I have not intended to condone profiteering; that in commending competition I have not intended to condone cut-throat business; that in commending the motive of selfinterest I have not intended to condone disregard for others. My feelings toward that type of "Rugged Individualism" that would leave economic power unrestrained in its opportunity to oppress on the ground of motive necessity are precisely the same as my feelings toward that type of beastiality which would leave physical power unrestrained in its opportunity to rob, to rape, and to murder on the ground of biologic necessity—a hatred so intense that in spite of life-time principles, and of stern resolution, it is not entirely safe. 

And I wish to point out specifically that I am not advocating any new principle, nor any theoretical experiment, in regard to money. In those cases we may well be extremely cautious in trusting our ability to foresee results clearly. But what I am advocating is simply an extended application of time-tested principles. Economists do not seem to have questioned the soundness and the feasibility of currency such as is here advocated; but their conception of the matter seems to have been nebulous, and their attention to its superficial; for they seem to have entirely overlooked its power to eliminate depressions and maintain uninterrupted prosperity. Not seeing the necessity for a normally producible medium of exchange, they dismissed any suggestion for an extended money base as we might dismiss a suggestion for dual wheels on a passenger automobile-as sound but superfluous. (Of course I am here speaking of the type of currency definitely advocated in this paper, and not of that highly theoretical scheme of so-called commodity currency that would leave us tied to gold, but with a sliding scale for the content of gold, operating according to the index of commodity prices.)

And I wish to point out specifically that I am not predicting that this depression will last forever if we continue with our present money system. Under our bottleneck money system it is the nature of depression finally to "wear itself out" by pro- 14

ducing its own antitoxin, or money activity and credit expansion; but it is equally the nature of prosperity to be inoculated with the germ of depression, or unstable credit expansion. THUS THE BUSINESS CYCLE. Almost certainly the cycle would have already matured in this depression if it had not been prevented by Hoover's and Roosevelt's tinkering. This kind of recovery is better than perpetual depression, and better than universal regimentation; yet it is always brutal, and naturally slow, and never permanent. FOREWORD 

I am not primarily a student of economics. But I have concentrated for twenty years on preparation for basic work in a line that indirectly includes all social problems within its scope.

The business cycle, or the alternation of prosperity and depression, has long been observed and deplored; but the obscurity of its cause, and the preposterous nature of remedies proposed, have caused it to be generally accepted as inevitable. But the experience of the past six years of depression has taught us that the very foundations of civilization are in danger unless a remedy for the malady can be found and applied. 

The seriousness of the problem has been fully recognized; and the ablest minds have concentrated their energies on its solution; but probably no person whose judgment is worthy of attention believes that any plan or effort yet applied constitutes a satisfactory and permanent remedy. 

Mr. Roosevelt assures us that we are progressing. But with public finances pointing dangerously toward bankruptcy; with the standard of living sharply lowered by a systematic destruction unparalleled in human history; with intolerable conditions of unemployment remaining nearly static; and-most damning of all-with a monstrous and irresponsible bureaucracy hell-bent to undermine both moral character and personal liberty by regimentation, we may well ask Mr. Roosevelt, WHERE? 

But Mr. Roosevelt objects to destructive criticism. And if he refers to those who have been weighed in the balances and found wanting, and who still offer no solution for our problem except the suggestion of a return to the fossilized formulas that brought us where we are, he may be justified. And however sharp my criticism of present or past policies may be, it is accompanied by what I am perfectly convinced is a practicable plan for restoration and maintenance of prosperity. 

Unfortunately the merits of this plan are not so "self-evident" as with those of the Townsend variety. However it is neither a Chinese Puzzle nor an Einstein 

Theory; and it can be fully understood by all classes, with a little study and discussion. EXPOSITION 

The subject of money is a tough one; and this exposition is intended only as a skeleton outline. It would have been easy to render each separate point clear by means of extended explanation and illustration; but the process would have defeated its own purpose by leaving the reader "unable to see the town for the houses". The character of money might be compared to the architectural character of a building: a correct conclusion as to architectural character may be reached promptly by viewing a building as a whole; while it may be slow and difficult, though it certainly is possible, to reach the same conclusion by means of calculations made only from the size and location of each separate piece of material in the building. And only common sense and common knowledge are necessary for arriving at a correct general conclusion concerning this plan; but it is an intricate and difficult matter to build up the premises that lead to that conclusion. Accordingly, I wrote this plan, and stated its general effects, in perhaps two hours; while I have worked for about two years in preparing this exposition; yet an analysis of the cause and cure of depression was compulsory to me in a presentation of the plan.

Therefore I purpose to show, in the briefest space that admits of clearness, that our economic breakdown lies in our currency system rather than in some other part of our economic machinery; that the inability of gold or gold base currency to maintain prosperity lies in its inherent nature, and is not any accidental thing; that no other medium of exchange that has been used or generally considered is capable of maintaining prosperity; that the plan set forth in this pamphlet will permanently solve our difficulty; and that the application of that plan will not create evils comparable with those that it will correct.

We are told that this depression is due to over-production—that there is too much food, while millions of our able bodied people are unable to earn enough money to avoid hunger; that there is too much clothing, while millions of our industrious people are unable to earn enough money to avoid raggedness; that our utilities are overexpanded, while millions of our best people are unable to earn enough money to obtain their services. If that is sense then I belong beneath a slab on which is inscribed the epitaph, "He Outlived His Faculties".

MANY MILLIONS OF US COULD AND WOULD USE MORE FOOD, CLOTHING, SERVICES, ETC., IF WE HAD THE MONEY TO BUY THEM. 

AND WE COULD AND WOULD EARN THE MONEY TO BUY THEM BY PRODUCING THEIR EQUIVALENT IN VALUE IN OUR OWN OCCUPA- TIONAL LINE IF WE COULD OBTAIN CONSTRUCTIVE WORK. AND ORDINARY ECONOMIC MOTIVES WILL FURNISH THE WORK IF THE MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE FUNCTIONS PROPERLY. Men, facilities, and resources that are now idle would be producing useful goods, and exchanging them for the useful products of other men, facilities, and resources that are now idle, as they did in the most prosperous times, except for paralysis of the medium of exchange. 

By way of illustration: if Jones can produce a large surplus of food, but no clothing nor fuel; and if Brown can produce a large surplus of clothing, but no food nor fuel; and if Smith can produce a large surplus of fuel, but no food nor clothing, then all will have ample food, clothing, and fuel as long as they have an effective medium of exchange. But if they are barred from barter, as we are in the modern economic world; and if their medium of exchange fails to function, then, regardless of the cause of that failure, Jones must go ragged and cold, or consume his reserves of clothing and fuel, though he may be able to produce food for a city; Brown must go hungry and cold, or consume his reserves of food and fuel, though he may be able to produce clothing for an army; and Smith must go hungry and ragged, or consume his reserves of food and clothing, though he may be able to produce fuel for a navy. 

Herein lies the difference between poverty and depression: if Jones, Brown, and

Smith lose out on production they are headed for poverty; but if they lose out on mutual exchange they are in the grip of depression-they can produce their usual surplus, but they can reap no benefit from it. 

Now it certainly is possible to have unbalanced production—too much of some things in comparison with others, or rather, too little of the others. But when men undertake to tell us that there is an over-production of useful commodities in general they are either lunatics or criminals. They are simply telling us that the richer we become the poorer we are. And when they tell us that the right way to balance production is deliberately to reduce production of the items on the heavy side they are little better. DID THEY EVER THINK OF STEPPING-UP PRODUCTION OF THE ITEMS ON THE LIGHT SIDE? If we can "cure the evil of want in the midst of plenty by destroying the plenty" then I would suggest that we proceed to cure the social evil by hanging all the women. If there is any such thing as a plan in deliberately reducing production it is a plan equivalent to that of cremating a sick man in order to rid him of disease germs.

But the only important unbalance of this depression has been the unbalance of commodities in general with money. Otherwise let someone tell us what commodities held their own in money price. What then? Simply this, that we must have either a superfluity of commodities in general, or an inadequate money system. To assume that we can have a superfluity of commodities in general while millions of our people stand in desperate need of them solely because of their inability to obtain useful employment at profitable compensation is conclusive evidence of mental unbalance; so we must have an inadequate money system-a medium of exchange incapable of fulfilling its purpose.

In the utter confusion of thought that has prevailed concerning the subject various other causes have been assigned for this depression. BUT NOT ONE OF THEM 

EXPLAINS THAT APPARENTLY IMPOSSIBLE CONTRADICTION-CAPI- TAL SEEKING INVESTMENT, FACILITIES SEEKING OPERATION, AND RESOURCES SEEKING USE, SIDE BY SIDE WITH MANY MILLIONS OF DESPERATELY NEEDY MEN SEEKING WORK. 

The poor are inclined to ascribe the depression to the deliberate machinations of the rich. Bunk. Depression injures the rich along with the poor. Capital, facilities, and resources have endured enforced idleness as surely as men have. 

The rich are inclined to ascribe the depression to the extravagance of the poor during prosperity. More bunk. Few of us got so pot-gutted during prosperity as to render us unfit for work; and lack of profitable work, not lack of wealth, is what constitutes depression.

Some ascribe the depression to deliberate tie-up by the banks. Still more bunk. 

Generally speaking, depression brings grief, not benefit, to banks; and bankers are partly human-they have no desire to cut their own throats. 

Many ascribe the depression to the World War. Why not ascribe it to Napoleon? 

War could produce poverty; but it is forced idleness, not poverty, that ails us. 

Recently the depression has been traced to debts. It may yet be traced to sunspots. Debt is a bad thing; but how can it force us into idleness when we have ample capital, facilities, resources, and men? 

If an automobile collides with a building, solely because of the lack of brakes on the automobile, we do not place the blame on, nor state the cause of the accident to be, the existence of the building, although that particular accident could not have occurred if the building had not existed. This depression could not have occurred if there had been no debt, and probably not if there had been no war; but neither debt nor war were the cause of the depression. Which one of these assigned causes could have produced this depression if gold had been producible by honest labor at the rate of an ounce per week per man; or if the plan set forth on page two had been in operation?

THE SOLE CAUSE OF THIS DEPRESSION WAS A FORM OF MONEY INCAPABLE OF MEETING THE MONEY-DEMAND. GIVE US A SOUND MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE, THE PRODUCTION OF WHICH DEPENDS ONLY ON CONSTRUCTIVE EFFORT, AND THE PROSPERITY OF 1929 WILL BE EXCEEDED WITHIN A YEAR; AND IT WILL BE PERMANENT. 

It is well understood that money is a standard and official medium of exchange. 

Business cannot be efficiently transacted by means of barter. We cannot profitably trade wheat for automobiles, nor cotton for furniture. Money must perform the functions of a medium of exchange for these and all other commodities. ALL MOD- 

ERN BUSINESS AND PROSPERITY IS BASED ON THE SALE OF THE PRODUCTS OF EACH MAN OR UNIT OF MEN FOR MONEY, AND THEIR PURCHASE OF THE PRODUCTS OF OTHER MEN OR UNITS OF MEN WITH MONEY. 

The functioning of money depends solely on the confidence of those accepting it that its full buying power will continue. And consequently any money, however unsound, will function while confidence holds. Cardboard money would do it. But since perfect confidence could hardly be created, and could not possibly be sustained, in regard to unsound money, it is absolutely essential to permanent prosperity that money be given the soundest and most stable form that is humanly possible. 

The soundest and most stable form of money conceivable is either a properly adapted commodity, the actual intrinsic value of which is the amount for which it is accepted as money; or, an authoritative certificate of the ownership of such commodity. Our gold coins were an example of the one form, and our gold certificates were an example of the other form. Any other form of money involves the factor of economic ability, as well as that of integrity; whereas this form reduces all uncertainty concerning money to the single factor of integrity of government; and human limitations forestall any advance beyond that. 

Gold was originally a commodity like any other commodity; but it was a commodity outstanding in the qualities of being valuable, durable, transportable, and regular in quantity, to a greater degree than any other generally known and distributed commodity; so it naturally became generally accepted for the purpose to which it was so admirably adapted—a medium of exchange for all other commodities, or money.

For the limited transactions of early times gold itself was well suited to the functions of money. But increasing transactions rendered the actual transfer of the metal inconvenient and undesirable; so the expedient of paper money was adopted; the fundamental idea being to issue certificates in exchange for gold, and store the gold, and redeem the certificates by delivering the gold in exchange for them when demanded.

Paper money is much more convenient than gold, and is perfectly satisfactory in its functions as a substitute for the metal, as long as it is backed by a one hundred per cent reserve. But if paper money is backed by a one hundred per cent gold reserve then it necessarily follows that, whatever convenience the paper may carry over the metal, it cannot add anything to the volume of it. 

AND FOR MANY DECADES THE RATE OF PRODUCTION OF GOLD HAS BEEN MORE AND MORE RAPIDLY FALLING BEHIND THE RATE OF INCREASE IN BUSINESS TRANSACTIONS REQUIRING THE USE OF MONEY, WITH THE RESULT THAT GOLD, OR PAPER WITH ONE HUN- DRED PER CENT GOLD RESERVE, CANNOT EVEN APPROXIMATELY MEET THE REQUIREMENTS OF BUSINESS FOR A MEDIUM OF EX- 

CHANGE. (I have stated that business transactions requiring the use of money have increased enormously faster than the supply of gold has increased, and not that commodities have increased thus. As an illustration: I probably eat less bread than my

grandfather ate; so the per capita consumption of bread may have declined. But how about the business transactions requiring the use of money that are necessary to get the bread to my table? My grandfather probably grew his wheat on homesteaded land; he paid for his labor by exchange labor; his wheat was ground at the mill for toll; his bread was baked by his family's labor, by heat from wood secured by his labor. For other ingredients he probably paid an infinitesimal amount of money. Is that practicable now? The farmer's investment and expense are such that he must have money for his wheat; and the transporting agency, the milling concern, the wholesale company, the baker, and the retailer are under the same necessity of receiving money. This factor of increased specialization can scarcely be overestimated in comparing the present with the past. We have simply outgrown our money system. But it is idle to talk of returning to an economy in which an individual produces what he consumes, for every general step in that direction is a step backward from prosperity and high living standards.)

Subterfuges, such as the reduction of the gold reserve for paper money, were long ago devised to overcome the inadequacy of gold as a medium of exchange. But the principal thing resorted to has been the substitute for money, credit. 

Our past periods of prosperity have admittedly existed in spite of the money inadequacy of which we complain; and credit, acting as a substitute for money, has rendered this possible. Credit can act as a medium of exchange while general confidence in it is maintained; but it is not and cannot be a sound and stable medium of exchange; for it depends largely on the economic ability of millions of individuals, and on several other uncertain factors, instead of on the single point of integrity of government.

When confidence in credit weakens people instinctively turn to money itself, or to fixed money values, as being the safest security against loss that may arise from forced liquidation or from price decline. Money being utterly inadequate to meet the demand for confidence-producing security, it promptly obtains an entirely artificial value in relation to commodities, forcing commodities down in money price without respect to their intrinsic values. The fall in commodity prices fans the flame of economic conflagration by bankrupting those who are caught with money liabilities and commodity assets, and by rendering general business unprofitable and general investment unsafe. When the process has largely paralyzed the activity of money, and practically destroyed the credit that had served as an overgrown substitute for money, stagnation inevitably prevails. 

By 1929 credit in its various forms had been expanded until it had produced extravagant expenditures, fallacious investments, and wild-cat speculation in nearly all lines. A national frenzy had been built up, causing constructive effort to be held in general contempt; while gambling and graft were elevated to the dignity of a profession; and those who were satisfied with moderate and constructive success were generally considered about as brilliant as a swine. At last the unsoundness of the structure began to be seen. It is not fully seen even yet, for it is only dimly recognized that a moral debacle contains economic dynamite. But enough was seen to cause confidence to waver: losses, inaction, and unemployment resulted; while prices went downward and credit tightened. That was sufficient to cause confidence to collapse: then liquidation or liquidity became the order of the day. Money, or some form of fixed money value, was the only thing that people retained full confidence in; and they naturally turned to it, and held to it, as being the safest security against loss. Credit virtually ceased to exist. Bankruptcy, unemployment, and stagnation became dominant. THE DREAMS OF 1929 HAD BECOME NIGHTMARES BY 1933. 

But all this was the natural result of an inadequate and inflexible medium of exchange; and any effort on the part of government to coerce people into spending, or banks into lending, is economically unsound, and is absolutely unjustifiable. Without proper assurance of safety money cannot sensibly be expected to be put into action; and without reasonable hope of profit any attempt to "scare" it into action is likely to do more harm than good. The innumerable links of the profit system

form a chain that automatically functions as long as the system proceeds on a profit basis; but it can neither be persuaded nor forced to function on a profitless basis; for every link in the chain instantly crumples and refuses to act without the profit motive. Nothing less than the most absolute regimentation and the most grinding tyranny conceivable could overcome this human factor in economics. 

Perhaps it is easier for us to perceive how gold or gold base money fails to maintain its effectiveness as a medium of exchange by considering some suppositional illustrations.

Suppose we should make radium our sole money. There is admittedly great value in radium; but its total quantity is not more than a few ounces, and it is not practically producible. It is easy to see that this small quantity might fall into a hoarded or inactive condition. And it is easy to see that it would make no difference whether held in inaction by one man or by many, nor whether held in inaction for vicious purposes or for reasonable purposes, as it would be the simple fact of hoarding or inaction that would keep the radium out of circulation, without regard either to the method or the motive. And it is easy to see that business would then be thrown completely onto barter and credit, which manifestly cannot maintain modern civilization.

Again: suppose that human hair had possessed a high intrinsic value, and had acquired the prerogative of being the sole medium of exchange. Suppose our grandfathers had possessed just enough surplus human hair for fairly satisfactory transaction of their business. Our supply of human hair would have increased over their supply, but the increase would have been vastly less than the increase in business transactions. Consequently we should have been forced into a vast expansion of credit, payable in human hair; and into a vast increase in the velocity of circulation of the human hair. Then when confidence in credit weakened the demand for human hair would have jumped; for every man would have been inclined to grab and to hold human hair, as being the only thing with which he could meet his obligations, and on which he could not suffer price decline. The same thing that increased demand would have stagnated circulation. Now human hair, like gold, is producible; but with impossible slowness. And men would soon have been shearing their women, and applying hair-growing nostrums to their own heads, in a vain effort to meet their desperate need for human hair.

What we manifestly must have is a money that is both sound and dynamic—a money that represents a full intrinsic value; and at the same time is producible by constructive effort to an extent that will forestall the possibility of serious shortage or inaction of it. In other words, we must have a money such as gold would be if it were producible by honest labor at the rate of an ounce per week per man. (This is only intended as a practical illustration. To be an exact illustration gold would have to be increasingly producible—tallying with general production progress.) 

But gold does not meet these requirements. It is abundantly sound, but it is fatally inadequate. This will hardly be denied concerning gold money in metallic form. We admit that its effective quantity as a medium of exchange can be increased by reducing the gold reserve for paper currency; but every step in this direction reduces the soundness of the currency; and the ultimate possibilities of the process are very limited. We may lessen debt burden, and obtain some temporary relief, by reducing the gold content of the dollar, if we issue currency against the profit; but that process adds nothing to the ultimate volume of our medium of exchange, and leaves the basic cause of our trouble untouched; and it is in every moral sense an action in bankruptcy.

Of course there is a possible method of forcing gold to perform the functions of a medium of exchange efficiently—a method that was boldly proclaimed by Mr. Hoover during a former administration, and that was kept amazingly clear of by Mr. Hoover

during his own administration. I refer to the proposal to guarantee reasonable income to all who are willing and able to earn it, by offering unlimited constructive governmental employment, with means provided by properly calculated taxation. This method would have no effect on the volume of money, but it would forcibly increase its constructive activity perhaps sufficiently to counteract its shortage. I formerly believed in this method, but I have instinctively doubted its practicality all through this depression. Yet, if the gold worshippers prefer this method to any other, and if they will translate that preference into suitable action, then I have little quarrel with them. But suitable action in this line does not comport with Hoover's inaction; and it does not comport any better with Roosevelt's R. A. & H. G. wage scale, nor with his boondoggling projects, nor with his mounting deficits.

It is frequently suggested that shortage of a medium of exchange can be overcome by printing fiat money. But the advocates of fiat money seem to overlook the fact that the only fixed value that can be given to money by governmental decree is the power to pay debts. The purchasing power of money is due ultimately to confidence on the part of those accepting it that its purchasing power will continue unimpaired. 

If that confidence could be sustained corn cobs would make perfectly good money.

But that confidence is not subject to regulation by law. That degree of confidence in money that is necessary for what we call prosperity is best secured by the assurance that the money is redeemable in full intrinsic value. 

Probably it would be possible to devise a technically fiat money that would be fully sound and efficient. But its method of issue would have to be fixedly such that it would automatically expand with the expansion of sound demand for money, and automatically contract with the contraction of that demand. Fiat money cannot but be dangerous if issued arbitrarily even by the wisest and best of men; and ruinous if issued thus at the whim of popular clamour.

And assuredly fiat money can be made to serve half-way the purposes of a medium of exchange; for there is a point at which the natural resistance to questionable money breaks down under the compulsion to transact business. But this factor can operate only when we are in that very condition which we wish to avoid, stagnation of constructive business.

FULL PROSPERITY IS ABSOLUTELY IMPOSSIBLE UNDER ANY MONEY SYSTEM THAT CREATES RESISTANCE TO CONSTRUCTIVE 

TRANSACTIONS. And the result is the same whether the resistance originates with the consumer or buyer, as is natural in case of money shortage; or whether it originates with the producer or seller, as is natural in case of questionable money. 

BUT A MONEY SYSTEM IS POSSIBLE THAT WILL ELIMINATE BOTH TYPES OF RESISTANCE-A SYSTEM THAT WILL ELIMINATE INADE- QUACY WITHOUT ENDANGERING SOUNDNESS—THE PLAN SET FORTH ON PAGE TWO. 

A gold dollar is valuable to anybody but a goldsmith only because it is exchangeable for a dollar's worth of the commodities we need. The value of money, as money, lies wholly in the commodity purchasing power; and nothing is more certain than that paper is as good for a medium of exchange as gold as long as we are assured that it will give us the same commodity purchasing power as gold. The usefulness of gold base for paper money is limited to the single point of sustaining confidence in the soundness, that is, the continued commodity purchasing power, of the paper money. 

Manifestly it can add nothing to that confidence above what would be produced if the commodities themselves could be used as the base; for in that case even the fluctuations of the gold would be eliminated, and the general purchasing power of the paper would be exact and perpetual. 

Gold, as we use it, is simply a convenient link between the paper and the commodities. It is the inadequate volume and the non-expanding nature of this link that 10

causes our trouble. Commodities are reasonably plentiful and may be easily increased, and paper is easily printed; but this link of gold has our money system, and consequently our business, bottle-necked.

Now if all commodities possessed the necessary attributes of a money base—if every form of wealth could be stored and held as a guarantee for paper money—the solution of the difficulty would be apparent; for admittedly gold as a wealth could not possess an advantage over general wealth itself, except for its liquidity; and the liquidity would be given to the general wealth by the paper. 

But most commodities and values in the final form in which we use them are utterly lacking in one or more of the essential qualities of a money base. Some are not transportable; some are not measureable; and few are sufficiently durable. However a sufficiently large number of commodities, in a comparatively raw state, possess the necessary attributes of a money base so that a list may be selected that will constitute in itself a substantially balanced wealth. 

MONEY ISSUED AGAINST THAT LIST OF COMMODITIES WOULD OPERATE ON EXACTLY THE SAME PRINCIPLE, AND WITH EXACTLY THE SAME SOUNDNESS, AS OUR GOLD CERTIFICATES DID. BUT IT WOULD POSSESS THE ALL-IMPORTANT ADVANTAGE OF INCREASING IN ACTIVITY IN PROPORTION TO THE INCREASE IN SOUND PRODUC- TION, AND OF EXPANDING IN VOLUME IN PROPORTION TO THE INCREASE IN SOUND ACCUMULATION. 

This would result in the specific effects listed on page three of this paper. 

We can certainly depend on the age-old economic factors of common sense and self-interest to give us balanced production when balanced production is absolutely assured of a profitable market, and when unbalanced production lacks that assurance. 

And unless the depression has turned men into something else beside men we can depend on them producing at general capacity speed when they are absolutely assured of a profitable market.

The purchasing power of the dollar in terms of general basic commodities would be permanently stabilized. If Mr. Roosevelt honestly "seeks the kind of a dollar which a generation hence will have the same purchasing power as the dollar we hope to attain in the near future" this is his kind of dollar. The uncertainties due to unstable employment would be reduced to a minimum by the best possible form of insurance—the elimination of the risk. Long-time investments, improvements, and expansion would be relieved of the uncertainties injected by boom and depression irregularities, and placed on a basis approaching an actuarial science. 

And a proper premium would be placed on productive efficiency. I admit that it is better to pay ten men a certain sum to dig a ditch by hand than to pay one man onefifth of that sum to dig it by machinery if the other nine must starve. But either of these dispositions of the machine problem is senseless. We must use and improve the machine; and turn those displaced by it to useful production in other lines. The currency suggested would do this automatically, and without arbitrary regimentation; with no harm except change in occupation. That may not be pleasant, but there is no remedy for it when it becomes necessary. Means can be and should be devised to soften the blow; but it cannot be avoided—it is useless for the buggy maker to mourn because his occupation has been killed by the automobile-but it cannot be considered disastrous when one can turn to something else. 

Stated briefly, the effects of this currency on business would be exactly the same as for the government to buy all surplus of balanced production at a fixed and profitable price. But the effect on the government would be very different; for the one thing would cost the government practically nothing, while the other would probably bankrupt it.

Stated differently, but in an equally understandable way, the effects of this currency on business would be the same as raising the price of gold and making unlim- 11

ited purchases would have been if all who were unemployed could have turned with reasonable profit to gold production at the increased price. 

Perhaps a superficial glance at the stated effects of this plan may leave the impression that it is fantastic. But a careful consideration will convince even those who are not economists that the plan is neither a sure-fire nostrum for the calamities of mankind; nor a lift-yourself-by-your-bootstraps proposition. It will not eliminate crime, nor ignorance, nor selfishness; and it will not produce wealth without work. 

BUT IT WILL PROMPTLY AND PERMANENTLY CURE ECONOMIC- ALLY ENFORCED IDLENESS. IT WILL ENABLE CAPITAL, FACILITIES, RESOURCES, AND MEN TO OBTAIN PROFITABLE AND CONTINUED WORK-AND THAT SPANS THE WHOLE GULF BETWEEN DEPRESSION AND PROSPERITY. 

Now full employment under modern methods and normal conditions must admittedly result either in fabulous accumulation of wealth, or in luxurious consumption of wealth. And I think it is unnecessary to prove that greater wealth and more abundant life are desirable.

Of course this plan does not completely forestall the possibility of a major disaster such as a radical climatic change, or a devastating war, holding even capacity production below necessary consumption. But it would go farther than any other conceivable plan toward the elimination of that danger; for the accumulations of a few normal years would serve to stabilize the dollar absolutely, and to meet the emergency needs completely, in any disaster for which our experience offers a parallel. And if such a disaster were unparalleled then we might become poor; but as long as we held together as a nation we should all be constructively busy in repairing the damage, instead of vainly searching for work; and we should have the satisfaction of knowing that our condition had been produced by causes beyond our control, and not by asinine stupidity. (Mr. Roosevelt has lately indicated that he opposes any program that tends to shipping of our soil fertility to other lands with inadequate compensation. Good! 

But when a man can see this highly important but highly subtle point, why can he not see the superiority of a balanced physical wealth over a metal that may lose most of its value under emergencies that are even now portentous?) 

This plan is undoubtedly subject to several objections, some specious and some valid, which are worthy of attention. But I am convinced that none of them are serious.

A specious objection to the plan is that the accumulated commodities would only constitute an ever-growing and useless surplus. The statement of this objection is exactly equivalent to saying that the nation would become too rich. The balanced list of commodities suggested would constitute real wealth. How then could we get too much of them? These commodities would absolutely constitute a more stable and a more perfect form of wealth than gold-and who feared the accumulation of too much gold? I think it is self-evident that the very statement of this objection would be an outstanding evidence of the merits of the plan, if the premise on which it is based were correct. But I do not think that premise that the accumulation of the commodities would continue uninterruptedly-is correct. I think it is quite certain that under the stimulus of full prosperity consumption would rapidly approach, and might even overtake, production; for there is no limit to possible consumption. 

Another specious objection to the plan is that it would not assure balanced production of anything except the commodities listed; and that this would leave many raw materials, and most finished products, subject to unbalanced production. But under normal competition this condition will take care of itself; not perfectly of course, but as nearly so as profit motives can produce calculations for. Some may ask, Then why did it not take care of itself in the past? I answer, Simply because of our straight-jacketed money set-up. Whatever unbalance of production existed at the beginning of this depression would probably have been corrected before now by the 12

producers changing vocation, or type of product, if they had not run into the snag of nothing to change to. Gold would have kept us reasonably near to balanced production if its production could have been sufficiently accelerated by a slight rise in its value. If, as men became unemployed in other lines, they could have turned with only slightly less profit to the production of gold there could have been neither serious unbalance nor serious depression.

A valid objection to the plan is that however wisely a commodity list might be chosen there would exist a possibility of some of the items losing their value through the discovery of substitutes, or the improvement of the commodity. But the objection is of little practical importance; for there could be no sensible danger of any considerable portion of the items taking such a course in relation to items not included in the list; and of course any transfer of value from one item to another among those included in the list would have no effect whatever. Serious danger from this source would be less than the danger of gold itself going wild through the discovery of an unprecedented amount of it, or of a cheap process of obtaining it. 

Another valid objection to the plan is the cost of physical storage of commodities. (Cost of deterioration practically resolves itself into cost of storage; for proper care practically eliminates it in regard to most commodities; while the occasional exchange of old crops for new practically eliminates it for others.) Now I am not interested in mathematical "demonstrations" that such costs would consume everything in a few years, for several reasons, the most patent of which is that the same kind of cost is chargeable against gold; and I have never known a mathematical nit-wit to waste his time figuring that gold storage costs would consume us, even in a million years. 

Considering the case practically, it must be kept in mind that only the surplus of production over consumption of the commodities would be stored; and that might be much less than we suppose; for most of the increase in national wealth must naturally go into other things than money base, whether that be gold or commodities. And if the accumulations should grow to prodigious proportions it could be only because of unparalleled prosperity, and unparalleled accumulation of wealth; and if any man thinks storage costs would be too high a price to pay for that he is mentally hopeless. 

It may be objected that the plan involves commodity price-fixing and price-inflation. But the price-fixing would apply only to the integrated list of commodities; leaving the relative price of the individual items practically subject to the law of supply and demand. And the price-inflation would be only the amount necessary to restore pre-depression parity with money and debts; and surely that is what we have long needed, provided that the price is frozen at that point, and has been reached by sound means. Ordinary money inflation is as dangerous as it is represented to be, but that is because it is neither fully sound nor fully controllable. And inflation of commodity prices by means of deliberately produced scarcity is the work of a criminal or a madman.

It may be objected that under the plan no listed commodity could be released from storage without release of a proportionate amount of all other listed commodities, however sharp the need for the single commodity. There might be danger in this if the list were necessarily inflexible, in case of an emergency due to a shortage of one or of a few of the commodities listed. But even then it would be far less dangerous than deliberately reducing production, for it could not cause more than expensive difficulty, while that deliberately invites disaster. But the list need not be absolutely inflexible. It certainly should not be tampered with for light reasons after being established; but it could be changed in case of emergency, without harm and with positive benefit. By way of illustration: suppose crude oil were on the list, with a hundred million barrels in storage as currency base; suppose a war arose requiring unexpected quantities of crude oil; suppose the price advanced to double the normal price, with the supply still inadequate to meet the necessity. Then Congress could remove crude oil from the currency base list; release the amount in storage; and replace it on the list with some other commodity in sufficient quantity to equal the value of the crude oil released at the time of its removal from the list. Neither the government nor the currency would suffer any loss from the transaction; and the 13

exorbitant rise in the price of the item would be checked. (Would not this help appreciably to curtail war profits, and at the same time to strengthen national defences; and do it without tyrannous regimentation?)

Any objection to the plan on the ground that it is radical must be answered by inquiring as to the meaning of the term. The term radical may mean drastic, or it may mean socialistic. And while even the Chief of the New Deal might call the concept of the plan drastic; yet even the shade of Warren Harding would not call it socialistic. And for our natural and very reasonable hesitancy in adopting drastic change I think it is sufficient motive that the disease itself is drastic, if not fatal; and that no less drastic remedy can effect a permanent cure. 

It is not claimed that this plan would be highly effective for a nation that necessarily depends for a prosperous standard of living on a heavy surplus production of a few items, and the exchange of that heavy surplus for foreign goods; or in other words, for a nation that is incapable of a reasonable approach to balanced production. 

For such a situation automatically throws the calculation of values very largely onto gold, or foreign currencies, regardless of the internal money system. But the United 

States is not thus situated. 

I am not attempting, in this paper, to discuss the details of practical application of the plan. Determination of the specific items to be included in the list; the method of integration of items to be followed; the details of changing systems; and many similar matters, would present problems for solution. But they would require only the application of that degree of intelligence and common sense that is necessary for the success of any important undertaking. And it is my purpose here to present merely an understandable plan, not a draft of a law. 

I wish to point out specifically that in commending profit motives I have not intended to condone profiteering; that in commending competition I have not intended to condone cut-throat business; that in commending the motive of selfinterest I have not intended to condone disregard for others. My feelings toward that type of "Rugged Individualism" that would leave economic power unrestrained in its opportunity to oppress on the ground of motive necessity are precisely the same as my feelings toward that type of beastiality which would leave physical power unrestrained in its opportunity to rob, to rape, and to murder on the ground of biologic necessity—a hatred so intense that in spite of life-time principles, and of stern resolution, it is not entirely safe. 

And I wish to point out specifically that I am not advocating any new principle, nor any theoretical experiment, in regard to money. In those cases we may well be extremely cautious in trusting our ability to foresee results clearly. But what I am advocating is simply an extended application of time-tested principles. Economists do not seem to have questioned the soundness and the feasibility of currency such as is here advocated; but their conception of the matter seems to have been nebulous, and their attention to its superficial; for they seem to have entirely overlooked its power to eliminate depressions and maintain uninterrupted prosperity. Not seeing the necessity for a normally producible medium of exchange, they dismissed any suggestion for an extended money base as we might dismiss a suggestion for dual wheels on a passenger automobile-as sound but superfluous. (Of course I am here speaking of the type of currency definitely advocated in this paper, and not of that highly theoretical scheme of so-called commodity currency that would leave us tied to gold, but with a sliding scale for the content of gold, operating according to the index of commodity prices.)

And I wish to point out specifically that I am not predicting that this depression will last forever if we continue with our present money system. Under our bottleneck money system it is the nature of depression finally to “wear itself out" by pro- 14

ducing its own antitoxin, or money activity and credit expansion; but it is equally the nature of prosperity to be inoculated with the germ of depression, or unstable credit expansion. THUS THE BUSINESS CYCLE. Almost certainly the cycle would have already matured in this depression if it had not been prevented by Hoover's and Roosevelt's tinkering. This kind of recovery is better than perpetual depression, and better than universal regimentation; yet it is always brutal, and naturally slow, and never permanent. Moreover, the machine now casts an ominous uncertainty over this process.

All of us can see this factor, but probably none of us can weigh it accurately. But there is a possibility that technological advancement will displace labor as rapidly as increased money activity and credit expansion absorb them. And if that should be the case, and if capital holds its own in profits and labor holds its own in wages, then in spite of any business recovery we shall find ourselves waiting for the wearing-out process to relieve unemployment when Gabriel blows his trumpet; and ever-increasing millions of our able bodied and industrious men and women will be forced into the economic status of the hopeless inmates of the poor farms in pre-depression days-IF- 15
